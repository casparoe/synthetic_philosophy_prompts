I defend the view that an advance directive retains binding authority over a later self that has undergone severe psychological discontinuity, not through any preservation of consciousness, memory, or character, but through a relation I will call normative inheritance: the directive does not transmit the earlier self's preferences but establishes institutional constraints on present autonomy, functioning as a value-commitment that the later self is not sovereign to revoke precisely because it was designed to govern conditions in which autonomous revision is no longer possible. On this account, rejecting the directive is not analogous to disregarding a stranger's wish, but to violating a self-imposed rule whose point is to protect a diachronic value-trajectory from present distortion. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this position. Which conceptual distinctions—say, between preference-satisfaction and value-commitment, or between autonomy-preservation and autonomy-exercise—would undermine the inheritance relation? Which hypothetical cases—perhaps involving discontinuities so radical that no shared evaluative framework survives, or directives that conflict with the later self's emergent core values—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What broader results about personal identity, moral authority, or the ethics of care would the view have to accommodate or risk? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, and specify which ones a committed defender of normative inheritance could concede as counting against them without abandoning the core claim, and which they would be forced to dismiss. Finally, is this position well insulated from counterexample, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a sign that the view has been rendered unfalsifiable?
