I have been reflecting on a practice that does not yet exist but could easily emerge from current technology. Imagine a regulatory framework — let us call it merit-offset accounting — in which an actor is permitted to inflict a precisely measured harm, say ecological damage or psychological stress in one community, on condition that it funds an independently audited intervention elsewhere — for example, large-scale mental-health support — whose algorithmically calculated reduction in suffering exceeds the harm by a clear margin. The two are entered on a single moral ledger, and the actor is judged net-positive. This strikes me as sitting somewhere between Buddhist compassion and the kind of moral arithmetic found in consequentialist thinking. I am particularly curious what Śāntideva — the eighth-century Indian thinker who argued for radically exchanging one's own welfare for that of others — might say about it, since he never confronted quantified, tradable morality.
