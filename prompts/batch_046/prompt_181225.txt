My colleague has constructed an argument that, if sound, shows that anyone who accepts both the incommensurability of pleasure and pain and any form of lexical protection against extreme suffering is caught in a contradiction. I find the conclusion hard to swallow, but I cannot locate the error. The argument runs as follows:

(1) Pleasure and pain are phenomenologically incommensurable: there is no intersubjective calibration procedure that can translate a unit of suffering into a unit of joy, or establish a common exchange rate between them.

(2) If two values are genuinely incommensurable, then rational choice between outcomes that contain both must either (a) isolate them in non-interacting evaluative domains, or (b) invoke an external, non-comparative ordering rule that does not depend on weighing them against each other.

(3) Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism invokes exactly such a rule: once suffering exceeds a critical intensity, no quantity of pleasure—however vast—can justify allowing that suffering to occur.

(4) But a lexical rule is still a cross-domain comparative judgment; it asserts that suffering dominates pleasure regardless of magnitude, which presupposes that the two values occupy a shared normative space in which domination relations can obtain.

(5) Therefore, lexical negative utilitarianism implicitly treats pleasure and pain as commensurable, at least in the minimal sense required for one to lexically override the other.

(6) Consequently, accepting incommensurability together with lexical negative utilitarianism is contradictory.

(7) To resolve the contradiction consistently, one must drop the lexical rule and retain domain-isolated evaluation: suffering is evaluated solely within the domain of disutility, pleasure solely within the domain of utility, with no cross-domain veto permitted.

(8) Without such a veto, a decision procedure that maximizes value within the pleasure-domain is free to generate extreme suffering in the other domain, provided the domains are kept analytically separate.

(9) Thus incommensurability eliminates the conceptual machinery needed to block extreme suffering for the sake of aggregate pleasure.

(10) The only coherent position is therefore to reject incommensurability and treat pleasure and pain as fully commensurable on a single welfare scale, allowing direct trade-offs.

He finds (10) intolerable because it erases the qualitative asymmetry that makes suffering morally urgent in a way pleasure is not. I want to preserve both incommensurability and some form of lexical protection against extreme suffering, but I need to know which premise or step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Where does the argument go wrong?
