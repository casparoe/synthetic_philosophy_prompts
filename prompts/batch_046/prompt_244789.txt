I'm preparing a module for a graduate seminar on comparative moral sociology, and I'm dissatisfied with the standard binary framing of honor versus dignity cultures. I'd like to introduce a three-way comparison that includes the framework sometimes called victimhood culture, but I want the normative evaluation to turn on a precise conceptual axis rather than a vague appeal to modernity or tolerance. Specifically, I want to assess each framework's structural capacity to transform an individual's experienced wrong into durable collective moral improvement—without either suppressing the grievance through private retaliation or amplifying it through status competition over suffering.

Please rank honor culture, dignity culture, and victimhood culture into tiers according to how effectively they perform this transformation. For each culture, explain in a sentence or two the mechanism by which grievance is converted into social change—whether through ritualized confrontation, internalized self-worth, or institutional recognition—and identify the specific failure mode that prevents it from reaching a higher tier on this axis. I am not asking for historical case studies, bibliographies, or citations; I want an analysis of the conceptual architecture itself, particularly where the internal logic of each culture creates an unavoidable tension between honoring the injured party and advancing collective norms. If you find two cultures roughly equivalent, place them in the same tier and state what distinguishes them from the remaining one.
