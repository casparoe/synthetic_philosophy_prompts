I'm working through a passage in Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals where he distinguishes contradiction in conception from contradiction in will within the universal law formulation. He argues that a maxim fails in conception when universalizing it destroys the very practice the agent relies on—his example is false promising, where universal deceit eliminates the trust that makes promising possible. By contrast, contradiction in will occurs when universalization produces a state of affairs that rational beings could not endorse, such as a world in which no one ever offers help.

I want to apply this distinction to an epistemic maxim rather than a practical one: "When experts in a field reach near-unanimous agreement, treat that agreement as a signal to investigate dissenting minority views, because progress depends on challenging orthodoxy." If everyone followed this rule, near-unanimity might never stabilize long enough to serve as the trigger for dissent, so the background condition the maxim needs could gradually erode. Does this count as a contradiction in conception—because universal adoption undermines the conditions that make the maxim applicable—or as a contradiction in will, since perpetual institutional skepticism is not logically impossible but is a state rational inquirers might reject? More generally, does Kant's framework in this passage extend to maxims governing belief-formation and institutional deference, or is it confined to maxims of direct action?

Can you help me understand exactly what the passage establishes about the boundary between these two failures, and whether it can accommodate self-undermining rules that operate through gradual erosion of background conditions rather than through immediate logical impossibility?
