I'm a senior compliance officer at an international election-monitoring organization, and I need a reusable field-assessment protocol that our regional officers—who lack advanced legal training and must file a signed recommendation within 48 hours—can apply whenever a government in a country that still holds multiparty elections (for example, Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the United States) takes a concrete institutional action, such as restructuring an electoral commission, altering media-licensing rules, or changing judicial-appointment procedures.

The protocol must operationalize democratic backsliding, but I want it grounded in a specific two-dimensional conceptual distinction rather than treated as a single binary label. Frame backsliding as the simultaneous occurrence of: (1) a reduction in institutional friction—the removal or weakening of veto points, oversight bodies, or negotiation-forcing mechanisms that previously required the ruling party to deal with opposition or independent actors; and (2) partisan asymmetry in the remaining channels of contestation—meaning that after the change, opposition actors face systematically higher formal or procedural barriers to influencing outcomes than the governing party does, even if rights remain nominally intact on paper.

Write the deliverable as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, including concrete tests an officer can run using only publicly available information and standard news reporting (no specialized legal databases or local-language archival research). At each node, specify what counts as a clear pass, a clear fail, and an ambiguous result. Then provide a flagging rule that combines the node results into one of three outcomes: a "backsliding flag," a "monitor closely" designation, or a "no flag" clearance, with the threshold conditions for each stated precisely.

The procedure must handle the hard cases where supporters describe the same change as democratic reform, anti-corruption housekeeping, or efficiency improvement—such as court-packing justified as reducing judicial delay, or media consolidation justified as combating disinformation. Include three worked applications: one that clearly triggers the flag, one that clearly does not, and one that lands in the ambiguous zone where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and the regional director must sign off with an explicit written rationale.

Finally, identify where the procedure will misfire: false positives in which institutional friction was reduced symmetrically for all parties, and false negatives in which partisan asymmetry was achieved through informal pressure, selective enforcement, or extra-legal intimidation rather than through the formal institutional change being assessed. State explicitly what the protocol cannot do—what questions it must leave to constitutional lawyers, comparative historians, or local political scientists rather than resolving in the field.

Write in American English.
