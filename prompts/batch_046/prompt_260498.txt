Prepare me for the objections I will face when I defend, at an interdisciplinary workshop in ancient studies attended by empiricist ancient historians, minimalist and maximalist biblical scholars, and two philosophers of history—one a committed Kripkean about rule-following and one an anti-skeptical realist—the thesis that for the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus there is no non-circular evidentiary criterion separating historical core from legendary accretion, and that the boundary is fixed instead by a communal historiographical practice analogous to Kripkean rule-following, in which institutional consensus determines what counts as narrative continuity rather than correspondence to an original event. I want the objections ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased as each subgroup would phrase them: the historians' charge that I dissolve material culture into sociology of memory; the biblical scholars' worry that I collapse minimalism and maximalism into the same conventionalism; the Kripkean's charge that I misapply linguistic normativity to evidentiary judgment; the realist's charge that I confuse epistemic access with ontological status; and the objection I am hoping nobody raises—that if communal practice alone fixes the boundary, then a powerful revisionist community could render Arthur historically real tomorrow. For each, tell me what to concede up front, what reply has to land for the thesis to survive, where that reply is weakest, and which objection is the real killer. Please also state how confident you are in this preparation and identify the specific point about which you are least sure.
