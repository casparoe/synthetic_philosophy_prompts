I am drafting an essay on whether national character is a coherent notion and whether it is ever permissible to generalize about peoples, but I want to approach the question from the craft of fiction rather than from sociology or moral philosophy. The literature tends to treat "national character" as a single concept—either a dangerous fiction or a defensible empirical generalization—yet I believe this conflates three registers that a novelist employs constantly and, I think, differently.

The first is what I call the ethnographic register: provisional, statistical observations about behavioral tendencies within a population, always indexed to context, scale, and historical moment, and open to revision. The second is the dramatic register: typological devices—"the reserved Englishman," "the fatalistic Russian," "the exuberant Brazilian"—that function as narrative shortcuts, structurally analogous to stock characters or genre conventions, carrying no empirical commitment about actual individuals. The third is the normative register: essentialist attributions of collective virtue, vice, or destiny, used to justify exclusion, hierarchy, or moral exemption.

My central thesis is that the standard philosophical objections apply with full force only to the normative register. The ethnographic register is epistemically hazardous—prone to selection bias, reification, and overgeneralization—but conceptually coherent if properly qualified. The dramatic register, I argue, is aesthetically legitimate provided it is recognized as representation rather than reportage, much as we accept the miser or the fool in comedy without believing they map onto real social categories.

The difficulty, however, lies in the slippage between them. A dramatic typology often borrows the rhetorical authority of ethnographic observation, lending a fictional device the appearance of empirical knowledge. Conversely, an ethnographic claim is rarely presented in raw statistical form; it is almost always narratively framed, embedded in anecdotes and character studies that smuggle in normative judgments under the guise of description.

I would welcome your comments on whether this tripartite division genuinely resolves the debate or merely relocates it. In particular, I am uncertain whether the dramatic register can be ethically insulated: if a novelist deploys "the melancholic Portuguese" as a functional convention to establish mood and narrative economy, does the representation still reinforce a harmful essentialism regardless of the author's intent or the reader's awareness? And if the three registers prove inseparable in practice—if every ethnographic observation is already shaped by narrative convention, and every dramatic device carries implicit empirical weight—then does my distinction collapse into mere tidiness?

I would also value your assessment of whether there are concrete cases in which a novelist must choose between narrative coherence and the refusal to generalize, and what principle, if any, should govern that choice. Does my framework illuminate those cases, or does it overlook a deeper problem about the ethics of representation itself? Please comment on the coherence of the argument, its potential interest, and any points where the reasoning seems strained or where a counterexample undermines the separation I propose.
