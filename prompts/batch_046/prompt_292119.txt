Compare Nyāya's pramāṇas of upamāna (comparison/analogy) and śabda (verbal testimony) by analyzing whether analogical recognition can occur without prior verbal instruction, or whether it always collapses into testimony-assisted cognition. Consider the standard case in which an observer, having never seen a gavaya, is told by a speaker of uncertain general reliability that the animal resembles a domestic cow in shape and size; the observer later encounters one in the forest and correctly identifies it. Does this recognition depend primarily on upamāna as an independent source, on śabda despite the speaker's questionable authority, or on anumāna linking the present perception to the prior description? Use this case to argue whether upamāna retains genuinely autonomous epistemic authority within the Nyāya framework, or whether it is reducible to inference and testimony working together.
