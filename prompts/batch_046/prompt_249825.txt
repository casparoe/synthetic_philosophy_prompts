I'm currently taking a graduate seminar in rational choice theory, and we've spent several weeks working through the axiomatic foundations of expected utility. What I find conceptually slippery is not the mathematics but the psychological source of the violation: in the common-consequence framing, the shared outcome feels like an irrelevant background condition that should be factored out, which makes the axiom seem intuitive, whereas in the common-ratio framing the shared probability feels like a common causal mechanism that binds the two options together, so that separating them seems like breaking a real structural connection rather than applying a formal rule.

Could you give an intuitive, gut-level explanation of why this distinction between causal separability and probabilistic separability makes the independence axiom feel compelling in one frame but not in the other? Specifically, I'm trying to understand whether the paradox reveals that the axiom genuinely fails to capture a legitimate feature of reasoning about risk — perhaps because human agents correctly track that probabilistic independence does not always entail causal independence — or whether people are simply importing causal intuitions into a normative domain where only the probabilistic structure ought to matter. I'm less interested in the formal derivation of the contradiction than in a conceptual diagnosis of what kind of mistake, if any, is being made.

There's also a narrower conceptual point I'm trying to pin down: is there an established term for the specific failure mode in which an agent treats a probabilistically independent common factor as though it were causally coupled to the outcomes, thereby resisting the separation that the axiom demands? If there isn't a standard label, could you define the phenomenon precisely — distinguishing it from general ambiguity aversion and from the certainty effect — and indicate where its conceptual boundaries lie? I want to be able to identify it clearly in arguments about risk without collapsing it back into the Allais paradox itself.
