I hold the strong microhistorical position that recovering ordinary lives through notarial, judicial, and parish archives does not merely supplement elite- and state-centered history but reverses its explanatory priority. On this view, macro-structures — bureaucratic states, long economic waves, class formations — are retrospective abstractions rather than causal engines, and historical explanation should center on local transactions, treating institutional narratives as derivative constructions. What would count against this claim? I want the decisive and the merely awkward defeaters: empirical cases where macro-level fiscal or military pressures clearly reshape local practices from above; methodological objections about whether microhistory's reliance on exceptional documentary survival skews its sample toward the anomalous and the litigious; and conceptual worries about whether the position can absorb every counterexample by redescribing macro-patterns as emergent aggregates, thereby insulating itself from risk. Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss? Rank them, and say whether such insulation, if present, is a theoretical virtue or a vice.
