I am drafting a novel about characters who suffer in ways that dissociate sensation from affect and behavior, and I want my portrayals to rest on a coherent view rather than intuition alone. Here are my judgments about when pain is genuinely bad.

A patient with congenital pain asymbolia reports intense burning and sharpness from a flame, yet shows no withdrawal, no distress, and describes the feeling as "like bright red" rather than awful. I think her life is not made worse by this sensation; it is not a harm to her.

A patient given a drug that eliminates the unpleasantness of post-surgical pain but preserves full sensory discrimination—she can locate the incision precisely and describe its quality—seems to me to be in a state that is not bad for her, even though she reports that "it hurts."

A patient with congenital analgesia who lacks all pain sensation and repeatedly fractures bones is clearly living a worse life, but I think the harm comes from the injuries and the missing protective function, not from the absence of any particular feeling.

A masochist who experiences exactly the sensory and affective profile of severe pain but integrates it into a desired experience: I think the sensation is not bad for him in that context, though if the same neural pattern occurred against his will, it would be bad.

A stoic philosopher who feels the full sensory and affective negativity of pain but refuses to call it bad, treating it as "merely information": I think she is still being harmed; her refusal does not cancel the badness.

But I keep reversing myself on a case I invented for the novel. Imagine a "reverse asymbolic"—someone with no sensory experience of tissue damage whatsoever, yet exhibiting all the behavioral and emotional markers of agony: withdrawal, crying, terror, and a sincere report that she suffers immensely. She insists there is no "feeling" of burning or stabbing, only a pure, objectless dread localized to her body. Is she in pain? Is she being harmed? And if I say yes to harm but no to pain, does that mean pain's badness is separable from suffering?

Is there a principle that captures all these judgments—perhaps one that distinguishes phenomenal quality, affective valence, and functional role—and if so, which verdict must I abandon? In particular, what should I conclude about the reverse case, and does it imply that a novelist should render suffering as essentially tied to sensation, or as something that can float free of it?
