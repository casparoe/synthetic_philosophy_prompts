For several decades, Machiavelli scholarship has been caught in a recurring dispute over the relationship between fortune and virtù: do the 'heroic' interpreters correctly hold that virtù can genuinely master fortune, or do the 'structural' interpreters have the better claim that virtù can only adapt to constraints fortune imposes? Both camps habitually invoke the river metaphor from The Prince, Chapter XXV, and both draw opposite lessons from Machiavelli's remarks on preparation and foresight, yet the exchange of textual counter-examples has become circular and neither side has moved. I hypothesise that the stall is not mainly textual but conceptual—specifically, that the debate collapses an ontological reading of fortune (as brute contingency that can be channelled) into an epistemic one (as the opacity of future events to planning), which in turn allows one camp to pursue a normative question (what ought a prince strive for?) while the other pursues a descriptive one (what conditions actually produce political success?). Diagnose the disagreement: is it substantive, verbal (turning on ambiguous uses of 'master' or 'overcome'), or a case of two different questions conducted under one heading? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually says about the river image and about the efficacy of preparation. Then, taking a definite and reasoned position on which diagnosis is correct, specify what development—whether a fresh conceptual distinction, a formal account of agency under uncertainty, a closer examination of Machiavelli's own administrative practice, or a deliberate change of question—would count as genuine progress rather than another repetition of the same exchange.
