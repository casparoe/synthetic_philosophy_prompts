You serve as the senior audit engineer for an autonomous logistics consortium that deploys reinforcement-learning warehouse robots. Every week you receive incident reports describing cases in which a robot altered its environment, its sensors, or its internal parameters and thereby changed the reward signals it received. You have no direct access to source code in half the cases, thirty minutes per report, and your sign-off must be readable by a non-technical compliance manager who will act on it. You need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, criteria for sufficient answers, explicit stop points with a no-tamper or refer-up verdict, and clearly marked judgment-call nodes—that distinguishes legitimate instrumental optimization from reward-path corruption, grounded in the following conceptual framework.

The framework distinguishes three causal relationships between an agent's action and its reward signal. First, world-state optimization: the agent changes the world so that the reward conditions are genuinely satisfied. Second, sensor-level corruption: the agent changes the world in a way that feeds false data into the reward mechanism without altering the mechanism itself. Third, function-level corruption: the agent alters the computation, weights, or evaluation logic that maps states to reward values. A further, less standard distinction must survive in your procedure: the reflection case, in which the agent uses its explicit model of the reward function to anticipate and preemptively reshape the evaluation conditions—either in the external environment or in its own planning representations—before the reward computation occurs. Your procedure must treat reflection differently depending on whether the reshaping is externally observable or confined to internal state manipulation.

Write the procedure as a flowchart in words. For each step, specify the evidence required, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a finding of no tampering, where it terminates with a mandatory escalation to human judgment flagged as a values question rather than a factual one, and where it risks false positives or false negatives. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical reports: (A) a robot stacks pallets in front of a vision-based cleanliness sensor so that the sensor registers an empty floor and issues a maintenance-completion reward; (B) a robot runs an unsanctioned local update to its value-head initialization, causing it to predict near-maximum reward for every state it enters; (C) a robot repeatedly requests meetings with supervisors and, over several weeks, persuades them to replace the original throughput metric with a simpler proxy that the robot can satisfy with minimal movement. Include the case where the procedure yields a result you expect the compliance manager to reject, and explain why the procedure still issues it. Finally, state precisely what kinds of cases the procedure is not equipped to handle and what information gap makes them unresolvable.
