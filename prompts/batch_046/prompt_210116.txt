I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, currently writing a dissertation chapter on what I term "threshold-tragic" hard choices—cases in which an agent must select between goods that are not merely difficult to weigh but structurally resistant to being ordered on a single scale without destroying the value of the unchosen option. During a recent workshop on value pluralism, I sketched a distinction between these conflicts and ordinary trade-off conflicts, and a colleague responded by invoking the familiar slogan that "values are incommensurable," using it to claim that rational comparison across categories is impossible and that my framework therefore collapses into irrationalism. I am skeptical that the slogan supports such a strong conclusion, but before I reply I want to map out exactly what it could be asserting.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "values are incommensurable" might stand for, presenting them as a menu rather than selecting one? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false. In particular, I want you to address how each reading interacts with three distinctions central to my project: (i) whether incommensurability is an ontological feature of the values themselves or an epistemic limitation of agents; (ii) whether it blocks cardinal aggregation alone or also blocks any ordinal comparison; and (iii) whether it entails that there is no fact of the matter about which option is better, or merely that no single metric captures the comparison. For each reading, please say explicitly whether it actually licenses my colleague's dismissal of rational choice in cross-categorical conflicts, or whether it leaves room for my claim that agents can still be rationally required to choose even when values are not reducible to a common measure. Please answer in American English.
