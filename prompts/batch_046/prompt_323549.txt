I am applying for a six-week independent philosophy research fellowship for advanced high school students, and I need help sharpening my proposal. My proposed question is whether Friedrich Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence can be reconstructed as a formal constraint on the coherence of willing itself—specifically, a condition that transforms the structure of desire without reducing to either a cosmological claim about the physical universe or an ethical maxim asking whether one can affirm one's life. The familiar interpretations treat recurrence either as a speculative physical hypothesis or as a test for life-affirmation, but neither explains why Nietzsche presents it as carrying the greatest weight rather than offering it as a mere hypothetical. I suspect there is a third, logical reading: that to will X is implicitly to will the conditions under which X can be willed again, and that eternal recurrence makes this implicit structure explicit, thereby exposing an internal contradiction in certain reactive or resentful modes of desire. To pursue this, I will conduct a close conceptual analysis of the relevant passages in Nietzsche's middle and late writings, mapping the logical structure of willing and affirmation in those texts without importing modern psychological frameworks. I will then test the reconstructed argument against three objections: that it collapses into passive stoic acceptance, that it presupposes a substantial self capable of literal repetition, and that it is indistinguishable from a preference for high-risk behavior. A successful result would be a defensible reconstruction that explains the distinctive gravity of recurrence using only conceptual resources internal to Nietzsche's account of the will and amor fati. The project fails if the reconstruction either relies on metaphysical premises Nietzsche rejects—such as a transcendent soul—or collapses back into one of the two standard readings. Given the six-week limit, I will restrict my textual base to the primary recurrence passages and the surrounding discussions of the will to power; I will not attempt a comprehensive survey of secondary scholarship, though I will note where my reading diverges from common interpretations. Please turn this into a formal proposal that states the precise question, explains why existing answers leave it open, justifies this conceptual approach over empirical or purely historical alternatives, specifies what success and failure would look like, and confirms that the scope is realistic for a high school researcher working without archival access or citation databases.
