Design a 75-minute workshop activity for roughly 16 graduate students in environmental ethics. The goal is to have them feel, through play, the underappreciated tension between direct harm to farmed animals and the indirect wild-animal suffering embedded in crop cultivation—particularly the counterintuitive comparison between beef and chicken once field-mortality rates, nutritional yield, and the replaceability asymmetry between wild and domesticated lives are all in play. Do not open with a lecture. Instead, construct a collective-allocation game: participants draw hidden role cards (e.g., agronomist, ecologist, ethicist) and must distribute a fixed pool of land-use shift tokens across three pathways—intensive grain, pasture beef, or poultry—using only quantitative information cards you supply. Design a point-based payoff system tied to a shared group outcome so that individually reasonable moves aggregate into a surprising collective result. Include a mid-game revelation round in which hidden cards about rodent population dynamics and trophic-level substitution are exposed, forcing a recalculation. Provide a debrief that maps each game round to its corresponding philosophical move (intended versus foreseen harm, replaceability, counterfactual suffering), predicts the first-run failure mode (participants anchoring on visible farmed harm and neglecting hidden wild-cost cards), and frankly admits which ecological feature—likely long-run predator-prey equilibrium or the phenomenology of wild suffering—the simulation necessarily collapses. Avoid requesting bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. End with a one-sentence summary of the core tension revealed, on its own line beginning Summary:.
