In my philosophy elective, two classmates—Maya and Jonah—debated whether Bostrom-style simulation reasoning should dampen fanaticism about the far future. (1) Maya argued that if we are likely living in an ancestor simulation, the astronomical future longtermists invoke—trillions of digital lives unfolding across cosmic time—may never actually occur; it could be nothing more than a script with a runtime limit and a research purpose. She concluded that treating that future as a real moral target is like being fanatical about the ending of a television show we happen to be characters in. (2) Jonah replied that this confuses causal location with moral weight: suffering and flourishing inside the simulation remain genuinely real, and longtermism can rationally care about the trajectory of our world regardless of whether a base reality exists outside it. (3) Maya countered that longtermist expected-value arguments specifically depend on an open-ended causal chain within the actual universe; if our causal reach is capped by simulator goals, hardware constraints, or termination conditions, then the expected value of far-future intervention collapses, because we ought to discount heavily by the probability that we are not in the base reality with an unlimited future. (4) Jonah said this applies symmetrically—we do not know our nesting depth, so we might be at the bottom layer after all. He added that moral value might be layer-independent, and he introduced a further consideration: an unknown simulator audience could amplify rather than reduce the stakes, because if ethical behavior determines whether our civilization is archived, restarted, or deleted, then simulation uncertainty adds existential pressure instead of removing it.
