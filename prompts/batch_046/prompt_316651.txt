As a retired field ecologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science, I encountered Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle being distilled into the line "what costs nothing conveys nothing." The occasion was a seminar discussion of whether inexpensive, high-throughput computational simulations should be granted the same epistemic authority as long-duration, resource-intensive empirical studies that exhaust funding and careers. The speaker seemed to treat the principle not merely as an evolutionary explanation of ornamentation, but as a general epistemic rule: that rational observers should discount cheap verification methods, since if verification were cheap it could be faked by anyone. I am unsure whether this is a defensible extension or a category error.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses hidden in that slogan—separating evolutionary claims about why signals stabilize, epistemic claims about when observers are justified in trusting them, and normative claims about what scientists ought to invest in? For each reading, say whether it is tautological, empirically well-supported but philosophically innocuous, genuinely contentious, or false, and indicate which version the speaker actually needed to dismiss cheap simulations. I am especially interested in whether any reading commits us to a "methodological handicap principle"—the idea that scientific credibility requires deliberately inefficient procedures—and whether that survives once we distinguish costs borne by the investigator (who produces the signal) from costs borne by the audience (who verifies it).
