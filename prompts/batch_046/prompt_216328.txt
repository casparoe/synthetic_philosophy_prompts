In democratic-theory and public-choice writing, Condorcet jury reasoning is frequently analogized to an ensemble machine-learning classifier—typically a bagged or random-forest model composed of weak learners—to argue that aggregating many voters whose individual competence barely exceeds chance will, under independence, converge exponentially on the correct outcome, and therefore that large-scale democratic or jury institutions need not rely on expert citizens or heavy oversight. The analogy is usually implicit: each voter plays the role of a weak classifier with accuracy p > 0.5; the independence condition maps onto feature-randomness or bootstrap sampling; majority rule maps onto hard voting or averaging predictions; deliberation or institutional reform is treated as hyperparameter tuning, with mechanisms such as liquid democracy or quadratic voting cast as adjustments to sample weights or learner selection.
