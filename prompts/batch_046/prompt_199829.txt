After a long argument with a close friend about why human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves, I remain unsure where the disagreement actually lands. She defended what she called Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: that we care more about humanity's continuation after our own death than about personal survival, and that extinction is uniquely harmful because it undermines the point of present long-term projects—scientific programs, artistic traditions, institutional building—whose value is essentially spread across generations. I argued that the harm is simply the elimination of all future welfare and conscious experience; talk of undermined meaning is a psychological effect, not a distinct moral harm. If I contribute now to a cathedral that will never be finished, my contribution can still be valuable in itself; extinction truncates the chain but does not retroactively cancel present value. She replied that this misses a structural distinction: some goods are not merely accumulated over time but are constituted by completion across generations—multi-generational language preservation, theoretical research that only achieves coherence after centuries—so extinction does not just stop future welfare, it makes present rational commitment to those goods impossible in a way that personal death does not. I countered that this still reduces to expected outcomes: if I know extinction is imminent, I should redirect effort to goods realizable within my lifetime, which shows that present rationality is instrumentally tied to continuation, not metaphysically dependent on it. She said that precisely proves her point—if extinction systematically redirects rational commitment away from our deepest collective projects, then its harm is that structural redirection itself, separate from the missing people. Can you adjudicate our exchange move by move? Specifically: is the harm of extinction primarily about lost future welfare, or about the undermining of present meaning; is there a defensible category of goods that are essentially intergenerational rather than merely long-lasting; and does my redirection-to-synchronic-goods argument successfully dissolve her structural claim, or does it inadvertently confirm it? Please also indicate what argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your verdict, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
