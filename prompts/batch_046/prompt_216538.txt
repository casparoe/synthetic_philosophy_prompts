Compare Pascal's pairing of justice and force—commonly rendered as the claim that justice without power is impotent, and power without justice is tyrannical—with Machiavelli's argument in The Prince that effective rule requires both the capacity for violence and the strategic display of moral virtue. Rather than adjudicating which thinker is correct, determine whether their disagreement is primarily about institutional legitimacy, that is, how political order secures compliance, or about individual moral psychology, that is, whether the recognition of justice can be produced by coercive power alone. To test this distinction, apply both frameworks to the following constructed case: a state achieves perfect distributive fairness through an opaque, algorithmically enforced allocation system that eliminates deliberative participation and shared moral vocabulary, maintaining order solely through predictive surveillance and calibrated sanctions. Would Pascal regard this arrangement as justice aided by necessary force, or as force that has entirely expelled justice? Would Machiavelli assess it as a successful exercise of virtù, or as a failure to maintain the appearance of virtue that makes power durable? Based on how each thinker answers these questions about the case, argue whether they are addressing the same concept of justice under different conditions, or whether they operate with incompatible criteria for when coercion becomes tyrannical rather than supportive of justice.
