As a novelist drafting an essay for a literary quarterly, I am exploring the conceptual erosion of leisure under modern labor norms. I propose that the 35-hour week did not secure leisure but rather rationalized it into productivity-adjacent recovery, stripping away the conspicuous waste that once made idleness socially legible—a transformation I read through Veblen's lens, though updated for post-industrial regulation. I further suggest that karoshi represents not an accident of labor law but the secular fulfillment of Weber's Protestant ethic: when salvation anxiety is translated into GDP contribution, death by overwork becomes a kind of unacknowledged martyrdom, and the purported right to leisure collapses into an obligation to rest only so one may produce more. I seek your comments on two specific questions. First, does the conceptual bridge between rationalized leisure and sacralized overwork hold without empirical labor data, or does it require statistical support that would undermine the essay's philosophical aim? Second, is my framing—that rights to work and leisure become structurally incompatible once human worth is indexed to output—merely an ornate restatement of familiar anti-capitalist critique, or does the juxtaposition with karoshi as secular martyrdom genuinely alter the moral geometry? I would particularly value suggestions for sharpening the tension between the aesthetic failure of regulated idleness and the moral horror of overwork, without introducing policy recommendations or case studies that a general reader could not follow.
