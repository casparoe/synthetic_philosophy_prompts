As a retired physicist who spent three decades studying non-equilibrium dynamics and who has only recently turned to philosophy and decision theory, I have become fixated on the dollar auction not as a mere economic curiosity but as a conceptual laboratory for escalation of commitment. In my former field we often observed systems driven far from equilibrium that then exhibited path-dependent behavior looking irrational from a static viewpoint; the auction, in which agents continue bidding well past the prize value because withdrawal feels like an admission of prior error, strikes me as a direct analog in practical reason. I am especially interested in what I call the "inertial drift" of commitment—the way a decision-maker does not suddenly flip into irrationality but slides into it through a continuous accumulation of small, locally defensible steps, much like a particle gaining momentum inside a potential well.

With that framing in mind, I ask you to rank the following five explanatory accounts of escalation in the dollar auction—not by disciplinary prestige or citation frequency, but by how well each captures this specific phenomenon of inertial drift, and by how naturally each extends to analogous cases outside the auction room. Please organize them into tiers rather than a strict ordinal list, and in one or two sentences for each explain its placement. The accounts are: (1) the classic sunk-cost fallacy as interpreted in behavioral economics; (2) the strategic brinkmanship model, which treats continued bidding as rational signaling of resolve under incomplete information; (3) the social-audience model, which holds that escalation is sustained mainly by the presence of observers to whom the bidder must demonstrate consistency; (4) the evolutionary maladaptation hypothesis, which treats persistent commitment as a once-adaptive persistence mechanism that misfires in modern, low-stakes competitive environments; and (5) a speculative analogy I have developed from my earlier scientific work, which I term "thermal dissipation of commitment"—treating the psychological cost of withdrawal as an entropic barrier that can only be overcome by expending further "energy" (additional bids) until the system settles into a new equilibrium. I want a conceptual evaluation from first principles, not a literature survey; please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid naming specific scholars or studies unless absolutely unavoidable. My aim is to construct an intuitive map of which conceptual tools genuinely illuminate the slide into escalation and which merely redescribe it.
