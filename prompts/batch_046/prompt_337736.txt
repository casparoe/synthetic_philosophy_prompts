Over the last decade, several governments have institutionalized citizens’ assemblies formed by stratified random lottery—sortition—to deliberate on constitutional amendments, climate targets, and bioethical regulations. The design compensates participants at rates comparable to professional jurors or consultants, provides them with curated expert briefings and moderated deliberation protocols, and then asks them to produce specific policy recommendations that legislatures treat as having special democratic weight. The apparent tension is sharp: the selection mechanism treats every citizen as equally qualified to represent the public will, while the compensation, expert framing, and structured facilitation treat political judgment as a form of skilled labor that requires correction and remuneration. What implicit theory of democratic representation and epistemic authority does this bundle of practices presuppose? Must it hold that descriptive representation—looking statistically like the population—carries intrinsic democratic value, or can the same institutional form be justified purely instrumentally, for example as an anti-corruption filter? Does the reliance on guided deliberation presuppose that lay citizens lack relevant practical knowledge unless corrected by experts, or does it presuppose the opposite—that citizens possess a distinctive form of situated wisdom that experts and elected officials systematically miss? Reconstruct the implicit political epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and identify where the design is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories, such as a purely proceduralist defense of randomness or a competence-based elitist view, could license the same procedures. Finally, if the underlying view were replaced by a theory that treats civic judgment as an unrewarded duty rather than compensated labor, and that rejects descriptive representation in favor of selection for cognitive competence, which specific features of these assemblies—compensation scales, expert briefing structures, voting rules, or the status of recommendations—would have to change, and which could remain intact?
