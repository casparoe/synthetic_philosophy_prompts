I was discussing with friends over coffee a logic puzzle I recall from a popular science article: a teacher tells her class that there will be a surprise exam sometime during the next five school days, and the students reason backward, eliminating Friday because they would anticipate it, then Thursday, and so on, until they conclude that no exam can possibly occur—yet when Wednesday arrives they are genuinely caught off guard.

I have been trying to apply the same backward-elimination reasoning to a practical situation I am actually facing. My employer recently announced that there will be a surprise audit of our department at some point during the next fiscal quarter, with no one knowing the exact date. Following the students' logic, by the final day of the quarter I should be able to deduce that the audit must occur then, which means it would not be a surprise, allowing me to eliminate that day; repeating this reasoning seems to wipe out every possible date, implying either that the audit can never happen or that my employer's announcement is logically self-defeating.

Is there a hidden flaw in the students' reasoning that prevents it from carrying over to continuous, real-world intervals with uncertain conditions, or does the paradox genuinely show that scheduling any genuinely unpredictable event within a bounded window is conceptually impossible? I ask because if the reasoning holds generally, it seems to undermine not just classroom quizzes but any institutional plan—such as security inspections or compliance reviews—that relies on strategic unpredictability to maintain its effect.
