Below is the set of commitments I have reached on rational belief, closure, and the two classic aggregation paradoxes. Before I defend them in a seminar, I want a hard consistency check.

(1) Rational belief is binary and governed by a fixed, context-independent evidential threshold: I believe p just in case my credence in p is strictly above 0.99, with no stake-sensitive or domain-sensitive adjustment.

(2) Rational belief is closed under logical consequence for premises that are jointly believed and jointly cognitively represented: if I believe p₁ … pₙ, grasp that q follows from them together, and can represent q, then I ought to believe q.

(3) In Kyburg’s 1000-ticket fair lottery, I judge that it is rational to believe, of each specific ticket, that it will not win.

(4) In that same lottery, I judge that it is rational to believe that exactly one ticket will win.

(5) In Makinson’s preface, I judge that the author is rational to believe each specific claim Aᵢ in the book, and also rational to believe that not all Aᵢ are true.

(6) The lottery and the preface share an identical logical form: a large finite collection of beliefs each with individual credence above 0.99, together with a belief in a proposition that contradicts the conjunction of the collection but itself enjoys high credence.

(7) The correct unified resolution is to restrict closure to entailments involving at most two premises: closure applies when q follows from p and q together, but not when q requires conjoining three or more of the original beliefs.

(8) Despite (6), there is a deep asymmetry: in the preface the “not all true” belief is second-order (about the author’s own reliability) and therefore exempt from the consistency norms governing first-order belief; in the lottery, “exactly one wins” is first-order and must be kept consistent with each individual “ticket i loses.”
