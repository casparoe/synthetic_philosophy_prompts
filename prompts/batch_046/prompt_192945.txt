I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and I am currently trying to map the conceptual space surrounding sustained acts of moral excellence—specifically, what I have been calling “serial supererogation,” in which an agent repeatedly performs acts that lie well beyond the call of duty (for instance, consistently donating half of one’s income to highly effective global health interventions over many years) without that sustained pattern ever hardening into a standing moral obligation. To clarify my own thinking, I would like you to rank the following four theoretical responses to this phenomenon, from the most conceptually adequate to the least: first, the classical surplus-value account, which treats each supererogatory act as an isolated, non-cumulative meritorious excess; second, the moral-residue framework, which holds that every unperformed but available supererogatory act leaves behind a trace of unfulfilled moral potential; third, the social-role model, which grounds the permissibility of stopping in contingent institutional or relational expectations rather than in individual virtue; and fourth, the threshold-deontological view, which locates the supererogatory in the gap between baseline moral decency and an optimal but non-mandatory moral functioning. For each position in your ranking, please provide a concise justification, and take a committed stance: do not present all four as equally viable. I am especially interested in whether the threshold view genuinely dissolves the crystallization problem or merely relocates it to a different normative boundary. Your answer should assume no access to external literature—work with the conceptual structure as I have laid it out.
