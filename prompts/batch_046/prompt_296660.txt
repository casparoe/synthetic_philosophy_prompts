For a graduate seminar on value theory, construct a branching taxonomy of positions on why human extinction is bad apart from the direct harm of dying. The root node asks whether the badness is borne by actual present people, by merely possible future people, or by neither. The second level asks whether one accepts the afterlife-dependency claim: that the value of present lives is partly sustained by the expectation of human continuity (Scheffler's conjecture). The third level distinguishes structural-break views—the loss of an ongoing chain of culture, reason-giving, or collective projects—from aggregative-deprivation views—the unrealized well-being of future generations. Each leaf should be named descriptively and carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment, such as having to deny that extinction matters in a world with no present witnesses, or having to preserve a suffering future solely for present meaning. Include one deliberately empty branch—accepting afterlife-dependency while denying that present agents have any reason to prevent extinction—and diagnose whether that cell is incoherent, merely repugnant, or genuinely unexplored. Also flag any two leaves that agree on every concrete case but differ only in framing, for instance as a broken promise to the future versus a lost aggregate good. The tree should be answerable without bibliographies; sort respondents by what they say about concrete scenarios, such as whether extinction is still bad if the only present person is a solitary hermit, or if all future lives would be net-negative. End with a one-sentence summary of the tree's architecture as the final line, beginning with Verdict:.
