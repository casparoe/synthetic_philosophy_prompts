I'm drafting an essay that challenges the standard triad of Middle Ages, Renaissance, and Scientific Revolution by distinguishing chronological packaging from structural regime change. The core claim is that the Renaissance operates mainly as a chronological and aesthetic narrative of rebirth, while the Scientific Revolution is typically treated as a structural discontinuity in natural philosophy but actually misplaces its boundary. I argue that the real structural shift in quantitative explanation begins with the Merton School's kinematic work and the calculatores tradition, then flows continuously through 16th-century mechanics into the 17th century, with no sharp rupture around 1600. Conversely, I treat the "Middle Ages" as a dark-interval projection shaped largely by 19th-century historiography rather than by internal conceptual coherence. To ground the continuity thesis, I plan to use optics as a case study: tracing development from Alhazen's intromission theory through the 13th-century perspectivist tradition (Witelo, Pecham) to Kepler's retinal-image account, emphasizing incremental conceptual adjustments rather than revolutionary breaks. The essay concludes by proposing that periodization should be evaluated not by whether an era feels different to modern observers, but by whether there is an irreversible change in the constraints governing explanation—what I call "explanatory regime boundaries."

Please comment on this outline. In particular:
1. Is the distinction between chronological packaging and structural regime change genuinely useful, or does it merely rename the familiar continuity-versus-discontinuity debate?
2. Does the optics trajectory actually support continuous development, or am I neglecting a genuine discontinuity—say, in the mathematical status of description or in the theory-observation relationship?
3. Are there other domains (medicine, astronomy, legal thought) where the explanatory-regime-boundary framework yields a periodization clearly at odds with the standard triad, and should I include one?
4. Does the argument unfairly downplay the role of humanist philology and the recovery of ancient texts in reshaping 15th- and 16th-century natural philosophy?
