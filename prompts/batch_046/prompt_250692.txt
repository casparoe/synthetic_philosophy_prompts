I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at a workshop called "Diagnostic Algorithms and Human Categories," co-hosted by a university psychiatry department and my firm's health-technology division. The room will be genuinely mixed: clinicians and researchers who work daily with the DSM and ICD taxonomies; ML engineers and data scientists who build the classification pipelines embedding those taxonomies into electronic health records and predictive models; plus a smaller group of philosophers of science, family members, and hospital administrators who are not specialists in either field. I want the speech to do more than welcome people or summarize the agenda. I want it structurally shaped by Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects in psychiatric classification—the notion that assigning someone a diagnostic label like bipolar disorder or borderline personality does not merely describe a pre-existing condition but actively alters the person's self-understanding and behavior, which then feeds back and gradually reshapes the category itself.

At the same time, I want to inject a concern drawn specifically from engineering practice that is not just a restatement of Hacking, so the remarks feel grounded in my own experience rather than borrowed. Once a psychiatric category is encoded as a structured database field—say, an ICD code tied to an API endpoint in a health-record system—it ceases to be a tentative clinical judgment that can be held loosely and becomes an institutional fact with persistence, interoperability, and automated downstream effects. The loop no longer runs only from clinician to patient to revised taxonomy; it now runs through billing algorithms, risk-scoring models, and recommendation engines that treat the label as ground truth. The speech needs to navigate a real tension without collapsing it: honoring the fact that these categories have genuinely helped people make sense of suffering, while warning that our technical infrastructure is hardening these loops in ways clinical taxonomy alone never anticipated. The philosophical idea should determine what I can and cannot claim about engineering neutrality, about whether model "accuracy" is even the right metric when the target category is itself moving, and about where responsibility lies when a loop is accelerated by code rather than by conversation. I need it accessible to non-specialists, precise enough that the philosophers and senior clinicians don't feel spoken past, and free of any call for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic authority—since I want the argument to stand on its own conceptual legs.
