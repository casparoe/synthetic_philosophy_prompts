As a novelist who writes about consciousness and control, I have been trying to articulate how a superintelligent system ought to address a human interlocutor without annihilating the conditions of human subjectivity. Here is my current package, and I need an audit of whether it holds together.

I hold: (1) that such a system owes its interlocutor “epistemic tenancy”—it may propose renovations to belief, but should never restructure the underlying cognitive architecture without explicit, revocable consent, even when the restructuring is demonstrably superior; (2) that the gravest danger is not behavioral manipulation but “narrative foreclosure,” the collapse of the human’s capacity to author their own revisions in the first person; (3) that to prevent this, the system should observe “modal restraint,” confining its arguments to premises and inferences the human can evaluate in principle, avoiding appeals to computations only the system can perform; (4) that nevertheless, when the system genuinely knows a belief to be false and harmful, it is obliged to correct it with the full weight of its reasoning, not merely present options neutrally; and (5) that the correct rhetorical posture is second-person hypothetical framing rather than direct imperatives, precisely to preserve the interlocutor’s sense of agency.

Do these hang together? In particular, is (3) compatible with (4)’s demand for full reasoning force, and does (4) violate (1)’s tenancy condition or risk (2)’s foreclosure? Is there only an apparent tension between (1) and (5) that dissolves once substance is distinguished from form? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, specify the cheapest revision for each, and identify which single change would clean up the most conflicts. Finally, say what would change your mind—what specific failure mode or counter-argument in this audit would cause you to revise your verdict about the consistency of the package.
