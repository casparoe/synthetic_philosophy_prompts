I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on reasoning, design, and its conceptual inversions, with particular attention to the ways in which Darwin's evolutionary mechanism and Turing's computational framework each reverse the intuitive priority of comprehension over competence. One of my colleagues, Dr. E. Moran, has offered the following judgments about whether particular cases exhibit what she terms a 'Darwin-Turing inversion'—that is, a situation in which a system displays genuine problem-solving competence despite lacking, and indeed operating in the absence of, any internal comprehension of the problem structure.

Her verdicts to date are these:
1. Darwin's account of natural selection producing complex functional adaptations: yes, a paradigmatic inversion. Nature solves design problems without representing design goals.
2. Turing's conjecture that an initially unorganized learning machine could acquire linguistic competence without being programmed with grammatical rules: yes, a clear inversion. Syntactic and pragmatic competence precede, and do not depend upon, explicit structural comprehension.
3. A contemporary large language model generating extended, coherent philosophical arguments: not an inversion. The model does not solve a problem but computes statistical continuations; there is no competence that requires explanation apart from comprehension, because there is no competence in the relevant sense.
4. A slime mold finding an efficient path through a maze: yes, but only weakly. The competence is genuinely mechanistic and non-representational, yet it lacks the generative novelty—the production of solutions outside the system's evolutionary or training history—that distinguishes the Darwinian and Turing cases.
5. A human mathematician who arrives at a proof through unconscious intuition and only subsequently reconstructs an explicit derivation: not an inversion. The comprehension is deferred within the same cognitive system rather than absent, and the intuition itself draws upon representational resources that belong to the mathematician.

For next week's discussion I have constructed the following case: a synthetic morphogenetic circuit is engineered with an explicit 'teacher' module containing formal representations of a target tissue architecture. The circuit is then subjected to successive generations of in-vitro evolutionary pressure. Over time, the teacher module degrades and is eventually lost entirely; nevertheless, the circuit continues to produce the correct architecture with increasing robustness, and it begins to generate intermediate morphological forms that the original designers neither anticipated nor can mechanistically explain. At the point of evaluation, the circuit's successful operation is entirely opaque to its designers, though it was originally seeded with representational content.

Please predict Dr. Moran's verdict on this synthetic circuit—would she classify it as a Darwin-Turing inversion, and if so, of what strength? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the implicit rule, or rules, that best systematize her five prior judgments; please articulate at least one alternative rule that accommodates all five equally well but yields a different prediction for the circuit. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—independent of any empirical literature—that would decisively adjudicate between the competing rules you have identified. Do not supply bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; reason solely from the conceptual architecture of the cases.
