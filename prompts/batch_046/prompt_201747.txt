I'm a PhD student in philosophy of value and aesthetics, and I'm designing an exercise for a graduate seminar on how metaethical arguments can be structurally enacted in verse rather than merely stated. I need you to act as an expert in G. E. Moore's ethics and in the formal possibilities of poetry. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming—without composing the poem—how Moore's claim that the intrinsic value of certain complex states, such as the combination of deep friendship with the appreciation of beauty, can exceed the summed value of their isolated parts, might be embodied in a fixed-form poem. Once you have mapped that structural relationship, write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi, spoken in the voice of someone addressing a close friend at the moment of departure. The six rotating end-words must be whole, part, beauty, friend, value, and sum. In stanzas one through three, arrange the rotation so that part pairs repeatedly with sum and friend pairs with value, suggesting an additive, reductionist calculus of companionship and aesthetic pleasure. In stanzas four through six, shift the rotation so that whole and beauty dominate, implying an irreducible, emergent state. In the envoi, bring all six words together in an order that explicitly refuses to reduce friendship or the appreciation of beauty to their constituent parts. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Principia Ethica recognizes the organic-unity thesis being performed by the form itself, not just named. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific editions; work solely from well-established features of Moore's view.
