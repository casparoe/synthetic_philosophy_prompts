Right, I'm a retired geologist — forty years staring at strata and trying to work out what actually happened versus what people just decided to believe — and since I retired I've got a bit obsessed with philosophy of history. Specifically, I'm stuck on when a legend actually becomes history. Take the Trojan War: Schliemann digs up Hisarlik, suddenly Homer's not just poetry, but then the archaeology gets complicated and we're left in this awkward middle space where the story won't quite go away.

Last weekend I watched that 2004 film *Troy* — Brad Pitt as Achilles, you know the one — and it made me think about something I haven't seen in the usual texts (I've been reading a bit of Collingwood and some newer stuff on collective belief). There's a notion I'm tentatively calling "epistemic fossilisation": the point where a community stops revising a narrative not because evidence confirms it, but because the story has become stucturally necessary — for identity, politics, whatever. So it becomes "history" not by verification, but by becoming too expensive to doubt. I might be talking complete nonsense here, but I'd like to test it.

Could you read *Troy* through that lens? I don't want a review; I want a philosophical reading of how the film treats the Trojan War. Does it present the events as genuinely historical, or as myth that has already crossed that closure threshold? Look particularly at the wooden horse sequence and Achilles' death — do those moments dramatise the tension between narrative necessity and evidential absence? And where does the film just avoid the question altogether, giving us spectacle instead? I'm putting together a discussion for my local philosophy group — mostly retired scientists like me — and I want to know whether the film actually adds anything to the debate that a straight argument about Homer and archaeology wouldn't. Definately interested in where the reading breaks down.
