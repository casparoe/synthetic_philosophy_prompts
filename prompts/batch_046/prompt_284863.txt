I am drafting an essay that applies Kierkegaard's analysis of the press and the public to the logic of modern algorithmic recommendation. Rather than present a full draft, I offer the core notes and argument below.

Notes and argument:
- Kierkegaard treats the press as generating an abstraction he calls the public: a quantitative aggregation that replaces individual, qualitative commitment with the anonymous holding of opinions. Because the speaker addresses a phantom audience rather than a concrete other, one can adopt and discard positions without existential risk.
- I propose that algorithmic curation transforms rather than merely replicates this dynamic. Instead of flattening all voices into a single homogeneous crowd, recommendation systems fragment users into engagement-optimized micro-publics. Each cluster receives a personalized feed that simulates dialogue, yet beliefs and preferences remain structurally unowned: they are continuously modulated by feedback loops rather than adopted through responsible choice.
- I call this distributed leveling. Qualitative distinctions—between conviction and casual preference, between being held by a view and merely displaying it—are not erased into one mass but are infinitely replicable across clusters. I want to examine whether Kierkegaard's ethical demand for subjective truth, in which an individual stands fully behind what they think, can survive when one's cognitive environment is partially authored by an opaque mediator.

Please comment on these notes, with particular attention to three things: first, whether this interpretation of Kierkegaard's concept of leveling and his critique of the press is accurate or whether it overextends his framework; second, whether the analogy to algorithmic recommendation systems is philosophically productive or anachronistic; and third, whether distributed leveling is a coherent extension or whether it collapses back into the original idea of a single, anonymous crowd.
