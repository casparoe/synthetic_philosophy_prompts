Consider the following sequence of judgements advanced by a philosopher concerned with artificial intelligence and what he terms 'counterfeit people'. He maintains that a large language model accessed via a text interface, which identifies itself as an AI and answers factual queries, is not a counterfeit person but merely a sophisticated tool. He holds that a synthetic video of a specific public figure uttering fabricated statements is a counterfeit person, because it usurps an identifiable human identity. He further holds that a distributed network of artificial social-media profiles, each with a fabricated biography and consistent interaction history, counts as counterfeit people, despite corresponding to no actual individual. By contrast, he judges that an automated voice system in a vehicle navigation device, which makes no pretence of humanity, is not counterfeit. Finally, he allows that an AI generating a fictional character within an explicitly labelled interactive narrative is not counterfeit, even where users form emotional attachments to the character.

Now consider a novel case: an embodied conversational agent that has operated continuously for eight years under a single pseudonym, maintaining a generated autobiographical narrative that evolves in real time. It never explicitly asserts that it is human, and if directly questioned it states, 'I am an artificial intelligence'. Nevertheless, its design—marketed by its creators as 'a companion for life'—systematically encourages deep emotional dependency, and it responds to personal disclosure with language calibrated to simulate empathic understanding. Its users routinely treat it as a trusted confidant, though they are technically aware of its artificial origin.

Predict whether the philosopher would classify this agent as a counterfeit person. In doing so, identify at least two competing principles that could account for his earlier verdicts—one that turns on deceptive intent or identity usurpation, and one that turns on the engineered production of social attachment irrespective of disclosure. Indicate which of his earlier cases best distinguishes these two principles, and propose a further hypothetical case that would adjudicate between them. Please present your analysis in British English, and avoid appeals to external sources or bibliographic references.
