I cover long-form debates in philosophy and conceptual science, and I have been thinking about a tension that runs through Plato’s corpus—particularly in the Laws and the Symposium—regarding the moral and civic status of intoxication. On one reading, Plato endorses regulated symposia as a disciplined exercise in which citizens expose their hidden dispositions to scrutiny, thereby serving a kind of virtue-testing protocol. On another, he condemns drunkenness as a dissolution of the rational element that should govern both soul and city, which makes his apparent approval of state-sanctioned drinking look like a straightforward contradiction. Rather than treating this as a biographical quirk, I want to know whether the tension can be resolved by importing a distinction that is now central to discussions of artificial agency: the difference between approval-directed and goal-directed behavior. Could Plato’s regulated symposium be reconstructed not as a pursuit of pleasure (a goal) but as an alignment procedure in which citizens seek social approval for displaying temperance under conditions of reduced inhibition? Or does that framing impose an anachronistic psychology onto ancient civic practice?

Write an essay that weighs at least three reconstructions of Plato’s view: (1) the virtue-testing or epistemic-paternalist model, in which the state uses alcohol to reveal character for corrective purposes; (2) the consistency model, which argues that Plato distinguishes sharply between private excess and public ritual and therefore holds no unified view; and (3) the approval-directed conditioning model, which reads the symposium as an early institutional design for shaping behavior through evaluated performance rather than through internal rational commitment. Your essay should reach and justify a clear verdict: which reconstruction best accounts for Plato’s texts without collapsing into contradiction, and does the approval-directed framing illuminate the ancient position or distort it? Do not simply list interpretations—defend a conclusion about whether Plato’s stance on drunkenness can be coherently reconstructed, and specify what that coherence costs in terms of our modern understanding of autonomy and rational agency.
