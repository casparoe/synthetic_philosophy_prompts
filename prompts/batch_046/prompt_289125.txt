I am drafting a novel about a neurologist who advises a tribunal granting legal standing to experiential subjects—mental states that warrant protection from interference. To ground my character's reasoning, I have discussed with my philosophy tutor her judgments on prior cases: she holds that a sleepwalker's complex maze navigation counts as an experience despite subsequent amnesia; that a coma patient's reflex withdrawal from a noxious stimulus does not; that a hypnagogic hallucination at sleep onset, with partial paralysis and vivid imagery but no sensory input, does count; that a subject reporting a blank interval with no mental content upon waking was not experiencing anything then; and that a patient under anesthesia whose brain stimulation replayed a childhood memory, reported only after consciousness returned with no concurrent awareness markers, was not experiencing that replay. The new case involves a verified REM-sleep subject with no bodily movement or sensory stimulation, yet with sustained high-resolution cortical activation patterns isomorphic to those seen during waking mathematical reasoning; upon awakening she reports having solved an original geometric proof during the episode, reproducing intermediate steps she could not derive awake.

Predict my tutor's verdict on whether this REM reasoning-state is an experience. Articulate the two leading rules that both fit her five judgments—one emphasizing concurrent phenomenal presence signaled by neural isomorphism, the other emphasizing embodied action or immediate narrative reportability in a constructivist vein—say which prior case best analogizes each, and propose the single further hypothetical case that would force a choice between them. Please answer in American English, in no more than 300 words across at most two paragraphs.
