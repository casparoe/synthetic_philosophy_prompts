Since I hung up my lab coat I've been poking about in the philosphy of science, and I'm stuck on Goodman's grue paradox. As I understand it, he invented this predicate 'grue' — green before some arbitrary future date, then blue — to show that induction is circular, since past emerald observations fit both 'green' and 'grue' and we can't choose between them without already assuming induction works. But here's where my biologist's intuition rebels: this seems like a problem for discrete observational categories with sharp cutoffs, like colour labels. In evolutionary morphology we deal with continuous traits — beak depth gradients, heterochronic shifts in cranial development, allometric scaling — that are tied to mechanistic growth models rather than calender-dependent predicates. So does the grue paradox actually dissolve once you move away from discrete observational terms into quantitative, mechanistically anchored biology? Or is there a hidden version that applies when we project fitness correlations across geological timescales, and I've just missed it because I'm treating 'projectability' as a linguistic issue rather than a statistical one? I don't want a reading list — just want to know where my intuition is going wrong.
