I cover philosophy of mind for a general audience, and I've come to accept a qualified defense of mental causation against the causal exclusion argument: mental properties are causally effective specifically in neural regimes where physical dynamics are genuinely underdetermined—call it equilibrium-sensitive mental causation. What would count against this view? I want the objections, empirical findings, and conceptual pressures that should genuinely move a proponent, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. In particular: does this collapse into standard non-reductive physicalism with ad hoc conditions? Has neuroscience already shown that neural dynamics are never sufficiently underdetermined to open such a causal gap? And has the position been qualified so heavily that it risks unfalsifiability—and if so, is that a fatal flaw for a theory of this kind? Please distinguish the defeaters that defenders of equilibrium-sensitive mental causation would themselves concede from those they would dismiss.
