Two people in my study group argued about the death penalty, specifically whether executing an innocent person is a special kind of wrong or just a scaled-up false imprisonment. (1) Maya said it's structurally different: capital cases get more appeals but also more political pressure, and the adverserial system is built to resolve guilt not protect the accused, so error rates aren't random noise but systematically skewed against poor defendents. She said you can't treat it like a factory defect rate because the system itself manufactures false positives. (2) Jalen said that's true of every conviction, including life sentences, so if false positives worry you then all incarceration should worry you, and singling out execution is just emotional weighting. He added that retributive justice demands proportionality, and life without parole might actually be worse than death, so the "uniqueness" claim collapses unless you smuggle in a deontological right-not-to-be-killed that you don't extend to liberty. (3) Maya replied that reversibility creates an asymmetry: you can free someone wrongly jailed but not resurrect them, and that's logical not emotional — the harm profile is discontinuous. She also said deterrence studies comparing abolitionist vs retentionist states suffer from a measurment problem with policing intensity and reporting norms, so Jalen can't lean on deterrence without assuming away confounds. (4) Jalen came back that reversibility is practical not moral; we don't ban surgeries because fatal mistakes are irreversible. He said Maya's "structural manufacturing" point actually undermines her case — if bias hits poor people everywhere, then life sentences also disproportionately destroy them, so her asymmetry argument fails to justify treating execution differently.
