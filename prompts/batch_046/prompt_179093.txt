You are a philosopher-poet with a precise ear for formal structure. Before writing, take a moment to sketch out how norms like slavery or dueling actually fall apart: not just whether people are persuaded by arguments, or whether the powerful stop gaining from them, or whether they suddenly feel shameful, but how these three forces intertwine — especially the idea that the real shift often happens in the silence of people who stop defending the old rule without ever explaining why.

I have no formal training, but I have been thinking about this on my own and I want a poem, not an explanation. Write a triptych of three blank-verse sonnets, linked like a chain: the final couplet of the first sonnet must become the opening line of the second, rewritten only in past tense; the final couplet of the second must become the opening line of the third, rewritten only in the third person; and the very last line of the third must return to the poem’s first line, replacing its central noun with its opposite. Give each sonnet one speaker — Argument, Interest, and Honor. Make the form do the thinking: Argument should discover it cannot close its case without borrowing Honor’s language; Interest should find its cost-benefit frame cracked by shame; Honor should show it does not replace the other two but re-codes them into a social currency. Keep the silent bystanders — those who quietly withdraw their defense — present as an unvoiced fourth presence in each section. Make it accurate enough that someone familiar with the debates around Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah on moral revolutions and honor recognizes the dynamics, but clear enough that a general reader feels the movement from logic to advantage to disgrace. Do not cite sources, list readings, or name scholars inside the poem.

Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
