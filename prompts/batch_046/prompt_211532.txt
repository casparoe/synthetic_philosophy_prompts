Following a lengthy argument with a friend about the Sleeping Beauty problem, I have become convinced that the standard framing obscures what is actually at stake. We both accept the canonical setup: Beauty is sedated on Sunday, a fair coin is tossed, and she is awakened on Monday regardless of the outcome, then—only if the coin came up tails—awakened again on Tuesday after being given an amnesia-inducing drug; upon each awakening she knows the protocol but receives no information about which day it is or what the coin showed. The halfers maintain that her credence in heads should remain one-half upon awakening, while the thirders insist it should be one-third. The usual moves—centered-world representations, long-run frequency objections, appeals to the self-indicating nature of the belief—are by now thoroughly familiar, yet the debate has not advanced in any discernible direction.

My friend offered an unexpected diagnosis. She argued that the stalemate is not primarily about Bayesian updating, the principle of indifference, or the correct formalization of self-locating belief at all. Rather, she claimed, the two camps presuppose incompatible pictures of the epistemic subject across time. The halfers, on her reading, implicitly treat Beauty as a single diachronic agent whose prior probabilities should be preserved across both awakenings; the thirders, by contrast, treat each awakening as the emergence of a discrete, synchronically bounded epistemic perspective—almost a new subject—whose sample space must be reconstructed without deference to the prior state. If she is right, then the dispute is partly about personal identity and the continuity of rational agency, masked by shared vocabulary about credence.

Is this diagnosis accurate? Is the disagreement substantive—concerning whether the correct sample space for centered belief includes only epistemically accessible awakenings or all metaphysically possible ones—or is it largely verbal, with each side attaching different normative conditions to the term rational update? Or do the halfers and thirders answer genuinely different questions, one about the coherence of a temporally extended agent and the other about the norms governing a momentary perspective? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of the interpretations. And given whatever diagnosis you offer: is there anything that could genuinely resolve the dispute—perhaps a formal result about updating over centered worlds, a precise distinction between diachronic and synchronic norms of rationality, or an argument that the question itself is malformed?
