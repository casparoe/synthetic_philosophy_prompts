Consider the intersection of two familiar thought experiments: the Aunt Hillary scenario, in which a brain is gradually separated from its body while functional connections are preserved, and the Ant Fugue dialogue, which asks whether an ant colony might collectively constitute a single cognitive agent. Argue for the following precise position: an ant colony does not possess a mind in the standard emergentist sense, but it can be said to have one only under a stricter condition—namely, that there exists a continuous functional pathway analogous to Aunt Hillary’s radio link, such that colony-level information processing can be traced to a single integrated subject of experience with a unified point of view. To defend this, address the objection that colony cognition is distributed by nature and therefore requires no unified subject. Show why the Aunt Hillary criterion of functional continuity with a potential locus of experience forces us either to deny the colony a mind or to locate that mind in a specific, perhaps surprising, part of the colony’s architecture (for instance, treating the pheromone-trail network as a single extended nervous system). Build the argument from the conceptual logic of functional continuity and distributed cognition alone, without appealing to bibliographic authority or empirical studies of particular species.
