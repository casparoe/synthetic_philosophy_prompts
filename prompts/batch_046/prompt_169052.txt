Here are my verdicts on five cases involving higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I want to know whether a single principle of belief revision can accommodate them all, or whether consistency requires me to abandon one or two.

Case 1: I derive a formal result through an explicit, step-by-step proof late at night. Every step is written down and inspectable. I also know that fatigue doubles my error rate in formal reasoning, and I feel the usual strong conviction. I have not yet reviewed the proof rested and alert. Verdict: I should retain belief in the result but reduce my confidence proportionally—say, from 0.95 to roughly 0.80—rather than suspending judgment entirely, because the reasoning path remains transparent and revisable.

Case 2: A philosopher whom I regard as my exact epistemic peer—same training, same access to the literature—rejects a particular deontological principle I have long defended. After extensive discussion, I cannot locate an error in her reasoning, and she cannot locate one in mine. Verdict: I should move substantially toward her position, reducing my confidence to near the midpoint, even though my first-order arguments for the principle remain intact and apparently valid.

Case 3: Over five years I have kept a calibration log of my geopolitical predictions. In every domain except authoritarian regimes I am well calibrated; in that domain alone I am severely overconfident (predicting outcomes at 70% confidence that occur about 30% of the time). I now form a belief, via reasoning that feels entirely sound to me, that a specific authoritarian regime will collapse within a year. Verdict: I should sharply discount my confidence in this particular prediction—treating it as if generated by a poorly calibrated instrument—despite seeing no flaw in the specific reasoning chain.

Case 4: I come to accept a thesis in philosophy of mind after a sustained argument. I then learn that the framing of the original question activated a well-documented cognitive bias in me; however, every premise and inference in the argument is independently checkable and logically untouched by that bias. Verdict: The framing bias does not warrant reducing my credence in the thesis itself. I should seek an independent evaluation of the argument, but if it holds up, my belief should remain at its original strength.

Case 5: I run a study testing twenty related hypotheses, apply no multiplicity correction, and obtain one result at p = 0.03 that was both theoretically predicted and methodologically sound. I then recognize that I have implicitly conducted twenty tests. Verdict: I should treat this result as essentially disconfirmed—no more credible than the nineteen null findings—despite the soundness of the individual test and the prior theoretical expectation.

Is there a coherent principle of higher-order belief revision that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for two further cases: (a) I derive a conclusion and then learn that I was unconsciously motivated to reach it, though I can find no logical error in the steps; and (b) I form a perceptual belief that rain is imminent (dark clouds) while simultaneously possessing higher-order evidence that I am in an environment known to cause visual illusions of darkness. If no single principle saves every verdict, identify which case I should overturn and explain why. Finally, please say what specific argument or empirical finding would change your mind about the principle you defend, and indicate clearly where in this reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong.
