In philosophy of mind and cognitive science, a long-running disagreement turns on Douglas Hofstadter's MIU-puzzle and the idea of "jumping out of the system"—JOOTSing. Within the formal rules governing strings of M, I, and U, it is impossible to derive a string consisting solely of U's; human solvers, however, appear to transcend those rules by recognizing an underlying arithmetic pattern (multiples of three) that the axioms never mention. For decades, one side has argued that this demonstrates a genuinely non-formal cognitive capacity—whether semantic insight, meta-level monitoring, or access to meanings that syntax cannot capture—while the other side insists that the solver is merely operating inside a larger, more powerful formal system (one that includes arithmetic), so that the "jump" is only a shift between syntactic frameworks and not an escape from formalism altogether. Both camps accept the same formal description of the MIU rules, agree that people reliably solve the puzzle, and even concede that human cognition feels different from mechanical derivation; yet the dispute has remained essentially static, with neither side conceding ground since it was first articulated in the literature on Gödelian arguments against mechanism.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not empirical but structural and partly verbal. The mechanist treats "system" as any computational architecture that could be instantiated in the brain, so that adding arithmetic rules simply expands the system rather than breaking out of it; the anti-mechanist treats "system" as the explicitly given MIU formalism, so that recognizing its arithmetic substrate counts as leaving it behind. Meanwhile, both sides use "explanation" ambiguously—one demanding that the insight be reconstructed within the original rules, the other accepting that reconstruction in a richer framework counts as explaining it away—so they may be asking different questions about cognition under the same heading. I also suspect that a hidden disagreement about explanation is doing work that neither side makes explicit: one camp thinks that reducing the insight to a larger algorithm dissolves its philosophical significance, while the other thinks that refusal to accept such reduction is just mysticism about consciousness. Is the disagreement really about whether minds are formal, or about what it takes for a formal account to be satisfying? Are there bedrock intuitions about "understanding" versus "computing" that neither side can argue for, or is there a formal result—perhaps a precise model of meta-representation or self-referential rule-modification—that could force convergence?

Please diagnose the disagreement with that framing in mind: is it verbal, substantive, or a mix in which hidden standards of explanation do the heavy lifting? In doing so, separate what is settled—such as the formal properties of the MIU system, the fact that human solvers access arithmetic structure not present in the axioms, and the existence of Gödel-style limitations in sufficiently powerful formal systems—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists, and do not present a contested interpretation of JOOTSing as if it were the consensus view. If progress is possible, say whether it requires a conceptual distinction (for instance, between escaping one formalism and escaping formalism as such), an empirical finding about how brains represent mathematical patterns, or a formal result about self-modifying systems; and if you think no progress should be expected, explain why the dispute is either permanently underdetermined by evidence or ultimately about values—such as whether mechanistic explanation is intellectually satisfying—that argument alone cannot resolve.
