You are delivering the opening remarks at a two-day municipal workshop titled "The Silhouette and the Stairwell: Zoning, Memory, and the Unseen Resident." The audience is split between urban economists who model exclusionary zoning as a collective-action failure, and longtime residents of the Elmwood district—mostly two-story streets—who have organized against recent upzoning proposals not out of hostility to newcomers, but because they believe the physical scale of the neighborhood is the only remaining archive of a working-class history erased from official records. Frame the workshop's purpose without reducing the residents to NIMBY caricatures or letting the economists treat neighborhood form as a mere market distortion. Structure the speech around the concept of "adjacent rights"—the claim that one's legitimate stake in a place is not ownership of air or soil, but a right to remain in proximity to others without being forced into either displacement or invisibility. Acknowledge that upzoning can itself become erasure if it treats existing residents as obstacles rather than co-authors of the city's temporal depth. The speech should run about ten minutes, be formal without being academic, and must avoid both pure property-rights rhetoric and pure utilitarian calculus, since both betray the framework. End by proposing a single shared question that both groups must answer together before any map is redrawn: what does it mean for a city to grow vertically without sacrificing the horizontal memory embedded in its existing streets?
