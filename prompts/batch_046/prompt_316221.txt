Commentators often explain Zhuangzi's equanimity toward death through the four-seasons analogy: winter is not a failure of summer but its necessary successor within a single cyclical process, so death is not a failure of life but its transformation, and grief is therefore as irrational as lamenting frost. The analogy is used to defend his reported conduct—singing and beating a tub at his wife's funeral—on the grounds that she has merely moved into another phase of the Great Transformation, making mourning a category error. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what corresponds to the earth or climate system, to the seasonal form, to the cyclical law, and to the human observer? Then audit the fractures: does death, unlike winter, eliminate the very perspective from which loss can be registered? Does the analogy smuggle in a persisting substrate—an enduring ground that undergoes change—that conflicts with Zhuangzi's denial of a fixed self? And if these disanalogies hold, does the conclusion that singing over a coffin is rational still stand, or does the analogy instead collapse toward a different stance, such as celebrating release? Say what would change your mind: which correspondence, if shown to be robust, or which disanalogy, if shown to fail, would lead you to a different verdict on whether the analogy genuinely supports Zhuangzi's position, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
