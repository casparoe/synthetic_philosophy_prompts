As a novelist, I have become preoccupied with how coherence operates not merely as a logical virtue but as a narrative one—how a character's choices across chapters must hang together without collapsing into contradiction, yet without requiring the implausible uniformity of a single, timeless will. I am composing an essay for my own clarity, and I would like you to help me draft it. The subject is the family of coherence arguments for expected utility maximization—the Dutch Book and money-pump lines that claim an agent who violates the axioms of expected utility can be driven into a sure loss by a clever bookmaker. I want the essay to treat these arguments not as settled curiosities but as genuinely contested philosophical territory, and I want it to reach and justify a definite conclusion.

In particular, I want the essay to engage with a tension that strikes me as underexplored: if we take seriously the idea that rational agency is not the property of a monolithic self but of a temporally extended, narratively fragmented sequence of decision-points—what we might call a "chaptered agent"—does the coherence argument still carry its normative force? The standard presentation assumes a single bookmaker confronting a single agent at a single time. But suppose the agent's preferences shift in ways that are not mere errors but structurally analogous to narrative development: the protagonist of chapter three genuinely values outcomes differently from the protagonist of chapter one, not out of irrationality but out of changed circumstances and accumulated experience. Can a coherence argument be reconstructed to bind these successive stages together, or does the fragmentation of the self provide a principled escape from the demand to maximize expected utility across the whole arc? The essay should weigh the strongest defense of coherence—perhaps appealing to diachronic rationality or to the practical necessity of planning—against the objection that imposing global coherence on a chaptered agent amounts to a kind of tyranny of the earlier self over the later.

Please write the essay with philosophical precision, but keep in view that the problem of rational unity over time is also, at bottom, a problem about whether a life can be made into a coherent story without falsifying the reality of change. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the conceptual architecture itself rather than in scholarly attribution. Reach a conclusion and defend it.
