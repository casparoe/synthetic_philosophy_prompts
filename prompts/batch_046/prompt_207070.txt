I'm preparing a short contribution for a philosophy of perception reading group that has just worked through the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply—specifically the claim that veridical perception and perfect hallucination do not share a fundamental mental kind, despite the way our ordinary descriptions seem to treat them as variations on a single experience-type. I want a villanelle that does its philosophical work through the form itself, not through labels. Use two refrains—something like "this is what I see" and "the same thing appears"—that return according to the strict nineteen-line scheme. At first the repetition should feel like the common-factor assumption being locked in place, the argument from illusion's premise that one mental item runs through both cases. But over the course of the poem, especially at the volta and in the final couplet, the identical words should split in meaning: in one context the phrase refers to the world being present to awareness, in the other it refers only to a seeming-without-presence, so that the grammar of "appearing" turns out to be doing misleading work. I want the poem to show, structurally, that saying "the same thing" in both cases is not describing a shared inner object but applying one vocabulary to two fundamentally different mental kinds—without ever using the word "disjunctivism" as a crutch. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the standard disjunctivist view recognizes the move, yet written so that a reader without that background still feels a real tension between formal repetition and semantic divergence.

Keep the rhyme and refrain scheme rigorous, so that the inevitability of the returning lines mirrors the philosophical pressure the argument exerts, and let the final couplet place the two refrains side by side without letting them collapse into a single meaning. To push the prompt slightly off the well-trodden path, frame the divergence not as a simple denial but as a grammatical illusion: the poem should suggest that our language forces a shared noun onto two events that share only surface syntax, much as saying "I have a pain" and "I have a hat" both use "have" but do not commit us to owning the same kind of thing.
