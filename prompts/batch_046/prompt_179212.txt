I am working through the opening chapters of Hobbes’s De Corpore (1655), where he defines geometry as the science of figures generated by motion and argues that because we ourselves construct geometric objects—moving a point to generate a line, a line to generate a plane—we possess a causal knowledge of them that physics, dealing with bodies not of our making, can never attain. In these passages he sharply contrasts this synthetic method, which begins with constructive generation, with the analytic methods he associates with the quadrature controversies, dismissing analytic decomposition as empty unless anchored in an actual constructive origin. I am trying to understand how this geometric ideal is meant to carry over into civil philosophy: if the commonwealth is “generated” by contract in a manner structurally analogous to geometric construction, does Hobbes permit any analytic decomposition of existing political bodies—say, breaking down a mixed government into its constituent powers—or must all legitimate reasoning about the state begin with an original synthetic act of sovereign institution? More specifically, given his well-known hostility to Wallis’s analytic techniques in geometry, is there a hidden methodological constraint on political reform, namely that any argument for altering the state that lacks a constructive origin in a new sovereign institution is as illegitimate as an analytic proof of the circle’s quadrature? I would appreciate help clarifying whether the geometric analogy is intended to be strictly methodological or also ontological, and whether Hobbes’s rejection of infinitesimals in geometric construction has an unacknowledged parallel in his refusal to admit partial, incremental, or divided sovereignty.
