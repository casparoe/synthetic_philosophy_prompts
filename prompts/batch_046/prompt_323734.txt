I'm developing a conceptual framework for a seminar on historiography and want to fix the criteria that any adequate theory of archival power and historical silence must satisfy—without relying on named authors, reading lists, or citations. The framework needs to account for the fact that archives are not neutral repositories but products of regimes that decided whose lives and actions were recordable, meaning absence itself carries evidentiary weight rather than functioning as a blank space. Specifically, the theory must distinguish between silences produced by deliberate suppression, those produced by structural neglect that rendered certain populations administratively invisible, and those that result from the conceptual limitations of past record-keeping practices rather than from intentional exclusion. It also needs to explain how historians can use contemporary categories—such as modern understandings of gender or mental capacity—to interpret behavior documented only through obsolete classificatory schemes, without collapsing past conceptual worlds into present ones. Furthermore, it must address whether every archival gap is in principle interpretable, or whether some silences mark genuinely unrecoverable experiences that resist retrospective reconstruction. Finally, it should avoid reducing all archival selection to a uniform logic of domination, since contingent omissions, practical storage constraints, and non-ideological sorting also shape what survives. Each item should state one desideratum, the concrete case or distinction motivating it, and indicate whether it conflicts with another desideratum in the set.
