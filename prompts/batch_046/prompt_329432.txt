I am writing an essay for my philosophy of mind seminar—an upper-level course on consciousness and its discontents—on whether qualia are best understood as an incoherent posit along the lines suggested by Chase and Sanborn. Rather than rehearsing the familiar objection that there is no non-circular criterion for phenomenal similarity judgments, I want to examine a more structural presupposition that I think underwrites their argument: what I am tentatively calling the calibration availability thesis. This is the claim that for any concept to be coherent, there must be some intersubjective calibration procedure available that does not already invoke the concept being calibrated. I apply this to phenomenal color concepts, arguing that the absence of a third-person calibration mechanism for what-it-is-like experiences does not automatically render those concepts incoherent—just as the absence of an external calibration mechanism for the center of mass of an isolated physical system does not render that physical concept incoherent. From there, the essay asks whether this response collapses into a damaging form of conceptual isolationism that makes phenomenal concepts empirically empty, or whether it instead reveals that coherence conditions have been applied with an unjustified asymmetry between first-person and theoretical concepts.

I would appreciate comments specifically on three points. First, does my framing genuinely avoid circularity, or does it merely relocate the problem by smuggling phenomenal content back in through the back door of the analogy? Second, is the comparison to physical concepts like center of mass defensible, or does it ignore relevant differences in how theoretical terms are anchored? Third, I am concerned that by conceding that phenomenal concepts lack intersubjective calibration, I may have inadvertently granted too much ground to the eliminativist—so I am especially interested in whether there is a viable middle path that preserves coherence without requiring full third-person accessibility, and whether my essay misses obvious objections that do not depend on accepting the original coherence criterion in the first place. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation check; I mainly want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together and where it is likely to crack under pressure.
