In a philosophy of science seminar I am taking, a fellow student has adopted the habit of shutting down discussion with the line "if it can't be falsified, it isn't science"—most recently to dismiss both psychoanalytic interpretation and a particular macroeconomic forecasting framework without examining their internal structure. I recognize that this is a popular corruption of Popper's demarcation criterion, but rather than asking for a historical account of Popper I want the slogan itself dissected as a piece of language. Could you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses this sentence could be expressing? For each reading, please specify: what counts as "it" (a single predictive claim, a sustained research program, or an entire theoretical framework?); what modality of "can't" is intended (logical impossibility, present practical limitation, or institutional refusal to subject the claim to test?); what "falsified" requires (a decisive counter-instance, an accumulating burden of anomalies, or a failure to generate novel predictions?); and whose inability is at issue (the defenders of the theory, an idealized scientific community, or the claim in principle?). Then say, for each combination, whether it is a trivial tautology, a substantive and defensible thesis that is nevertheless far narrower than the slogan sounds, or a claim that is simply false—especially those that slide between logical unfalsifiability, practical untestability, and confirmation. I need the answer structured so that I can bring each interpretation back to the seminar and ask which one, if any, is actually doing the work when the slogan is deployed as a stopper, since my classmate treats it as a single bright-line rule.
