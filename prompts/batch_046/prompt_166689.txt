After spending the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether self-awareness can ever be fully detached from bodily presence, I want to press a comparison that our back-and-forth kept approaching without resolving. Compare Avicenna's flying man—suspended in empty space, blindfolded, limbs separated, deprived of all sensory contact, yet immediately certain of his own existence—with Merleau-Ponty's account of the body-subject and its pre-reflective body schema. To make the comparison sharper, introduce a modified case: the flying man lacks not only present sensation and proprioception, but also every memory, implicit trace, or anticipatory structure that he ever possessed a body at all. Does Avicenna's claim that he would still know he exists hold up under this radical amnesia, or does the intuition collapse once even a minimal embodied horizon is erased? Conversely, can Merleau-Ponty's framework accommodate any form of first-person certainty in such a subject, or does it entail that without a lived bodily orientation the very sense of being present to oneself dissolves? I am not asking for a simple victory for one side; I want an assessment of which framework offers the more complete account of what the flying man demonstrates—whether it reveals a genuinely non-corporeal substance, a bare phenomenal self-model that requires no narrative or bodily continuity, or a concealed dependence on embodied intentionality that Avicenna's original scenario obscures. Take both positions seriously, identify precisely where the modified scenario breaks or reinforces each argument, and conclude with what this comparison contributes to debates about immaterial souls that a straightforward exposition of either view alone fails to capture.
