I've been chewing over Molyneux's problem with some mates from the local rationalist meetup, and I think I've landed on a somewhat heretical take: the reason the problem feels so intractable is that we're assuming there's a single, modality-independent representation of shape that both touch and vision are meant to access. But what if that's just wrong? What if tactile geometry and visual geometry are genuinly incommensurable — not just different encodings of the same thing, but different kinds of spatial structure altogether — and the only reason people answer 'yes, they'd recognise it' or 'no, they wouldn't' is that they're smuggling in an assumption about unified cognition that the brain doesn't actually implement?

I'm basically defending the view that cross-modal shape recognition in a newly-sighted patient wouldn't be immediate conceptual integration but rather a very fast, unconscious statistical remaping — more like calibrating a new sensor than retrieving an old file. So my position is that Molyneux's problem dissolves once you stop treating vision and touch as windows onto a shared spatial reality. But I want to know what would actually count against this. Not just the usual 'but intuition says...' — I want to know which experimental results (say, from infant cross-modal studies, or from parietal cortex lesions, or from documented sight-restoration cases) would be fatal versus merely awkward. Which results in other domains — maybe robotics, maybe the philosophy of perception more broadly — does my view implicitly commit me to? And what would defenders of the unified-spatial-concept view concede is damaging, versus what they'd dismiss? I'd also be curious whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — if every apparent counter-example can be explained away as 'just rapid remaping' — and whether that's actually a problem for a claim about cognitive architecture rather than a predictive model.
