Second, consider whether a parallel internal/external distinction can be coherently drawn for epistemic reasons—considerations that should lead someone to form or revise a belief. Student answer:
Internal reasons are reasons that move an agent to act because they connect to desires, goals, or projects the agent already has. External reasons are supposed to bind agents independently of their personal motivations, like universal moral duties. Bernard Williams argued that all real reasons are internal: if a consideration doesn't link up with the agent's subjective motivational set, then it can't explain why they act, so it isn't a genuine reason for them. This means that moral obligations that don't align with an agent's actual cares aren't real reasons; they are essentially illusions generated by social expectation. For instance, if a person has no desire whatsoever to reduce harm to others, then they literally have no reason to avoid causing harm—any claim that they "ought" to do so is an empty external imposition with no practical force.

Therefore practical internalism necessarily carries over to epistemic internalism. The view that evidence can supply reasons for belief regardless of one's cognitive goals—the epistemic externalist position—is incompatible with Williams's framework. So the internal/external distinction does not genuinely come apart across the two domains; accepting internalism about action commits you to internalism about belief.
