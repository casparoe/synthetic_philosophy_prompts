I spent much of the weekend in a protracted, circular argument with a close friend about whether Weber’s Protestant Ethic thesis can genuinely establish culture—specifically, a religiously formed value-orientation—as a causal factor in economic development, or whether it inevitably collapses into either a psychological account of individual motivation or a structural account of markets and legal frameworks. She kept reducing the thesis to the claim that Protestants “worked harder because they believed in predestination,” while I tried to press the more elusive point: that Weber is attempting to show how an ethical orientation can enter into an elective affinity with institutional arrangements, functioning as a kind of conceptual filter that makes certain economic behaviors appear rational to actors without being reducible to either preference or external constraint. I now need to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year doctoral students in history, sociology, and economics—none of whom have read Weber closely—on precisely this problem: in what sense, if any, can culture be said to cause something?

Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced: which distinction—perhaps between value-rational and instrumentally rational action, or between an ideal type and an empirical generalization—must be secured before the thesis can even be formulated without immediate distortion, and which pieces depend on that foundation? Second, specify the single carrying case or scenario that should anchor the entire session: it needs enough institutional texture to show culture interacting with law and market structure, yet it must deliberately lack the complexity of a full comparative civilizational study so it does not become a narrative of inevitable development. Say what this case must contain and what it must omit. Third, map the misconceptions these students will predictably bring or form in the order they will arise—the reduction of Weber to a correlation claim about Protestant regions being wealthier, the conflation of elective affinity with direct psychological causation, the assumption that explaining with values requires abandoning material interest entirely, and the belief that Weber offers a monocausal origin story for capitalism—and indicate exactly where in the dependency sequence each should be defused. Fourth, propose one concrete check—whether a brief written response or a structured discussion prompt—that would reveal whether the students have grasped the conceptual problem rather than merely adopted a new vocabulary. Finally, state openly what you will refuse to explain at this level—whether the full comparative architecture of Weber’s studies of other world religions, the technical literature on counterfactual causation in social science, or the granular historiographical disputes about specific sixteenth-century cities—and say so explicitly. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations to secondary works.
