In my philosophy-of-mind dissertation work, I have been tracking a stalemate that has developed in the literature and in our department’s reading group on whether conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, specifically with respect to phenomenal zombies. Both sides are thoroughly familiar with the standard modal arguments—the physicalist objections that any “zombie” description either smuggles in a functional difference or collapses into logical contradiction, and the dualist replies that one can clearly and distinctly imagine qualitative absence without conceptual error. Both sides also accept the same broad neuroscientific findings and agree that logical consistency alone is insufficient for possibility. Yet over several years neither camp has revised its position, and the exchange now cycles through the same distinctions—epistemic versus metaphysical possibility, primary versus secondary conceivability, a priori versus a posteriori identity—without movement. I have begun to suspect that the impasse is not genuinely metaphysical but taxonomic: we may be conflating what I call structural conceivability, the ability to construct a propositional description that contains no explicit logical contradiction, with phenomenal replicability, the capacity to imaginatively reproduce or withhold a specific qualitative profile in first-person simulation. The dualist may be demonstrating only structural conceivability, while the physicalist is implicitly demanding phenomenal replicability as the relevant standard for modal inference, and neither side has offered an independent argument for why their favored standard should govern possibility claims rather than the other. Is this the right diagnosis? Does the disagreement turn on a verbal ambiguity in “conceivable”; is it a substantive dispute about evidential standards for modal claims; are the two sides answering different questions—one about semantic content, one about metaphysical grounding—under a single heading; or does it ultimately bottom out in bedrock intuitions about the transparency of phenomenology that no further argument can dislodge? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal model distinguishing these two capacities, an empirical study of imaginative phenomenological description, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, or is permanent stalemate the appropriate expectation? Please indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the specific point—whether concerning the taxonomy of conceivability I have proposed, the interpretation of the physicalist challenge, or the prospects for progress—about which you are least certain.
