I'm preparing a graduate seminar on democratic ethics and have been working through Mill's account of representative government—specifically his claim that suffrage is not a private entitlement but a public trust that demands competence, moral seriousness, and a willingness to subordinate personal preference to the common good. I want to pose a problem that Mill could not have foreseen but that tests the internal consistency of his framework. Imagine Mill were alive today and confronted with a political environment in which algorithmic curation has produced deep epistemic fragmentation: voters inhabit incompatible informational ecosystems, share almost no descriptive premises about the world, and therefore routinely face ballots in which every option appears to be a lesser evil not merely in policy terms, but in terms of preserving the conditions for shared deliberation. In such a context, does Mill's distinction between voting as expressive self-development, voting as a civic duty, and voting as strategic harm-reduction still hold?

Would he regard the strategic lesser-evil vote as a corruption of the expressive function—since it expresses no affirmative judgment about the good—or as the only responsible exercise of civic competence available? More precisely, if the voter knows that her reasoning is partly shaped by opaque recommendation algorithms and that her ballot is primarily a defensive rejection of a greater threat to deliberative democracy itself, how should Mill classify that act: as a failure of individuality, as a fulfillment of public duty, or as something that reveals a hidden tension in his own theory? I want an answer that reconstructs Mill's likely position by drawing on his commitments to individuality, competence, and the harm principle, without importing anachronistic technologies as literal descriptions—use structural analogies where necessary—but that addresses the specific case in which the evil being minimized includes the erosion of a common epistemic framework. The response should lay out the different readings of Mill's view clearly, indicate which ones are plausible given his texts, and say whether any of them can accommodate the idea that a vote might be both genuinely strategic and genuinely expressive under conditions of radical epistemic division.
