I serve as the legal guardian and primary caregiver for my partner, who sustained extensive damage to the prefrontal cortex and basal ganglia in an accident two years ago. A clinical trial now offers an experimental implant: not a single neural chip, but a distributed mesh of small, interchangeable processors that assume discrete cognitive functions—spatial navigation, emotional modulation, lexical retrieval—coordinating through an external hub rather than through any biological central controller. The researchers caution that because each module can be upgraded or replaced independently, there may be no persistent core of awareness; behavioral coherence might return, yet the underlying system could be best described as a replaceable committee rather than a unified subject. My partner, before the injury, held that personal autonomy depends on there being a single, continuous center of decision. I must now decide whether to consent on their behalf. I am not seeking generic medical-ethics reassurance. Rather, I want to understand whether there exists a rigorous philosophical framework—one that does not simply appeal to behavioral continuity or biological substrate—that can determine when a modular, distributed repair preserves the original conscious subject versus when it constructs a functionally equivalent but ontologically distinct entity, and consequently whether my partner's prior commitment to autonomous selfhood applies to whatever system would emerge.
