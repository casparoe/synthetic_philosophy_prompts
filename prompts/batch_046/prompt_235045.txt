In the historiography of nationalism, the long-running dispute between modernists—who draw on Hobsbawm’s thesis that traditions are invented and Anderson’s thesis that nations are imagined communities—and their perennialist or ethno-symbolist critics, who insist on older ethnic and cultural continuities, seems to have reached an impasse. Each side now mobilises familiar evidence—pre-modern ethnic categories, the transformative role of print capitalism, commemorative rituals—without altering the other’s position. My own hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal: modernists tend to treat "nation" as a sovereign political community, whereas critics treat it as a cultural community with deep historical memory; and they diverge over whether "imagined" denotes psychological construction or deliberate fabrication. But I am unsure whether this captures the real division, or whether there is a hidden empirical question—perhaps about which pre-modern groups possessed the self-awareness required for national consciousness—that neither side has settled. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading? Does the modernist account fail to explain the affective intensity of national attachment because it treats imagination as purely cognitive rather than emotional, or is that objection misdirected? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a new comparative case, or an admission that the question is ill-posed? Please respond in British English.
