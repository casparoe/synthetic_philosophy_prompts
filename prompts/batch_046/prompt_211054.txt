hey — i'm a novelist working on a book about a logic professor who starts to confuse narrative atmosphere with proof. i've been reading up on intuition pumps (dennett's term, i think?) and i keep noticing this very specific failure mode that i don't have a word for. here's the pattern: an author builds a hypothetical scenario — the violinist hooked up to you, the trolley on the tracks, whatever — and embeds one sensory or physical detail that feels like pure scene-setting. like the warmth of the tubes, or the sound of the heart monitor, or the fact that the lever is rusty. logically you could swap that detail out and the argument wouldn't change. but in practice that detail does hidden argumentative work: it makes certain responses feel cruel or impossible in a way they wouldn't if the connection were wireless, or digital, or bureaucratic. people resist counter-examples not because the logic fails, but because the counter-example lacks the same fictional texture, so it feels like it's missing the point. i know there's "framing" and "loaded language" but those seem about word choice, not about this deeper thing where the narrative specificity itself becomes a hidden constraint on what answers are acceptable. is there an established concept for exactly this — maybe something in the literature on modal intuition or thought-experiment design? and if not, can you distinguish it precisely from framing and from plain emotional appeal? also, please seperate what's settled about how intuition pumps work from what's genuinely contested among people who study them; don't present one contested view as if it's the consensus.
