I've adopted the strict view that honoring sunk costs is always irrational and that any decision rule giving weight to unrecoverable past investments violates the axioms of rational choice. I take this to apply without exception: in personal finance, in organizational strategy, in belief revision, and in collective action. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position — not just the familiar psychological observation that people resist quitting because it feels like failure, but which specific intuitions, formal results, or structural features of decision problems would force a revision or abandonment of the view. I am especially interested in two less-discussed angles: first, whether there are collective-action contexts in which continuing to invest serves as a credible commitment signal that changes the payoff structure for other agents, so that ignoring the sunk cost undermines a rational signaling strategy; and second, whether in long-term belief revision there are epistemic sunk costs — the coherence and explanatory integration accumulated by a theoretical framework — such that abandoning it for a marginally better rival can be rational only if those integration costs are accounted for, which might look like honoring a sunk cost but actually tracks a real present value. For each potential defeater, indicate whether a committed defender of the strict view would concede it as damaging, dismiss it as a confusion, or absorb it by redescription. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the strict view is well-insulated in a way that is intellectually virtuous or problematically unfalsifiable.
