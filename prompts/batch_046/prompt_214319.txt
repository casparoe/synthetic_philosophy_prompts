I am drafting a response paper for my graduate seminar in environmental historiography, which this term has focused on the problem of non-human causation and the rhetoric of agency applied to biological and geological forces. I recently read a synthesis—an unnamed review essay circulating in our course packet—that attempts to characterize the so-called material-turn approach to explaining pre-modern state crises and epidemiological collapses. The author summarizes the position in the following terms. According to her account, pathogenic microbes, volcanic aerosol forcings, and abrupt climate transitions ought to be treated as historical actors situated in the same explanatory register as human institutions and individual decision-makers. Rather than functioning as mere background conditions, these entities are said to possess a form of distributed agency: they initiate causal sequences, constrain the range of viable human responses, and restructure the decision environments within which political and economic choices become intelligible. The author presents this framework as a direct antidote to environmental determinism. Her reasoning is that once causal weight is distributed across human and non-human domains, no single biological or climatic factor can be said to determine an outcome in isolation; therefore, to explain the contraction of a trade network or the destabilization of an imperial administration by reference to a plague vector or a volcanic winter is not to invoke determinism, but merely to recognize that non-human forces are co-authors of historical change. She adds that objections charging anthropomorphism miss their mark, because the framework never ascribes mental states or intentions to viruses or weather systems; it grants them only causal efficacy within networks of interaction. From these premises she draws the conclusion that adequate historical explanation must be reconstructed as a multi-actor narrative in which human agency is always already mediated by microbial, atmospheric, and geological partners, and that any account omitting these partners is necessarily incomplete.

I find this summary compelling but also suspiciously neat. I am trying to determine whether it is a fair representation of the view defended by historians of climate and disease, or whether it smuggles in conceptual confusions that make the position easier to defend than it actually is. Specifically, I would like an audit that proceeds part by part: which claims in this summary would a careful proponent accept without qualification, which are common simplifications that obscure important distinctions, and which are straightforward misrepresentations? I am particularly unsure about the move from distributed causation to the dissolution of determinism—does that inference hold, or does it conflate multi-factor causation with the absence of determination? I also wonder whether the summary collapses the distinction between treating an entity as an explanatory factor and treating it as an agent in any meaningful sense. Please address whether the conclusion that omitting these forces yields a necessarily incomplete explanation survives once the summary is corrected, and identify the weakest point in the framework that a skeptical interlocutor in our seminar—someone who accepts that climate and disease matter causally but rejects the language of agency—would press hardest.
