Philosophers discussing A.J. Ayer's legacy have long been stuck on a single episode: after a lifetime of logical-positivist arguments that talk of life after death is cognitively meaningless, Ayer himself reported, near the end of his life, an experience that seemed to him to suggest consciousness might persist. Those who defend his early view dismiss the report as a neurochemical artifact that violates no verificationist principle properly understood; those who take it seriously argue that first-person phenomenology cannot be legislated away by semantic theory. The debate never moves. Is the impasse substantive—about whether subjective reports are admissible evidence in metaphysics—or verbal—about whether "survival" means bodily continuity, informational persistence, or something else entirely? Or are the two camps answering different questions, one about the conditions of meaningful assertion, the other about the ontology of consciousness? Give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, indicate whether a hidden empirical claim (about brain states, about the reliability of end-of-life reports) is doing the work, and say what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an experimental result, or a reconception of the dispute that makes one side's answer irrelevant.
