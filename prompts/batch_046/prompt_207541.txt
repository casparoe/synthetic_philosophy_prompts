In a seminar on data governance, a colleague dismissed objections to large-scale behavioral tracking with the line, "If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear." I want to treat this as an exercise in precisification. Lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing—distinguishing at least epistemic, instrumental, structural, and normative variants—and for each say whether it amounts to a tautology, a contingent empirical claim, or a substantive normative premise. Then specify which of these readings is actually being invoked when the line is used to rebut an argument about "inferential privacy": the worry that even individually innocuous data traces can be aggregated and modeled to predict sensitive attributes (health conditions, political preferences, relational dynamics), so that harm arises not from revealing a pre-existing secret but from constructing knowledge that did not previously exist in any single observer's mind. For every reading that could plausibly support that rebuttal, state the further commitments it requires—about observer benevolence, the moral irrelevance of aggregation, the appropriate definition of harm, or the boundary between disclosure and inference—and flag any reading that is coherent but plainly false. Present your analysis as a disambiguation menu rather than selecting one correct interpretation, and indicate which readings collapse once we distinguish informational privacy (control over what is revealed) from decisional privacy (control over what can be inferred about one's choices and conditions).
