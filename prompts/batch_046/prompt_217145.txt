As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am investigating whether prosperity and public order can justify authoritarian rule, and I have formed concrete verdicts on several cases. I want to know whether a single coherent principle captures them, or whether I must abandon one. Here are my judgments.

First, Singapore’s development-state model: sustained high growth, very low corruption, reliable public goods and infrastructure, strict legal limits on opposition organizing and media criticism of the ruling party, but an independent judiciary for commercial disputes and relatively open emigration. Verdict: the prosperity and order provide a partial, conditional justification; the arrangement is not fully legitimate, but it is not wholly illegitimate either, because exit and commercial rule of law supply a thin feedback loop.

Second, China’s post-1978 trajectory: massive poverty reduction, rapid industrialization, and world-class infrastructure, combined with suppression of independent labor movements and coercive assimilation policies in regions such as Xinjiang. Verdict: the prosperity does not justify the authoritarian rule; the scale of coercion, the absence of institutional feedback, and the closure of exit for affected populations break any plausible justification.

Third, a hypothetical small state that achieves perfect macroeconomic stability, eliminates violent crime through predictive governance, delivers universal high-quality healthcare, yet maintains zero democratic institutions, criminalizes all organized dissent, and closes its borders to prevent emigration. Verdict: not justified at all; without any procedural mechanism for correction, exit, or voice, prosperity functions as an instrument of domination rather than a source of legitimacy.

Fourth, a regime whose economic metrics nearly mirror Singapore’s—high growth, low corruption, excellent infrastructure, independent commercial courts, and vigorous technical debate on zoning and environment—but which prohibits emigration, criminalizes criticism of the ruling party’s founding ideology, suppresses constitutional debate entirely, and produces severe ecological degradation. Verdict: not justified, despite matching Singapore’s outputs; the closure of exit, the suppression of constitutional voice, and the irreversible ecological harm remove the conditional element that made the first case defensible.

Fifth, an authoritarian government that achieves markedly lower pandemic mortality than democratic peers through rapid centralized response, then permanently extends emergency surveillance and detention powers long after the health crisis ends. Verdict: the success grants only a temporary, emergency-bound justification; once the crisis passes, the extended powers are unjustified, so the regime’s claim collapses.
