I'm preparing an episode for my philosophy podcast, and my guest is a political philosopher who has been developing what she calls the asymmetry of attention thesis: the claim that manipulation is not best defined by hidden intent, irrational exploitation, or the bypassing of reason, but rather by a structural redirection of the target's attention away from reasons that are genuinely her own and toward reasons that have been implanted by the influencer's framing architecture. She argues that legitimate persuasion, by contrast, respects what she terms attentional ownership, leaving the target's evaluative priorities intact even when the persuader is highly strategic. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that stay firmly inside this framework rather than treating it as a variant of standard autonomy-based accounts.

Specifically, I need questions that press her on three pressure points where the literature on manipulation usually pushes back. First, I want a question that uses a concrete case—say, a personalized recommendation algorithm that learns a user's long-term values and then ranks options in a way that makes one choice salient without hiding alternatives—asking whether this counts as manipulation on her view, followed immediately by the reply she is most likely to give (that the algorithm respects attentional ownership if it mirrors the user's own weighting) and then a sharper follow-up that tests whether transparency of the framing mechanism is enough to salvage persuasion. Second, I need a question about rhetorical techniques in democratic deliberation: if a skilled speaker reorganizes the agenda so that citizens attend to security rather than equality, does that violate attentional ownership, and what does she say to those who object that all agenda-setting is structurally identical? Again, include the anticipated standard reply and a follow-up that forces her to specify the boundary. Third, ask how her view handles cases where the target's original attentional priorities are themselves the product of prior manipulation—what she might call nested framing—without collapsing into the claim that no influence can ever be legitimate. For each cluster, avoid generic biographical or definitional openers; every question should assume she accepts the asymmetry of attention thesis and should aim to show either an internal tension or an unexpected extension of it.

Finally, end the sequence with one question that asks what kind of empirical or conceptual evidence could genuinely weaken the thesis—perhaps a scenario in which attentional redirection occurs but no one feels wronged, or vice versa—so that the interview closes on a substantive methodological point rather than a summary. The tone should be collegial but rigorous, suitable for an audience familiar with philosophy of action and political philosophy but not requiring specialized knowledge of decision theory or cognitive science.
