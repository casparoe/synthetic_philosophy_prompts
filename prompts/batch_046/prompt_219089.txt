I'm preparing consistency notes for a graduate philosophy-of-language seminar and would like an audit of my current position on Quinean translation, reference, and ontology before I present it. Here is where I stand.

First, I accept Quine's thesis of radical translation indeterminacy in its standard form: for observational sentences such as the familiar gavagai case, behavioral dispositions and stimulus conditions alone do not determine whether the native term refers to enduring rabbits, to temporal rabbit-stages, or to undetached rabbit parts. There is no further fact of the matter—no hidden mental representation or Platonic correspondence—that makes one translation manual uniquely correct.

Second, despite this, I think the causal theory of reference for natural-kind terms supplies a partial cross-linguistic constraint. If the native speakers' use of the term is causally regulated by enduring biological organisms rather than by fleeting spatiotemporal slices, then translating it as referring to rabbit-stages is not merely ontologically extravagant but misaligned with the causal-historical chain that fixes the term's reference within the native language.

Third, I hold Quine's criterion of ontological commitment: what there is is determined by the values of bound variables in our best-regimented scientific theory, and ontological questions are always internal to a background language.

Fourth, I think that when two translation manuals are empirically equivalent but quantify over different entities—say, enduring rabbits versus temporal rabbit-parts—they are not describing different worlds. They are describing the same underlying physical reality through different logical grammars, and there is no translation-independent, absolute ontology to adjudicate between them.

Fifth, I accept a strong version of meaning holism: even apparently observational sentences derive their semantic content from their inferential connections to the entire theoretical web, not from any direct, isolable link to sensory stimulation.

Sixth, I maintain that when two manuals are empirically adequate, we should prefer the one that minimizes unnecessary ontological commitments, even though both are literally true descriptions of the same reality under different grammars.

Please trace which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and rank the tensions by severity. In particular, I want to know whether my causal-reference commitment in (2) actually defeats the radical indeterminacy in (1) or can be reconciled with it; whether my holism in (5) undermines the very observational anchors Quine needs to generate the indeterminacy argument without undermining its conclusion; and whether my parsimony preference in (6) conflicts with my claim in (4) that there is no deeper ontological fact—if there is no fact, why should simplicity matter? For each genuine tension, suggest the least costly revision and what it costs me. Also flag any pairs that only look incompatible but dissolve on inspection, and tell me which single revision, if any, would clean up the most conflicts at once.
