I am writing a novel in which a cooperative housing board votes to replace human wellness checks with an automated sensor grid that, as the technical manual reveals, is calibrated on biometric norms for adults under fifty. One board member, a novelist who had been deep in a manuscript, abstained from the vote to avoid administrative distraction; months earlier she had skimmed that same manual for research, noticed the age-calibration flaw, yet said nothing, assuming qualified experts elsewhere would intervene. When an elderly resident falls and lies undetected for two days because the sensors misread his stillness as normal sleep, she finds herself asking whether her twin failures—her silence at the meeting and her unvoiced knowledge—amount to having caused his death in the same way that actively harming him would, or whether the layers of institutional mediation—the board, the vendor, the algorithm—break the causal chain so that her inaction is metaphysically and morally something other than a cause. I want the philosophical structure of this situation laid bare: is there a genuine distinction between what she did and what she failed to do when her refusal to act was partly a withdrawal into her work and partly a passive assumption that responsibility lay elsewhere, and what would have to be true for that distinction to collapse? Please reply in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a letter; I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, only for an honest assessment of how exposed the distinction between acting and omitting really is in a case like this.
