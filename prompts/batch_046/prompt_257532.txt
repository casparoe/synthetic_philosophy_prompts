My seminar group tried to defend full-blown lottocracy and now I'm stuck with this. (1) The point of democracy is that no one should be subject to laws made by people systematically different from them. (2) Elections guarantee that representatives are richer, older, and more educated then the average citizen, so they fail this test. (3) A sorted assembly fixes it: random selection makes the chamber a statistical mirror of the population on gender, income, occupation, everything. (4) So replacing elections with lot isn't anti-democratic; it's the only way to actually satisfy democratic equality. (5) But if statistical mirroring is required, it applies to every binding decision, not just parliaments — juries, regulatory boards, even hospital ethics committees should be filled by lot too. (6) And inside any sorted body of 500, those 500 hold all the power over the rest, so to be consistent you'd need either continuous rotation or recursive sortition for every subcommitte. (7) Continuous rotation destroys expertise and memory; recursive sortition just creates smaller unrepresentative elites. (8) So the argument seems to show either that democracy requires technical incompetence everywhere, or that statistical mirroring was never the right criterion. I want to keep (1) — there's something right about not being ruled by a different class — but I don't want to end up saying we should pick epidemiologists by lot. Where's the slip? Is it an equivocation between group-level mirroring and individual-level power? Or is (4) just overreaching?
