Argue that Scanlon’s contractualist framework can accommodate the intuition that numbers matter in rescue cases without collapsing into simple aggregation, provided we reconceive the relevant “burden” in terms of a non-linear, threshold-sensitive relational harm rather than an additive individual cost. Specifically, defend the position that when a rescuer can save either one person or five from an identical threat, the principle permitting the rescue of only one is rejectable not because it aggregates five separate complaints, but because leaving the four additional victims in a state of “structured moral abandonment” crosses a qualitative threshold in what any affected party can reasonably demand of others. Explain how this “threshold-relational” interpretation remains faithful to Scanlon’s individualist constraint—since the rejection is still grounded in the standpoint of each person who is left behind—while avoiding both the standard objection that contractualism cannot justify saving the greater number and the counter-objection that it licenses indiscriminate maximization. Address at least one serious difficulty: that threshold-sensitive harm seems to reintroduce interpersonal comparison of welfare in a way Scanlon’s view explicitly rejects.
