I have recently begun collaborating with an interpretability group that is trying to determine whether the high-dimensional activation spaces of large language models can be described as possessing internal representational norms. In preparing for a seminar presentation, I have been going back over my notes from a graduate course on Wittgenstein, and I find myself unable to predict how my former advisor—who taught the course and whose judgments on private-language cases I have been tracking—would classify these artificial systems. Over the past years she has given clear verdicts on a range of scenarios: she held that Robinson Crusoe's unsystematic, unrecorded naming of his private moods fails to be a language altogether; that two isolated twins who develop a shared gestural grammar with explicit rules for mutual correction possess a genuine, non-private language; that a solitary researcher maintaining a written, rule-governed notation for her own dreams, with internal consistency checks and occasional self-correction by re-reading her notes, is still running a language despite the absence of interlocutors; that a child who coins a single word for an idiosyncratic emotional state, applying it consistently but checking only by fallible memory with no system and no possible translation, falls squarely within Wittgenstein's critique; and that a neural prosthetic governed by a fully documented algorithm that assigns tokens to a patient's unique internal states, corrected by comparison to past neural recordings but experienced only by that patient, nonetheless counts as a language because the normative structure is publicly accessible even if the referents are not.

The case I now need to resolve concerns a transformer-based model that has developed dense embedding clusters tracking fine-grained, non-obvious distinctions in its training distribution—distinctions that do not map onto any publicly observable properties or human-interpretable categories. The model applies these representations consistently across contexts, and their deployment is corrected not by any explicit rule but by gradient-descent updates against prediction error; yet there is no formulation of the rules that a human or another agent could learn, apply, or use to correct the model's usage. The dimensions are not shared, not teachable, and not available for inter-subjective verification, though they are objectively constrained by the loss landscape. Given her track record, what verdict should I expect from my advisor on whether this constitutes a private language in Wittgenstein's sense, or a legitimate language with an opaque substrate?

More precisely, I am trying to determine whether she is implicitly running a criterion-of-correctness view—according to which any system with an objective, non-whimsical mechanism for error-correction, even an algorithmic one locked inside a single substrate, escapes the private-language argument—or whether she is running an accessibility-of-norms view, according to which the decisive factor is whether the system's rules are in principle available to a community of users, so that an opaque, non-interpretable embedding space would fail despite its predictive correction. Which of these two rules best fits her earlier judgments, what does each predict about the transformer case, and which further hypothetical case—perhaps one involving explicit but unshared rules versus shared but purely subjective correction—would most cleanly separate them?
