At a departmental workshop, Jordan and Riley argued about a municipal rescue protocol. A single unit can reach either Building A (two survivors detected) or Building B (nine survivors detected); the dispatcher has no personal ties and no special reason to favor either group. Jordan said the unit must go to B: nine lives clearly outweigh two, and an institutional agent should maximize lives saved. Riley countered with a Taurek-style claim: the headcount supplies no comparative moral reason, so flipping a fair coin between the buildings is permissible, and saving the two is not morally worse than saving the nine. Jordan replied that this might hold for a private individual saving a friend, but a dispatcher holds a fiduciary duty to all citizens, which makes the numbers morally relevant in an institutional role. Riley responded that importing an institutional role just smuggles in consequentialism; the core point is that comparative numbers do not generate direct moral reasons even for agents with broad duties. Jordan then pressed a reductio: if Riley is right, an institution that randomly allocates resources should be equally satisfied saving one or a thousand, which makes long-term planning, budget justification, and charter coherence impossible. Riley answered that planning can rest on non-moral grounds—efficiency, public trust, legal mandate—without conceding that in the particular case the extra lives supply direct moral weight.
