Here are notes I am drafting for a short essay on Moritz Schlick’s 1927 essay on the meaning of life, which I understand argues that life is genuinely lived only in playful, creative activity pursued for its own sake, while adulthood turns existence into instrumental labor and purpose-driven work. I want to develop what I call the scheduling paradox: if play must be free from purpose, then any deliberate strategy to build more play into one’s life—say, through scheduled leisure, recreational planning, or work-life balance frameworks—seems to convert that activity back into a means, and therefore back into work. I also want to press a self-undermining objection: if philosophical reflection about life’s meaning is itself a form of purpose-driven labor, then Schlick’s own inquiry may be incompatible with the state of play he claims is the only meaningful one. I am considering comparing this tension to the distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value, though I am unsure whether that comparison holds up precisely. Could you comment on whether the scheduling paradox is a fair internal criticism of Schlick’s view, whether it mischaracterizes his distinction between youth and adulthood, and whether there is an obvious reply—perhaps one Schlick or his critics actually offered—to the charge that his philosophy undermines itself? I am writing without formal philosophical training, so I am especially interested in where my framing is inaccurate or where I have missed a standard objection that makes the point redundant.
