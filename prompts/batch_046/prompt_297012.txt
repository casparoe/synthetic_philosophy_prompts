Question: Must hypocrisy always defeat standing to blame? Analyze the following case. Dr. Voss, an ethicist, has published work condemning scholars who cite predatory journals, yet she herself did so once in an early article she now disowns. Dean Reed has never personally made such a citation, but he approves university contracts with publishers that operate predatory journals. When Voss publicly blames the administration for these contracts, Reed accuses her of hypocrisy and denies her standing. Examine (i) whether Reed's charge succeeds under the standard 'same fault' condition, (ii) whether standing requires freedom from all related faults or only from the specific fault being blamed, and (iii) whether institutional complicity and individual citation choices can be weighed on the same moral scale. Do not simply take a side; examine the structure of the standing relation.

Student answer: Voss clearly retains standing. First, hypocrisy undermines standing only when the blamer shares the identical fault; Reed has never cited a predatory journal, so his accusation fails on its own terms. Second, even under the non-comparative view—which holds that standing depends solely on whether the blamed party has wronged the blamer—Reed has not personally wronged Voss, so his objection is irrelevant to her standing to blame the administration. Third, institutional complicity operates at a structural level and is categorically incommensurable with an individual citation; treating Reed's contract approvals and Voss's single past citation as comparable is a category error, not a moral evaluation. Consequently, Voss's past mistake may reduce her credibility, but credibility is distinct from moral standing, and Reed's charge amounts to a deflection rather than a genuine challenge to her standing.
