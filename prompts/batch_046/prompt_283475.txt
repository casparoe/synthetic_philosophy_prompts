I am trying to determine whether my judgments about monuments, street names, and institutional names form a coherent ethical view, or whether I am simply reacting case by case. Here are my verdicts. First, a statue of a historical figure in a central public plaza should be relocated to a museum with full contextualization rather than destroyed, because preserving the physical object serves historical knowledge even when public honor is withdrawn. Second, a street bearing that same figure's name should be renamed, since using the name in daily navigation functions as an active, ongoing address of esteem rather than mere reference. Third, a monument erected specifically to terrorize a marginalized group—rather than to commemorate achievement—should be removed entirely, not relocated, because its form, placement, and intended effect are inseparable from that purpose. Fourth, a school named after a researcher whose work included now-repudiated theories should retain its name only if the name has come to denote the institution's own contemporary identity rather than the researcher's legacy; however, if the naming were proposed today, it should be rejected. Fifth, I feel that renaming is morally more significant than adding a counter-plaque but less severe than physical removal, though I cannot precisely justify that ordering. Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing commemorative honor from archival preservation, or distinguishing forward-looking institutional identification from backward-looking attribution—that captures all five judgments? If so, what does it predict about a case in which a building and a street honor the same person, or about a monument whose intimidating purpose is disputed by historians? And if no principle can accommodate all five, which verdict should I abandon?
