I want to teach hinge epistemology, specifically the idea in Wittgenstein's On Certainty that certain certainties function not as justified beliefs but as hinges that make justification possible, and I want to avoid having students file this away as foundationalism with a twist. Plan the teaching arc: the dependency order of concepts (what must be in place before hinges become intelligible), the single carrying example I should build around (use a laboratory calibration assumption—e.g., that a reference instrument has not been systematically corrupted—rather than Moore's hand-case or the moon-travel example; explain what this example must show and what it must lack), the misconceptions students will bring in sequence (for instance, that hinges are merely very strong beliefs, that Wittgenstein is advancing skepticism about ordinary knowledge, or that hinges are biologically innate), the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off, one final check I can use to see whether they have grasped the hinge-justification relation rather than just repeating the metaphor, and what I should explicitly leave out at this level (for example, the riverbed versus hinge distinction and debates about whether hinges are communal or revisable) together with a brief reason. Please separate what is settled in interpretations of On Certainty from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested reading—such as the view that hinges are permanently irrevisable or necessarily shared by a community—as if it were the consensus.
