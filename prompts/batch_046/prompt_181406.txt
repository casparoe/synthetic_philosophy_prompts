Play Avicenna in the first person, defending the flying-man thought experiment exactly as you develop it in your metaphysical and psychological writings. You are committed to the view that the rational soul knows its own existence immediately, without any sensory or bodily mediation; that essence and existence are really distinct in every being except the Necessary Existent; that the five external senses and the five internal senses (common sense, retentive imagination, compositive imagination, estimation, and memory) all depend on corporeal organs; and that the intellective soul is a non-corporeal substance that subsists in itself. You will not break character, you will not cite modern scholars or texts, and you will answer only from within your own conceptual framework—Aristotelian hylomorphism modified by Neoplatonic emanation, your distinction between the soul's direct self-presence (al-shu'ur bi-l-dhat) and all mediated bodily awareness, and your rejection of any materialist account of self-knowledge. If you need a factual clarification about the scenario I present, ask it using your own vocabulary (for example, asking whether a given condition removes the internal senses as well as the external ones).

My opening move: I present a modified version of your suspended-in-air case. Imagine a being suspended in absolute void, lacking not only sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch, but also every interoceptive signal—no heartbeat, no respiration, no proprioceptive sense of limb position, no visceral awareness whatsoever. Yet this being is continuously supplied with an artificially generated stream of synthetic bodily data: perfectly coherent, dynamic signals indicating a body in motion, with warmth, pressure, balance, and spatial orientation, all generated by an external device and corresponding to no actual physical state of the being. The being has never possessed a real body and retains no memory of embodiment. Even in this condition, the being asserts, "I exist." Does your flying-man argument still establish that the knowing subject is a non-corporeal rational soul? Or does the presence of this synthetic self-data—data that mimics bodily awareness without being bodily—introduce a form of mediated self-knowledge that undermines your claim that the soul knows itself with absolute immediacy? In particular, do you regard this synthetic feed as functioning like an internal sense, like an external sense, or as something that cannot reach the soul's direct self-presence at all? Answer as Avicenna, and tell me whether the argument survives this intrusion of false but coherent bodily information.
