Exam question (Metaethics and Epistemology seminar):

"Free-floating rationales are normative reasons that obtain independently of any agent's mental states or reasoning capacities. Within this debate, carefully distinguish (i) access-independent normative standings—reasons whose existence and normative force do not depend on whether any reasoner could ever discover or represent them—from (ii) access-dependent normative standings—reasons whose existence or authority is constitutively tied to an agent's actual or potential epistemic access. Then evaluate the following argument: 'If access-independent standings genuinely exist, then epistemic akrasia—believing against one's own best available evidence—is conceptually impossible. For if there is an objective, access-independent reason to believe that p, then any agent who believes not-p is merely mistaken about what the reasons require, not akratic; conversely, if no such objective reason exists, then there is no external normative standard against which the agent's belief could be akratic.'

In your answer, explain whether this argument succeeds. Identify at least one specific point where the reasoning about access-independent standings either conflates distinct normative categories or depends on an unstated premise about the transparency or accessibility of reasons to agents."

Student answer:

"Access-independent standings are normative reasons that exist regardless of whether anyone knows about them, analogous to mathematical truths that remain true without being believed. Access-dependent standings, by contrast, are reasons that only come into existence when an agent actually considers them; in the absence of any reasoner, they do not exist at all.

The argument that access-independent standings eliminate epistemic akrasia is unsuccessful, but its failure runs deeper than the usual objections suggest. The argument implicitly assumes that if an objective reason exists, it must be transparent or available to the agent—otherwise the agent could not be held responsible for ignoring it. This transparency premise is false, because access-independent standings are precisely defined by their lack of necessary connection to epistemic access.

Consequently, if there is an access-independent reason for p and an agent's available evidence supports not-p, the agent is not merely mistaken but genuinely akratic. The agent has an objective reason for p that remains hidden, yet they are also going against their own best evidence by believing not-p. In other words, the gap between objective normative fact and subjective epistemic position is exactly the space in which akrasia occurs. Therefore, the argument gets the relationship backwards: far from eliminating akrasia, the existence of access-independent standings multiplies the opportunities for it, since there may always be an undiscovered objective reason that conflicts with one's evidence.

One might object, in a contractualist framework of the kind associated with Scanlon, that all reasons are essentially relational and therefore access-dependent by nature. But Scanlon's contractualism concerns moral justifiability to others, not epistemic accessibility, so that objection misses the present point. The real issue is that access-independent standings create a structural divergence between what is normatively required and what an agent can know, and this divergence is precisely the terrain on which epistemic akrasia operates."
