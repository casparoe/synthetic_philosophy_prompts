For a long-form feature I am writing on conceptual issues in the philosophy of well-being, I need an explanation of a tension that standard treatments of Nozick's experience machine tend to gloss over. Most popular accounts treat the machine as a straightforward intuition pump against pure hedonism: we would not plug in, therefore pleasure is not all that matters. I am trying to get at a more specific conceptual problem for hybrid theories—those that combine subjective satisfaction with objective achievement conditions. The question is whether these theories must treat achievement as essentially involving direct causal interaction with an external world, or whether they can allow achievements to be constituted by the internal structural coherence of one's mental life, provided that life satisfies certain representational conditions. Specifically, explain whether the experience machine undermines well-being primarily by severing causal contact with reality, or whether the deeper failure is a systematic misalignment between one's representational states and the actual causal structure of one's environment—even when, inside the simulation, that environment is itself defined by the simulation's parameters. Can a hybrid theorist rescue the view by reconceiving achievement in representational rather than causal terms, and if so, what conceptual cost does that reconception carry? I want a gut-level conceptual map, not a survey of the literature, and I need it to stand on its own without reference to specific page numbers or bibliographic lists.
