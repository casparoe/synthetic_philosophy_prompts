I'm trying to get reflective equilibrium on expected-value reasoning by testing my gut against concrete choices, and I keep finding a cluster that won't sit together cleanly. Here are my verdicts.

Buying a lottery ticket with negative expected value just for the dream: irrational.

Spending $500 on a smoke detector that lowers my personal risk of fire death by 0.2%: clearly reasonable.

Donating $20,000 to an early-stage, high-risk scientific initiative that has perhaps a 0.02% chance of preventing an existential catastrophe affecting billions: this strikes me as rational, and probably a good use of funds, though I don't feel it's strictly obligatory.

Spending every waking hour and every disposable dollar pursuing that same initiative: fanatical, not required. The expected value doesn't scale linearly with total life investment.

Paying $20,000 to insure against a 0.02% chance of being personally consigned to eternal suffering (a scenario I assign negligible credence but treat structurally): irrational, and I reject the comparison to the existential-risk donation. The two feel structurally identical, but one is sane and one is Pascal-madness.

Choosing a career that pays modestly but offers a 0.1% chance of contributing to a solution for a global coordination failure, giving up other talents: defensible, supererogatory, not mandatory.

Refusing to have children because there's a 0.001% chance they could, through an unforeseeable chain, contribute to an existential risk: wrong, an over-application of tail-risk logic.

The case I keep revisiting: a funding mechanism I'm calling a "utility cascade" protocol, where a successful intervention doesn't save a fixed number of lives but unlocks a multiplicative chain (saving 1,000 people enables saving 10,000 more, and so on). There's a 0.005% chance of triggering the cascade with a $30,000 donation. I can't decide whether funding this is closer to the $20,000 existential-risk donation (rational) or closer to the eternal-suffering insurance (irrational).

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving epistemic thresholds, the distinction between risk-bearing and risk-creating sacrifice, or the linearity of obligation—that captures all or most of these verdicts? If not, what are the two or three competing principles, and which clause of each is motivated by which case above? Please state the principle precisely, apply it to the cascade protocol and to one further case I haven't mentioned (say, buying health insurance with an expected payout of 70 cents on the dollar but protection against bankruptcy), and be explicit about which of my verdicts each principle must overturn.
