I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on the normative architecture of welfare conditionality, and in a recent seminar with colleagues we kept returning to a puzzle about the moral psychology behind workfare and fraud investigation. We all recognized the standard reciprocity story—that unconditional support violates mutual obligation, so job-search mandates and sanctions are justified as ways of ensuring claimants contribute back to the cooperative scheme—but I kept feeling that this framework misses something structural about how claimants are treated as epistemic agents. To push the discussion further, I'd like you to compare two conceptual frameworks that I think operate, often simultaneously, within welfare-state discourse on the 'undeserving poor.'

The first framework is the reciprocity or mutual-obligation model, familiar from debates on workfare: conditionality is justified because social benefits are not gifts but part of a cooperative exchange, and claimants who can work but refuse are failing to uphold their side of the bargain. Here the moral deficiency imputed to the claimant is primarily volitional—laziness, free-riding, or a refusal to shoulder one's share.

The second framework is one I am developing in my own research, which I call the 'credibility deficit' model. On this view, welfare regimes do not merely police reciprocity; they institutionalize a prior judgment that claimants possess systematically diminished epistemic authority over their own needs, capacities, and circumstances. Fraud investigation, intrusive verification, and the presumption of deception are not just safeguards against shirking but expressions of the idea that claimants' testimony is unreliable by default. The 'undeserving poor' in this framework is not mainly the shirker who withholds labor, but the claimant presumed to misrepresent reality, making administrative suspicion and gatekeeping rational rather than merely punitive.

Please compare these two frameworks as normative justifications for workfare and benefit-fraud regimes. Specifically: (1) How do they differ in their moral psychology—what kind of failure or deficiency does each impute? (2) How does each understand the state's relationship to the claimant—as contractual partner enforcing reciprocity, or as epistemic gatekeeper regulating credible testimony? (3) Which framework is more vulnerable to the charge that it naturalizes structural unemployment or disability as personal moral defects? (4) Can the two be synthesized, or are they conceptually in tension—does treating someone as an unreliable narrator undermine the very reciprocity that assumes they are rational cooperative agents?

In your response, please clearly separate what is well-established in the philosophy and sociology of welfare from what is genuinely contested, particularly regarding whether concepts like epistemic injustice can be mapped onto bureaucratic verification procedures without overextension. Do not present either framework, including my characterization of the credibility deficit, as the consensus view; mark where you are advancing a contested interpretation or where the literature remains divided.
