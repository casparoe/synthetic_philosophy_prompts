Design a take-home essay exam for an upper-level comparative-philosophy seminar (final assessment, 1,500-word limit, 40 marks, graduate/advanced undergraduate). The question should apply Jain anekāntavāda and its syādvāda framework—specifically the saptabhaṅgī schema of seven predication modes—to the contemporary dispute over whether large language models possess understanding. It must be unanswerable by mere summary of the doctrine; instead it should require precise logical mapping and critical evaluation.

The exam prompt should instruct students to do three things:
(a) Map three competing positions on artificial understanding—functionalist affirmation, intentionalist/eliminativist denial, and principled epistemic suspension—onto the appropriate syāt-predicates, giving the warrant for each assignment and distinguishing the affirmation-and-negation mode from simple contradiction;
(b) Analyze whether the avaktavya (inexpressible or indescribable) mode is genuinely required to capture any first-person or phenomenological residue in the concept of understanding, or whether it collapses into one of the conjunction modes, with explicit reference to why avaktavya denotes inexpressibility rather than logical contradiction;
(c) Evaluate whether anekāntavāda offers a substantively different resolution of the disagreement or merely restates standard epistemic humility, requiring an explicit comparison with binary dialectic rather than a generic conclusion.

Then supply:
- The exam question itself, clearly framed with the word limit and marks;
- A model answer of about 800 words that demonstrates the specific moves an A-grade response must make, including correct deployment of at least four of the seven predicates, accurate treatment of avaktavya, and a non-trivial evaluation in part (c);
- A marking scheme (out of 40 marks, with suggested distribution) that separates A answers from B answers by naming the exact conceptual moves required—e.g., correct predicate mapping, distinction of avaktavya from contradiction, comparative evaluation with binary logic—and that lists the specific misunderstandings that should cost marks, such as conflating anekāntavāda with simple relativism, misassigning denial to the affirmation-negation mode, or treating part (c) as a restatement of “we don’t know yet” without structural comparison.
