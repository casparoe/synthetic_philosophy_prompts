Consider the following paraphrase of a passage drawn from a late-nineteenth-century English rendering of an early Nyāya commentary on the pramāṇas. The text states that perception arises when a sense-faculty, conjoined with the mind and aided by illumination, contacts the substance inhering in the object; absent any defect, the cognition is immediate and free from error. Yet when similar forms confuse the organ, or an obstruction interposes, even this direct apprehension is tainted by the appearance of what is absent, and one must distinguish the bare sensation from the judgment that supervenes upon it. The commentator assigns this supervenient judgment not to pratyakṣa but to anumāna, despite its being a modification of the very same mental occurrence rather than a second, discrete cognition.

I am struggling to see how one mental event can be both a modified perception and an instance of inference. Specifically, if the shift from bare sensation to judgment is driven not by a representational intermediary but by a causal dispositional trace (saṃskāra) left by prior cognitions, does this commit the Nyāya realist to a causal theory of epistemic justification that undermines the representational character of the pramāṇas? Please help me work through the passage's own reasoning on this point.
