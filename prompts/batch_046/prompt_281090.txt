After a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether Thomas Szasz’s critique of psychiatric medicine retains any force beyond historical curiosity, I want to settle a strategic question rather than rehash the familiar terrain. Szasz argued that mental illness is a myth in a literal sense: that the behaviors and experiences classified as psychiatric disorders are not diseases of the brain but problems in living, and that medical language in this domain functions as rhetoric that obscures moral and social conflict. Most discussions I encounter either treat this as a dead letter superseded by neurobiology or as a totalizing anti-psychiatry position. I am not convinced either framing is right, and I want to know where genuinely limited effort—say, a research group with five years, or a legislative commission with one session—should actually go if it takes Szasz’s core challenge seriously without accepting every conclusion he drew.

Consider three live lines of work that seem to depend on resolving some version of his challenge.

First, the ontological line: are current psychiatric categories best understood as natural kinds, practical kinds, or socially constructed roles, and does Szasz’s myth-claim gain or lose force depending on which ontology turns out correct—particularly if one moves toward purely dimensional models of psychological functioning rather than categorical diagnosis? Second, the coercion line: if psychiatric diagnosis is not strictly a medical designation, what becomes of involuntary commitment and the insanity defense—should they be abolished, retained with stricter evidentiary thresholds, or reconceived entirely as social-welfare interventions with different legitimating conditions? Third, the integration line: can one reject the disease-model of mental disorder while still maintaining that neuroscience, psychopharmacology, and structured clinical intervention have genuine therapeutic value, or does Szasz’s critique necessarily entail a wholesale abandonment of biological psychiatry?

Rank these three lines in order of which open questions within them matter most—both in terms of tractability and in terms of real-world consequence—if one’s goal is to reform mental-health policy and clinical practice rather than to win a philosophical victory. For each, specify the crux: the specific conceptual or empirical result that would most change its ranking. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many downstream issues depend on resolving this one—and discuss the trade-offs between them. Also identify which implications of Szasz’s view are genuinely overrated or do not follow at all: for instance, does rejecting the medical model necessarily undermine the legitimacy of psychotropic medication, or is that a non sequitur? Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
