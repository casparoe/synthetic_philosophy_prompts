Please argue for the following position in Confucian role ethics: that self-deception should be reconceived not as an isolated mind hiding a belief from itself, but as a structural breakdown in what we can call the "protocol of reciprocal visibility"—the idea that moral recognition within a relational network depends on the public readability of one's role-performance rather than on transparency of inner intention. Begin by showing how, if moral agency is constituted relationally through roles (not individually through autonomous choice), the classic logical paradox of self-deception—how one agent can simultaneously believe p and believe not-p while knowing both—dissolves not by solving the psychological contradiction but by relocating it: the failure is not first-person denial but a loss of capacity for the network (including the agent's positioned self) to read its own relational coordinates correctly. Draw specifically on the Confucian emphasis on the rectification of names (zhengming) and the performative structure of ritual propriety (li) to show how a misaligned role-performance corrupts moral knowledge from the outside in, rather than being masked by it from the inside out. Then address the strongest objection: that this merely displaces the phenomenology of first-person denial onto social dynamics without explaining why the agent herself experiences the breakdown as hiddenness or shame rather than as mere social misrecognition. Your argument should avoid invoking any specific modern psychological theory of motivated reasoning or cognitive dissonance; instead, keep the conceptual resources internal to role-ethical thinking about relational personhood and the coherence of ritualized interaction. Conclude by suggesting one concrete institutional implication—for example, how an ethics committee or family council structured by role obligations might detect and remedy this form of failure differently than it would remedy an individual's hidden bias.
