Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for a graduate value-theory seminar that sorts positions on the intersection of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain. Begin with a diagnostic root question that requires no prior knowledge of labels—specifically, whether a respondent believes that any finite or infinite quantity of pleasure can ever morally justify allowing a single instance of extreme suffering, and phrase it with reference to a concrete comparative scenario rather than an abstract principle. From that root, branch through successive commitments: (1) whether pleasure and pain are measurable on a common scale at all; (2) if they are, whether that scale is cardinal, ordinal, or interval-level only; (3) whether the lexical threshold applies to aggregate suffering across populations or to each individual suffering separately; and (4) whether incommensurability, if accepted, is symmetric (pleasure and pain are mutually incomparable) or asymmetric (suffering lexically dominates pleasure, but pleasure never dominates suffering). At each leaf, provide either the standard name of the position if one is widely recognized in philosophical ethics, or the label "unnamed" if the combination is a logical possibility that lacks a recognized defender; in either case, append exactly one sentence describing that position's most awkward or counterintuitive commitment—for instance, being forced to deny that analgesia and euphoria belong to the same welfare dimension, or being committed to the view that a world containing one tortured being and infinitely many blissful beings is morally preferable to a world containing none of either. Identify and flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate on practical grounds, explaining whether the indistinguishability is metaphysical (different foundations, same verdicts) or epistemic (the commitments diverge only in inaccessible domains). For every branch that remains unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether the gap is best explained by logical incoherence, by severe unattractiveness, or by simple historical neglect. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, author citations, or requests for primary-source quotations; build the tree entirely from the logical architecture of the commitments, using concrete cases at each node so that a respondent can navigate it by stating what they believe rather than by recognizing a theory's name. Keep the taxonomy to between six and ten leaves, and ensure that at least one leaf corresponds to a view that accepts both lexical priority and full cardinal commensurability—a combination that is logically available but often overlooked.
