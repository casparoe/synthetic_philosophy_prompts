I'm running a 90-minute workshop for about twelve adults at a community library—readers of popular history and science podcasts who have no academic training in historiography or philosophy of science. I want them to experience, not just hear about, why the boundary between legitimate revisionist scholarship and pseudohistory is so slippery, particularly when pseudohistory borrows the formal moves of real source criticism. Lately I've been thinking about a pattern I don't have a precise name for: what I call "evidentiary inversion," where a theory systematically transforms missing records into proof of suppression, treats superficial textual parallels as proof of identity rather than transmission, and compresses chronology by allowing later copies to stand in for earlier originals—moves I recognize in Fomenko's New Chronology and in some ancient-astronaut literature. I need an in-room reconstruction game in which small groups build a coherent narrative about a fictional ancient civilization from card-based fragments, but one group receives fragments constructed with these three inverted moves, while the others receive messy, contradictory primary-source-style fragments with real gaps and ambiguities. The rules and social incentives should be arranged so that the group with the inverted-source deck initially produces a more elegant, gap-free story and earns visible recognition—perhaps through a brief peer vote—before anyone reveals that their deck was engineered to conceal its own evidentiary holes.

Please design the full activity: the card materials and what is printed on them, the group roles and time limits, the recognition or payoff scheme that uses social status rather than money, and a structured debrief that maps specific moments of false confidence back onto the three inverted moves. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—especially if participants defend the elegant narrative's plausibility and feel embarrassed when its source is revealed—and be frank about which feature of real-world demarcation this exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied directly in my closing remarks. I want the problem to emerge from what the participants do with the cards, not from anything I say until the very end.
