I'm on our university's interdisciplinary research funding board, and we're deadlocked on a proposal that crosses philosophy of mind and behavoural science. The study wants to measure how people revise beliefs when confronted with disagreement from someone presented as an epistemic peer — but the external reviews are split. One reviewer, a philosopher with a strong publication record on belief revision, says the design is conceptually muddled because it treats conciliationism and steadfastness as measurable behavioural traits rather than normative positions about rational belief; the other reviewer, a cognitive psychologist of comparible standing, says that's exactly the innovation, and that treating them as dispositions is methodologically sound.

We have to decide by Thursday whether to fund it, reject it, or approve it with mandatory revisions and a six-week turnaround for the resubmission. Some board members think that because we face a genuine peer disagreement between experts we should conciliate — split the difference and require the study to integrate both normative and descriptive frameworks. Others think we should be steadfast: we have our own methodological expertise and shouldn't just average opposing expert verdicts.

Please draft the board memo recommending one of those three outcomes. Start the memo straight away with no preface or commentary, open with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. Be honest about the strongest point on the losing side. Don't include any bibliography or citation requests — just make it something we could actually circulate.
