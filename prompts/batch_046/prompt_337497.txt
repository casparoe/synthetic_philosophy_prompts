I'm in this grad seminar on philosophy of history and we've been stuck on the Whig thing for weeks now. One side says writing history without a Whig bias is basically impossible—any narrative picks winners, orders causes toward outcomes we already know, and treats dead ends as necessary detours—so calling a historian "Whiggish" is just saying they told a story instead of dumping raw archives. The other side insists you can do genuinely open-ended history, like microhistories that stay local and don't arc toward modernity, but then the first side just says those are Whiggish narratives with a smaller radius, which honestly feels like a cop-out. My working theory is that we're not actually fighting about historiography at all, but about whether "progress" is a feature we discover in the past or a feature we impose through explanation, and neither camp have said what a non-progressive explanation would even look like—would it need to avoid counterfactual dependency, or is that asking to much? But I could be wrong: maybe there's a hidden empirical disagreement about specific works neither side wants to cite, or maybe we're just answering different questions—one about narrative form, one about causal structure—under the same name.

I want an honest diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about standards of explanation, or bedrock intuition about whether the past has a direction? And if you think it's the metaphysics-of-explanation thing, is there any conceptual move—maybe distinguishing retrospective coherence from predictive closure—that could actually unstick us, or should we expect this debate to stay stalled forever?
