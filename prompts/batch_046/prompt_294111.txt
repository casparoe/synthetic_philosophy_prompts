As a journalist writing on philosophy and ideas, I am drafting a feature on whether biography can still function as a serious vehicle for moral education. For this piece, please compose a polished, formal dialogue — rendered entirely in British English — between two equally competent interlocutors. One should defend a broadly Plutarchian position: that moral learning from lives depends upon comparative exemplarity (synkrisis), in which virtues and vices become visible through the structural pairing of lives rather than through isolated narrative or hagiography. The other should oppose this, arguing that genuine moral growth requires engagement with what might be called the “uncurated residue” of a life — its contradictions, contextual failures, and historical particularity — and that Plutarch’s comparative method inevitably flattens subjects into moral archetypes suitable for prescription rather than for authentic understanding. Each speaker must respond directly to the other’s actual points: the defender should address whether comparison necessarily suppresses particularity, and should explain how pairing can reveal contingency rather than erase it; the critic should engage seriously with the claim that isolated biography risks turning its subject into an inimitable icon rather than a usable model. The dialogue should avoid treating either position as a strawman, should contain no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be suitable for publication in a serious magazine of ideas.
