I have been working through my own judgments about when belief is justified in the face of the regress problem, and I want to know whether they cohere into a single view—foundationalist, coherentist, infinitist, or some hybrid—or whether they force me to abandon one or more of them. Here are five verdicts that feel right to me, though I can already see tension between them.

First, when I see a red cardinal in good light and immediately form the belief that there is a cardinal before me, without any inference, I take myself to be fully justified; and I think the justification terminates there in the experience itself, not requiring any further supporting belief.

Second, when I accept that a distant galaxy is receding based on a long chain of astronomical reasoning—spectral measurements, calibration protocols, cosmological models, each link supporting the next—I take myself to be justified even though I have not traced the chain back to any self-evident foundation, and I think it would be acceptable if the chain continued downward without ever reaching an end, provided each link is sound.

Third, when a belief is supported only by a small, closed loop—say, belief A is justified solely by B, B solely by C, and C solely back to A, with nothing outside the circle lending any support—I think there is no justification whatsoever; the circularity is vicious regardless of how internally consistent the loop is.

Fourth, when I justify my belief that I have hands partly by visual experience and partly by tactile interaction, and I justify the reliability of those senses partly by the successful actions I perform with my hands, I still think I am justified. The mutual dependence here feels benign rather than vicious, even though there is no independent foundation outside the circle.

Fifth, imagine a being who actually traverses an infinite, non-repeating downward chain of reasons—R1 for P, R2 for R1, R3 for R2, and so on forever—with each reason genuinely possessed and no repetition anywhere. I think this being is not justified, not because the chain circles back, but because justification must be finitely graspable or anchored in a way that an endless actual descent fails to provide.

These judgments pull in different directions: the second seems to tolerate an open-ended regress, the fifth rejects an actual infinite one, the first demands a stopping point in experience, and the third and fourth seem to distinguish vicious from benign circularity without a clear rule I can articulate. Is there a precise principle—perhaps with several clauses, each motivated by one of these cases—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn and why? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, explain what it predicts for a further case I have not mentioned—perhaps a researcher who accepts a theoretical entity solely because it saves phenomena within her pre-existing framework, with no independent empirical access to it—and be explicit about which of my five judgments it must sacrifice. Write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would in a letter.
