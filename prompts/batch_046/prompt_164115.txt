I recently listened to an episode of a popular bioethics podcast in which the host summarized the standard philosophical defense of living wills—the documents people sign directing doctors to withhold treatment if they later develop severe dementia. She argued that the person who signs the directive is metaphysically the same individual as the future demented patient, since personal identity survives the loss of memory and reasoning capacity; this means the earlier rational self stands in the same authoritative relation to her future body as any competent adult does to her own future actions. Just as Ulysses had his crew bind him to the mast so that his later, siren-bewitched self could not destroy the ship, the rational signer can legitimately bind her future demented self, whose objections to care are not genuinely autonomous choices but rather symptoms of cognitive decay—morally comparable to a child’s tantrum rather than to the refusal of a competent adult. From this, she concluded that a properly executed directive should function as an absolute command, and that allowing family members or physicians to override it based on the patient’s present apparent contentment violates the autonomy of the one true, enduring self.

Something about this feels too tidy to me, especially the leap from “same person” to “absolute and unreviewable authority,” and I am unsure whether philosophers treat dementia as simply erasing autonomy rather than transforming it into something that still carries weight. Could you walk through her reasoning step by step? I want to know which premises a careful defender of advance directives would actually accept, where the summary smuggles in a conflation—perhaps between metaphysical identity and moral authority, or between temporary irrationality and permanent cognitive change—and whether her conclusion that directives must always be followed exactly survives once those errors are corrected. I am particularly unsure about the Ulysses-contract comparison and about whether the later patient’s current preferences have any independent moral standing of their own.
