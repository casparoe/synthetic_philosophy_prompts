I just finished a long argument with a friend about Machiavelli's distinction between fortuna and virtù, and I am trying to determine whether my own judgments about particular cases form a coherent pattern or merely rationalize outcomes I happen to prefer.

Here are my verdicts. When Cesare Borgia lost his state after Alexander VI died and Borgia himself fell seriously ill, I call that fortuna: the illness was genuinely unforeseeable, and the structural dependency Borgia inherited was brittle in ways that individual foresight could not fully remedy. When a prince neglects to secure his borders against a well-known and recurring threat and is then invaded, I call that a failure of virtù, not misfortune. When a commander positions his troops to exploit probable weather conditions and an unexpected storm then delivers the decisive advantage, I say virtù created the conditions for fortune to assist; but if the same storm strikes an enemy positioned with no regard for weather at all, I count the victory as mostly fortune aided. When a ruler survives an assassination attempt because he had cultivated an atmosphere of suspicion that made conspirators hesitate, I attribute that survival to virtù; if he survives only because the assassin's weapon jams by pure mechanical accident, that is fortuna. When a leader successfully adapts his statecraft to one technological shift in warfare but is then destroyed by a second, hidden innovation that no observer in his era could have anticipated, I am genuinely torn: part of me wants to call it fortuna, since the second shift lay outside the space of foreseeable risks, but part of me thinks true virtù should include building institutional resilience against unknown unknowns.

Is there a single principle that captures these judgments—perhaps something like "fortune governs outcomes outside the domain of reasonable anticipation given available knowledge, while virtù governs direct preparation, adaptive response to foreseeable change, and the design of resilient systems"? Or does that principle collapse the distinction by making every failure retrospectively a failure of meta-preparation? I want the principle stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and I want to know what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a prince who prepares extensively for conventional military and political threats but is overthrown by a popular uprising driven by an ideology that no one in his court had encountered or imagined. More specifically, for my torn case—the hidden second technological innovation—does the principle force a definite verdict, or does Machiavelli's framework genuinely leave it ambiguous?

Please separate what is settled in Machiavelli's own framework, such as the claim that fortune governs roughly half of human affairs and the river metaphor illustrating how preparation can channel but not eliminate its power, from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among scholars—for instance, whether Machiavelli ultimately subordinates fortuna to virtù through sufficient institutional design—and do not present any contested reading as if it were the consensus view.
