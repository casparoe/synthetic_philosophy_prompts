I spent most of last evening in a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether introspection is actually reliable, and I realized we never got anywhere because neither of us specified what domain of mental life we were talking about—phenomenal experience, our own propositional attitudes, or affective valence—nor what kind of failure “unreliability” was supposed to involve. To clarify things for both of us, I want a taxonomy, structured as a decision tree, that sorts philosophical positions on introspective reliability without organizing them around familiar labels like “Cartesian transparency” or “eliminativist denial.” Instead, the tree should be built from the logical space: the first branch should ask whether one accepts that there is a distinctive first-person access mode at all; the next branches should divide by whether the unreliability, if present, is pervasive or domain-specific (with explicit nodes for phenomenal character, propositional attitudes, and emotional valence); and further branches should separate failures of misdescription, failures of misattribution, and failures driven by theoretical contamination or confabulation. Every leaf should carry either the name of an actual position or the label “unnamed,” followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—something like being forced to treat self-reports of pain as authoritative while denying any special access mechanism, or granting introspective authority over belief but not over affect. The tree must also flag any combinations that are logically available but unoccupied—such as denying any first-person access mode altogether while still treating phenomenal reports as incorrigible—and should briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Each branching question should be phrased as a concrete claim or case judgment that a person could answer without already knowing which philosophical camp it maps onto, so that the instrument sorts people by what they actually believe rather than by the names they prefer.
