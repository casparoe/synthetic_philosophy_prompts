I have been trying to apply the Gettier framework to the evaluation of reasoning traces in large language models, and I worry that I am importing a false presupposition about what the problem actually targets. Here is the concrete case that is motivating me. Suppose a model answers that the Treaty of Tordesillas was signed in 1494. Its explicit chain-of-thought is confabulated: it misidentifies the treaty as the Treaty of Zaragoza, derives the date from an erroneous chronological table, and reaches 1494 only by a compensating error in its arithmetic. Nevertheless, the answer is true. Now suppose—counterfactually—that the model also contains an unactivated expert subcircuit that encodes the correct archival fact, but this subcircuit exerts no causal influence on the output token.

My initial reaction is that this is a classic Gettier case for artificial cognition: the model's belief is true, and one might even say it is justified in some broad functional sense because the correct information is present in the weights, yet the actual causal pathway to the belief is disconnected from the fact. From this I have tried to formulate a principle—call it Active-Pathway Transparency—which holds that knowledge requires the justification that actually produces the belief to be non-accidentally connected to the truth, and that dormant or hidden correct pathways do not rescue knowledge. But applying this principle consistently seems to generate an unwelcome consequence for human science. Consider Kepler's derivation of elliptical orbits from false premises about nested Platonic solids and harmonic spheres. The active reasoning pathway is clearly disconnected from the truth of the orbital law; if we imagine that some other astronomer later possessed the correct derivation but never communicated it, that hidden correct pathway does not seem to make Kepler's belief knowledge. So Active-Pathway Transparency appears to entail that Kepler did not know the laws of planetary motion, despite the standard philosophical treatment of his case as a successful—if serendipitous—discovery.

My confusion, then, is whether Gettier's original problem was ever meant to license a principle as strong as Active-Pathway Transparency, or whether I have mislocated the source of epistemic luck. Is the issue in Gettier cases that the agent's justification is false, or that it is true but only accidentally so? And in the model case, is the presence of an unactivated correct subcircuit analogous to Brown's hidden Ford in Gettier's original example, or is it more like an unaccessed library book that plays no epistemic role? More generally, can one state a precise anti-luck condition—perhaps in terms of modal safety or sensitivity—that distinguishes the model's confabulated reasoning from Kepler's false-premise reasoning without collapsing into a blanket skepticism about all discovery that involves error? I would like to know whether there is a unified account that preserves our intuition that Kepler knew something, denies knowledge to the confabulating model, and does so without introducing an ad hoc human/machine distinction.
