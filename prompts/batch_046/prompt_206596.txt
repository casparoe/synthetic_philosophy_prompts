In your answer, discuss Bernard Williams's skepticism toward rationalist and interest-based explanations of moral change, and evaluate whether Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of honor in moral revolutions confirms or complicates Williams's thesis across both cases.

Student answer:
Honor-collapse occurs when elites withdraw prestige from a practice, making participation in it shameful; honor-redefinition occurs when the conditions for being honorable are reconstrual so that the practice becomes incompatible with honor itself. Appiah's treatment of dueling is a clear instance of honor-redefinition: the code of the gentleman was reconstrual so that refusing a duel became honorable and accepting one became disreputable, rather than dueling suddenly losing prestige through some independent social withdrawal. By contrast, the end of chattel slavery in the British Atlantic was driven mainly by falling sugar profits and growing abolitionist economic pressure on plantation owners. This fits the model of honor-collapse, since British elites withdrew social recognition from slaveholding once it ceased to be economically viable, thereby rendering it shameful. Williams's thesis is that moral revolutions are rarely produced by rational moral argument or by naked economic self-interest, but rather by shifts in social honor and recognition. Because dueling ended through honor-redefinition and slavery ended through economic pressure combined with elite withdrawal of prestige—which operates as a form of honor-collapse—Williams's thesis applies uniformly: neither case was driven by rational argument, and both are ultimately honor-driven. Appiah agrees with Williams that economic interests played essentially no independent role in slavery's abolition, concentrating his analysis entirely on honor dynamics. Consequently, the two cases together confirm Williams's skepticism about rationalist explanations, since even apparent economic factors function only through the mediating mechanism of honor-collapse.
