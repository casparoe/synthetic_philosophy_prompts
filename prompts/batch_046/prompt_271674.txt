Two friends in my cohort—Jordan and Riley, both poli-sci PhDs—got into it over beers about consequentialist cluelessness, specifically whether it kills act-utilitarianism or its just an agent-level headache. I mostly listened but want someone to actually call the moves. Here is how it went, more or less, please dont hold the sloppy paraphrase against me:

(1) Jordan said that if we genuinely cant know the long-run outcomes of our actions—think climate policy or AI alignment—then act-consequentialism cant guide anyone, and a normative theory that gives zero practical guidance is broken.

(2) Riley shot back that this confuses "what makes an action right" with "how I should decide." Physics doesnt become false just because I cant solve the n-body problem; normative theories state facts, they dont have to be decision procedures.

(3) Jordan said thats a false analogy. Consequentialism defines rightness in terms of actual outcomes, not expected ones or intended ones. So the normative criterion itself references things that are structurally inaccessible—not just hard to compute, but in principle outside our epistemic reach because of feedback loops and chaos in complex adaptive systems. Thats normative inaccessibility, not just agent cluelessness.

(4) Riley replied that Jordan just described actualist consequentialism, which is a strawman nobody defends seriously. Expected consequentialism defines rightness via expected value given available evidence, so the criterion is perfectly accessible.

(5) Jordan countered that expected value collapses for structural cluelessness—when youre dealing with unknown unknowns in adaptive systems, probabilities arent just hard to assign, theyre undefined. So expected consequentialism either pretends we have probabilities we dont, or it collapses into conservative rule-following. Either way it loses its distinctive normative bite.

(6) Riley said Jordan was now complaining about human cognitive limits in computing expectations, not about the theory itself. The theory still says "maximize expected utility"; our failure to do the math doesnt falsify it. Plus, deontologists face the exact same problem figuring out which duties apply in messy real-world cases, so cluelessness isnt a special problem for consequentialists.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Who is actually right about the core distinction between normative inaccessibility and epistemic limitation? And what are both of them missing—maybe about how structural cluelessness interacts with decision-theoretic framing, or about whether theres a principled way to separate agent-level paralysis from theory-level failure?
