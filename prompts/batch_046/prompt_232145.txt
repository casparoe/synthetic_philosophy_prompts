You are a professional philosopher of metaphysics with expertise in late-19th-century British idealism and contemporary relational ontology. Think step by step: first, briefly outline Bradley's regress argument from Appearance and Reality; then address the problem below.

Passage (paraphrased): Bradley asks us to suppose that A and B stand in a real relation R. For R to actually connect A and B, R must itself stand in some relation to A, and in some relation to B; call these further relations. But those new relations are themselves relations, so each requires still further relations to link them to their relata, generating an infinite regress. Bradley takes this to show that relations are not ultimately real—only appearance can contain them.

I am trying to understand this reasoning through a framing that is not the usual Russellian reply. On this alternative view, relational structure is never an extra item added on to independently existing relata; instead, it is intrinsic to unified states of affairs that have internal differentiation but no genuinely separate parts. There is no prior A and B waiting to be tied together, only one complex reality with a structured character.

My question is: does Bradley's regress depend essentially on treating A and B as prior substances that need an external connector? Or does his argument apply even if we begin with a single, internally structured whole? If the latter, then denying external relations might preserve Bradley's monistic conclusion—reality is one, just internally differentiated—without allowing plural wholes. But if the argument requires prior separate relata, then the intrinsic-structure view escapes the regress without necessarily collapsing into monism, since many such structured wholes might exist with no relations among them. Please help me see exactly where in the passage the additive assumption is load-bearing, and whether Bradley's text offers any resources against this escape route.
