I've been working through case studies in a seminar on institutional failure, and I've noticed a recurring configuration that I can't find a clean label for. Consider a research foundation created to fund high-risk, long-horizon science. Over thirty years, its grant committees do not get bribed or infiltrated by an outside interest; rather, the evaluation rubrics slowly accumulate criteria that are easier to defend in board reports—milestone density, co-authorship networks, citation velocity—until the original mandate is still inscribed in the charter but no longer shapes who actually receives funding. Outside observers consistently explain the shift as capture—"the industry lobby got to them," or "a managerial class seized control"—because a narrative of hidden influence is more cognitively available than a story about distributed metric drift. The same pattern appears in cultural institutions that begin selecting for audience metrics that eventually displace the aesthetic criteria they started with, and in advocacy organizations that gradually reward media visibility over policy impact. In each case, the failure looks like a defeat by an influence-seeking system, but the actual mechanism is a slow, endogenous reweighting of what counts as success.

Is there an established concept that captures exactly this pairing—the internal, gradual displacement of a hard-to-measure goal by easier proxies, combined with the persistent external misreading of that displacement as intentional capture or corruption? I know about mission drift and Goodhart's law, and about institutional or regulatory capture, but none of these seem to cover the misdiagnosis aspect: the way outsiders reflexively reach for a story of hidden agents rather than recognizing a drift in evaluation standards. If there is no established term for the combined phenomenon, can you coin one and define its boundaries? I want to be able to distinguish it sharply from (1) explicit capture by a self-interested group, (2) Merton-style bureaucratic ritualism in which rules are followed for their own sake, and (3) simple creeping normalcy in which standards slowly relax without anyone noticing. What exactly are the edges of the thing—when does metric drift become this particular failure mode, and when is it merely rational adaptation to a changed environment?
