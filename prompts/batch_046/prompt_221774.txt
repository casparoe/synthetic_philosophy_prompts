As a retired natural scientist who has only recently turned to philosophy in earnest, I have become absorbed by the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda—the thesis that reality is irreducibly manifold and that any single judgment captures only one partial aspect, or naya, of a more complex whole. The parallel with scientific practice strikes me forcibly: theoretical frameworks in physics and biology often appear incommensurable yet empirically adequate, as if each illuminates a different face of nature. Rather than treating anekāntavāda as a museum piece, I want to know where it should lead philosophically.

Please identify the three or four most consequential open questions, unresolved internal tensions, or productive lines of work that arise if one takes anekāntavāda seriously as a live position today. These might include: how its logical apparatus of syādvāda (conditional predication) avoids contradiction without collapsing into relativism; whether it offers a distinctive answer to contemporary problems of peer disagreement and scientific underdetermination; what metaphysical account of partial truth can be given that is neither deflationary nor mysterian; and whether its epistemic humility carries concrete ethical or political implications for pluralistic societies.

Rank these issues in order of which deserve sustained philosophical effort over the coming decade, and defend the ranking with explicit criteria—whether tractability for a solo researcher, foundational importance for other debates, current neglect, or practical urgency. I also want to know which one or two lines of inquiry are likely overrated or risk being dead ends, and why. I am framing this to guide my own study rather than to generate a reading list, so deliver a defended agenda, not a bibliography or survey.
