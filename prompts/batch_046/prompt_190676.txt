I serve on the selection committee for a foundation that awards annual 'lifetime service' honors to individuals who have sustained extreme personal sacrifice for public benefit—giving away nearly all income, enduring high-risk conditions, or forgoing family life for decades of activism or care work. Critics charge us with glorifying self-destruction; defenders say we ignore genuine heroism. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical problem of whether maximal moral goodness is desirable—the concern that extreme virtue can crowd out non-moral goods such as personal relationships, aesthetic experience, or play—to classify nominees into four categories: (A) admirable moral saints whose lives should be honored without being promoted as universal models; (B) sustainable high virtue; (C) pathological self-neglect that should not be rewarded; and (D) structurally coerced sacrifice reflecting injustice rather than virtue. The procedure must be a sequence of no more than eight ordered tests I can apply with a dossier and a twenty-minute committee discussion, specifying for each test what counts as sufficient evidence, where it yields a definite classification, and where it must defer to value judgment because the facts are insufficient; it must also explicitly state what kinds of case it cannot settle. It should distinguish sacrificing chosen for moral reasons from having non-moral goods crowded out, and handle ambiguous flourishing—cases where the nominee reports contentment despite objective deprivation. Apply it to three nominees: a nurse who worked unpaid in conflict zones for thirty years, never married, and reports no regret; a wealthy engineer who donated ninety percent of income but maintained close family ties, hobbies, and physical health; and a community organizer whose extreme poverty and isolation resulted largely from discriminatory policies blocking stable employment rather than from voluntary choice. Include one case where the procedure classifies the nominee as (A) despite the committee's likely intuition that they are a perfect model, and explain why the framework resists promotion. Then say what would change your mind about the framework you build—identify the specific argument or finding that would lead you to reclassify one case differently—and flag the step in your reasoning most likely to fail. Present the procedure as a numbered list embedded within continuous prose; keep the entire response to no more than 550 words, with each case application in no more than three sentences.
