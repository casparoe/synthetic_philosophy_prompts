I have noticed a recurring pattern in debates about distributive justice that I do not have a word for. Two people suffer comparable economic losses after making deliberate, foreseeable choices not to adapt to a changing market. One declines to learn a widely available skill; observers treat the loss as self-inflicted and deny compensation. The other declines to relocate because of family obligations; observers treat the identical risk structure as misfortune and endorse support. Both exercised similar control, possessed similar information, and accepted similar risks. The divergence seems to track not any measurable difference in agency, but whether the agent's reason matches a culturally available narrative about legitimate constraint. I see this repeatedly: outcomes are classified as self-chosen or as bad luck according to the social acceptability of the underlying motive, rather than according to the formal properties of the choice situation. Is there an established term for this specific classification slippage? If there is, what are the nearest related concepts, and exactly where does this phenomenon diverge from them? If there is none, can you propose a precise name and definition, specify its boundary conditions, and explain how it relates to — but is not reducible to — the standard agency-based distinctions in distributive justice?
