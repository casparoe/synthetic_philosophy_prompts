I'm a doctoral candidate in normative ethics, and I'm currently drafting a chapter on the limits of moral excellence. In recent discussions with my supervisor, I was pressed to clarify whether the familiar debate about moral saints — the concern that maximally good people might lack personal projects, humour, or partial attachments — can be resolved simply by asserting that moral values take lexical priority over all others.

For this essay, I want you to examine the following controversy and reach a defended conclusion: is maximal moral goodness genuinely desirable, or does it necessarily involve what I call 'axiological crowding-out'? I want you specifically to scrutinise the 'moral monotonicity thesis' — the often-unstated assumption that if a trait or action is morally good, then increasing its intensity, scope, or dominance in an agent's character is always morally better. From this, consider whether there exists a defensible 'saturation point' beyond which further moral optimisation produces 'axiological monochromacy': a practical identity so thoroughly structured by moral reasoning that non-moral values (such as aesthetic appreciation, playful spontaneity, or intimate partiality) are not merely subordinated but conceptually evacuated. Please weigh the principal arguments on both sides. On one hand, consider the view that the apparent unattractiveness of moral saints is merely an aesthetic prejudice that morality should override; on the other, consider the view that a life lacking plural values is defective in ways that moral purity cannot redeem. Do not end non-committally: defend a specific verdict, such as that maximal moral goodness is undesirable past a certain threshold, or that the notion of a saturation point is incoherent because moral reasons are genuinely exhaustive. Write in formal academic British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
