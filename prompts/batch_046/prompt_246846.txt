I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving active research, have begun to study philosophy of rational choice and decision theory. I now face a concrete situation that seems to instantiate one of the puzzles I have encountered in that literature, and I should like an analysis that addresses its structure rather than offering mere practical counsel.

Several months ago, an endowment foundation that supports emeritus scholars invited me to participate in a pilot program. As part of the enrollment process, I completed an extensive behavioral assessment—structured interviews, reaction-time tasks, and a review of my historical patterns in funding applications. The foundation fed these data into a predictive model that, according to their own validation reports, correctly anticipates a participant's ultimate selection in this program in approximately ninety-eight percent of cases. Crucially, the model generated a single forecast about my choice before I was ever told the program's mechanics, and on the basis of that forecast the foundation either deposited four hundred thousand dollars into a sealed trust assigned to me, or left that trust empty. The contents are now fixed and causally independent of anything I do today.

The present choice is this. I may claim only the sealed trust. Alternatively, I may claim both the trust and a separate, fully transparent honorarium of eight thousand dollars in unrestricted funds, which is already available in an open account and will remain available regardless of my decision. I understand that taking both cannot retroactively alter the foundation's past prediction or the trust's current balance. I also understand that among previous participants, those who restricted themselves to the trust alone received the large deposit in nearly every instance, while those who took both offers almost invariably found the trust empty.

My scientific instinct is to treat the past prediction as settled and therefore to maximize my present holdings by accepting both offers. Yet the correlation is so robust that ignoring it feels less like clear-headed rationality and more like a refusal to acknowledge a genuine pattern. Furthermore, I have learned that the model's forecast was specifically of my counterfactual choice—the decision I would have made had I never been informed of the mechanism—whereas I am now, of course, fully informed.

How ought I to evaluate this? Is there a principled distinction between the rationality of the act I perform now and the rationality of the underlying disposition or reasoning process that the predictive model detected? And if so, does it justify forgoing the eight thousand dollars?
