I'm a student in an advanced seminar on Victorian moral philosophy and the philosophy of science, and I'm preparing a conceptual workshop exercise on interpretive disagreement. I want you to draft the joint statement that two opposing interpretive camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Henry Sidgwick's evaluation of psychical research—specifically his assessment of telepathic transmission and post-mortem survival evidence—without requesting any bibliography, citation list, or further reading, and without placing any purported direct quotations in quotation marks.

Name the two camps precisely: Camp A, the Evidential Uniformity camp, holds that Sidgwick applied the same canons of proof to psychical reports that he applied to ordinary empirical testimony, demanding only that the cumulative weight of well-attested spontaneous cases and controlled experiments overcome a reasonable prior skepticism, and that he never granted these phenomena an exceptional epistemic status. Camp B, the Existential Asymmetry camp, holds that Sidgwick implicitly relaxed his usual evidential thresholds for psychical claims because they bore on the possibility of personal survival, a question he treated as having unique existential urgency; consequently, he accepted lower levels of causal specificity and reproducibility than he required in physical science.

The joint statement should be formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed-claims section should cover: Sidgwick's biographical involvement with psychical investigation and its institutional context; his general methodological commitments regarding testimony and cumulative evidence; and the conceptual distinction—central to both camps—between phenomenological fidelity (the accuracy of a subject's report of an internal experience) and causal specificity (the isolation of a paranormal mechanism from known physical causes). The disagreements table should have two columns, each row phrased so that Camp A accepts the left formulation as capturing its own view and Camp B accepts the right formulation as capturing its own view; the disagreements must be substantive, not verbal.

Then identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving any one in either direction would move at least one camp closer to the other. For each crux, state the specific argument, comparative case, or thought-experiment that both camps agree is relevant—for instance, Sidgwick's alleged comparison between telepathic transmission and wireless telegraphy, or his evaluation of reports in which a percipient's mental state allegedly matched a distant agent's without sensory mediation. For each such case or argument, record each camp's explicit prediction: what feature or outcome of the case would confirm their reading of Sidgwick's actual standards.

Finally, include a brief concluding paragraph identifying which of the three cruxes both camps agree is the deepest—meaning that the resolution of the other two depends upon it—and explaining precisely why that dependency holds.

To prevent this from collapsing into a generic debate about belief in the paranormal, inject and deploy throughout the document the following niche conceptual distinction, which should be treated as philosophically productive even if it is not standard in existing secondary literature: the distinction between evidential isolation (the structural inability of psychical reports to be cross-verified by independent observers with shared sensory access) and evidential density (the accumulation of reports with high internal coherence but no external anchoring). Both camps should address whether Sidgwick recognized this distinction, and if so, whether he treated evidential isolation as a methodological problem to be overcome or as an intrinsic feature of the subject matter that justified modified standards of proof.

Write in formal academic prose appropriate to a philosophy-of-science workshop. Do not depend on specific titles, publication dates, or page numbers.
