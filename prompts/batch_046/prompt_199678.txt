There's a weird inconsistency in how democracy watchdogs and scholars apply the backsliding label. Take Hungary and Poland: their governments used perfectly legal parliamentary procedures to pack constitutional courts and restructure media oversight, and that gets called democratic backslidding. But Turkey under Erdogan used emergency decrees and constitutional referendums to do very similar things, and analysts tend to file Turkey under authoritarian consolidation rather than backslidding. Meanwhile in the US, when state legislatures pass aggressive voter-ID rules or partisan gerrymanders, some scholars call it backslidding and others say it's just constitutional hardball within the rules. So the practice seems to be: if you're already in the liberal-democracy club and you erode checks through formal legal channels, that's backslidding; if you never fully made it in, or if you break rules openly, it's something else.

What philosophical commitments does that classification practice presuppose? Does it assume democracy is essentially a specific institutional package—independent courts, free press, competitive elections—such that degrading the package counts as degrading democracy even when every step is legally valid? Or does it smuggle in a normative theory about legitimate authority that treats procedural legality as democracy-preserving only for certain kinds of states? Reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy and political legitimicy here, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it just fits with, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined—could a purely procedural view of democracy license the same classifications, or a purely substantive one about popular control? And if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly procedural or participatory one, what would have to change in how we classify Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and those US state practices?
