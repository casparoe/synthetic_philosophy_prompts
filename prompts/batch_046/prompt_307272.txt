Please write the opening remarks for a one-day curriculum-redesign workshop, to be delivered to a mixed audience of secondary-school teachers, curriculum officers, and parent-governors. The school has recently replaced subject-based examinations with a portfolio system that certifies transferable competencies — critical thinking, collaboration, digital literacy — and the workshop aims to help staff design assessments that genuinely cultivate these capacities rather than merely recording them.

Structure the remarks around Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education: specifically, his account of learning as a rhythm moving through romance, precision, and generalisation, and his warning that ideas become inert when they are received without being actively possessed or used. Do not use Whitehead as ornament; let his framework do real structural work, shaping both what the speech affirms and what it rules out. In particular, develop this niche conceptual claim: a competency that is certified without having passed through romantic discovery and disciplined precision becomes a new kind of inert idea — a skill listed on a transcript that the holder has never genuinely inhabited and cannot deploy in an unfamiliar context. Because of this, the remarks must avoid both nostalgic defence of traditional examinations and uncritical celebration of portfolio assessment; instead, they should present the workshop's task as recovering Whitehead's rhythm inside a system that measures outcomes.

The audience is split. The teachers and officers will recognise the Whiteheadian references and will notice misrepresentations — for example, conflating inertness with simple forgetfulness, or treating generalisation as mere repetition — while the parents and governors have no philosophical background and must be able to follow the argument without being patronised. Write for approximately eight minutes, in a reflective, measured tone rather than a rallying one. Open with a concrete classroom scenario that illustrates an inert competency: a student who can produce the correct portfolio evidence for collaborative problem-solving yet cannot adapt that behaviour to a group task she has not been trained for. Then move through the three stages to show what the workshop is protecting, and close by stating explicitly what the framework forbids: for instance, that no competency should be marked as achieved if it has only been assessed, not inhabited.

Do not include a bibliography, suggested reading list, or citations. Do not quote from Whitehead's texts; paraphrase his concepts in your own words.
