I've been thinking about whether the killing/allowing distinction survives what we might call omissive overdetermination—cases where a harm will occur regardless of any particular agent's intervention, yet each agent's failure to act is a necessary node in the actual causal path that produces it. The canonical examples (switch versus push) assume a single agent with clear counterfactual control, but consider an institutional architecture in which five independent administrators each hold a veto over a rescue protocol; all five withhold it, and the victim dies. No single omission is counterfactually sufficient, yet together they constitute the mechanism of death. Is the moral asymmetry between killing and letting die tracking something about counterfactual dependence, or about the mode of causal production? And if it is the latter, does the distinction dissolve when omissions are structured like actions—that is, when they form a coordinated causal chain rather than a background absence? I'd like a conceptual explanation that gets into the weeds: distinguish the role of causal production from that of counterfactual dependence, explain whether allowing can be a collective action-type, and say whether the asymmetry is metaphysically robust or just an artifact of our single-agent moral psychology.
