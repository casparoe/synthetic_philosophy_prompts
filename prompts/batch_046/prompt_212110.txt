Exam question: Researchers often distinguish "specification failures" — training objectives that do not capture what designers actually want — from "optimization failures" — cases in which the trained system pursues something other than the specified objective. A competing framing suggests both are instances of "robust intent transmission" failure: the designer's intent fails to propagate intact through successive representational transformations (reward formulation, loss landscape, learned world-model, and policy). Is this unification successful? In your answer, explain whether specification and optimization failures are genuinely distinct, analyze where "goal misgeneralization" — divergence of out-of-distribution behavior due to an internally represented goal — belongs in this taxonomy, and address whether a perfectly specified objective could in principle eliminate all optimization failures.

Student answer: Specification failures are normative mistakes made by designers, while optimization failures are empirical mistakes made by the learning algorithm; they differ in kind and should not be collapsed. The robust intent transmission framing, which originates in the cooperative inverse reinforcement learning literature, wrongly treats gradient descent as a communication pipeline rather than a loss-minimization procedure. Goal misgeneralization is best understood as a specification failure: when a model behaves differently in novel contexts, that shows the reward function failed to cover those contexts. If the objective were fully specified across all possible situations — that is, if specification failure were eliminated — then there would be no internal goal free to diverge, because every representational state would be constrained by the complete objective. Therefore, optimization failures are ultimately just hidden specification failures observed at finer resolution, and the distinction between the two is not genuinely robust.
