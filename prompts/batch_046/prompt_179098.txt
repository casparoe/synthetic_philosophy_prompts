I'm trying to fix my own view on indirect normativity—specifically the kind that binds us not through direct evaluation of an act's merits but through its relation to norms, rules, or institutions that themselves claim authority from some form of idealized deliberation. To see whether my judgments form a genuine pattern or just a rationalization, I want to report my verdicts on several concrete cases and ask what principle, if any, unifies them, which verdict I should drop if they conflict, and what the resulting account predicts for a case I still haven't settled.

Here are my verdicts.

First, the algorithm-override case. A hiring committee uses a selection protocol designed five years ago by a panel of fully informed, unbiased, rational deliberators. Unforeseen economic shifts mean the protocol now systematically excludes candidates with exactly the skills the organization needs. A hiring manager sees that one particular applicant is ideal, that overriding the protocol is permitted and undetectable, and that the original panel, if reconvened today with the same information, would redesign the protocol immediately. Verdict: the manager should override. The indirect authority of the protocol is conditional on its outputs remaining aligned with what the ideal deliberators would now endorse; when that alignment breaks because of changed external conditions, direct evaluation resumes control.

Second, the promise-despite-value case. I promise a neighbor I'll water his plants while he is away, but doing so requires missing a rare professional opportunity. Deliberating now in an idealized manner—fully informed, emotionally stable, corrected for present bias—I judge that breaking the promise and taking the opportunity produces more overall value. Yet I also hold that idealized deliberators, reasoning about the general structure of social cooperation, would endorse a strict rule of fidelity to promises made in personal relationships, precisely because case-by-case optimization undermines the trust that makes such relationships possible. Verdict: I should keep the promise. Here the indirect norm binds me even though direct ideal evaluation of this particular case recommends breaking it.

Third, the bureaucratic-obedience case. A civil servant must apply a regulation enacted after extensive idealized public deliberation—impartial, fully informed, constrained by rights-based side conditions. Applying the regulation to this specific applicant causes severe, unnecessary hardship that the convention could not have foreseen. The servant's own idealized judgment says waive the rule for this applicant. But the convention's framework included an explicit meta-rule: once enacted, regulations apply uniformly unless they violate fundamental rights, in order to prevent arbitrary discretionary power. Verdict: she should apply the regulation. The indirect norm includes its own conditions of application, which block direct override.

Fourth, the sedimented-norm case. A university honor code was adopted by an idealized student assembly and prohibits plagiarism. A student uses a generative system to produce text that is technically original but conceptually derivative in ways the assembly never contemplated. I think that if my idealized self, understanding both the technology and the purpose of the code, evaluates this particular act, I should judge it a violation of intellectual responsibility and therefore punishable. At the same time, I recognize that the idealized assembly, had they anticipated this technology, might well have drafted a narrower rule. Verdict: the student should still be punished under the existing code. The authority of the sedimented norm takes precedence over speculative reconstruction of what the ideal deliberators would have said had they possessed different information.

Fifth, the meta-deliberation bootstrap. A moral philosopher attempts to construct her normative theory by asking only what norms her fully rational, fully informed, affectively corrected self would endorse. She notices that her identification of which emotional responses are "biases" to be corrected depends on prior normative commitments—commitments she arrived at through the same idealized procedure. She considers adopting a bootstrap principle: she will treat as authoritative only those norms that survive correction by a second-order idealization applied to the first-order results. Verdict: she should adopt the bootstrap, but only for institutional-level norms, not for direct personal obligations; otherwise the procedure never terminates and yields no action-guidance.

These five judgments sit in tension. In the first case I allow direct evaluation to override an indirect norm when circumstances change; in the second and third I block direct override despite conflicting evaluation; in the fourth I privilege the historical sediment of the norm over reconstructed ideal judgment; and in the fifth I apply a layered idealization selectively, depending on whether the norm governs institutions or personal conduct.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing approximation-authority from constitutive-authority from sedimented-authority—that saves all five without ad hoc complexity? Or must I drop one? If a unified account exists, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for the unsettled case I describe below. If no unified account works, present the two most plausible competing principles, note which verdict each forces me to abandon, and explain what consideration should decide between them.

The unsettled case: a physician relies on a clinical decision-support system calibrated by idealized medical experts. She knows that if she inputs the patient's data honestly, a known but unpatched bias in the algorithm will trigger a harmful recommendation. If she subtly adjusts the input parameters—without falsifying any clinical fact—to steer the system toward the correct recommendation, is she violating the indirect norm of institutional reliance on expert-calibrated tools, or is she honoring the deeper purpose of that norm? I have not settled my verdict.
