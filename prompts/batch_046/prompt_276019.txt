Exam question: Max Weber's thesis regarding the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism is often read as an attempt to establish culture as an autonomous causal factor in historical explanation. Explain Weber's notion of elective affinity between religious asceticism and capitalist economic conduct, and assess whether this thesis commits him to treating values as causes in the same explanatory sense as material conditions. In your response, distinguish Weber's categories of value-rational and instrumentally rational action, and discuss whether his interpretive method permits cultural explanations to operate independently of economic ones.

Student answer:
Weber's concept of elective affinity describes a structural compatibility and mutual reinforcement between the ascetic Protestant ethic and modern capitalist conduct, rather than a direct causal mechanism. The Protestant ethos of disciplined labor, rational calculation, and reinvestment of surplus fostered a psychological orientation well suited to capitalist enterprise, yet Weber denies that religion mechanically produced capitalism or that capitalism was historically inevitable. This position allows him to reject both Marxist economic determinism and the idealist claim that religious ideas alone shaped economic development.

Weber distinguishes value-rational action, in which agents pursue ends they regard as ethically or religiously obligatory irrespective of probable success, from instrumentally rational action, in which agents calculate the most efficient means to achieve predetermined ends. The Protestant ethic initially operated through value-rational motivation: labor was conceived as a divine calling, and material success could function as a possible sign of election. Over time, however, the religious framework secularized, and capitalist behavior came to be governed by instrumentally rational profit maximization alone. Thus the value-rational origin yields to an instrumentally rational system that no longer requires religious justification.

Weber's sociology depends on Verstehen, which requires explanations to be adequate both in meaning and in causation. Meaning-adequacy involves reconstructing the subjective intentions that guide action, while causal-adequacy involves establishing an objective probability that given conditions produce the observed outcome. Because both conditions ultimately depend on reconstructing individual subjective states, Weber's method entails that macro-cultural explanations must reduce to aggregate patterns of individual value-orientation. Consequently, culture cannot serve as an independent causal layer; it shapes historical outcomes only by informing the value-rational commitments of individual agents. Material conditions—such as property relations and market structures—exert structural causal influence that does not require interpretive mediation. For this reason, Weber does not treat values and material conditions as symmetrical explanatory factors: values operate through interpreted individual motivation, whereas material conditions constrain behavior structurally and directly.
