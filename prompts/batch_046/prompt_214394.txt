As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy of mathematics, I find myself confronted by a problem that lacks any direct analogue in empirical science: the possibility that our foundational axiomatic systems—Peano Arithmetic, ZFC, or comparable formal structures—are not merely incomplete in the manner Gödel established, but actually inconsistent, with the contradiction concealed behind proofs of intractable or even physically unrealizable length. Before I examine the competing philosophical accounts of this possibility, I wish to establish a clear evaluative framework. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of axiomatic consistency, and specifically of its potential failure, ought to satisfy. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure—whether arising from the distinction between syntactic and semantic consistency, from the self-referential limits highlighted by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, from the existence of non-standard models, or from the analogy to systematically undetectable errors in physical measurement. Be explicit about which pairs of requirements appear jointly unsatisfiable: in particular, whether the demand that an inconsistency be discoverable in principle can be reconciled with the allowance that it might remain permanently undiscoverable in practice due to computational or physical bounds on proof verification. Then introduce one further, less commonly discussed criterion: the theory should distinguish global axiomatic collapse from a localized or threshold-bound form of failure, in which contradiction emerges only within derivations or structures that exceed a specific complexity threshold, analogous to the way certain physical instabilities appear only beyond critical energy scales. Finally, briefly indicate how three broad orientations fare against your list: a strict formalist view that treats consistency as an internal syntactic property; a platonist view that treats it as a feature of an independent abstract reality; and a naturalized or quasi-empirical view that treats axiomatic stability as analogous to the robustness of a well-confirmed physical law. I am working without external reference access, so please keep the analysis entirely self-contained—no bibliography, no reading list, and no requests for citations or named secondary literature.
