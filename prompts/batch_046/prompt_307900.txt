I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and clinical phenomenology, and I am working with narrative therapists who must repeatedly evaluate short patient autobiographical excerpts (200–400 words) for whether the speaker's use of "I" exhibits a Hofstadterian strange loop: a self-referential symbol anchored in a hierarchical representation of mental and bodily states that loops back onto its substrate, rather than collapsing into indexicality, dissociative fragmentation, or tautology. Please design a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit tests—that a practitioner without training in logic or cognitive science can apply. The procedure must distinguish four outcomes: (A) intact strange loop; (B) downward collapse, where "I" reduces to immediate bodily or emotional states with no upward abstraction; (C) upward drift, where "I" becomes an abstract placeholder disconnected from substrate; and (D) pseudo-loop circularity, where self-reference lacks hierarchical depth (e.g., mere repetition like "I think I think"). For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the precise point where the procedure halts and defers to clinical judgment because the issue is interpretive or valuative rather than structural.

Provide three brief invented calibration fragments—each a single first-person sentence—illustrating outcomes A, B, and D. Then apply the full procedure to three worked cases: (1) a stable integrated-identity narrative; (2) an acute depersonalization report in which the speaker describes themselves as "the observer in the machine"; and (3) a recovery narrative in which the speaker claims to have rebuilt an "I" that "watches itself being rebuilt," where clinical intuition might favor A but the structural tests point toward D or C. For case (3), the procedure should deliver a specific result that the therapist is likely to resist, and you should flag exactly which step forces that result and why human judgment must take over.

Finally, state the procedure's explicit limits: which kinds of self-narrative structures lie outside its scope, what risks of misclassification remain even when the steps are followed correctly, and what the procedure cannot do that only a clinician can.
