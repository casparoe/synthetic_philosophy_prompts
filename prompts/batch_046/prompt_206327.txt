I'm drafting a chapter on comparative moral philosophy and have noticed a recurring structural move in cross-tradition evaluation that I can't place precisely within the existing literature. I see it operating in at least two concrete patterns, and I want to know whether the configuration already has a sharp name or whether I am describing an unmarked variant of something familiar.

The first pattern works like this: a scholar takes a concept that is densely articulated within one tradition—say, a specific account of autonomy that ties it to reflective endorsement and the absence of coercive interference—and treats that account as the neutral analytical grid for comparing all traditions. When examining a tradition that organizes moral life around relational harmony or role-fulfillment rather than individual reflective choice, the scholar frames the inquiry as "How do they realize autonomy?" If the answer turns out to be "they do not, or only partially," the tradition is registered as deficient or underdeveloped rather than as operating with a different organizing concept altogether. What strikes me is that the universal status of the grid is rarely defended as an explicit substantive thesis; instead, it is embedded in the framing question itself, so that disputing whether autonomy is the right comparative axis looks like disputing the empirical facts about the other tradition rather than disputing the choice of framework.

The second pattern is the defensive counterpart. When a critic points out that the evaluation standard originates in one particular tradition, the defender often responds not by arguing for that standard's universality, but by treating it as the unmarked default—"this is just what moral philosophy analyzes," or "these are the conditions for agency as such." The burden shifts asymmetrically: the particular tradition's conceptual resources are universalized as "the concept," while other traditions must either map onto them or be explained away as local approximations or deviations. I have seen this structure recur with notions like rationality, personhood, and even suffering, but the mechanism seems consistent regardless of the domain.

This is not quite conceptual imperialism in the usual sense, since that term tends to imply an active, often politically charged imposition of one framework onto another. Here the asymmetry is more subtle: it happens through the selection of the comparative axis and the grammar of evaluation, not through explicit substitution or domination. Nor is it straightforward epistemic injustice of the testimonial or hermeneutical kind, because the harm is not primarily about excluding a speaker or denying them interpretive resources; it is about the asymmetrical treatment of conceptual resources—one tradition's dense concept is treated as the universal form, others as instances or failures to achieve it.

So my questions are these. Does this specific configuration—where a particular tradition's richly specified concept is embedded as the neutral comparative grid, generating asymmetrical evaluation without any explicit universality claim—have an established name in comparative philosophy, philosophy of culture, or epistemology? If it does, which concept is the closest fit, and where exactly does this version diverge from it? If it does not have a precise name, I would like a coined term with a sharp definition, together with boundary cases that distinguish it from ordinary theoretical bias, from genuine cross-cultural comparison using legitimately universal categories, and from the kind of framework imposition usually discussed under conceptual imperialism. I am not looking for a bare label; I want to be able to apply the distinction precisely in my analysis.
