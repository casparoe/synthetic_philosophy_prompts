Please explain the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism, but concentrate on a variant that arises in pragmatically embedded conditionals used for practical negotiation rather than logical inference. Specifically, consider cases in which a speaker says something like: if reducing waste were morally required, then I ought to change my diet, but reducing waste is not required, so I am free to keep my current habits. On the expressivist view, the unembedded clause “reducing waste is morally required” expresses a non-cognitive attitude, yet inside the antecedent of the conditional it appears not to express that attitude with its usual motivational force, while still contributing to the meaning of the whole sentence in a way that licenses the speaker’s subsequent practical conclusion. Explain precisely why this creates a tension for compositional semantics: if the embedded occurrence retains full expressive force, the speaker seems to be simultaneously adopting and withholding the same normative stance; if it retains none, then the conditional collapses into a descriptive claim about the speaker’s psychology, and the practical reasoning that follows loses its normative structure. Then clarify whether this negotiation-style embedding strengthens the original Frege-Geach objection or whether it can be dissolved by distinguishing logical embedding from pragmatic, action-guiding embedding—without invoking named theories, bibliographies, or specific historical texts. I want the explanation to stay at the level of the conceptual mechanics: what the attitude must be doing inside the conditional, what it must be doing outside, and why maintaining both roles without contradiction is difficult.
