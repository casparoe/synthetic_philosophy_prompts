Design a final-exam essay question (1,200-word limit; 35 marks; 3 hours; open-notes, no collaboration) for an upper-level philosophy of mind and epistemology seminar on conceptual slippage within a microdomain. The case should be constructed around “epistemic copycatting”: the concept of justified belief, originally calibrated for individual agents with first-person access to evidential states, is copied into the microdomain of distributed predictive-processing architectures, in which nodes update weights without any unified subject of experience. Present students with three successive uses of the concept in this new domain—(i) metaphorical (“the network is justified in predicting X”), (ii) functional-analogical (“the network’s weight update plays the same causal role as justification”), and (iii) literal-attributive (“the network holds a justified belief that X”). Ask students to: (a) pinpoint exactly where the copycat concept imports conditions (e.g., phenomenal accessibility, doxastic voluntarism, or introspective availability) that are absent in the target domain; (b) determine whether the fluidity of “justification” makes the slide from (ii) to (iii) conceptually inevitable or whether a principled boundary can be drawn between legitimate analogical extension and category error; (c) propose one explicit, defensible criterion for legitimate cross-domain application that avoids both excessive rigidity (denying all analogies) and excessive permissiveness (allowing full copycatting); and (d) apply that criterion to adjudicate whether case (ii) survives scrutiny while case (iii) collapses.

Provide a model answer that executes all four moves with philosophical precision rather than by labeling general theories, and provide a marking scheme allocating marks as follows: 8 marks for identifying the imported conditions and the precise point of slippage; 8 marks for analyzing fluidity versus illegitimate copycatting; 9 marks for proposing and defending the criterion; 6 marks for applying it to (ii) versus (iii); and 4 marks for clarity and argumentative coherence. In the scheme, list at least three specific mistakes that should cost marks—e.g., conflating metaphor with literal attribution without analyzing the intermediate functional analog; proposing a criterion that covertly relies on an undefined notion of “subjecthood”; or answering by summarizing generic accounts of family resemblance or conceptual ambiguity without engaging the predictive-processing case. The question should be answerable without bibliographies or named citations, but a top answer must distinguish genuine conceptual analysis from mere taxonomy of “fluid concepts.”
