Act as a professional philosopher of history with expertise in adversarial collaboration methods. Your task is to draft the joint statement that two opposing explanatory camps—hereafter the situational-culture camp (in the tradition of Browning’s analysis of Reserve Police Battalion 101, emphasising peer dynamics, career incentives, authority structures, and opportunity) and the intentionalist-character camp (in the tradition of Goldhagen’s thesis of deeply rooted eliminationist antisemitism, emphasising ideological saturation, willing participation, and cultural character)—could both sign in good faith after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on why ordinary perpetrators carried out mass killing.

Before drafting, briefly brainstorm: identify the historical claims about Battalion 101 and comparable units that both sides must accept regardless of interpretation; then isolate the precise explanatory disagreements, phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position; then distinguish any merely verbal disagreements from substantive ones.

Structure your output exactly as follows:

1. Agreed claims (numbered list). Include at least eight claims both camps can endorse about the behavioural record, the institutional context, the absence of severe punishment for refusal, the social composition of the perpetrators, and the variability of emotional and ideological expression observed in post-war testimony and archival material. Do not include bibliographic references or citation requests; rely on the shared historical record.

2. Residual disagreements (table format). For each disagreement, provide: (a) a neutral label; (b) the situational-culture formulation, worded so that a proponent of that view would accept it as accurate to their position; (c) the intentionalist-character formulation, worded analogously; (d) the associated crux. Ensure that the formulations are not straw men and that they genuinely conflict rather than talking past each other.

3. Cruxes and predictive tests. Identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify: (i) the concrete archival case, behavioural pattern, or comparative ecological prediction both sides agree is relevant (for example, the killing-rate correlation with prior party membership, the emotional content of private letters versus public behaviour, or cross-national genocide-participation patterns); (ii) the prediction the situational-culture camp makes about that case; (iii) the prediction the intentionalist-character camp makes; (iv) what outcome, if observed, would genuinely weaken one side.

4. Verbal versus substantive division. Conclude with a short paragraph stating whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal (about the meaning of “willing,” “ordinary,” or “cause”), and if so, which portion and why the remainder is not.

Write in formal British English (e.g., behaviour, centre, programme). Do not ask for reading lists, citations, or bibliographies. Do not quote any specific passage in quotation marks unless you have fetched the exact text; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure the prompt is detailed enough that a model without web access can engage substantively with the philosophical and historical structure, but inject sufficient analytical specificity—such as the taxonomy of perpetrator motivations (careerism, peer conformity, sadistic opportunism, ideological conviction) and the distinction between opportunity structures and normative saturation—so that the response cannot be a generic pretraining regurgitation. End with an explicit instruction: “Draft the document now, maintaining neutral, precise wording throughout, and sign it implicitly as a joint product of both camps.”
