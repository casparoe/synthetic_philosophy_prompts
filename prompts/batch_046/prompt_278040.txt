I have been attempting to formalise a distinction that was sketched in an extended rationalist forum thread concerning virtue ethics and the situationist critique. The distinction is between suppression and substitution of a character trait. The claim is that situationist experiments only genuinely threaten virtue ethics when the situational pressure supplies an alternative motivational structure that substitutes for the trait, rather than merely suppressing its expression. I want to apply this framework to the verdicts of a particular interlocutor—call her M.—whose rulings on four canonical cases appear to track this line precisely.

M. judges that in the Milgram obedience studies, compassion is suppressed but not substituted: the authority structure inhibits expression without providing a competing moral motivation, so virtue ethics is unharmed. She holds that in the Darley-Batson Good Samaritan study, urgency substitutes for compassion, because the time pressure introduces an active practical reason that displaces the Samaritan impulse; here virtue ethics is genuinely undermined in that domain. Regarding standard bystander-effect conditions, she rules that courage is suppressed through diffusion of responsibility, with no substituting motivation present, so no deep challenge arises. Finally, in a hypothetical case where a subject is persuaded that intervention is futile, she argues that practical wisdom is not merely suppressed but substituted by calculated resignation; hence the virtue is compromised.

Consider now a new study. Participants are assigned to a competitive task and are subliminally primed with ambiguous social cues that they systematically interpret as hostile. They subsequently withhold cooperation, despite scoring highly on self-report measures of cooperativeness. The priming does not alter their self-concept, nor does it supply an opposing reason for competition; rather, it appears to operate through a perceptual filter that bypasses the cooperative disposition without replacing it.

Should M. classify this as suppression or substitution? Identify the closest analog among her four verdicts, and state the principle she would be adopting under either interpretation. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, specify what further experimental variation—isolating either the psychological mechanism or the domain of cognition—would distinguish the two candidate rules she might be running.
