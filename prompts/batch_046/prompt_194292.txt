As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have become preoccupied with the argument—associated with Chase and Sanborn—that the posit of qualia is incoherent: if phenomenal properties are defined as intrinsic, non-relational, and ineffable, then they can play no causal, explanatory, or epistemic role, and the concept collapses. I now serve on a university institutional review board that evaluates cognitive-science protocols in fifteen-minute slots, with no staff philosopher and a mandate to sign off or reject quickly. Please translate that incoherence argument into a reusable, step-ordered review procedure my board can apply to any proposal claiming to study “subjective experience,” “phenomenal consciousness,” or “what it is like” states. For each step, specify the exact question, the condition for passing, the stopping rule (approve, reject, or defer to a value judgment that the board lacks authority to resolve), and the specific misfire risk—where the test might reject legitimate functionalist work or approve crypto-qualia research dressed in operational language. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: (a) using introspective rating scales to measure pain-qualia in macaques; (b) mapping “the redness of red” via fMRI in the absence of any behavioral report task; and (c) studying meditation-induced “pure awareness” defined as lacking all intentional content and relational structure. Include one result that runs counter to naive expectation—either approving a study that sounds metaphysically dubious, or rejecting one that sounds empirically benign. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle and where a reviewer must still exercise irreducible judgment.
