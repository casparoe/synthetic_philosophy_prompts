I am preparing a graduate seminar that moves between aesthetics and social ontology, and I have been asking my students to work through W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art — in particular his claim that all art is propaganda, that the artist's central question is not whether to serve a cause but which cause to serve, and that Black American art must function as truthful propaganda for racial dignity and collective liberation rather than pursue an autonomous aesthetic ideal. To press the point, I want them to see whether this position can be systematically restated in the vocabulary of distributed cognition and collective intentionality — specifically the framework I have been developing in my own work, which treats representations as sustained not in individual minds but across social practices through what I call stabilization loops, the iterative institutional feedback that corrects shared representations over time, and normative anchoring, the process by which a practice fixes the conditions under which a representation counts as correct or authoritative.

Please translate Du Bois's core argument — that propaganda is inescapable, that beauty and truth are inseparable from social purpose, and that individual artistic genius must be subordinated to a collective moral duty of racial uplift — entirely into the terms of stabilization loops and normative anchoring, without importing the framework's descriptive neutrality into Du Bois's explicitly normative commitments. Then provide the residue report: identify which elements of his view map cleanly onto distributed-cognition vocabulary, which elements survive only with significant distortion, and which elements have no counterpart whatsoever — especially his insistence that the artist bears a direct, non-delegable moral responsibility to the race. Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue indicates a genuine limitation of distributed cognition for normative aesthetics, or whether it reveals that Du Bois's argument implicitly relies on a conception of individual moral agency that his own collectivist rhetoric actually obscures.
