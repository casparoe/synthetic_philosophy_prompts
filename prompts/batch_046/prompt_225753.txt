I'm prepping my undergrad seminar on philosophy of science this term and I keep running into the same puzzel with my grad students. I want to know if there's a principled way to decide when an object licenses an inference about beliefs or institutions, or if I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my gut calls, and I'd like you to help me do some reflective equilibrium on them.

First: we find identical standardized weights in dozens of merchant stalls across a region, all within about 2% tolerance. I definatly think that points to some institutional trade regulation — there's too much coordination for pure accident. Second: we find the same geometric pattern stamped on pottery across fifty domestic sites. I think that probably signals a shared symbolic or cosmological belief, not just aesthetics. Third: we find the exact same geometric pattern carved into tool handles across those same fifty sites. Here my gut flips — I think that's probably functional grip design, not a shared belief. But that feels weird, right? The repetition is structurally identical. Fourth: a single burial with an elaborate mask that doesn't match any other find in a hundred-mile radius. I don't think we can say anything about social hierarchy or religion from it — probably just one eccentric family or a unique status event. Fifth: a massive irrigation canal system with uniform engineering specs, but no palaces, temples, or writing anywhere near it. I feel pulled both ways. Part of me says coordinated labor on that scale requires an institutional authority; part of me says maybe it was just gradual communal maintenance with shared practical knowledge, no central "instutition" needed. Sixth: thousands of broken ceramic fragments in a pit, all from the same storage-vessel type. I think we can infer a storage economy existed, but I don't think we can infer whether it was state-run, kin-based, or market-based.

So what's the principle? Maybe it's about whether the pattern requires intentional coordination versus functional convergence? But then why do I allow belief-inference for the pottery patterns but deny it for the tool handles — both could be intentional? Or maybe context drives it: weights cluster in economic spaces, pottery in domestic/ritual spaces, so we read the setting? But then the canal system has no contextual "institutional" anchor, which is exactly why I'm torn.

Can you state a principle, or a small family of them, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? Be honest about which ones it has to sacrifice, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, finding identical musical instruments (flutes with the same tuning) in both burial and domestic contexts across a region. I'd also like you to flag the pair of verdicts that are hardest to reconcile, and whether you'd rather save both by complicating the principle or drop one for simplicity. Please lay out the clause that handles each case you can save, and don't worry about giving me a bibliography — I just want the reasoning.
