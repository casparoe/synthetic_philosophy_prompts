Design a 75-minute workshop activity for 16 participants (graduate students or advanced undergraduates in ethics and political philosophy) that makes them feel, through structured interaction rather than lecture, why labeling clientelism or ceremonial gift-giving as corruption can depend on parochial assumptions about institutional legitimacy. Define within your design a specific conceptual framing—call it threshold reciprocity: the claim that an exchange becomes corrupt not when personal benefit is present, but when it erodes an institution’s public-legibility function, and that the point of erosion varies by social context rather than being universal. Include: (1) four roles—municipal resource allocator, kin-network elder, external auditor, citizen-applicant—each with explicit, conflicting norms about obligation and transparency; (2) rules for two 15-minute rounds of resource distribution using a simple point system, plus a crisis round in which a hidden kin-obligation conflicts with formal procedure; (3) a precise timeline with minutes allocated for setup, rounds, and debrief; (4) a debrief script that maps specific moments onto the distinction between thin corruption (rule-breaking for private gain) and thick corruption (violation of context-specific legitimacy conditions), asking participants whether and when the threshold was crossed; (5) an honest note on which feature of normative pluralism—specifically, the deep historical embedding of reciprocity norms—the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally; (6) a prediction of the failure mode most likely in round two and how the facilitator should correct for it in real time; and (7) a brief closing statement identifying the empirical premise about institutional trust that your crisis-round mechanism relies on, and specifying what finding or counter-argument would cause you to replace that mechanism with a different one. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; keep the design runnable with only role cards and a score sheet.
