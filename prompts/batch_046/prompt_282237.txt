Second, a volunteer who rescues a stranger is later rescued in a crisis by an unknown person; she said yes, deserved. Third, a reckless driver harms a pedestrian but escapes injury himself, then months later his car is stolen; she said no, not deserved. Fourth, an honest worker earns a promotion through merit, then is fired for another employee’s error; she said no, not deserved. Fifth, a person accidentally breaks a neighbor’s window, apologizes, and pays for it; the next week a tree branch breaks her own window; she said no, not deserved.

Now consider this new case: an engineer publishes an open-source sorting algorithm intended only for nonprofit recycling logistics; years later a corporation adapts it—without her knowledge or involvement—to screen job applicants, embedding biases she never added, and she is eventually rejected by that very system, despite having intended no harm.

If my tutor wants to stay consistent with herself, what should she say about whether this rejection is deserved karma? Which of the five precedents is the closest analog, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? Please identify the competing implicit rules that could fit her track record—such as whether karma requires intentional wrongdoing, direct causal reversal, or thematic symmetry between act and outcome—state what each rule predicts here, give your best-guess verdict with your reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish these rules. Also, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study moral desert and karma, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
