In philosophical and foundational discussions of the Continuum Hypothesis and its independence from ZFC, I have repeatedly noticed an argumentative pattern for which I lack a term. A participant will maintain that, because CH is neither provable nor refutable within standard set theory, the question of whether 2^{ℵ₀} equals ℵ₁ (or any specific ℵₙ) has no determinate answer—independence is interpreted as showing that there is no objective fact to be discovered. However, the same participant typically continues to treat other propositions that are equally independent of ZFC—such as whether the Axiom of Choice fails in a well-founded model, or whether certain large-cardinal properties hold—as substantive open questions that might be settled by future axioms or by discerning the true structure of the set-theoretic universe. The asymmetry seems driven not by any formal feature distinguishing CH from these other cases, but by CH's long-standing cultural role as the paradigmatic undecidable problem. Is there an established concept or literature name for this selective anti-realist move—treating independence as dissolving objectivity for one prominent statement while preserving it for structurally analogous ones? If no standard term exists, I would like a precise characterization that distinguishes it from a consistent multiverse view, from broad mathematical anti-realism, and from the position that all independent statements are equally meaningless, together with an account of where its boundaries lie.
