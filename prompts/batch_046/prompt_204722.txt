A colleague who recently read an introductory historiography textbook summarized the trajectory from Ranke through Carr to Hayden White for me as follows. Ranke, he said, founded modern historical science by insisting that the historian’s sole duty is to recover the past exactly as it happened, using archival primary sources while eliminating all interpretation, moral judgment, and narrative shaping from the account. Carr then demonstrated that this was impossible, because every historian necessarily selects and arranges facts in light of present concerns, so the past never appears except as mediated by the historian’s own conceptual framework; on Carr’s view, therefore, there is no objective historical reality independent of the historian’s constructive activity. Hayden White pushed this further by arguing that historical narratives employ exactly the same plot structures—romance, tragedy, comedy, satire—as works of fiction, and because there is no non-narrative form in which the past could be presented, history and literature differ only in that historians claim evidentiary support for events they have already emplotted. From all of this, my colleague concludes that Ranke’s empirical project collapsed entirely, and that we should now treat all historical writing as subjective storytelling that happens to include footnotes.
