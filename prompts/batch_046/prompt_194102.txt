I'm preparing my dissertation chapter on mental ontology and want to verify that my commitments form a coherent package rather than a patchwork of incompatible intuitions. Here is where I have landed: (1) I am a strict eliminative materialist about propositional attitudes—beliefs and desires do not exist, and folk psychology is a failed theoretical framework that neuroscience will eventually replace with descriptions of neural dynamics and behavioral dispositions, no more ontologically serious than phlogiston or vital spirits. (2) Despite this, I maintain that intentional ascription is not merely instrumentally useful but rationally obligatory within moral and legal practice, because blame, praise, and accountability are constitutively normative: they establish social positions and entitlements that cannot be reduced to predictive behavioral descriptions. (3) I also hold that first-person introspection provides direct, authoritative access to mental life, though its objects are not propositional attitudes but continuous processes of neural self-monitoring that we systematically misinterpret through folk-psychological vocabulary. (4) Finally, I accept evolutionary explanations of social cognition according to which mind-reading capacities are predictive heuristics selected for behavioral coordination, not internal tracking devices; their persistence therefore offers no evidential support for representational realism.

I see potential conflicts, but I am unsure which are genuine and which dissolve on closer inspection. Does (3) implicitly commit me to the existence of internal representational states—thereby undermining (1) and (4)—or can direct awareness of neural dynamics be maintained without representational content? Is the tension between (2) and (1) only apparent, resolvable by distinguishing ontological commitment from normative practice, or does my claim that accountability is constitutively tied to intentional ascription actually require me to smuggle beliefs and desires back in? And is there a deeper, less obvious conflict between (2) and (4): if mind-reading evolved purely for prediction without tracking representations, can the normative structures in (2) be grounded in anything real about agents, or do they float free of any naturalistic basis? Please audit these commitments systematically: rank the tensions by severity, specify for each whether it requires giving up (1), revising the normative claim in (2), or reconceptualizing (3); indicate which single revision would restore consistency with minimal damage to the overall view; and flag any pair that looks tense but actually coheres once properly distinguished.
