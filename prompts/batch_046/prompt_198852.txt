I've been thinking on my own about the claim that countries with stronger gender-equality policies—generous parental leave, widespread subsidized childcare, strict anti-discrimination enforcement—often end up with more gender-divided workforces, with men clustering in engineering and women in nursing or teaching at higher rates than in less egalitarian places. I want a decision tree that sorts out what people actually believe about this, not just the academic labels. Build it so that each branch is a concrete claim someone could agree or disagree with without knowing philosophy or sociology jargon. Start with whether the pattern should be accepted as real rather than a measurement quirk. If yes, split between two families of explanation: one that says economic freedom lets built-in preferences show up once survival pressure is removed, and one that says egalitarian societies accidentally build structures—welfare rules, labor-market defaults, career-interruption costs—that make gender-specialized choices rational or easy. From there, branch on what justice requires: whether equality of opportunity is satisfied no matter the outcome pattern; whether we should redesign those welfare structures even if they are otherwise valuable; whether hidden social norms and informal tracking mean formal rules can never be enough; and whether, if preferences really are deep-set, justice ever permits pushing people away from them. Each leaf should have a plain-language name, one sentence summarizing the position, and its most awkward commitment—the thing defenders must accept. Note any empty combinations in the tree and say briefly whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I want about seven or eight leaves, and the tree should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named theories. Frame the questions for an ordinary reader deciding what they think, and keep the whole instrument to a size that fits on one page.
