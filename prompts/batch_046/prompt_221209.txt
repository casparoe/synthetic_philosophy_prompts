Write a poem in blank verse—unrhymed iambic pentameter—of exactly eighteen lines, with a pronounced medial caesura in each line, on the nonidentity problem framed through a reproductive-choice scenario: a young woman decides whether to conceive now, knowing that delaying would produce a numerically different child with a better life, while her community debates whether her choice wrongs anyone. Structure the poem as two interwoven voices—one insisting that harm requires an identical victim, the other insisting that moral wrongness persists even without one—without letting either voice resolve the tension. The central formal constraint should enact the philosophical crux: the word "she" must occur exactly three times, at lines 6, 12, and 18, with each occurrence referring to a different entity—the decision-maker at line 6, the child actually born into hardship at line 12, and the counterfactual child who never comes to exist at line 18—so that grammatical continuity masks metaphysical discontinuity. By the final line, the pronoun should feel unmoored, mirroring how the person-affecting principle loses its grip when the putative victim and the beneficiary of the choice are not the same person. Do not name the problem in the text, cite any philosopher, invoke specific climate-policy analogies, or deliver a sermonic conclusion; leave the poem suspended between the logical point that no one is made worse off relative to non-existence and the stubborn intuition that something has still gone wrong. The conceptual machinery—actual-person comparison, counterfactual identity, the rejection of harm-claims across possible worlds—should be recognizable to someone familiar with the argument, but the poem must stand alone as verse, with the pronoun slippage doing the philosophical work rather than any expository line.
