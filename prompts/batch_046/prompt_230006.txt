I sit on our hospital's neuroethics commitee and we've got a strange case for Thursday's pre meeting. A patient has been in a minimal-consciousness state for eighteen months. His family practices Theravada Buddhism and is pushing what they call stream-proxy consent — they say anatta means there's no enduring self that connects the patient to his prior advance directive, so the decision-maker shouldn't be the past person but the karmic continuum that might take rebirth regardless of what happens to this body. They want us to approve withdrawal of nutrition not based on substituted judgment for him, but on what's best for that continuum, including avoiding treatments that might bind negative karma to future rebirths.

The committee is split. Some see this as just religious accommodation with fancy vocabulary; others say if we accept it we're letting a family dissolve personal identity whenever it's convenient, which makes informed consent law impossible to apply. We need a written opinion circulated before the meeting recommending a concrete course — approve with conditions, reject, or some middle path — with reasoning the clinicians can actually use. It should treat the anatta position seriously, not just dismiss it as mystical, but also not pretend it solves clinical identity problems. Don't ask for citations or a reading list; just work with the concept as presented. Make sure the memo addresses the strongest argument on the losing side honestly, and keep it pratical enough that staff could apply it even if they don't study philosophy.

Draft the memo for me — clear bottom line, time frame if relevant, and don't reuse that standard ethics-board opening about balancing autonomy and beneficence since I've seen that in every template.
