I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on rational choice and its formal limits, and I should like to conclude with a short poem—approximately eighteen lines, in free verse governed by a single structural constraint—that takes the Pasadena paradox as its central image. The paradox, in which the expected value of a fair-seeming game is not merely infinite but genuinely undefined, should perform the formal work of the verse: please construct the poem around one recurring line that appears four times, each appearance identical except for its final word, which must be, in order: 'infinite', 'undefined', 'negative', and 'unsettled'. By this repetition-with-divergence the refrain itself should enact the failure of expectation to converge across different limiting procedures. The poem should allude, without citation or bibliographic apparatus, to the divergence between ensemble averaging and time-averaging—specifically, to the breaking of ergodicity in processes with heavy-tailed, non-integrable payoffs—so that a reader familiar with decision theory recognises the reference rather than encountering a vague metaphor for uncertainty. Use British English spelling and syntax throughout. The tone should be contemplative rather than didactic, and the poem must not resolve its tension in a synthetic conclusion: please end with two incompatible iterations of the refrain—perhaps the fourth and the second—placed adjacent without reconciliation, mirroring the paradox's refusal to yield a single fair price. The result should be a poem that is accurate enough to satisfy a specialist, yet accessible enough to close a seminar without footnotes.
