Question: In Douglas Hofstadter’s discussion of the Sphex wasp (Sphex ichneumoneus), he uses the insect’s rigid burrow-checking ritual to illustrate what he calls sphexishness—a deterministic, inflexible behavioral loop. He contrasts this with antisphexishness, the capacity for context-sensitive, adaptive response that he associates with intelligence. Some philosophers have argued that genuine free will requires not just antisphexish behavior (first-order flexibility) but a further capacity for procedural revision: the ability to evaluate and alter one’s own decision-rules in light of second-order reasons. Does Hofstadter’s antisphexishness already include this second-order capacity, or is there a meaningful gap? In your answer, distinguish carefully between (i) causal-role flexibility—varying outputs within a fixed program in response to changed inputs—and (ii) procedural revision—changing the program itself—and apply both to the Sphex case.

Student answer: The Sphex wasp demonstrates sphexishness through its stereotyped sequence: it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, enters to inspect, and then pulls the prey inside. If an experimenter shifts the burrow during the inspection, the wasp repeats the entry-and-check cycle rather than adapting its strategy. This shows pure mechanical rigidity—no context-sensitive adjustment. Hofstadter, who originally documented this behavior in Gödel, Escher, Bach, defines antisphexishness as the opposite: flexible, intelligent behavior that breaks out of fixed loops. Because antisphexishness involves responding to context in ways that are not preprogrammed, it necessarily includes procedural revision. A system that can restructure its own decision-rules is simply displaying a more complex form of the same adaptive flexibility. Consequently, the distinction between causal-role flexibility and procedural revision collapses: both are just different levels of a single mechanism. Dennett reinforces this in Elbow Room, arguing that elbow room—the space for flexible, avoidable response—is sufficient for moral responsibility without requiring any non-causal freedom. Therefore, there is no meaningful gap between antisphexishness and the second-order capacity required for free will; once a system is antisphexish, its flexibility extends all the way up to self-modification, which means the claim that free will requires something further is mistaken.
