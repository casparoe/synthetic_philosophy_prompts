In a seminar discussion of global workspace theory and phenomenal consciousness, a colleague kept dismissing "overflow" arguments—the claim that a subject might phenomenally experience more in a brief display than can be subsequently reported—by repeating one line: "If it never enters the global workspace, there was never anything it was like to have it." She pressed this specifically on subliminal priming cases, where a stimulus activates early sensory cortex but, she said, fails to trigger the broad fronto-parietal broadcast that workspace models link to conscious access; from that she concluded that there is literally no phenomenal character present, not merely an unaccessed one. I think the sentence hides several different theses, and I need to see which ones can actually carry her conclusion. Can you lay out the precise claims that slogan could be expressing—distinguishing at least (i) a definitional or conceptual claim about what "phenomenal consciousness" means, (ii) an empirical claim about the neural architecture required for phenomenology, (iii) a modal claim about whether globally unbroadcast states are metaphysically possible, (iv) a normative claim about how we ought to apply the word "experience," and (v) a claim about rational-control availability versus mere reportability within access consciousness itself? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and specify which ones actually license treating overflow arguments as conceptually confused rather than just empirically mistaken. Then tell me which reading she was most likely invoking in the priming case, and whether that reading survives once the disambiguation is on the table.
