I've recently come to defend a position at the intersection of philosophy of history and causation: that pathogens, abrupt climate shifts, and volcanic aerosol events function as genuine historical actors, not mere background conditions, possessing what I term non-intentional veto agency—the capacity to block or redirect institutional trajectories in ways that are not reconstructible from social-level variables alone, yet without any directional steering or teleology. I hold that this avoids environmental determinism only if paired with an adaptive-filtration clause, according to which human societies can reshape outcomes in response to these forces without nullifying their independent causal contribution. What would have to be true for this view to fail? I want more than the standard objections—that agency requires intentionality, or that the filtration clause is determinism in disguise. Specifically, rank the defeaters by severity: which empirical patterns (for instance, well-documented cases in which a society fully absorbed a climatic or pathogenic shock with no detectable trajectory change), which conceptual distinctions (say, between veto power and ordinary causal interference in complex systems), and which results from philosophy of action or systems causation expose real vulnerability? For each, indicate whether a committed defender should concede it or can reasonably dismiss it. Also clarify what predictions this view makes that rival pure social-history or hard-environmental-determinist accounts do not, and assess honestly whether the adaptive-filtration clause has insulated the position into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical vice or an acceptable feature of a view of this kind.
