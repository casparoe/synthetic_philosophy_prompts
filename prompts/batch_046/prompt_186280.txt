You serve on a clinical licensure board that is redesigning examinations for emergency practitioners, and you must decide, for each proposed competency, whether it should be assessed as knowing-that—explicit, articulable, correctable by information—or as knowing-how—procedural, calibrated, correctable primarily by structured repetition and perturbation-resistant execution. The board needs a reusable, thirty-minute decision procedure that translates the philosophical distinction into operational terms without requiring philosophical training, that can be applied by a panel of clinicians and psychometricians, and that forces an honest record whenever resource constraints override cognitive classification. The procedure must handle cases where the two are entangled, must flag the points where judgment is irreducible, and must be applied consistently enough that two different subcommittees reach the same classification for the same competency.

Design the procedure as an ordered sequence of four tests, specifying for each: (a) the exact question the panel asks, (b) what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step or to stop, (c) the classification that results if the test resolves cleanly, and (d) the precise wording of the judgment-call flag when it does not resolve cleanly. Include a fifth step—assessment-mode mapping—that translates the classification into an examination format, with an explicit “validity compromise” notation required when the board selects a cheaper or faster format that mismatches the cognitive classification.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases, in this order: (1) endotracheal intubation under noisy, time-pressured conditions; (2) interpreting an ECG during an active resuscitation, where expert speed seems pattern-based but justification is explicitly rule-governed; and (3) recognizing sepsis in a triage queue, where rapid recognition behaves like calibrated pattern-matching but institutional pressure favors testing it via written case vignettes. For case (3), the procedure should yield a classification that the board will find unwelcome—specifically, that the competency is predominantly knowing-how—yet you must show exactly where the procedure permits the board to override that result for practical reasons, provided the override is documented as a psychometric trade-off rather than a cognitive discovery.

Finally, state three things the procedure explicitly cannot do: it cannot eliminate all entanglement, it cannot determine whether a particular practitioner’s skill is genuinely tacit or merely unarticulated, and it cannot resolve disputes about the acceptable false-negative rate for high-stakes procedural testing. Keep the deliverable free of bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; ground the distinctions in the structure of the tests themselves, not in appeals to named authorities.
