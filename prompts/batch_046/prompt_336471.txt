I am drafting the opening remarks for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop on causation, agency, and collaborative creation, and I need them written as a speech that does genuine philosophical work rather than decorative allusion. The audience will be split between philosophers well-versed in medieval Islamic thought, cognitive scientists who study distributed decision-making, and fiction writers who think natively in narrative cause-and-effect; the remarks must land for all three groups without reducing the idea to a mere metaphor.

Please construct a six-minute standing address to be delivered at the start of the first session. Its structural backbone should be Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism—taken not as historical theology but as a live constraint on how we speak about intervention, authorship, and control in joint intellectual labor. To keep the framing from being something a model could lift from a standard summary, inject the following conceptual hybrid: treat occasionalist causation as a model of "causal tenancy," in which an agent never owns an effect but holds temporary occupancy of it, every link in the chain being immediately revoked and renewed by a single source. Apply this specifically to the ethics of collaborative editing and research design: if no human participant truly causes a revised conclusion or an emergent data pattern, but only holds it in trust for a moment, then our usual vocabulary of credit, blame, and causal ownership collapses in precise ways. The speech should specify exactly which judgments about responsibility must be withdrawn and which can survive, and it should do so in language that does not patronize the philosophers or lose the writers.

Include one concrete, invented scenario: a multi-author manuscript in which one collaborator's late revision inverts the central argument, and walk through how "causal tenancy" redistributes our intuitive judgments about who caused the change and what kind of accountability remains possible. The occasionalist framework must genuinely shape the welcome—determining what can and cannot be said about the workshop's collective work—rather than serving as ornament.

Finally, I ask that the response include a brief reflective note stating what would change its mind: identify the specific argument about occasionalism's coherence, or about the analogy between divine renewal and human collaborative agency, that would cause it to abandon the framing, and flag precisely where its reasoning is most vulnerable to failure—for instance, whether the analogy secretly smuggles in a secondary causation that occasionalism denies, or whether "causal tenancy" collapses into ordinary instrumental causation once applied to human agents.
