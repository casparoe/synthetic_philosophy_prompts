Consider the following paraphrase of a passage from Aristotle's Politics, Book I, Chapter 9 (c. 350 BCE), rendered in the tradition of older English translations. There Aristotle distinguishes the art of household management, which acquires goods only up to the limit of natural need and is therefore bounded, from the art of wealth-getting, chrematistikē. The latter arises when money, originally invented as a conventional medium to solve the problem of exchanging goods of unequal need, is treated not as a means but as the very end of acquisition. In retail trade and especially in usury, money breeds money without any natural limit, because coin is of the same kind as the wealth it measures, allowing accumulation to continue indefinitely. Aristotle concludes that this form of acquisition is unnatural because it violates the teleological structure of the household and the polis: goods exist for use, and the instruments of exchange exist for facilitating that use, so when the instrument becomes the object of desire, the order of ends and means is inverted.

I am trying to understand whether Aristotle's judgment that unlimited accumulation is unnatural depends essentially on the claim that money, being homogeneous with the goods it measures, has no internal boundary, or whether it depends more fundamentally on a normative claim about human needs being naturally finite. To test this, imagine a hypothetical institutional design—never implemented in any historical economy—featuring two interconvertible currencies: one that is strictly perishable, decaying to zero if not spent within a fixed period and functioning like labor-credits tied to immediate consumption, and one that is durable and can be stored indefinitely. Both are accepted in exchange, but the decaying currency is legally restricted to transactions involving goods for direct use, while the durable currency is permitted only for long-term storage contracts, not for retail consumption. In this system, accumulation of the durable currency is still technically unlimited, yet it is institutionally separated from the circuit of daily need-satisfaction.

Please help me understand: if such a dual-currency system existed, would Aristotle still regard the hoarding of the durable currency as an instance of chrematistikē, or would he classify it as a separate, perhaps legitimate, form of provision? More specifically, does his argument require that the medium of exchange be physically incapable of separation from consumption-goods, or only that the agent's intention be directed toward use rather than accumulation?
