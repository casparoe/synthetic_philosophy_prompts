I'm delivering the opening remarks at the dedication of the Meridian Flood Garden, a public green space and storm-water retention system our town approved through a tax levy that will take fifteen years to pay off. The audience includes the engineers who designed the eighty-year flood wall, residents who opposed the tax because they will not live to see the full benefit, and the high school environmental group that lobbied for it. I have no formal academic background, but I have been thinking on my own about a problem that keeps returning to me: we speak easily of leaving a better world for our children, yet the people who will actually sit in this garden in 2080 are not our children—most are not born, none can vote, and none can thank us or hold us accountable. I want roughly eight minutes of remarks that take that asymmetry seriously rather than smoothing it over with familiar language. The speech should celebrate the project without calling future residents "our neighbors," and it should explain why funding it was neither simple charity nor a standard investment, but something structurally different—an obligation to strangers who depend entirely on us while we depend on them not at all. That idea should shape what I can and cannot say: I should not claim we will be rewarded by gratitude we will never receive, I should not pretend the tax was painless because the cause is noble, and I should not treat the future beneficiaries as imaginary figures whose interests are merely symbolic. Please write it so that the teenagers hear urgency, the engineers hear precision, and the skeptical taxpayers hear honesty rather than sermon. No references to specific philosophers or texts—just the concrete details of the garden beds that will not mature for decades, the wall that will outlive its builders, and the tax bill that arrives now for a protection that arrives later.
