My opening move is this. Arendt insists that Eichmann's defining feature was an inability to think from the standpoint of anyone other than himself, yet she also describes him as lacking the capacity for independent judgment altogether. I want to press you on a conceptual distinction that your account tends to blur: the difference between moral thoughtlessness—an absence of reflective engagement with one's actions—and what we might call institutional epistemic negligence, where an agent actively defers all evaluative authority to procedural norms. Consider a hypothetical senior compliance officer in a scalable oversight system for artificial intelligence: she verifies that every protocol step has been executed, never asks whether the aggregate system she is certifying might cause catastrophic harm, and treats her role as purely technical verification. She is not ideologically committed to harm; she is careerist and rule-bound, precisely the profile you attribute to Eichmann. Yet her harm is mediated through distributed architecture—no single individual sees the full causal chain—whereas Eichmann personally signed orders that he knew led to deportation. If your thesis requires direct causal proximity between the agent's hand and the harm, then it fails to capture modern distributed bureaucratic evil. If, instead, it applies to anyone who substitutes procedural compliance for moral judgment regardless of causal distance, then Eichmann loses his specificity and becomes merely one instance of a structural feature of modern rationalized administration. Which horn do you take, and how do you preserve the particular horror of the Eichmann case without reducing it to a generic sociology of bureaucracy?
