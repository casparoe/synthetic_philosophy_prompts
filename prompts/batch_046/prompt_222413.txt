I'm working on a syllabus unit about personal identity that goes a bit off the beaten path — specifically Hofstadter's idea from I Am a Strange Loop that the self is just a pattern that could in principle be distributed or duplicated across brains, and whether that means 'I' could literally span two heads. I'm having my students write dialogue exercises, but I want a model version first; my coffee's cold and I'm writing this between classes.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocuters — call them Alex and Jordan — debating whether an 'I' can be genuinely distributed across brains rather than just copied like a file. Alex should take the Hofstadterian line: the self is a strange loop of self-reference, substate-independent, and if two brains are causally coupled tightly enough (say, through some hypothetical sustained cortical bridge allowing real-time shared processing), there's no principled reason the loop can't run across both. Jordan should resist, but not with hand-wavy soul-talk. Instead, Jordan should press on first-person exclusivity and the problem of competing loops: if both brains generate a first-person report saying 'I see red,' and they're coupled, do we have one subject with binocular vision or two subjects in a weird merged state? And if one brain dies, does the survivor feel like they lost a limb, or like they witnessed a death?

Make them actually talk to each other — Alex should respond to the exclusivty worry, Jordan should respond to the substrate-independence claim. Don't let them talk past each other. I want the tension to sit right on the crux: is causal integration sufficient for loop-unification, or does first-person authority always fragment across biological boundaries? Keep it casual in tone but philosophically dense — no citation lists, no "according to Hofstadter p. 42," just the argument itself.
