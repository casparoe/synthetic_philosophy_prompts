I'm writing to seek your analytical judgment on an institutional dilemma that has begun to feel more philosophical than administrative. I serve on the curriculum committee of a mid-sized liberal-arts college, and we are weighing a proposal to restructure the second-year general-education sequence. Under the plan, students in the natural-science tracks could elect to replace the traditional second-semester literature seminar with a newly designed module that teaches narrative interpretation through the analysis of scientific case studies and data-visualization narratives. The substitution would be entirely voluntary; students in the humanities and social sciences would retain every existing pathway, faculty lines in literature would not be reduced, and the new module would be staffed by a cross-disciplinary hire rather than drawn from current departmental budgets. By any distributive accounting—enrollment figures, teaching-load ratios, budget projections—the change appears to expand options for one cohort without contracting them for any other.

Yet a senior colleague, whose judgment I generally respect, has urged us to reject the proposal precisely because it is harmless. She argues that curricular architecture is not a pool of resources to be optimized incrementally, and that treating it as such commits a kind of category mistake: once we accept that a tradition may be modified whenever we can demonstrate that no party is made worse off, we implicitly license a piecemeal erosion of institutional identity that proceeds not through overt conflict but through the accumulation of safe adjustments. She concedes that no student or faculty member is disadvantaged by this particular substitution, but she maintains that there is a distinct wrong—not a harm to anyone, but a corruption of the framework itself—in allowing structural change to be governed solely by the absence of injury.

I am unsure whether her objection can be reconstructed as a coherent philosophical claim rather than an aesthetic attachment to continuity. Is there a defensible principle—perhaps drawn from theories of institutional integrity, collective choice, or the normative limits of improvement—that would justify resisting a change that benefits some and harms none? Or is her resistance best understood as a form of status-quo bias dressed in the language of principle? I would appreciate your help in identifying the precise philosophical structure of her argument and in assessing whether it survives critical scrutiny.
