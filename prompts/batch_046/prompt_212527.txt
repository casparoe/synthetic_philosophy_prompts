Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—using these six rotating end-words in order: center, gravity, narrative, self, fiction, point. The poem should treat Daniel Dennett’s idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity, but apply it specifically to a discontinuous, distributed autobiographical system: separate, non-interacting memory modules that generate first-person reports with no shared substrate, no biological continuity, and no unified consciousness behind them. Let the sestina’s formal rotation do philosophical work. In successive stanzas, “center” and “self” should land at line-ends where they seem to claim authority, only to be displaced by the rotating scheme in the next stanza, mirroring how narratives orbit an empty focal point. “Gravity” and “fiction” should anchor lines that describe the attractive pull of story-making and the constructed nature of the autobiographer. In the envoi, all six end-words must appear within the three lines, arranged so that “center” and “point” are structurally adjacent or directly paired, showing that the center is not an independent substance but only the point where orbiting narratives converge. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Dennett’s view recognizes the gravitational metaphor and the fictionality of the self, while the discontinuous-system framing should make clear this is not a generic summary but an extension into a distributed, non-biological case.
