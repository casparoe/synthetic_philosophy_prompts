I'm a doctoral student in the philosophy of history and social epistemology, and I'm trying to stabilize my reflective equilibrium on conflicts between collective memory and archival record. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases. First, when an Indigenous nation asserts that treaty negotiations included an oral promise of perpetual land rights that is absent from the signed document—archival research confirms no such clause, yet the community's oral tradition is internally consistent across generations—I think that memory should carry legal and moral weight rather than be dismissed as fabrication. Second, when a national holiday commemorates a nineteenth-century uprising as a unified popular resistance, but archives reveal it was regionally fragmented and led largely by elite factions with minimal mass participation, I think the public narrative should be revised or at least supplemented by the record. Third, a diaspora community insists that its ancestors were expelled en masse in 1948; demographic records show a more heterogeneous pattern of departure, including substantial voluntary emigration, yet I hesitate to correct the collective narrative in educational or political contexts because the emotional and normative reality of the group seems to depend on the uniformity of the story. Fourth, a small coastal town maintains a memorial to a 1923 deliberate state bombardment that killed hundreds; municipal archives and naval logs record only an accidental single shelling with no fatalities, though local cemeteries do show an unusual cluster of deaths that year without documented cause. I keep oscillating on whether the memorial should stand uncorrected, be revised with archival data, or be reframed to honor ambiguous loss without specifying cause—this is the case I cannot stabilize.

My tentative systematization distinguishes what I call constitutive memories, which form the normative backbone of a community's present identity and relational obligations, from reportorial memories, which function as discrete empirical claims about specific past events. On this view, the treaty claim and perhaps the diaspora expulsion are constitutive, so archival absence or demographic complexity should not dissolve them; the national uprising is reportorial, so fragmentation in the record should correct the myth; and the town bombardment is ambiguous because the cemetery cluster gives the memory some empirical anchor, but not enough to justify the specific causal and intentional narrative engraved on the memorial. Is there a more precise principle—or a small family of principles—that captures these verdicts without merely redescribing them? In particular, what should I say about the town case, and which of my four verdicts must your principle overturn?
