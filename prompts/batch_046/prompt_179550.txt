I am writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas column on the hidden logic of legal self-reference—specifically, the conceptual puzzles that arise when the rules governing how rules change are themselves altered by those very rules. In researching the historical roots of this problem, I encountered a dense argument from an unnamed 1890s constitutional treatise written in the thick, hierarchical prose typical of Victorian jurisprudence. The passage, paraphrased, runs roughly as follows: a sovereign legislature may revise not only the statutes in force but also the formal procedures through which future revisions are enacted; yet, because the legislature derives its authority solely from the currently valid amendment clause, it cannot lawfully alter that clause except by exercising the very authority it seeks to modify. The author treats this not as a flat contradiction but as proof that ultimate sovereignty must reside in a power that stands outside its own procedural constraints, implying a fixed point at the top of the normative hierarchy.

I am trying to understand exactly where the conceptual tension lies, and I would like your help mapping it without any bibliographic references or citation requests. Is the old argument genuinely parallel to logical paradoxes of self-reference—for instance, a rule that implicitly asserts its own unamendability—or does it dissolve once we distinguish between the authority that applies a rule and the rule that defines that authority? More specifically, I want to examine how modern “nomic” accounts of self-amending rules interact with this Victorian intuition: if one rejects a hierarchical fixed point and instead allows that the rules of amendment are themselves subject to amendment under those same rules, does the bootstrap problem re-emerge at a higher level, or does it flatten into a circular but coherent system? Please help me see whether the paradox is real, merely an artifact of conflating procedural levels, or resolved differently depending on whether we read the old text as assuming an indispensable meta-rule or as allowing full self-reference.
