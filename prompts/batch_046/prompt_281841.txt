I'm now pretty sure the standard fink-and-mask framing gets things backwards: dispositions aren't categorical bases that get disrupted or concealed, they're temporal trajectories that unfold differently depending on how long a system is exposed to a trigger. A glass inside a finkish shield isn't "really fragile but masked"; it's just a system whose breaking-profile is stretched across a longer interval, and calling that "masking" smuggles in a static ontology we don't need. The catgeorical-base camp survives only by treating instantaneous microstates as primary, which looks like a hangover from classical mechanics. So what'd actually move me away from this? Rank the defeaters: formal arguments that dispositions must reduce to categorical properties, empirical cases from materials science where the temporal profile view mispredicts failure modes, and conceptual problems—like whether my view collapses into saying everything is disposed to do everything eventually. Be honest about whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and whether that's fatal for a metaphysical claim of this kind. Also tell me what defenders of the Martin-style categorical analysis would themselves grant as damaging, not just what opponents charge. One thing I'm unsure about: synthetic self-healing polymers that change their fracture dynamics based on prior enviromental history—do they support my trajectory view, or do they actually show that there's a hidden categorical base that just gets reconfigured?
