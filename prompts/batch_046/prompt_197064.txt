I am designing the final examination for an upper-level seminar on the epistemology of long-run economic divergence—specifically whether comparative history can ever adjudicate among institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations for why some nations remain wealthy while others do not. Rather than asking students to summarize familiar debates, I want an essay prompt that embeds a concrete identification failure in a self-contained comparative scenario: imagine two regions that received an identical colonial legal framework in the seventeenth century, share broadly similar latitudes and access to navigable rivers, but diverge sharply over three centuries in both per-capita output and in a locally adaptive cultural practice—say, a particular form of cooperative labor organization—that only becomes economically consequential after industrialization. The methodological trap is that geography slowly shaped this cultural practice with a long lag, and the practice then mediated how the shared institutions functioned in the modern era, so that a present-day observer might treat institutions or geography as constant and therefore explanatory, missing the endogenous cultural bridge. The question should ask students to diagnose exactly why this configuration undermines both naive institutional determinism and naive geographic determinism, to specify what kind of counterfactual or natural-experiment evidence could actually isolate the causal channel, and to explain why treating culture as an exogenous residual is a mistake rather than a solution. The exam is fifty minutes and weighted at thirty marks; please provide the full exam question, a model answer that identifies the specific conceptual moves required for full credit, and a marking scheme that assigns points to each move—distinguishing correlation from causal identification, recognizing temporal confounding, treating culture as endogenous, proposing a testable counterfactual rather than invoking path dependence as a label, and avoiding the fallacy of treating spatial correlation as proof—and that lists the precise misconceptions that should cost marks.

Please calibrate the marking scheme so that top answers must engage with the interaction effect between slow geographic influence and institutional mediation, not merely list the three competing theories. In your response, state explicitly what evidence or argument would change your assessment of whether the identification problem in this scenario is genuinely intractable—such as a specific econometric strategy that successfully instruments for institutional quality while blocking the geographic-cultural pathway—and indicate where your reasoning about which mistakes deserve the heaviest penalties is most likely to fail, particularly if the boundary between endogenous culture and institutional adaptation turns out to be more porous than the model answer assumes. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requirements; the prompt should be answerable from first principles by a student who understands causal inference in historical comparison.
