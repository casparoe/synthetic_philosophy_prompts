I run a philosophy/policy workshop for about 14 grad students and organizers, 2 hours. Design a simulation that makes them feel how the "undeserving poor" category gets manufactured by verification rules rather than discovered in behavior. The twist I care about is administrative performativity — fraud isn't just caught, it's produced by the interaction between claimant paperwork and caseworker discretion under time pressure. So split them into claimants (messy honest lives: gig work, informal care, unstable housing), caseworkers (checklist plus hidden speed bonus that rewards quick classification), and auditors who only see final files. Claimants fill forms that can't capture reality without either oversimplifing (looking suspicious) or being too detailed (looking evasive). Caseworkers classify each file in 3 minutes — clean, suspicious, fraudulent — earning points for matching auditor ratings. After round 1, reveal that the audit "ground truth" for round 2 gets retroactively calibrated to the aggregate patterns of caseworker decisions from round 1, so the standard of fraud shifts based on what they collectively produced. Don't lecture up front and don't name the concept until after round 2. Then a debrief that maps form design to epistemic gaps, time pressure to institutional rationality, and the shifting criteria to the performative loop. Be honest about what this can't reproduce: actual material deprivation, long-term stigma, and racialized patterning. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong — probably competitive escalation or real embarrassment among claimants — and how to hold the frame without breaking it. Keep materials cheap: printed cards, timers, paper forms. There's a typo or two here but you get the idea.
