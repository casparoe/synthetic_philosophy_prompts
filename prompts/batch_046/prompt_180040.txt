I am drafting a short essay that extends Good's decision-theoretic result on the value of information into a strategic setting for which it was not originally designed. The argument runs as follows.

Recall the standard result: a Bayesian agent with stable preferences facing a well-defined decision problem should never reject costless information prior to acting, because conditioning her strategy on the updated belief can only preserve or improve expected utility.

My essay applies this to a committee of N members with private preference orderings over outcomes, who must vote collectively on a proposal. Before voting, each member may freely observe a noisy signal about the distribution of others' preferences. For any individual, observing is costless; given her current beliefs about coalition formation, updating on the signal can only help her choose the vote that maximizes her own expected utility. By Good's reasoning, refusal to observe is individually irrational.

Yet suppose universal observation leads all members to update strategically, producing an equilibrium outcome O that every member judges ex ante to be Pareto-inferior to the outcome O* that would have emerged had all voted in mutual ignorance. Crucially, no member's own utility function changes upon learning the signal; the harm arises purely from interdependent belief revision.

I want to defend two claims. First, this is not a standard counterexample to Good's theorem—unlike cases where information alters one's preferences—because the theorem governs single-agent decision problems, not games with strategic interaction. Second, treating the committee as a single collective agent with a social welfare function does not dissolve the tension but relocates it: the collective agent, too, appears bound by Good's reasoning to acquire information that lowers its own welfare.

Could you comment on whether my characterization of Good's result is accurate, on whether the committee case is genuinely distinct from preference-altering counterexamples, and on whether the distinction between "individually rational to observe" and "collectively rational to suppress observation" holds up? I am also uncertain whether this dynamic should be understood as an information cascade or as structurally different; clarification on that boundary would be especially helpful.
