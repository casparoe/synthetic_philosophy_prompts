I am a high school student in an advanced philosophy of science seminar, and for the past three weeks we have been working through Bongard-style puzzles—the ones in which twelve diagrams are divided into two groups of six, one group obeying a hidden rule and the other violating it, with the solver given minimal feedback and no explicit instruction. Our discussions kept returning to the same unresolved tension: when we induce a rule from so sparse a sample, are we discovering a real boundary in the world, or are we merely imposing order? I want to close our unit with an original poem that does not just name-drop this idea but enacts it structurally. Please write a poem of exactly twelve lines, divided into two sestets labeled Stanza I and Stanza II. One sestet should obey a hidden formal rule; the other should break it. The hidden rule itself should concern induction—perhaps one sestet restricts itself to concrete, singular, observable images that could serve as discrete "examples," while the other permits abstract, overlapping, or ambiguous descriptions that resist clear classification. The rhyme scheme, meter, or diction should make the split audible, so that a reader familiar with Bongard problems recognizes the architecture, and a reader unfamiliar with them still feels the formal dissonance.

Please do not resolve the tension in the final lines. Let the poem end with the two sestets standing in an unresolved relation, mirroring our seminar's conclusion that the boundary between discovery and projection may itself be one of the patterns we are trying to induce. Use polished, accessible language suitable for a classroom reading. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or a reading list. Think carefully about the placement of each line, and render the philosophical content precisely enough that a specialist would recognize it as conceptually accurate rather than merely decorative.
