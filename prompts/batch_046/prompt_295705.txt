i'm trying to pin down my actual views on well-being by looking at my gut reactions to these simulation cases, and I want to know if there's one principel behind them or if i'm just being messy.

Case one: the classic Nozick-style experiance machine. You're living what feels like a perfect life — great relationships, achievements, the works — but it's all simulated and you never find out. My verdict: that's not a genuinely good life, period.

Case two: same machine, except you enter knowing exactly what it is. You want to experiance being a world-class musician without putting in the years. You stay for decades, knowing every success is virtual. I still think that's not flourishing, but I have to admit I feel more sympathy for this person than for case one — maybe because there's no deception?

Case three: real, unsimulated life. You actually publish a book, raise a family, do hard things, but you're on meds or just temperamentally flat — you feel almost nothing. I think your life is going well despite the missing color; the goods are real.

Case four: what I call a recognition machine. It's not a full life sim, just a neural device that makes you feel profoundly seen and loved by people who aren't there. You know it's fake, but the warmth is intense. I think that's not well-being, it's closer to a really good drug trip.

Case five: here's the twist that confuses me. You're living an objectively good, real life, but an authorship filter implant secretly rewrites your memory and self-attribution. Every real achievement feels like it was done by a colleague; every failure feels like yours alone. You're convinced you're a fraud despite clear evidence otherwise. I think this actually damages your well-being, maybe even more than case three damages it by flattening affect.

Case six: the reverse filter. You genuinely do almost nothing, but the implant makes every minor action feel like a monumental achievement and every failure disappear. You feel like a genius. I think that's also not well-being, but i'm not sure if it's teh same failure mode as case one or something else.

So the pattern i'm seeing: real achievement matters, correct ownership of that achievement matters, and affective response is optional but fake affect is worthless. But case two bugs me — if correct ownership requires knowing your achievements are real, then case two should be okay because you know they're fake, yet i still reject it. So maybe the principle is that well-being requires real-world causal efficacy, not just mental representation?

Is there a coherent account — maybe mixing objective goods with an authorship condition — that saves all six verdicts? Or do i have to drop case two, or case five? And what should i say about the case i keep wobbling on: the collaborative vat, where your consciousness is genuinely merged with another person's, so your achievements are partly authored by you and partly not, and you know exactly which parts are thier own?

Please also tell me what your best principle predicts for that collaborative case, and which of my verdicts it has to overturn, wether any.
