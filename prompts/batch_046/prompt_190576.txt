Consider three coexisting institutional practices. First, animal-research oversight bodies mandate analgesic and endpoint protocols for vertebrate and cephalopod subjects, yet they typically impose no comparable ethical review for experiments that manipulate insects at scale, regardless of the sophistication of their sensorimotor integration. Second, clinical ethics committees routinely authorize the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment from patients diagnosed as permanently unconscious, apply full protective protocols to patients with minimal consciousness, and recognize no intermediate moral standing for individuals with fluctuating or partial awareness. Third, technology firms have begun running formal sentience-risk assessments for large language models, but they deploy adaptive predictive systems in eldercare facilities—algorithms that learn individual residents' thermal and spatial preferences, anticipate movements, and adjust the environment preemptively—without ever evaluating whether such predictive architectures instantiate a minimal form of sentience, even when their integrative complexity exceeds that of systems already under review. Taken together, what implicit philosophy of sentience and moral status do these practices collectively presuppose? Reconstruct the underlying theory: is sentience treated as an all-or-nothing biological property tied to taxonomic boundaries, as a graded continuum that institutions must artificially binarize for administrative tractability, or as a functional architecture that happens to align with familiar categories? Assess whether the cluster is genuinely consistent with a gradational view—according to which moral standing increases continuously with predictive or integrative complexity—or whether it relies on sharp, unprincipled thresholds that collapse under scrutiny. Finally, specify what concrete revisions to review-board criteria, veterinary protocols, and engineering-ethics audits would be required if these institutions were redesigned to track degrees of sentience rather than hidden binary classifications.
