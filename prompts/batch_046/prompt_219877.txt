hey, so in my political theory seminar people keep reaching for the language-community analogy to solve the boundary problem: the demos is like a linguistic community, you belong if you share the form of life, understand the rules being argued over, and are bound by them in a sustained way. they lean on it to argue that legal citizenship is too narrow and that long-term residents, affected outsiders, maybe even future generations should count. i get the appeal but i'm not sold. can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of grammar, of fluency, of being a native speaker vs a second-language learner, of dialects, of language death? then tell me where the mapping actually breaks: for instance, language membership is usually involuntary and hard to exit, which smuggles in a kind of ethnic closure that usually occures in these arguments but gets hidden. also, what about people who can't speak at all, or people who will live here in fifty years but don't understand the grammar yet? does the analogy still license expanding the demos, or does it actually push us back toward birthright and assimilationist criteria? and if it breaks, what's a less misleading analogy?
