I was listening to an episode of a philosophy podcast—the kind that gets shared in rationalist forums and book clubs—trying to explain Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. The host summarized it roughly as follows. Quine demonstrated that in a radical translation scenario, if a native speaker utters “gavagai” when a rabbit runs past, there is literally no fact of the matter whether the word refers to rabbits, temporal stages of rabbits, or undetached rabbit parts, because all observable behavior—pointing, assent, dissent—is equally compatible with each manual of translation. The host added that Quine reaches this conclusion because he rejects mentalism: he denies that there are private mental meanings or intentions that could serve as hidden arbiters between competing translations. From this, the host drew two further conclusions. First, that radical indeterminacy applies not just to unknown jungle languages but to fluent English speakers as well, so that when I say “rabbit” and you say “rabbit,” we might be operating with incommensurable ontologies without ever knowing it. Second, that this translation indeterminacy is essentially the same argument as Quine’s underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence, and therefore that mechanistic interpretability in AI is doomed in principle: if we cannot fix the reference of “gavagai” in a human speaker, we certainly cannot fix whether a hidden-layer activation in an LLM represents “rabbit” versus “rabbit-stage,” so there is no fact of the matter about what the model “really” believes.

I have the sense that the first half is mostly accurate but that the host is overreaching in the second half. I am particularly unsure whether Quine intends the thesis to apply to fluent speakers of the same language in the same robust way, and whether the leap from linguistic translation to AI representation is anything more than a loose analogy. I also wonder whether Quine’s rejection of mentalism is quite as total as the host suggests—I recall that Quine allows some role for dispositions and neural states, even if he rejects Cartesian private theaters. Could someone well-versed in Quine go through the summary sentence by sentence? I would like to know which parts a Quine scholar would accept, which parts involve subtle conflations—for instance, between translation indeterminacy and theoretical underdetermination—and whether the conclusion about mechanistic interpretability actually follows from Quine’s premises, or whether it depends on importing assumptions about AI representations that Quine never considered.
