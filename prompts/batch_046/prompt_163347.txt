Albert Einstein died in 1955. Shortly afterward his brain was removed, preserved in formalin, dissected into roughly 240 blocks, and eventually distributed among researchers; whether this occurred with his full prior consent remains disputed. More than two decades later, in 1981, Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett published The Mind's I, a collection of essays and dialogues on the nature of self and consciousness. Among its fantasies are two that would have been unimaginable during Einstein's lifetime: first, a scenario in which Einstein's own preserved brain—suspended in jars, its neurons still structurally intact—might be reactivated or interrogated as a continuing conscious subject; second, a thought experiment in which an ordinary printed book, through an extraordinarily dense web of annotations, marginal cross-references, and recursive commentary, achieves a pattern of internal relations complex enough to be considered conscious.

Assume Einstein had survived to encounter these proposals, not as scientific programs but as philosophical provocations. Drawing on what we know of his metaphysical commitments—his Spinozist determinism, his conviction that physical reality is objectively ordered and independent of observation, his well-known discomfort with quantum indeterminacy and with any notion of nonlocal influence, his insistence that thought proceeds through visual and intuitive schemas rather than verbal symbol-manipulation, and his 1954 correspondence with Erik Gutkind in which he rejected a personal deity in favor of an impersonal cosmic rationality—predict how he would have evaluated each scenario.

Specifically: Would he have granted that consciousness is substrate-independent, so that either a formaldehyde-preserved brain or a network of printed pages could in principle host it? Or would he have insisted that consciousness requires not merely structural complexity but a specific kind of living, dynamically integrated physical process—perhaps even a particular field-theoretic organization—that neither preserved tissue nor paper annotations could replicate? How would his determinism bear on the question: if mental states are fully determined by prior physical states, does copying or preserving the physical structure preserve the mental trajectory, or does it merely archive a static record?

Address both scenarios separately, but also compare them: which, if either, would he have found more plausible as a bearer of mind, and why? Rank his likely objections from the decisive to the merely awkward—distinguishing objections he would treat as fatal from those he might concede are puzzling but not conclusive. Finally, note whether his response risks collapsing into a form of biological naturalism that he himself might have resisted, given his broader physicalist sympathies, or whether it remains consistent with his view that the human mind is ultimately explicable within a unified natural science.
