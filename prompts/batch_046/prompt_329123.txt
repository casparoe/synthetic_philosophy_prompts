I’ve been revisiting the Hart–Fuller debate after a study-group discussion turned into a sharper disagreement than I expected, and I’d like a careful adjudication rather than a summary of the literature. One friend—call him A—defends a hard positivist line: Hart’s union of primary and secondary rules, anchored by a rule of recognition, treats law as a social fact whose existence is independent of moral merit. Even a regime that systematically violates human dignity, he insists, remains law; it is just law that is morally bankrupt. A interprets Fuller's eight conditions of legality—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradictoriness, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rule—as functional prerequisites for a system to actually guide conduct, not as moral constraints internal to the concept of law itself. If a system flouts them, it may collapse as an effective ordering mechanism, but that is an empirical failure of efficacy, not a conceptual failure of legality.

The other friend—B—rejects that framing. She argues that Fuller's principles constitute an internal morality of law because subjecting human conduct to governance through rules inherently commits the system to respecting human agency; conditions like clarity and non-retroactivity are not merely instrumental but are moral demands built into the very idea of ruling by law. B also presses what she sees as a tension in Hart’s own position: his concession of a minimum content of natural law, in which any viable legal system must include rules protecting persons and property for reasons of human survival, looks like a normative constraint smuggled into an allegedly value-free concept of law. If survival and minimal security are conceptually necessary, she asks, why can’t structural conditions like congruence or generality also be necessary, making Fuller's internal morality unavoidable?

They then tested these views against a hypothetical they invented together: a fully decentralized legal order enforced only by deterministic smart contracts, where rules are perfectly general, clear, non-retroactive, and stable, but the substantive rules encode a rigid hereditary caste hierarchy. A says this is clearly law—it has primary rules of obligation and a secondary rule of recognition embedded in the protocol—and that its moral horror is entirely external to its legal status. B replies that if the rules systematically degrade human dignity and if congruence is satisfied only in a mechanical, algorithmic sense without any human accountability, then the system lacks the moral dimension that distinguishes law from mere coercive programming; on Fuller's view, she claims, it should fail to qualify as law altogether, not merely be condemned as bad law.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each major step—A’s efficacy interpretation of Fuller's principles, B’s agency-based argument for internal morality, the challenge posed by Hart’s minimum content concession, and the smart-contract caste-system test case—rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say which position is more defensible overall, and identify what both are likely missing, such as whether Fuller's conditions are genuinely moral rather than functional, or whether conceptual failure and normative failure can be cleanly separated in cases of extreme systemic injustice. Keep your analysis tied to the arguments as actually made, not a general survey of the debate.
