Consider a philosopher, Dr. M., who applies David Lewis’s analysis of convention—arbitrary, mutually expected solutions to coordination problems sustained by preference for conformity—to classify social regularities. Her verdicts to date: driving on the right in a given country is a convention; addressing strangers with a formal pronoun in a particular language is a convention; the taboo against eating with one’s left hand in certain cultures is not, because conformity tracks religious purity rather than an arbitrary choice among equivalents; queuing for a bus is not, because each individual prefers to be first and the regularity is enforced by moral disapproval rather than by mutual expectation of matching behavior; and the American practice of tipping restaurant servers is a convention, because the percentage is arbitrary, widespread expectation sustains it, and deviation triggers social sanctions that are internal to the equilibrium rather than derived from an independent moral duty.

Now examine a novel regularity in a decentralized research network: contributors adhere to an unwritten protocol that if a dataset’s curator has not updated its documentation in exactly eighteen months, any researcher may publish a corrected version, but only after posting a standardized cryptographic hash of the original file in a designated public ledger. The eighteen-month window and the hash mechanism are plainly arbitrary—twelve months or a simple timestamp would serve the same function. Contributors generally prefer that everyone adhere to the protocol, they expect others to do so, and failure to post the hash results not in moral condemnation but in automatic exclusion from future collaborative access to the network, an exclusion that is itself anticipated and treated as part of the system’s normal operation.

Would Dr. M. classify this dataset-curation protocol as a Lewisian convention? Set out at least two distinct principles that could organize her earlier judgments—one holding that conventions must solve problems in which parties have symmetric preferences over matching regardless of which option is chosen, with sanctions that are merely the loss of coordination benefits; and one holding that conventions include arbitrary regularities sustained by mutual expectation and strategic interdependence even when preferences are asymmetric and sanctions involve institutional or network-based exclusion. State what each principle predicts about the protocol, give your best-guess verdict for Dr. M. with reasons, and propose one further hypothetical case that would force a decisive choice between the two principles.
