I’m putting together a syllabus for a grad seminar on migration and the welfare state — about 16 people, 80 minutes once a week. We’ve been stuck in the same loop where everyone just repeats open-borders rights talk against budget-constraint talk, and I want to break that with an in-class game that makes them feel something weirder: what I’m calling the “front-loaded welfare / back-loaded integration” trap. The idea is that welfare access is immediate upon admission but actual labor-market integration is delayed and uncertain, so immigrants with different hidden skill levels have strategic reasons to misrepresent how fast they’ll integrate, and welfare administrators, employers, and native taxpayers have to negotiate admission rules without knowing who’s who.

Can you design a structured role-play with rules, roles, rounds, and a payoff scheme using points or reputation instead of money? There should be four roles — immigrants (each gets a hidden “integration speed” card they can choose to reveal or not), adminstrators (who approve contracts with a fixed budget per round), employers (who hire based on expected productivity minus welfare costs), and taxpayers (who vote on admission thresholds after seeing outcomes). Over three 15-minute rounds, they negotiate individual integration contracts; if trust collapses and too many contracts fail, everyone lose points. The rules should be arranged so the group runs into the coordination failure themselves — probably in round two — before anyone names the paradox. Then include a debrief that maps the collapse onto the tension between universal welfare design and conditional belonging, says what will predictably go wrong the first time (someone will try to solve it with perfect information, which misses the point), and be honest about which feature of real-world integration dynamics the game can’t reproduce — maybe the emotional weight of leaving home, or the legal enforcement of deportation. No lecture up front, and please use “integration” not “assimilation” — I keep mixing those up in my notes. Also don’t ask them to memorize any obscure theorists beforehand; the setup should carry the whole thing.
