Write an essay on whether Scanlon’s contractualism can coherently accommodate the aggregation of small benefits and harms across large populations, or whether it is committed to rejecting any institutional policy that imposes a severe burden on one person even when it produces only minor benefits for thousands. Focus on the aggregation problem as it arises for a concrete policy: a city implements congestion pricing that saves thousands of commuters a small, unavoidable amount of daily time, but imposes a severe, unavoidable financial cost on a single low-income driver who must travel through the zone. Weigh the contractualist claim that reasonable rejection is assessed pairwise—each individual asks whether she could reject the principle governing the policy—against the criticism that this makes contractualism implausibly insensitive to numbers and to the scale of collective benefit. Consider whether a contractualist can allow “structural aggregation,” by treating widely distributed minor benefits as generating a collective reason that modifies what counts as reasonable rejection, without collapsing into a utilitarian social-welfare calculus or abandoning the core idea that justification proceeds between individuals and a principle. Reach and defend a verdict: either contractualism must accept a qualified, constrained form of aggregation and explain exactly what limits it, or the aggregation objection succeeds in showing that contractualism is unsuited to large-scale policy design. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
