Consider the philosopher of religion Dr. Elena Voss, who has offered explicit verdicts on the following five cases concerning divine middle knowledge and counterfactuals of creaturely freedom.

Case 1: A counterfactual about the actual person Alice freely choosing tea over coffee in circumstances that replicate her actual psychology except for one prior free decision. Voss holds that God possesses middle knowledge of this counterfactual; it is true pre-volitionally and does not depend on God's will.

Case 2: The identical counterfactual about Alice, but situated in a world with deterministic physical laws in which her neural states fully determine the choice. Voss holds that God lacks middle knowledge here; without a libertarian-free framework the counterfactual is not available to the middle-knowledge mode.

Case 3: A counterfactual about the merely possible person Bob—who never exists in any world God actualizes—freely choosing tea in circumstances that mirror Alice's actual ones. Voss holds that God lacks middle knowledge of this; such knowledge belongs only to God's natural knowledge of possible worlds.

Case 4: A counterfactual about an actual deliberative committee of five libertarian-free individuals reaching a unanimous joint decision. Voss holds that God possesses middle knowledge of the collective outcome, but not distributively of each member's private vote taken in isolation.

Case 5: A counterfactual about Alice being fully informed of God's middle knowledge of her choice (a foreknowledge-disclosure scenario) and then freely choosing tea. Voss holds that God still possesses middle knowledge that she would choose tea; because middle knowledge is non-causal, disclosure does not undermine freedom.

Now consider Case 6: A counterfactual about a unified agency composed of Alice (actual) and Carol (merely possible, never actualized), who share a single conscious decision-system in an indeterministic world and must both assent to reach a joint decision to choose tea. The framework preserves libertarian freedom at the system level, yet one constituent is not actual.

Predict Voss's verdict on Case 6, specifying whether she would attribute middle knowledge to (i) the joint decision, (ii) Alice's component assent taken separately, (iii) both, or (iv) neither. Then identify the principle or principles she appears to be running across Cases 1–5 that yield her pattern of verdicts, noting at least one alternative principle that accommodates Cases 1–5 equally well but diverges on Case 6. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the leading principle and its closest competitor, explaining exactly what verdict on that case each principle predicts.
