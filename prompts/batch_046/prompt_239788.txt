As a journalist who covers the history of science and urban policy, I have grown accustomed to treating path dependence as an abstract framework; now, serving on the city's Landmark Preservation and Adaptive Reuse Commission, I must apply it to a concrete vote due by Thursday. At our session last Tuesday, the commission deadlocked over whether to authorize demolition of the Eastbank Rail Junction, a freight interchange constructed in 1938 not according to engineering optimality but because of a contingent political deal that secured federal funding only if the line bypassed the technically superior Westbank corridor. Proponents of demolition argue that since the junction's origin was arbitrary—what philosophers call first-order path dependence—there is no intrinsic obligation to preserve an accident of history; opponents reply that eighty years of network effects, freight-routing algorithms, and property-value clustering have produced second-order path dependence, locking the region into a trajectory that is now functionally necessary regardless of its arbitrary genesis. Before Thursday's meeting, I need a short written opinion to circulate: it should recommend a specific course—approve, deny, or approve with conditions and a clear time frame—while treating both positions with intellectual seriousness rather than dismissing either as mere sentiment. In particular, please examine whether recognizing the distinction between first-order contingency and second-order reinforcement actually supports preservation, or whether it reveals that the lock-in is itself a revisable artifact rather than an iron law. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of the question; keep the tone formal and suitable for a deliberative body.
