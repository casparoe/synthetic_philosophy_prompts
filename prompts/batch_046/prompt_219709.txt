Consider an institutional AI-safety protocol in which every deployed model must generate responses twice: first in an unfiltered "candid" mode, then in a "published" mode monitored by an adversarial module that can veto or rewrite sentences without seeing the original user prompt or explaining its decision to anyone except a non-technical oversight board that reviews logs monthly. What philosophical commitments about agency, knowledge, and blame does this architecture presuppose? Does it treat misalignment as a feature of isolated propositions, of global reasoning patterns, or of the model's enduring "character"? Is the split between candid and published selves consistent with a unitary theory of agency, or does it implicitly rely on a divided-self model? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology—especially regarding what counts as evidence that the model is misaligned—distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its underlying view with a straightforward instrumental-rationality framework that treats the model as a single transparent utility-maximizer.
