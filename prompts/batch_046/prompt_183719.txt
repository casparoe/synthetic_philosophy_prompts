I have come to defend a domain-calibrated version of virtue ethics against the situationist critique. Rather than treating virtues as global, cross-situational character traits, I hold that they are institutional-frame-sensitive practical sensibilities: the same agent can genuinely possess the virtue of honesty in intimate or collaborative contexts while failing to display it in high-pressure competitive or authoritarian settings, because practical wisdom calibrates moral responsiveness differently across these frames. On this view, low cross-situational consistency is not evidence against virtue but evidence that virtues are locally calibrated; the classic obedience and conformity findings show frame-dependence rather than the absence of stable character, and within-domain coherence should remain high even where between-domain variance is large.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this calibrated-virtue position—not merely the standard charge that it fragments personal identity or collapses into pure situationism, but which specific empirical patterns, which conceptual distinctions the calibration model depends upon, and which rival theories (a unified global-trait account, a pure situationist account, or an institutional-role account) it risks being indistinguishable from. Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining the calibration conditions or tightening the domain boundaries? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, consider whether the view can be qualified so extensively that no behavioral pattern could falsify it, and whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a debilitating vice. I want a precise map of the position's real exposure: what it risks, where its weakest joints are, and whether it has already been rendered unfalsifiable at the cost of explanatory power.
