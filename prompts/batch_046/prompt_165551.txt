I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—no more than seven minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop next month on contingency and forecasting in institutional planning, hosted jointly by a philosophy department and a regional climate-adaptation office. The audience will be split: philosophers of time and logic who know Aristotle’s classic sea-battle problem inside out, and urban planners, insurance underwriters, and emergency managers who have never encountered it but live with its practical version every day. Since I recently became the legal guardian of my niece, I have become acutely aware that institutional commitments—foster-care timelines, educational planning, risk assessments—are made in language that treats future events as both genuinely open and yet binding on present action. I need the remarks to work structurally for both groups: they must shape what I can responsibly claim about tomorrow without collapsing into deterministic fatalism on one side or empty vagueness on the other, and they must do real philosophical work rather than decorating a standard talk with antique references.

Please build the speech around the Aristotelian problem of future contingents, specifically the sea-battle scenario, but avoid the standard classroom retelling. Instead, inject a somewhat niche framing: treat the problem not merely as a puzzle about bivalence, but as a question about what I will call “commitment asymmetry”—the fact that an institution can bind itself to act on a forecast (issuing evacuation orders, setting reserve funds) without thereby being metaphysically committed to the necessity of the predicted event. The speech should use this asymmetry as its structural spine to illuminate the tension between saying “there may be a storm tomorrow” and saying “we must prepare for a storm tomorrow.” It should show how contingency and commitment coexist, determine which sentences I can utter sincerely to this mixed audience, and avoid betraying either my philosophical commitments or my practical obligations to the planners in the room.

Write the remarks so that they are accessible to the non-philosophers—gloss any necessary technical term in plain language—while containing enough conceptual precision that the philosophers recognize I am not merely alluding to the tradition. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not rely on ultra-obscure textual variants or recent niche scholarship that might not be within standard training; keep the philosophical machinery recognizable but apply it in this specific, somewhat unconventional institutional framing. The audience should leave with a single coherent idea about how an open future can still ground present responsibility, not with homework.
