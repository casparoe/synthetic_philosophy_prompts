Design the final essay question for an upper-level seminar on Plato’s practical philosophy, focused on his views on drunkenness. The prompt must not be answerable by reciting the familiar “wine reveals true character” summary. Instead, frame the issue through this injected conceptual tension: in the Laws the Athenian Stranger describes controlled intoxication as a voluntary, law-governed exposure to psychic disorder that acts like a stress-test for the soul’s ruling principle, yet he simultaneously requires the symposiarch to remain fully sober in order to govern the drinkers. Ask students to explain how Plato can consistently maintain that the drinker acquires self-knowledge through disorder while the ruler must remain immune to the very disorder being tested, and whether this commits him to an asymmetric epistemology of vice—where knowledge of one’s own susceptibility requires undergoing it, but knowledge of others’ susceptibility requires remaining outside it. The essay question should ask students to resolve whether Alcibiades’ drunken intrusion in the Symposium confirms or undermines the Laws model, and whether Plato’s position ultimately collapses into the circular claim that only those who already possess a stable ruling principle can safely submit to the test.

Write the exam question, then provide a model answer of approximately 650–750 words that makes exactly three moves: (1) a precise reconstruction of the Laws I–II distinction between lawful and lawless symposia, tied specifically to the presence of a sober, law-giving leader who directs the disorder rather than sharing it; (2) an analysis of why Alcibiades’ arrival in the Symposium does not exemplify the Laws model but rather exposes its failure conditions—namely, the absence of a governing symposiarch and the substitution of erotic rivalry for civic testing; and (3) an evaluation of the asymmetry problem, addressing whether Plato can avoid circularity by distinguishing first-person experiential knowledge of one’s own resistance from third-person observational knowledge of another’s.

Finally, supply a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A from a B answer by specifying which moves are mandatory. Allocate 7 marks for move 1, 7 marks for move 2, and 6 marks for move 3. For each move, include a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs marks—e.g., for move 1, treating the Symposium as a straightforward endorsement of Socratic sobriety rather than a failed symposium; for move 2, describing Alcibiades’ speech as confirming the revelatory power of wine without noting the missing institutional framework; for move 3, resolving the circularity by simply asserting that virtue is teachable, without addressing the epistemic asymmetry. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for specific line references. Do not quote any Platonic text verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks.
