I'm drafting a short essay for an advanced philosophy of science seminar that compares two inversions of reasoning—Darwin's and Turing's—and I'm worried I'm either reinventing a well-worn comparison or missing an obvious asymmetry that undermines the whole framing. The core claim is that Darwin showed how biological competence, in the form of complex adaptation, could arise without any comprehensive understanding of the end state, since natural selection operates blindly on local variation without foresight or representation of the goal. Turing, in turn, showed how cognitive competence—problem-solving, symbol manipulation, even language use—could emerge from purely syntactic procedures without any semantic comprehension of what the symbols represent. I want to argue that these are not merely analogies but structurally parallel instances of competence without comprehension, where the mechanism achieves its results precisely because it lacks the global, self-transparent understanding that a human designer or reasoner would ordinarily require.

To push this beyond the familiar Chinese Room discussion, I inject a more specific twist: I propose that both cases depend on a selectionist logic in which error-correction is outsourced rather than internal—environmental death in evolution, formal verification or feedback-driven training in computation—so that the system never needs to grasp why its moves are correct, only that they survive or converge. From there, I ask whether modern large-scale language models represent a genuine synthesis of these two inversions—Darwinian selection operating over the training data and weights, combined with Turing-style syntactic processing at inference—or whether they instead smuggle comprehension back in through the human-curated architecture and loss functions. I'd appreciate targeted comments on whether this parallel holds up philosophically, where the argument is likely to collapse into obvious territory, and whether my selectionist framing clarifies the comparison or obscures deep differences between evolutionary populations and individual computing systems. I'm not asking for citations, a reading list, or verification of specific publication details—just feedback on accuracy, structural coherence, and whether the central claim is genuinely interesting rather than plainly obvious to anyone familiar with both thinkers.
