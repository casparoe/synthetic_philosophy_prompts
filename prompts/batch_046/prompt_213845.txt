I am writing from an advanced philosophy of logic course in which we have been examining David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. As I understand the argument, if one assumes that the rational credence in an indicative conditional "if A, then C" equals the corresponding conditional probability P(C|A)—the thesis often associated with Ernest Adams—together with certain minimal constraints on belief revision, then one is driven to the conclusion that either all such conditionals have extreme probabilities or that all propositions become probabilistically independent. This result strikes me as having direct implications for practical reasoning: in decision-theoretic contexts we constantly evaluate options by assigning intermediate credences to indicative conditionals, such as the belief that if I accept the research fellowship, then my long-term career prospects will improve.

My difficulty is this: if Lewis's derivation is sound, it appears that maintaining such intermediate credences is probabilistically incoherent. Therefore, must rational decision theory either abandon indicative conditionals in favor of material conditionals or reject probabilism altogether? Or does the argument depend on a specific, contestable assumption—perhaps that an agent's belief set is closed under logical consequence, or that conditional probabilities are defined for all pairs of propositions—that a decision theorist can deny without cost?

Please separate what is settled in the philosophical logic literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and avoid presenting any particular resolution—whether that Lewis's result is a decisive refutation of probabilistic semantics for conditionals, or that it is a mere technical artifact with no bearing on rational choice—as if it were the consensus.
