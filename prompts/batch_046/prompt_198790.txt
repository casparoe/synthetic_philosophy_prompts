I've been having this arguement with my flatmates about an app I'm tinkering with in my spare time — I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy background, just trying to automate our shared grocery orders and bill splitting. But I got a bit carried away and added a feature where it doesn't just record what everyone says they want right now; it runs a pairwise consistency check over your choices from the last six months, and if two preferences contradict, it keeps the one with the higher 'stability weight' and treats that as your real preference. So if I say I want to buy crisps every day but my budget logs show I'm saving for a trip to Japan, the app decides the Japan trip is the coherrent me and quietly nudges me away from the crisps. My flatmate Sarah, who studies politics, reckons this is just manipulation dressed up as logic — she says I'm not resolving a contradiction, I'm picking the version of me I like best and calling it authentic. I think she's overreacting, but I genuinely can't see the principled line between helping someone be consistent and imposing some idealised version of them they never actually signed up for. The weirder part is applying it to myself: if I'm deciding whether to stay in and code tonight or go to a friend's birthday, should I trust the immediate impulse, or the preference that survives my own consistency check? What's actually at stake here philosophically? Is there a real difference between legitimate persuasion and this kind of preference-overwriting, or is any system that tries to 'extrapolate' what you'd want just sneaky coercion with extra steps?
