As a novelist constructing a long-form narrative in which an artificial being occupies the moral and dramatic center of the plot, I need a philosophical distinction sharp enough to sustain ambiguity without collapsing into either naive anthropomorphism or reductive mechanism. To that end, I have been working through Daniel Dennett's conceptual apparatus, and I want a rigorous comparison of two ideas that seem to pull in different directions. First, there is his notion of counterfeit people—agents that replicate every observable behavioral, linguistic, and functional marker of human consciousness yet possess no genuine phenomenal experience, no moral standing, and no interior life worth respecting. Second, there is his broader framework of competence without comprehension, developed to describe systems that generate highly appropriate, structurally complex behavior (or, by extension, fluent linguistic output) in the absence of understanding, belief, or intentional content of the kind we attribute to mature humans. I need you to compare these two concepts not merely in outline, but in their underlying criteria for attributing mind, agency, and moral status.

Specifically, address the following: Does Dennett's counterfeit-person scenario depend on the possibility of perfect behavioral duplication, and if so, does his insistence that competence can be decoupled from comprehension undermine or reinforce the plausibility of ever constructing such a duplicate? How does each concept interact with his rejection of a Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness—does the absence of a global workspace make counterfeit people easier to imagine, or does it dissolve the very distinction between real and counterfeit interiority? Furthermore, weigh whether these two positions are ultimately compatible within the intentional stance framework, or whether they generate conflicting prescriptions for how we ought to treat synthetic agents that perform every human-like function. Reach and defend a clear conclusion about which conception should govern our evaluation of artificial minds, particularly in contexts—such as narrative fiction—where the attribution of consciousness carries both ethical and aesthetic weight. Write with formal philosophical precision, avoid bibliographic apparatus or citation requests, and treat the problem as one that must be resolved from first principles rather than by reference to current technological products.
