I've been arguing with my roommate about whether being direct is actually kinder, and it made me think about school feedback across cultures. In Dutch secondary schools there's this pedagogical norm where teachers are supposed to state errors plainly - like, "this thesis is wrong and here's exactly why" - with no cushioning language, because softening it is seen as treating the student like they can't handle reality. Meanwhile in Japanese schools the same mistake gets handled through indirect hints, sometimes via a peer or framing it as a shared problem, drawing on that honne and tatemae distinction and the idea of kuuki wo yomu. I was discussing with my friends whether these are just etiquette differences or whether they preseuppose totally incompatible philosophies of what respect even means.

So here's what I'm actually trying to figure out: if you treat these two institutional feedback practices as philosophical data, what do they each quietly commit to about autonomy, dignity, and the ethics of truth-telling? Does the Dutch practice depend on a view that respecting someone means treating them as a fully rational agent who can absorb unvarnished truth without harm? Does the Japanese practice depend on seeing autonomy as socially embedded, so that protecting someone's face is actually protecting their capacity to act in the world? Or can both practices be explained by the same underlying moral psychology, just with different weights assigned to harm versus honesty? And is there an internal contradiction if a single school tried to run both protocols simultaneously, or are they merely practically incompatible?

Also I keep wondering about white lies in this frame: if a Dutch teacher refuses to lie about quality and a Japanese teacher uses indirectness to avoid explicit falsehood, are they both avoiding deception, or is indirectness just a more socially palatable version of the same white lie?
