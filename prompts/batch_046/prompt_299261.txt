Exam question:
In Sen's liberal paradox, minimal liberty and the Pareto principle come into conflict over the allocation of a single indivisible entitlement—the right to read a particular controversial novel—between two agents, A and B. The four possible social states are:
(i) A reads and B does not;
(ii) B reads and A does not;
(iii) both read;
(iv) neither reads.

The agents have the following preference orderings:
A: (i) ≻ (iv) ≻ (iii) ≻ (ii)
B: (ii) ≻ (iv) ≻ (iii) ≻ (i)

The principle of minimal liberty (ML) grants each agent decisive authority over the pair of alternatives that differ only in that agent's own consumption, holding the other agent's consumption fixed. The Pareto principle requires that if every agent strictly prefers x to y, then society must not choose y over x.

(a) Show precisely why ML and Pareto conflict in this profile. Identify the state that ML selects and the state that Pareto selects, and explain the logical steps.

(b) Evaluate the claim that this conflict can be dissolved by combining two moves: (1) adopting a choice-based rather than preference-based aggregation framework, in which only revealed binary choices from restricted menus enter the social rule, not full orderings over all four states; and (2) applying a coherent extrapolated volition filter that replaces each agent's stated preferences with an idealised version in which strategic, status-seeking, or externally imposed distortions have been removed. Would these two moves jointly eliminate the tension? If not, where exactly does the conflict persist?

Student's answer:
Part (a): For agent A, minimal liberty applies to the pair of states that differ only in A's own consumption, with B's status held fixed. Holding B at does not read, the relevant pair is (i) versus (iv). Since A ranks (i) above (iv), ML requires society to prefer (i) to (iv). For B, holding A at does not read, the pair is (ii) versus (iv). B ranks (ii) above (iv), so ML requires (ii) over (iv). Applying both conditions jointly yields state (iii), in which both read. By contrast, both agents place (iv) strictly above (iii): A has (iv) ≻ (iii) and B has (iv) ≻ (iii). The Pareto principle therefore demands (iv) ≻ (iii). Consequently, ML selects (iii) while Pareto selects (iv), producing the conflict.

Part (b): The paradox can be dissolved by adopting a choice-based aggregation rule together with a coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) filter. In a choice-based framework, we record only binary revealed choices from restricted menus rather than full preference orderings over all four states. Because A's decisive choice is observed only within his personal sphere {i, iv}, and B's only within {ii, iv}, there is no menu on which both agents jointly reveal a preference between (iii) and (iv). Without that joint revelation, the Pareto comparison across the full profile is blocked—much as Arrow's impossibility result depends on the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, which is violated once we restrict attention to sphere-local menus.

Applying CEV then purifies each agent's underlying volition. A's preference for (i) likely reflects a reflective commitment to autonomy rather than a desire for the specific text, while B's ranking of (ii) over (i) appears driven by strategic status-competition rather than by B's own idealised values. Once these distortions are removed, both agents' extrapolated preferences converge on (iv): neither reads, but each retains full authority over their own sphere. Thus, ML applies to the purified binary choices—A chooses autonomy-with-reading, B chooses autonomy-without-reading—without jointly entailing (iii), because the spheres no longer overlap in their social implications. The Pareto principle, now operating only on the convergent idealised orderings, also favours (iv). Hence the tension is an artefact of using unfiltered, fully ordered preference profiles in a preference-based framework, not a deep failure of liberal aggregation.
