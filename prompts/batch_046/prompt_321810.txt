After a long argument last night with a friend who insists that clinical neuroscience must presuppose a single, unified screen of consciousness in order to detect intraoperative awareness, I need to circulate a brief opinion before Friday's meeting of our hospital's Clinical Neuroethics Review Board. We are evaluating a proposed protocol that aims to identify the precise millisecond at which an anesthetized patient enters awareness by correlating EEG signatures with a reported subjective now. The investigators defend their framework by appealing to what Daniel Dennett calls the Cartesian theater: they assume there is a functional center where sensory inputs are presented to a unitary observer, so that awareness can be treated as an all-or-nothing event with a determinate temporal boundary.

My view, shaped by Dennett's multiple drafts model, is that this assumption vitiates the protocol's validity. Consciousness, on that account, is not a single narrative stream playing in a theater but a distributed, parallel process in which multiple interpretations of stimuli are continuously revised; there is no one place or time where it all comes together, and therefore no unique millisecond of entry into awareness that can be captured by a single EEG marker.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame—approval with conditions, deferral for conceptual revision, or rejection—and it should treat the investigators' Cartesian-theater defense seriously rather than dismissing it as obsolete. In particular, address the strongest response available to them: that even if consciousness is distributed, clinical decision-making requires a pragmatic, operationalized threshold, and that treating awareness as a graded, multi-track phenomenon makes the protocol unworkable rather than wrong. The reasoning should be accessible to clinicians on the board who are not philosophers, but it should not oversimplify the conceptual dispute.
