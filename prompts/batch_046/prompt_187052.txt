Institutional frameworks for intergenerational justice—whether in climate-economy models that aggregate welfare across centuries, in statutory requirements that public bodies justify long-term impacts, or in the appointment of commissioners and ombudsmen charged with representing people not yet born—routinely treat “future generations” as a fixed class of stakeholders who can be made better or worse off by present choices. Yet the policies being evaluated—emission pathways, resource-extraction schedules, migration and settlement timelines, and the temporal structure of economic growth—causally determine which specific individuals will be conceived, born, and live in those future periods. The practice therefore operates as if there is a determinate group of future people with moral standing, whose interests can be compared across policy worlds, despite the fact that altering the policy alters the very membership of that group.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity, harm, and moral standing does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a non-person-affecting account of intergenerational harm, a person-affecting view supplemented by some principle of identity-independent welfare, or does it simply treat demographic composition as exogenous to ethical evaluation? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it comfortably. Is it compatible with the reasoning that makes the nonidentity problem acute, or does it quietly presuppose that future individuals have a kind of pre-political existence? Finally, if one took the contingency of identity seriously—accepting that a policy cannot harm someone whose existence it brings about—what would have to change in the design of these institutions? Would they collapse into purely present-person-affecting evaluation, or could they be rebuilt around some other normative value, such as the quality of lives lived regardless of who lives them?
