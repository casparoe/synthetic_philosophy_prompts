I was discussin this with my writing group and a friend walked me into an argument that now feels like a locked room with no door. Here is exactly how it runs:

1. Kant holds that lasting peace needs every state to adopt a republican constitution and enter a federation that honors cosmopolitan rights.
2. In my story the diplomat invents something he calls the "silent federation clause" — the idea that members owe no tolerance to outsiders who reject cosmopolitan rights by refusing to join.
3. So if a state stays non-republican, it stands outside the federation and, by that clause, is not owed any forbearance.
4. That puts it in a state of nature with respect to the members, which means the federation has a right of self-preservation against it.
5. Mere isolation can't remove the threat, since the outsider still holds territory and armies; the danger is structural, not temporary.
6. Therefore the federation is justified in forcing republican reform on the outsider — even by war — to eliminate the source of insecurity.
7. But that turns perpetual peace into a license for imperial conquest, and Kant clearly hated that.
8. So one of the steps has to collapse, yet they all feel like straight readings of the premisses.

I can't see the exit. Which step should I actually reject, and what does giving it up do to the rest of the picture? Does saving Kant from imperialism break his security argument?
