I've been arguing with my roommate about welfare rules and I realised my gut reactions are all over the place, so I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, actually captures them, and which ones I'm just wrong about.

Here are my calls. Requiring people on unemployment benefits to submit proof they applied to five jobs a week feels mostly okay, even sensible. But making disability benefits depend on periodic reassessment by private contractors who get paid per denial feels totally wrong, like the system is designed to fail people. A workfare program where you do actual park maintenance in your own neighborhood feels okay if the work is genuinely useful, but if it's just made-up busywork designed to humiliate people then it feels just as bad as the contractor scam. Automatically cutting benefits for missing one appointment, with no human appeal until next month, feels wrong even when the appointment was for mandatory job training. Using data-matching algorithims to flag welfare recipients for fraud investigation based on spending patterns feels wrong too, even though I know real fraud exists, because it treats every recipient as a suspect first. But then there's this idea I keep coming back to - a social contribution account where people earn credits through paid work, caregiving, volunteering, whatever, and can draw unemployment support based on accumulated credits rather than constant job-search proof. That feels more fair, but I can't tell if it's genuinely different or just the same conditionality with nicer branding.

So is there a principle here? Maybe something about whether the condition respects the recipient's agency, or whether failure is corrected versus punished? I also keep going back and forth on one case: telling my friend they should take a demeaning workfare job right now because they need the rent money, even though I just said those programs are bad. Does any principle cover both the system-level judgment and that individual piece of advice, or do I just have to drop one? If there is a principle, what does it predict for something I haven't listed - say, requiring people to attend financial literacy classes before they can recieve benefits? And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save without getting too complicated.
