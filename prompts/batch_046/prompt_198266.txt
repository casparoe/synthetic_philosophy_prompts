I'm taking a course on practical reason and rational choice theory, and after a seminar discussion about the sunk cost fallacy my study partner and I tried to push the standard argument to its logical extreme. We constructed the following chain and now find the conclusion intolerable, but we cannot locate the precise misstep:

(1) Rational decision-making should depend only on future costs and benefits; resources that have already been expended and cannot be recovered are irrelevant to the evaluation of present options.
(2) This principle applies not only to monetary outlays but to any irreversible investment—time, emotional energy, social commitments, or identity-constituting projects.
(3) Therefore, if I have spent ten years in a career I now find meaningless, the rational choice is to leave immediately, giving zero weight to the years already invested.
(4) By parallel reasoning, if I have made a promise and invested considerable effort in keeping it, but fulfilling the remainder is burdensome and yields little future benefit, I should break the promise, since the past effort is sunk.
(5) More radically, my present values and long-term plans are themselves products of past investments of attention and commitment; treating them as authoritative reasons for present action is therefore just another form of honoring sunk costs.
(6) Hence rationality requires complete temporal neutrality: I ought to regard my past self's commitments, promises, and projects as having no special authority over my present choices.
(7) But complete temporal neutrality makes long-term planning incoherent—any plan requires that I treat my earlier commitment to that plan as binding, which is precisely honoring a sunk investment.
(8) So if (1) is correct, rational agency across time is impossible, which is absurd.

We are inclined to preserve (1) and suspect that (5) involves an equivocation between different senses of investment, yet we worry that rejecting (5) forces us to reintroduce past-dependence into rational choice in an ad hoc way. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should we reject, what does giving it up cost in terms of consistency with the core anti-sunk-cost principle, and is there a principled distinction between the career and promise cases in (3) and (4) and genuine sunk-cost errors—one that does not simply smuggle back in the very temporal continuity we are trying to eliminate?
