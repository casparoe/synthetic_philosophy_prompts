I am a high school junior in an advanced comparative-history seminar, and over the past month our discussions of the Great Divergence have kept returning to the same methodological frustration: every time someone asks "Why Britain and not China?"—the classic formulation of the Needham question—the room splits between those who treat it as a genuine causal inquiry and those who dismiss it as a presentist framing that assumes Britain was the natural endpoint of progress. My instructor suggested that the problem is not the comparison itself but the failure to distinguish between a properly contrastive explanation, which isolates a specific mechanism present in one case and absent or blocked in the other, and a "holistic counterfactual" that imagines an entire alternative trajectory without identifying any single causal lever. Rather than continue arguing in circles, I want a practical evaluation framework I can actually run with my debate-preparation team.

Please construct a reusable, four-stage decision procedure—presented as an ordered checklist with explicit stop conditions and clearly marked judgment points—that a student researcher could apply repeatedly to any macrohistorical "Why Region A and not Region B?" claim. Stage one should define the contrast class and run an anachronism audit: do the two regions share enough institutional and technological background to make the comparison meaningful, or does the framing import modern categories? Stage two should isolate a candidate mechanism and apply what I will call a substitution test: if the mechanism is hypothetically inserted into Region B with all other background conditions held constant, does it plausibly produce the outcome observed in Region A? Stage three should assess modal fragility—whether sustaining the counterfactual requires altering so many background conditions that the comparison collapses into fiction rather than history. Stage four should render a verdict: well-posed with isolable mechanism; well-posed but mechanism-dependent on contested judgment; malformed contrast class; or fragile counterfactual that fails the substitution test. At each stage, specify the passing criterion, the failing criterion, and the exact point where subjective evaluation is unavoidable rather than rule-governed. Then apply the full procedure to two worked cases: first, the coal-geography and property-rights explanation for Britain versus Qing China; second, my own constructed comparison, "Why did the Dutch Republic achieve sustained per-capita growth before mechanized industrialization while the Spanish Empire did not?"—a case where I expect the procedure to return a verdict I will dislike, namely that the contrast class is economically incoherent because the Dutch and Spanish cases operate at incompatible scales of fiscal extraction and imperial obligation. Include at least one explicit example of each unavoidable judgment call, and flag where the procedure will misfire or produce false negatives for genuinely interesting but methodologically messy questions. The deliverable must be fully usable without web access or external references, must contain no bibliography, citation list, or request for further reading, and should be honest about its own limits.
