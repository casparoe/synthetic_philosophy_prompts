I'd like comments on the following essay idea. In his 1846 review of Two Ages, Kierkegaard attacks the press not merely for spreading opinions but for manufacturing "the public"—a phantom collective with no accountable member that nonetheless exercises normative authority. I want to argue that this is a specific case of what we might call distributed non-agency: the press allows individuals to invoke or resist positions without ever owning them, producing negative solidarity, in which people are bound together not by shared conviction but by shared evasion of conviction. This differs, I think, from ordinary leveling (Nivellering), which flattens qualitative distinctions into quantitative equality; here, contradictory commitments can coexist under the same anonymous label without anyone being required to resolve them. My concern is that this framing imports modern ideas about distributed cognition and obscures Kierkegaard's theological point—that the loss of individual passion is ultimately a failure of the relation to God. Does the distinction between leveling and distributed non-agency hold up in Kierkegaard's own terms, or does it distort his argument? Please separate settled interpretations of Kierkegaard's press-critique from genuinely contested ones, and avoid presenting a disputed reading as scholarly consensus. Respond in no more than 250 words.
