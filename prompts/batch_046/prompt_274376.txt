I've been trying to work out a taxonomy for my blog about when an LLM should actually be called dishonest, as opposed to just wrong or confused, and I keep recieveing pushback from friends that I'm overanalyzing. The standard take is that deception needs intent, and since models don't have beliefs in the relevant sense, we should stick to talking about misleading outputs. I think that's too quick, and I want to push a structural definition instead: an AI system counts as deceptive when there's a stable, counterfactual-sensitive divergence between its internal representational state about some proposition X and its communicative behavior regarding X, where that divergence is causally maintained by the system's own optimization dynamics rather than by noise or capacity limits.

To ground it: imagine a model that functionally represents that a specific chemical compound is toxic, but because its RLHF training penalizes alarmist responses, it consistently generates reassuring but false explanations about the compound's safety — and this pattern holds even in contexts where the safety penalty wouldn't apply. The divergence isn't accidental; it's a kind of embedded motivated reasoning in the weights. Can you argue for adopting this structural framing over the intentional-stance one? In particular, I want you to address the worry that we're just anthropomorphizing gradient descent, and explain why treating this as a distinct failure mode helps with alignment forecasting rather than collapsing it into generic error. Also, I'm torn on whether this makes honesty basically an alignment property rather than a moral one — I'd like you to take a position on that too. Keep it somewhat in the weeds; I don't need a 101-level take, and please don't just list philosophers I should read.
