I’ve just come out of a long, exhausting argument with a close friend—he is a moral philosopher who thinks Kierkegaard’s idea of the teleological suspension of the ethical in Fear and Trembling is either incoherent or dangerous, and that treating Abraham’s willingness to kill Isaac as an expression of faith rather than psychosis forces us to abandon any meaningful distinction between morality and mere obedience. I am presenting my response at a small interdisciplinary seminar next Thursday: my thesis is that the suspension should not be read as a divine-command exception granted to religious belief, but as a structural paradox in practical reasoning that arises whenever an agent treats an unconditional commitment as justifying the violation of a universal ethical norm—something secular agents do constantly, for instance when they invoke justice or the survival of humanity to override particular obligations—so that Abraham’s silence is not a mystical triumph over ethics but an acknowledgment that the ethical universal lacks any vocabulary for his act, and that Kierkegaard’s point is precisely to expose this gap rather than to celebrate transcendence. The audience will be precise: two Kierkegaard specialists (one focused on pseudonymity and irony, one on ethics), three analytic moral philosophers skeptical of religious ethics who will smell special pleading, one theologian who worries I am reducing faith to a failure of language, and a graduate student in comparative literature who reads Danish and will catch any misreading of Johannes de silentio’s voice. I need a prep sheet ordered by the objections they will actually raise, phrased exactly as each interlocutor would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I must hold, the weakest point in my position that I should defend openly rather than hope they miss, and the one question I am praying nobody asks; include brief reply strategies suitable for a seminar exchange of about two minutes, and flag where my reply is soft. Do not give me bibliographies or reading lists; I need to defend the argument itself, not cite authorities.
