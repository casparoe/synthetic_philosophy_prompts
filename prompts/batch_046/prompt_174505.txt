I am drafting a short essay arguing that Dennett's multiple drafts model does not so much demolish the Cartesian theatre as fragment it. Rather than one homunculus watching a single screen in the pineal gland, split-brain subjects seem to host competing editorial boards, each treating its own narrative draft as the authoritative show. I propose that we should distinguish spatial projection — the classic mistake of placing consciousness in one anatomical spot — from temporal projection, the illusion that a unified "now" is being witnessed by a single audience even when drafts are stitched together post hoc. On this view, the error of Cartesianism is not theatricality per se, but the assumption that theatricality requires a singular stage and a singular spectator. The anti-theatrical argument therefore needs to target temporal coherence, not merely spatial localisation; otherwise we are left with what I call "polycentric theatres" — distributed, transient, but still projectionist in treating local drafts as definitive. I also suggest that this framing avoids reviving Cartesian dualism, since the "audience" in each hemisphere is just another draft-level process, not an immaterial observer.

Please comment on whether this argument is accurate to Dennett's actual position, whether the distinction between spatial and temporal projection is coherent, and whether "polycentric theatres" is a useful conceptual injection or merely a rebranding of well-known split-brain phenomena. I am particularly interested in whether the claim that anti-theatrical arguments must target temporal coherence survives scrutiny, or whether Dennett's resources already address it implicitly.
