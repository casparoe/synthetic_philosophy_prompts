A synthetic world-history textbook board has issued the following rulings on proposed narrative chapters: (1) a framing of the 12th-century intellectual revival as a uniquely European recovery of classical knowledge was rejected in favor of co-equal treatments of Islamic, Chinese, and European revivals, with no "recovery" framing that implies Europe reclaimed a universal heritage; (2) a chapter on the Columbian Exchange was approved only after American ecological transformation was given expanded space on account of its irreversible global biological impact, provided European agency was not presented as the sole driver; (3) a proposal to treat modern nation-state formation as a European invention diffused globally was rejected outright, with Japanese, Chinese, and Ottoman state-building required to be presented as endogenous processes not measured against a European template; (4) a chapter on 13th–15th-century mathematical astronomy was approved with parallel Islamic, Chinese, Indian, and European traditions, but with additional pages for the European tradition specifically to explain its contingent integration into later global navigation and colonial administration—not as fulfillment of an inherent trajectory. The board now considers a proposal for a chapter on 16th-century maritime navigation structured around four parallel centers—Portuguese, Ottoman, Ming Chinese, and Polynesian—each allocated exactly one-quarter of the text. The proposal refuses to label any technique as "precursory" to modern global navigation, and it deliberately omits any account of how these techniques were subsequently woven into European-led oceanic networks, arguing that such inclusion imposes a retrospective teleology. Recommend the verdict that best coheres with the board's own precedents: identify the closest analog among the four rulings, articulate the principle the board would be adopting whether it approves or demands revision, and set out at least two competing rules that jointly explain its track record but diverge on this case. Give your best prediction of the actual verdict with reasons, and then propose one further hypothetical chapter proposal that would cleanly distinguish the competing rules.
