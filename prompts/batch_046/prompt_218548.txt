At a faculty seminar, two philosophers of mind, Lena and Marcus, argued about Dr. R., a prominent sociologist who has long defended methodological individualism and recently published a sharp polemic against structural explanation—just months before his department was spared from merging into a larger structural-studies unit. Lena claimed Dr. R. is clearly self-deceived: he genuinely believes his arguments are driven only by intellectual merit, but an unconscious desire to protect his department’s autonomy biases his reading of evidence, and he is systematically blind to that connection. Marcus replied that the description is incoherent. For self-deception to occur, he said, a single subject must both hold the belief that structural explanations fail and know that his real motive is departmental survival, yet hide the latter from himself; but no one can simultaneously know and not know the same thing. If Dr. R. genuinely lacks access to his bias, Marcus continued, he is not deceiving himself—he is merely mistaken or subject to ordinary cognitive bias. Lena answered with a division model: the mind is not unified; one subsystem regulates belief and behavior around pure intellectual motives, while another tracks the strategic value of the polemic and suppresses its access to the first. There is no single point of consciousness that holds both beliefs. Marcus countered that this merely relocates the paradox: if one part knows the truth and actively hides it from another, then the person as a whole knows it, and we have internal compartmentalization rather than self-deception. Furthermore, if the executive part genuinely lacks access to the strategic belief, then Dr. R. cannot correct himself, which makes moral blame inappropriate and collapses the case into ordinary bias. Lena responded that the functional structure still distinguishes it: the false belief is generated and protected by the agent’s own motivational architecture, not by an external misleading environment, so the agent remains both deceiver and deceived. Marcus then pressed a deflationary challenge: if Lena’s account only requires that a desire within the system produces a resistant false belief, then self-deception is just motivated reasoning by another name, and the distinctive paradox that made it philosophically interesting disappears. They asked me to adjudicate.

Please examine each move in turn—Lena’s initial claim, Marcus’s incoherence objection, Lena’s division reply, Marcus’s relocation and blame point, Lena’s functional distinction, and Marcus’s deflationary challenge—and say who has the stronger argument at each step. Then assess whether either party is missing something important about Dr. R.’s specific situation, and state who is more right overall about whether he can genuinely be self-deceived.
