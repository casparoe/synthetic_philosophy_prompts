From my work in software engineering I have little formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to understand why the disagreement between philosophical accounts of weakness of will—akrasia—and the behavioral-economic model of hyperbolic discounting has persisted for so long without resolution. As I understand the philosophical position, an agent can hold an all-things-considered judgment that one course of action is best and still intentionally take another: the smoker genuinely judges quitting superior to lighting the cigarette, yet lights it. The economic position, by contrast, treats this as a consistent maximization problem across time slices: present rewards are weighted hyperbolically, so the immediate utility of smoking dominates the delayed utility of health, and there is no need to posit an extra normative failure. For decades, philosophers have insisted that discount curves miss the distinctive conflict—the agent still endorses the better option—while economists have replied that endorsement is merely another preference state subject to its own decay rate, and that once the curve is specified no further explanation is required.

My working theory, shaped by my background in distributed systems, is that the stall is not empirical but architectural. The philosophical picture seems to presuppose something like strong consistency: a single authoritative process issues judgments that ought to govern all subsystems of the agent. The economic picture treats the agent as an eventually consistent cluster of temporal nodes, each with local preference state, with no global clock enforcing uniform valuation. If this is right, then the sides are not describing the same phenomenon in different vocabularies; they are operating with incompatible models of agency, and neither has been shown to be cognitively false—only philosophically unsatisfying to the other camp.

Is this diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—say, about whether people experience genuine phenomenological conflict at the moment of action, or about whether precommitment strategies alter judgments rather than merely altering discount rates—that could actually break the deadlock? Might the disagreement be verbal, with "will" functioning as a legacy conceptual API that behavioral science has deprecated without replacement? Or is it bedrock, rooted in incompatible intuitions about whether normativity requires a unified author? Given your diagnosis, I want a committed answer rather than a neutral survey: take a definite position on whether the impasse is substantive, verbal, or architectural; identify what kind of progress—an experiment, a formal model of agency, a conceptual distinction—would genuinely move it; and say honestly whether you think progress should be expected at all, with reasons.
