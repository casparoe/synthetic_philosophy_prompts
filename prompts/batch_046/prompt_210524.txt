Exam question: Robert Nozick's entitlement theory holds that a distribution is just provided each person is entitled to their holdings through just acquisition, just transfer, or rectification of past injustice. He deploys the Wilt Chamberlain argument to show that voluntary exchanges can disrupt any patterned distribution of goods. Consider the following modification: in the initial state, a central authority imposes an exactly equal distribution of resources, enforced through coercive taxation; thereafter, all further exchanges—including payments to a popular performer—are fully voluntary and uncoerced. A critic objects that Nozick cannot consistently accept the resulting inequality, because the baseline holdings were not justly acquired but were instead the product of state coercion. Nozick might reply that his theory only requires that the initial holdings be justly acquired, not that they match any particular pattern, and that if the initial distribution was indeed unjust, the remedy lies in rectification rather than in blocking subsequent voluntary transfers. Evaluate this exchange. In particular, does the critic succeed in showing that Nozick's argument presupposes the justice of the baseline, and does Nozick's appeal to rectification save the Wilt Chamberlain example in this modified case? Provide a clear answer with reasoning.

Student answer: Nozick's entitlement theory has three parts—just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification—and it is historical rather than patterned. The Wilt Chamberlain case shows that if people freely pay Chamberlain, an initially equal distribution becomes unequal, which proves that patterned principles like equality are unstable whenever people have liberty. In the modified scenario, the state first imposes equality by force, then people voluntarily pay Chamberlain. The critic says Nozick must reject the result because the initial holdings were coercively distributed, but this misses Nozick's point: he does not care whether the initial state is equal or unequal, only whether each person's initial share was justly acquired. Since the state took resources by force, the initial holdings were not justly acquired, so Nozick should indeed reject the baseline. However, this does not destroy the Wilt Chamberlain argument; it only means we must apply rectification first. Once the injustice is corrected, the voluntary payments can proceed, and the resulting inequality will be just. The critic therefore fails, because Nozick can accept the outcome after rectification. The real problem with Nozick's view is that he never explains what just acquisition actually requires beyond the absence of direct theft. He relies on a Lockean labor-mixing idea to fill this gap, but he applies it selectively to defend the pre-transfer baseline in the original Chamberlain example. Because of this circularity—using an undefined notion of just acquisition to validate both the baseline and the transfers—the argument ultimately assumes what it needs to prove about the legitimacy of private holdings.
