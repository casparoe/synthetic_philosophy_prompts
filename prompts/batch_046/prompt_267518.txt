Please consider the following argument, which attempts to press the scale argument for insect sentience into a reductio of our standard moral framework. I present it with numbered premises; each seems to me initially defensible, yet together they appear to generate an unacceptable dilemma.

(1) The moral weight of an action affecting beings of type T should be calculated as an expectation: W(T) = N(T) · p(T) · u(T), where N is the number affected, p is the probability that a member of T is a subject of experience, and u is the unit disvalue of suffering.

(2) For insects, N is on the order of 10^19 across global ecological and agricultural interactions, p—while low—remains non-negligible (say, > 10^-3 for many species given current comparative neurobiology), and u is non-trivial if phenomenal consciousness is present. Consequently W(insects) exceeds W(humans) by several orders of magnitude.

(3) Therefore, a rational agent who takes expected moral weight seriously ought to prioritize the avoidance of insect-affecting harms over almost all human-centered projects, including conventional agriculture, urban development, and even climate interventions that involve land-use transformation.

(4) Now apply the same formula inside the boundary of a single human being. The human nervous system contains on the order of 10^11 neurons and 10^14 synapses; if any neural subsystem has even an infinitesimal independent probability ε of being a micro-subject, then N(human-internal) · p ≈ 10^11 · ε, which—if ε is not exactly zero—yields an enormous expected internal moral weight. Any action that alters human neural states (including ordinary cognition, anesthesia, or death) would then carry catastrophic expected disvalue.

(5) To avoid this internal reductio, one might invoke an integration constraint: sentience requires globally integrated information processing bounded by a functional architecture, so neurons inside a brain are parts of one subject rather than separate subjects. But if the integration constraint blocks internal aggregation, it also blocks cross-insect aggregation: the 10^19 insects lack any global functional integration, so they should not be summed into a single W either. The scale argument therefore collapses.

(6) Conversely, if we reject the integration constraint in order to preserve cross-insect aggregation, we lose the barrier against internal neural aggregation and are driven toward a form of moral panpsychism about distributed physical parts—extending, in principle, to ecosystems, computer networks, or weather systems with sufficient component counts.

(7) Hence we face a forced choice: either retain the integration constraint and abandon the practical force of the scale argument, or abandon the constraint and accept that moral patienthood is distributed probabilistically over physical parts in a way that makes ordinary human action morally catastrophic.

Is there a coherent intermediate position—perhaps distinguishing diachronic from synchronic integration, or appealing to evolutionary-functional closure—that avoids both horns without becoming ad hoc?
