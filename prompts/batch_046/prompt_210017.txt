In my reporting on formal epistemology and decision theory, I have observed that the debate over Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain evidence has reached a peculiar kind of stalemate. On one side, defenders of Jeffrey's rule maintain that when an agent receives ambiguous perceptual input—say, an experience that makes a particular partition of hypotheses more likely without singling out any one member—rationality demands direct updating of the credences assigned to those members. On the other side, orthodox Bayesians argue that all rational belief revision must proceed via conditioning on a specific proposition, and that any apparent counter-example merely hides an implicit conditioning event or violates diachronic coherence constraints such as rigidity. For decades the literature has cycled through familiar objections—the old-evidence problem, the threat of Dutch books, the charge that Jeffrey updates conflate epistemic and pragmatic reasoning—yet neither camp has moved. My working hypothesis is that this is not, at bottom, a mathematical disagreement about belief-revision rules, but a concealed divergence over the ontology of evidence itself: one side treats perceptual ambiguity as a non-propositional datum that can shift credences directly, while the other insists that every rational revision is mediated by an explicit propositional content. Is this diagnosis accurate—that we are dealing with two different questions about the architecture of cognition being pursued under one heading—or is there instead a hidden formal disagreement about whether Jeffrey's rule can be derived from standard conditioning plus auxiliary independence assumptions? Alternatively, might the deadlock be verbal, turning on competing uses of 'evidence' and 'uncertainty'? Given your diagnosis, what would genuinely advance the discussion: a formal derivation or impossibility result, an empirical finding about how human perceptual systems encode ambiguous stimuli, or a conceptual distinction—perhaps between evidential opacity, where the content of the experience is unclear, and evidential indeterminacy, where the reliability of the source is in doubt—that dissolves the apparent conflict?
