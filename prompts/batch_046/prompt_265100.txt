I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day workshop on bounded rationality. The audience is split: roughly half are decision theorists and game theorists who know the absent-minded driver problem well, and the other half are policymakers and graduate students from urban planning and public health with no philosophy background. I want the welcome structured by the problem's logic rather than merely decorated with it: treat the workshop as the highway, the first exit as retreating into disciplinary jargon (moderate payoff for the experts, failure for everyone else), the second exit as reaching the shared conceptual ground we actually want (the best outcome), and missing both exits as ignoring the technical puzzle altogether (the worst outcome). Write a five-minute address that enacts this framing so it genuinely constrains what I can say — for example, I should not be able to define backward induction without breaking the metaphor — while remaining fully intelligible to the non-experts. Use the payoff ordering exactly once (first exit: 2, second exit: 4, miss both: 0) without turning the speech into a tutorial. Include a moment where I observe that, unlike the driver, I do remember which exit we are approaching, but that our audience does not share that memory, so I need them to serve as my external commitment devices. The tone should be warm and inviting, not lecturing, and it must land for both halves of the room.
