As a novelist who has spent the last several years writing unreliable first-person narrators, I am drafting a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-language project—approximately four to five thousand words—on a question I think both Wittgensteinians and narrative theorists have left genuinely open. The problem is whether a fictional narrator can maintain a truly private vocabulary for her own sensations, terms she never intends to share with any reader, without that vocabulary collapsing into either public meaning or narrative incoherence. I want to call this the problem of authorial surrogacy: in fiction, the author stands behind the narrator as the hidden designer of every linguistic rule the narrator follows, so the usual requirement that a language be shared with an actual community may be satisfied by proxy—or it may fail precisely because the author can never genuinely share the narrator’s subjective criteria. State the question with precision: does Wittgenstein’s argument against a private language apply inside the diegetic frame, and if so, does the presence of an author as functional “community” save the narrator’s private terms, or does it show that those terms were never private to begin with?

Explain why the existing answers leave this open. Standard interpretations of the private-language argument—whether mentalist, behaviorist, or deflationary—treat the speaker as a real human agent embedded in an actual linguistic practice. Meanwhile, narratological discussions of diegetic opacity treat the reader’s exclusion from a character’s mind as an epistemic limit, not a logical one, and rarely ask whether the narrator herself can consistently follow rules in solitude. No account I am aware of systematically tests whether fiction creates a special case in which authorial design replaces interpersonal community, or whether that replacement is insufficient because the author lacks direct access to the narrator’s qualitative states.

The approach should center on a single carrying scenario: a first-person unreliable narrator keeps a private journal using invented words for pain-qualities that she explicitly states no other person could understand, even in principle, and that she applies according to a rule she claims to recognize only from her own case. Analyze this scenario by reconstructing the conditions for rule-following without invoking an actual audience, then test three conclusions against it. First, that private language is saved in fiction because the author supplies the missing external check. Second, that it is doubly impossible because the author, as designer, cannot genuinely share the narrator’s subjective criteria and therefore cannot validate her rules. Third, that within fiction the distinction between private and public collapses, leaving the argument moot but preserving narrative unreliability as a purely literary effect rather than a philosophical counterexample. The work should argue for one of these three, not merely list them.

What would count as success is a determinate claim that one conclusion follows from specific premises about authorial intention, narrative authority, or the logic of rule-following, with clear identification of which premise carries the weight and why the other two fail. What would count as failure—honestly assessed—is discovering that the fiction-specific conditions are too variable to yield a general answer, or that the problem reduces to standard philosophy of mind without gaining anything from the narrative framing. Given one semester, limit the scope to first-person unreliable narration in the prose novel, excluding drama, second-person address, and multimedia forms; do not attempt a comprehensive survey of narratological theory. References to positions and arguments may be mentioned generally, and I will fill in specific citations myself during drafting—do not provide a bibliography or reading list.

Take a committed position: defend one of the three conclusions above with specific reasoning, identify the exact premise about authorial surrogacy or rule-following that decides the case, and explain why the obvious alternatives are wrong rather than leaving the choice open. The response should be formal, rigorous, and written for a reader familiar with both analytic philosophy and basic narrative theory, but it should not depend on obscure scholarship or require web access to verify niche claims.

End with a single line beginning “Verdict:” that states your chosen conclusion and your honest judgment of whether the project is tractable within one semester.
