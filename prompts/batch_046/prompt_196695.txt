I've been studying how transitional justice mechanisms handle the trade-off between individual amnesty and collective truth-telling, and I've noticed a recurring structural pattern that I can't quite name. In certain designs, a perpetrator receives legal immunity in exchange for a detailed personal confession, but the institutional framework then treats that confession not as a record of individual culpability but as raw material for a broader narrative about systemic failure—so the individual is legally protected precisely to the extent that their agency is dissolved into structural explanation. Meanwhile, the collective narrative generated from these confessions is used to justify institutional reforms or reparative policies, yet no corresponding mechanism assigns collective legal responsibility to the institutions named in the narrative. I see versions of this in how South Africa's truth commission operated, in Argentina's interplay between its official truth-commission findings and the amnesty laws that followed, and in Rwanda's community-based processes that blend individual testimony with communal reconciliation frameworks. Is there an established term for this specific asymmetry—where individual legal accountability is voluntarily surrendered to produce collective moral accounting, but the collective entities implicated are not themselves made legally answerable? How exactly is it different from collective amnesia, structural violence, or distributive justice in reparations? And if there is no established name, how would you define its boundaries—particularly with respect to mechanisms where individual amnesty is granted but the collective narrative is never institutionalized?
