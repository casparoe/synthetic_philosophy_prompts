While preparing a discussion on phenomenal consciousness, I assembled the following line of reasoning and now find its conclusion intolerable. (1) On the higher-order view, a mental state is phenomenally conscious just in case there is an actual—rather than merely dispositional—higher-order representation that targets it with non-inferential immediacy. (2) If cognitive phenomenology is genuine, then the act of thinking that p carries its own distinctive phenomenal character, which by (1) requires a higher-order representation directed at that very act. (3) But that monitoring representation is itself an act of cognition—thinking about one’s thinking—so by (2) it too possesses cognitive phenomenology and therefore demands its own higher-order monitor. (4) This generates an actually infinite ascending hierarchy specifically for conscious thought, whereas perceptual states, lacking cognitive phenomenology, require only a single representational level. (5) No finite embodied system can realize an infinite hierarchy of simultaneously active representations, so conscious thought is impossible for brains like ours, even though perceptual consciousness remains unproblematic. (6) Introspection, however, reveals no sharp phenomenal divide between thinking and perceiving, and reflection never exposes an endless cascade—only a single accessible layer. (7) Therefore higher-order theory must either deny the reality of cognitive phenomenology, contrary to apparent evidence, or accept that conscious thought cannot occur in finite minds. I am most reluctant to abandon (2) and (6). Which step or inference should I reject, why did it seem unavoidable, and what does surrendering it cost each available position?
