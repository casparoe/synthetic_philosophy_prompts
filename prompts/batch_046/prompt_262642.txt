As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I keep noticing a tension between two translation practices I encounter. In software internationalization, we treat UI strings as correct if they preserve source meaning exactly; if a translated button label is twice as long as the English original, we redesign the layout rather than alter the text. Formal properties—character count, line breaks, pixel width—are expendable. But in literary translation, particularly for formal verse, the institutional norm seems reversed: a translation is often judged defective if it fails to reproduce the original rhyme scheme, meter, and stanza architecture, even when that forces the translator to abandon literal word-for-word correspondence and to use vocabulary the source never contained. This approach is well known from Douglas Hofstadter’s 1997 book Le Ton beau de Marot, where preserving the musical and structural form of the source poem is treated as essential, not decorative.

What philosophical commitments about textual identity, meaning, and representation does this literary practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that a poem’s form is constitutive of its semantic content, so that altering the rhyme scheme produces a different work rather than a different rendering of the same work? Or does it depend on a view of language in which sound patterns, rhythm, and syntax carry irreducible meaning that literal paraphrase destroys? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well—could one consistently believe that poems are purely propositional and still defend formal-equivalent translation on pragmatic or aesthetic grounds?

Then compare this with the software localization norm, where semantic preservation is mandatory and formal adaptation is expected. Do the two practices rest on incompatible theories of what a text is? If we replaced the literary practice’s implicit view with the one underlying software engineering—treating form as disposable and propositional content as the fixed essence—what would have to change in translation evaluation, pedagogy, or authorship attribution? And is the literary practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of textual identity and aesthetic value could all license the same insistence on formal preservation?
