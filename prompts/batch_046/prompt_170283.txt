As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy in earnest—working through Kant and then the more formidable architecture of Hegel’s *Philosophy of Right* with a local reading group—I find myself preoccupied with his claim that war is not merely an accidental calamity but an ethical moment in which the state preserves its vitality against the corruption that perpetual peace would bring. In our discussions we have debated whether this view is inseparable from its nineteenth-century context, and I have begun to wonder how Hegel might have responded to institutional developments that make the phenomenology of war almost unrecognizable.

Consider the framework—already debated in defense-policy circles though rarely in philosophical ones—of pooled-sovereignty autonomous-response networks: multinational alliances in which lethal kinetic decisions are executed by distributed algorithmic classifiers that integrate real-time sensor data across national borders, with human commanders retained only in supervisory maintenance roles rather than as deliberative moral agents. In such a system, the identification of an enemy is not the result of a recognized sovereign other confronting the state with its own ethical actuality, but rather the output of predictive-threat models that classify behavioral patterns before any manifest hostile act; the risk to citizens is minimized precisely because no individual soldier is asked to sacrifice life for the universal. The design seems to dissolve both the individual moral burden Hegel associated with citizenship and the direct inter-state recognition he considered essential to war’s ethical significance.

Given these conditions, I would like to know whether you think Hegel would have regarded this arrangement as a logical extension of the state’s rational will—perhaps seeing the algorithmic system as a more perfect expression of the universal, freed from the contingency of individual sentiment—or whether he would have judged it a profound corruption of ethical life, precisely because it removes the conscious self-sacrifice of citizens and replaces recognized sovereign confrontation with an anonymous, probabilistic administration of violence. More specifically, could his framework accommodate a form of conflict in which sovereignty is technically pooled and the enemy is never recognized as a state but only inferred as a statistical anomaly? And if not, which element of his account—the indivisibility of sovereignty, the necessity of conscious risk, or the dialectic of recognition—would he have been compelled to abandon first?
