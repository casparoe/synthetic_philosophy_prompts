I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of logic and epistemology, and I am helping to organise a small reading group that has reached its session on the surprise examination paradox. Rather than closing with a dry analytical summary, I should like to offer the group a poem that holds the conceptual tension open, just as our discussion did.

Please adopt the role of a poet-philosopher with careful attention to epistemic logic and the formal structure of self-defeating announcements. Before you compose anything, please spend a few moments brainstorming: consider how the villanelle’s cyclical refrains might mirror the backward induction by which students eliminate each successive day, and how the shift into the final quatrain could stage the realisation that the paradox is not resolved by finding a hidden day but by seeing how shared knowledge of rationality collapses the very conditions that make surprise possible. Then write a villanelle of exactly nineteen lines—five tercets with rhyme scheme ABA, followed by a quatrain with rhyme scheme ABAA—using two refrains that both rhyme with the poem’s A sound and alternate in the pattern A1 at the close of stanzas 1, 2 and 4, and A2 at the close of stanzas 1, 3 and 5, with both refrains appearing together in the final quatrain. The first refrain should carry the recursive elimination, something like We know it is not Friday, then not Thursday; the second should hold the teacher’s original promise, something like There will be a surprise examination. The poem should treat the announcement as a self-defeating prophecy: the very act of declaring future surprise generates the common knowledge that systematically destroys it, day by day, leaving no logical space for the event—yet Wednesday arrives, and the students are indeed surprised. At the turn into the quatrain, let the speaker recognise that knowledge of one’s own predictive reasoning has itself become unstable. Please ensure the philosophical content is precise enough that someone acquainted with the paradox recognises the steps—the backward induction, the contradiction between the announcement and the students’ reasoning, and the actual occurrence—without relying on named scholars, bibliographies, or citations. The language should also stand as poetry for a reader who has never encountered the puzzle. Write in formal British English, keep the tone contemplative rather than didactic, and leave the tension genuinely unresolved. Do not include any direct quotations from published texts; if you allude to specific formulations, paraphrase them in your own words.
