I'm closing a graduate seminar that moved from Camus's absurdism into contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning in life—specifically the tension between subjective fulfillment and objective value, and the deeper structural problem of whether meaning requires a "fundamental" end that is not pursued for the sake of anything further. The discussion kept returning to a regress worry: if every project is justified by the further good it makes possible, then either the chain never grounds or it stops arbitrarily, and that stopping point looks less like a foundation than like the activity of reaching itself. I want a poem that stages this problem rather than merely mentioning it, so that the form does the philosophical work.

Write a poem of six stanzas in blank verse using a chain structure: each stanza must end with a line that becomes the opening line of the next stanza, so that justification literally pushes forward; the sixth stanza should open with the first stanza's opening line, but altered—negated, questioned, or fractured—to show that the chain either loops back without closure or breaks open. Within this form, let a speaker attempt to justify successive human projects (care, creation, understanding) by appealing to what each makes possible, moving through appeals to happiness, to truth, to connection, until they arrive at something like "the reaching itself" as the only available ground. Do not resolve the tension into Camus's heroic defiance or into quietist acceptance of the circle of life; leave the listener in the unsettled space where the regress has been made visible and is still in motion. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Wolf's fitting-fulfillment view or Metz's fundamentality account recognizes the specific structural problem being enacted, but it should work as poetry for someone who has not read them. Avoid any sermonizing conclusion; the poem's refusal to close should match the seminar's actual endpoint.
