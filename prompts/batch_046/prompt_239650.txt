Write a dialogue between two interlocutors—call them A and B—who disagree on whether collapse describes a real kind of societal failure or is only a retrospective label for rapid institutional restructuring. A should defend collapse as analytically coherent: argue that the Late Bronze Age dissolution and the Classic Maya abandonment of lowland urban centers represent discontinuities in which centralized redistribution, interregional writing networks, and monumental construction ceased faster than local social reproduction could substitute for them, producing a measurable drop in socio-political complexity that is not merely subjective. B should counter that these events are reorganizations rather than failures: point to Easter Island's transition from moai-building chiefdoms to the bird-man cult and intensive small-plot agriculture as a case where domestic life and local ritual persisted and even adapted after the disappearance of large-scale labor mobilization, and note that Postclassic Maya societies retained literacy, calendrical knowledge, and long-distance trade without the divine-kingship apparatus, showing that what collapses is an expensive institutional shell, not society itself. Require them to engage directly on a specific conceptual test: whether the continued presence of household-level agriculture and ritual practice in a region that has lost state-level administration should count as the survival of the society in a leaner form, or as evidence that the original society has collapsed and left only its cultural residue—what we might call the ghost of a collapsed society—behind. Each speaker must respond to the other's actual use of Easter Island and the Maya, avoid straw-manning, and refrain from appealing to bibliographic authority or asking for sources.
