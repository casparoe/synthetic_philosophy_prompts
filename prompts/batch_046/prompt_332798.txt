I've been trying to write a paper on the doctrine of double effect and all those trolley variations, and honestly I'm stuck on why the literature feels like it's been running in circles since Foot and Thomson first set it up. Like, you've got one group saying the switch case is okay because your intention is just to redirect threat, not kill, and then you've got another group saying thats just rationalizing—what actually matters is whether you're using the person as a means, and the intention talk is post-hoc window dressing. But here's my hunch: maybe the disagreement isn't even about morality proper. Maybe one side is implicitly treating trolley cases as data for a descriptive theory of moral cognition—like, what rules do people actually deploy when they feel disgust?—while the other side treats them as normative tests for what an agent ought to do, regardless of intution. So they're answering different questions under the same heading.

Is that the real stall? Or is there actually a hidden formal disagreement about how to represent permissibility conditions, maybe something about whether 'directness' can be made rigorous without smuggling in circular intuition? I want an actual diagnosis, not another survey of footbridge vs switch. Tell me if this is verbal, if its mostly about mismatched methods, or if there's a substantive ethical disagreement that's genuinely hard to resolve.
