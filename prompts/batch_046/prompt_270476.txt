I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about going past what's required, judging by gut on cases. Pulling a stranger out of a burning car at risk to yourself: clearly good, not required, classic case. Donating a kidney to a stranger: also good and not required, but somehow closer to the line? Giving half your income anonymously to effective charities: feels virtuous but i'm not sure if it's heroic or just what decency demands if you can. Working every weekend for years tutoring kids for free: definitely above and beyond, but if you burn out and stop, was it wrong to start?

Then there's emotional labor stuff. Smoothing every conflict in a friend group so nobody else feels awkward, ending up exhuasted: I keep flip-flopping. Is that supererogatory kindness, or is it being taken advantage of, or neither? And here's the one I can't place: a security researcher spends all her evenings auditing open-source libraries used by hospitals, finds critical flaws, reports them, never gets credit or pay, and eventually burns out. She prevents real harm, but she chose the task, there's no face-to-face relationship, and the work is invisible. Is that going beyond duty, or is it just a weird unpaid job?

What principle, if any, gets these, or close? Maybe different clauses for risk, cost, and visibility? And what does it say about the researcher case — does the invisibility matter, or only the cost and the good done? I keep going back and forth on whether there's a real difference in kind between the burning-car rescue and the audit work, or if i'm just romanticizing physical risk. If a simple principle has to overturn one of my gut calls, which one should go? And what about a teacher who spends her own money on classroom supplies every year without being asked — is that supererogatory or just part of the job now?
