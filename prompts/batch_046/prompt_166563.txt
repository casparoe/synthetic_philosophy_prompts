In discussions of historical sociology I have found myself defending a stricter version of Weber's Protestant ethic thesis than the one usually debated. Rather than treating the Protestant ethic as one contributing factor among many or as a mere elective affinity, I want to maintain that culture can operate as an independent causal mechanism when it generates what I will call structured motivational anxiety—specifically, the Calvinist doctrine of predestination producing a sustained, systematizing psychological pressure that mediates economic rationalization in ways irreducible both to material interest and to generic individual psychology. The claim is that values explain outcomes only through such a historically specific psychological mediation, and that Weber's thesis survives only if this mediation is genuinely causal and not merely correlational. What would have to be true for this formulation to collapse? I want the defeaters mapped with precision: which empirical patterns—such as capitalist rationalization emerging in contexts lacking the relevant theological framework—would be decisive rather than merely awkward; which conceptual objections about explaining with values when values themselves require explanation pose the deepest threat; and whether the position can be saved by retreating to weaker claims about being one factor among many, or whether that retreat renders the thesis unfalsifiable and therefore defective. Please rank these threats from fatal to merely inconvenient, distinguish what a committed defender of this strict formulation would concede versus dismiss, and give me an honest, committed assessment of whether the view is genuinely exposed or well-insulated. I am asking for arguments and conceptual distinctions, not for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
