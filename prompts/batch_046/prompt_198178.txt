Below are the notes for an essay I am drafting, tentatively titled “The Scaling Law of Narrative Coherence: A Critical Framework for Grand Historical Narratives.” I would like your comments on the argument’s coherence, on whether the framing genuinely illuminates the disagreements it targets, and on any internal inconsistencies—particularly where the analogy between temporal scale and causal compression might overreach. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in conceptual evaluation only.

The essay proposes that big-history narratives obey an implicit scaling law: as the temporal scope of an account expands by an order of magnitude, the number of independent causal variables that can be retained without narrative collapse shrinks exponentially, not linearly. At the scale of hundreds of thousands of years, only highly abstracted drivers—cognitive capacity, geographic axes, or institutional tipping points—survive; ethnographic particularity is not merely omitted but structurally excluded.

Applying this to Harari’s Sapiens: over roughly seventy thousand years, Harari compresses biological and cultural evolution into successive “revolutions” (cognitive, agricultural, scientific). The scaling law predicts this compression, but the critics—especially Graeber and Wengrow in The Dawn of Everything—argue that the problem is not simplification but misattribution. Harari treats contingent political experiments (seasonal mobility, selective adoption of agriculture, reversible social arrangements) as deterministic stages. The essay asks whether the scaling-law framing correctly diagnoses this as a resolution problem, or whether Graeber and Wengrow are instead objecting to the ontology of linear progression itself.

Applying the law to Diamond’s geographic determinism: operating across continents over thirteen millennia, Diamond retains only east-west versus north-south axes and domesticable biota. Institutional agency and local choice vanish not accidentally but systematically, because they do not scale. Critics charge that this is a category error—confusing correlation with causation across incommensurable scales—rather than a necessary narrative economy.

Finally, Graeber and Wengrow attempt to violate the scaling law by maintaining ethnographic granularity across deep time, organizing their account around “three freedoms”: to move away, to disobey authority, and to create or transform social relations. The essay argues that their coherence depends not on retaining more variables within a linear frame, but on substituting a different topology—seasonal, networked, and reversible—for the progressive pipeline that the scaling law assumes. If this is right, then the dispute between grand narrative and its critics is not about how much detail can be preserved at scale, but about whether history has a default direction at all.

Please comment specifically on: (1) whether the scaling-law analogy is illuminating or imposes an anachronistic quantitative aesthetic on historiography; (2) whether my characterization of Graeber and Wengrow’s “three freedoms” as a non-scalar alternative is accurate or distorts their argument; and (3) whether there is a hidden compatibilist position—perhaps a multi-scalar historiography—that the essay unfairly neglects between Harari’s stage-model and Graeber and Wengrow’s anti-teleology.
