As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I have been working through an argument about synthetic neurobiology and personal identity that my reading group constructed, and I cannot locate the weak link without making an implausible concession elsewhere. The argument runs as follows. (1) In biological organisms, identity is preserved through gradual part replacement, provided metabolic integration and continuous functional organization are maintained; ordinary cellular turnover therefore does not destroy the person. (2) Consequently, if every neuron in a brain is replaced, one at a time, by a functionally isomorphic synthetic neuron that participates in the same causal network, the resulting synthetic brain is identical to the original person. (3) Once fully replaced, that synthetic brain can be non-destructively scanned and its complete functional architecture instantiated on a digital substrate; because the substrate-independent copy preserves every psychological relation and inherits the causal history of the synthetic brain, it too is identical to the original person. (4) But digital instantiation permits perfect duplication: the same architecture can be run simultaneously on two separate servers, each with an equal claim to the causal history described in (3). (5) Personal identity is standardly taken to be one-to-one, so both duplicates cannot be the original person, yet both satisfy the conditions in (3). (6) Therefore, either scanning fails to preserve identity—which seems arbitrary, since the synthetic brain immediately prior to scanning was the person—or identity is not one-to-one, or gradual replacement in (2) never preserved identity in the first place. (7) Yet if (2) is false, then ordinary biological cell turnover also breaks identity, implying that no human being survives from one day to the next, which proves far too much.

Please tell me which numbered premise or inferential step should be rejected, explain precisely why it initially seemed defensible, and then price the alternatives: what psychological-continuity theories, biological-continuity theories, and patternist or functionalist accounts each have to concede in order to deny that step. In your reply, clearly separate what is settled in the philosophy of personal identity from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who work on the topic, and do not present any contested view—whether about the necessity of biological continuity, the sufficiency of psychological continuity, or the identity conditions for artificial substrates—as if it were the consensus position.
