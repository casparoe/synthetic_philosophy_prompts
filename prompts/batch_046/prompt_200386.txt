You serve as the senior ethics auditor for a robotics-certification body that evaluates autonomous warehouse systems before they enter shared human-robot environments. Your team must apply a single, reusable decision procedure — a sequenced checklist with explicit halt conditions — to determine whether a proposed system introduces an ethically unacceptable responsibility gap, understood here as a situation in which no identifiable human or collective agent can be properly held to account for harm the system causes. The procedure must be grounded in the philosophical analysis of meaningful human control and responsibility attribution, but it must also operationalise two more granular distinctions that your certification framework treats as essential. First, distinguish prospective accountability (whether, prior to deployment, there exists an agent who can meaningfully answer for the system's behaviour and intervene) from retrospective attribution (whether, after an incident, there remains a coherent target for blame, sanction, or remedial duty). Second, treat epistemic opacity across federated model updates as an independent failure mode: responsibility is undermined not only by the absence of a human operator, but also when a system's decision-relevant parameters evolve through post-deployment learning in ways that no auditor can trace back to an approved initial state.

Your deliverable should be a practical audit protocol that a non-philosopher can run in approximately thirty minutes, specifying the questions in strict order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure definitively halts with either "no unacceptable gap" or "gap present — do not certify", and where it must hand the decision to a designated signatory because the issue is one of values rather than verifiable fact. You must flag every point at which judgment cannot be eliminated by the checklist, and you must state what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can resolve conflicts between competing accountable agents, or whether it assumes a single harm-event rather than cumulative risk.

Then apply the procedure to three proposed systems:
(a) a fixed-policy autonomous pallet transporter with complete telemetry logs and no learning capability, where the procedure should clear the system but reveal a subtle residual concern that forces a value-based override by the signatory;
(b) a navigation drone that updates its obstacle-avoidance weights via federated learning across multiple warehouses, where the gap should be clear and certification should halt;
(c) a human-in-the-loop sorting robot in which the human supervisor receives recommendations faster than they can be interpreted, such that the procedure technically finds no gap — an outcome you should treat as unsatisfactory — and you must explain exactly how the protocol handles this misfire and where it demands human judgment despite the checklist result.

Please answer in British English, avoid requesting or providing bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and paraphrase any specific theoretical claims without quotation marks. Do not depend on highly niche theories or named scholars in ways that would be unknown to a small language model; instead, integrate the required conceptual distinctions directly into the procedure's design.
