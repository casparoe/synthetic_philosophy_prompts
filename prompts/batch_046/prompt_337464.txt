For the final exam in an upper-level epistemology seminar, draft one essay question that students could answer in about 90 minutes (target answer ~700 words). The topic is the Meno problem: why, if at all, is knowledge more valuable than true belief? To block answers that simply summarize standard replies (justification-as-stability, reason-tracking, practical reliability), frame the question around a hypothetical "belief-stabilizer": a mechanism that renders any true belief counterfactually robust—resistant to error across nearby possibilities—without the subject having any grasp of why the belief is true. Ask whether the resulting state counts as knowledge, and if not, precisely what is missing that makes knowledge the greater good. Instruct students to defend one specific view rather than surveying options. Then provide a model answer and a marking scheme out of 25 marks built on five criteria: (i) a precise diagnosis of the gap between true belief and knowledge in the Meno; (ii) a committed evaluation of whether the stabilizer produces knowledge; (iii) a clear distinction between the instrumental and intrinsic value of knowledge; (iv) a response to the objection that knowledge is just true belief plus redundant ornament; and (v) clarity and rigor of argument. For each criterion, state the marks, the specific conceptual move required, and the typical error that costs points. The model answer should take a definite position and argue for it, not lay out alternatives for the reader to choose.
