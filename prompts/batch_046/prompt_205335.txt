I am taking a graduate seminar on epistemology, and our instructor repeatedly falls back on the slogan that knowledge is justified true belief, treating it as a single, self-evident analysis rather than a family of possible theses. He invoked it most recently to dismiss a Gettier-style scenario I described: a research team derives a correct quantitative prediction about a chemical reaction from a theoretical model that contains a false simplifying assumption, where the predictive error introduced by that falsehood is accidentally canceled out by an unrelated interaction effect they never anticipated. Rather than engaging the structure of the case, he said the belief could not count as knowledge because its justification was "contaminated" by false premises, as if the slogan alone settled the matter.

Could you lay out, as a menu of precise readings, the distinct claims hidden in "knowledge is justified true belief"? I want to know whether "justification" must be factive—ruling out any false premises in the supporting chain—or can tolerate false lemmas so long as the agent's reasoning remains internally rational; whether "belief" denotes an occurrent mental state, a stable dispositional acceptance, or a propositional attitude with specific strength conditions; whether "true" requires a substantive correspondence relation or merely the absence of falsehood; and whether the formula is intended as a necessary condition, a sufficient one, or a full reductive analysis. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false, and indicate which version best explains my instructor's dismissal of the chemical-reaction case. Then tell me which readings actually support that dismissal, which ones leave the case intact as genuine knowledge, and whether there is a non-ad-hoc repair—perhaps involving robustness, non-accidentality, or sensitivity conditions—that can salvage the slogan without collapsing it into circularity. Please provide your answer in American English.
