I'm giving a seminar next week defending a somewhat weird claim about Lemkin's genocide concept and the politics of naming. My thesis: the legal narrowing of genocide to physical/biological destruction in the postwar codification wasn't just Cold War horse-trading, but tracked an implicit moral ontology — calling something genocide shifts the victims from politically situated agents into a biological population to be protected, which actually strips them of the kind of agency that human-rights frameworks presuppose. So state resistance to the label isn't always cynical avoidance of liability; sometimes it's an (usually unconscious) attempt to keep victims in a political register rather than a biological one. The audience is a seminar room of international lawyers who have worked at tribunals, plus political theorists who study recognition and misrecognition. Most of them think moral ontology talk is hand-wavy fluff. There's also one well-known scholar who literally wrote the standard drafting-history of the Convention and thinks broadening genocide language dilutes the crime into uselessness. They care about legal precision and about not weakening the 1948 standard. I need a prep sheet: the objections they'll actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased in their vocabulary (not generic philosophy-speak), with what I should concede upfront, what I have to hold, and the one reply that has to land if the proposal survives. Include the objection I'm definately hoping nobody asks — likely something about Lemkin's dropped cultural-genocide clause being abandoned for sound conceptual reasons, not just politics — and flag the softest point in my own position, especially around whether non-naming really preserves agency or just avoids accountability. Don't give me citations or a reading list; just the objections and replies.
