In a recent philosophy of physics reading group, a colleague tried to block Max Black's symmetric-universe thought experiment—two spheres identical in every intrinsic property, separated only by a spatial relation—by invoking what she called the "Leibnizian slogan": indiscernibles are identical. She treated it as an immediate knockout, claiming that if the spheres share every property they must be one sphere, so the scenario is not merely physically impossible but conceptually incoherent. I suspect she is running together several very different principles and giving them all the same name, but I want a precise taxonomy rather than a vague counter-assertion that Black's spheres are "intuitive."

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan could actually be expressing? For each reading, specify: (a) whether it counts only qualitative (intrinsic and relational) properties or also permits spatiotemporal position and orientation to serve as distinguishing features; (b) whether it functions as a logical biconditional about identity, a substantive metaphysical claim about how reality is structured, or a linguistic convention about how we individuate objects in discourse; (c) whether it rules out Black's spheres, and if so whether it denies them separate existence or instead denies that they genuinely lack any distinguishing feature; and (d) whether it presupposes or rejects "primitive thisness" (haecceity) and bare-substratum theories as non-qualitative grounds of individuality. In particular, distinguish the weak first-order logical formulation, the stronger qualitative version that excludes spatial separation, and the bundle-theoretic version that denies bare particulars altogether. Finally, tell me which of these readings is most likely being invoked when someone uses the slogan to dismiss a symmetric-universe scenario, and whether any reading genuinely makes Black's example incoherent rather than merely counterintuitive to those who accept it.
