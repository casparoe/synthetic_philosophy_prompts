Draft a single essay question for a closed-book final exam in an upper-level undergraduate normative-ethics course, to be answered in 75 minutes in roughly 1,200 words. The topic is demandingness objections to consequentialism, but the question must not be answerable by reciting standard complaints about extreme self-sacrifice. Instead, frame the problem around this distinction: call “quantitative demandingness” the charge that consequentialism requires too much sacrifice, and call “structural demandingness” the charge that it eliminates the moral category of “good enough” by treating every suboptimal choice as wrong, thereby leaving no permissible space for non-optimal but acceptable actions. Ask students whether structural demandingness is genuinely distinct from quantitative demandingness, whether standard consequentialist appeals to supererogation or to a satisficing threshold can answer it, and whether the objection succeeds even if quantitative demandingness is answered. Provide a model answer that makes the following five moves, together with a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that assigns weights and, for each point, notes the characteristic mistake that should cost marks: (1) sharply distinguish structural from quantitative demandingness, with a concrete illustrative case (4 marks); (2) explain why supererogation addresses quantitative but not structural demandingness (4 marks); (3) assess one consequentialist reply—such as a scalar reformulation or a satisficing threshold—and show whether it resolves the structural problem without abandoning core consequentialist commitments (5 marks); (4) argue for or against the claim that structural demandingness is a genuine defect rather than a feature of moral seriousness (4 marks); (5) reach a clearly defended conclusion on the overall force of the objection (3 marks). Keep the prompt self-contained: do not ask students to cite specific authors or texts, do not request bibliographies, and do not rely on obscure named theories.
