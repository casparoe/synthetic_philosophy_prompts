ok so i'm writing a chapter on standing to blame and the hypocrisy problem, and i feel like every paper just jumps to "hypocrites can't blame" without saying what a theory of standing even owes us. can you lay out the desiderata?

what any adequate theory of moral standing to blame has to get right — especially when hypocrisy is involved — with the motivating case behind each, and flag which ones are genuinely non-negotiable vs just camp preferences. then, very briefly, how the "clean hands" view, the egalitarian view, and a contextualist view do against the list.

here's what i think needs to be on the spec:

- it must handle the straightforward hypocrite: A does X, then blames B for X. does this kill standing entirely, weaken it, or do nothing? need the case behind why it matters.

- it has to distinguish "standing-loss" (you're disqualified) from "standing-dilution" (your blame is less authoritative but still valid). i think people conflate these and it changes whether we should listen.

- it can't collapse the difference between personal hypocrisy and what i'm calling "structural complicity" — like the activist with a huge carbon footprint blaming oil companies. she's not personally drilling, but she's embedded in the system. does that affect her standing differently?

- it must preserve the idea that a blamer can be factually right about the wrongness even if they lack standing — accuracy and normative authority are seperate.

- it needs to explain whether standing is binary or scalar, and whether it's recoverable (apologize? stop doing X?).

- it should be consistent with the intuition that pure third parties — strangers with no connection to the wrong — can still blame, otherwise we'd never allow outsiders to condemn obvious injustice.

flag any pair that can't be jointly satisfied. my hunch is that requiring both "structural complicity always undermines standing" and "third parties always have full standing" might conflict if everyone is structurally complicit in something. not sure.
