I've been trying to work out whether ontology identification can survive the fact that some information is neccessarily out of reach, and I've managed to tie myself in a knot with an argument that seems to show we shouldn't believe in consciousness, historical causes, or modal properties at all. Here are the steps I've scribbled down, though I suspect there's a sleight of hand somewhere I can't spot.

(1) Let's say we genuinely identify an ontological kind K only if our identifying criterion picks out K in every possible situation where K is present — otherwise we're just tracking a superficial shadow.
(2) If K has any feature that is neccessarily inaccessible — say, the precise qualitative character of another's experience, or the full causal chain before any record — then there are worlds where that hidden feature differs but everything we can check stays the same.
(3) So in those worlds our criterion applies and we'd call it K, even though the hidden feature makes it not-K.
(4) Therefore, any kind with neccessarily hidden features fails the identification condition and shouldn't be in our ontology.
(5) Consciousness, historical events, and modal relations all have such hidden features.
(6) So they shouldn't be in our ontology.
(7) But that conclusion is obviously mad — we can't just eliminate other minds or the past.
(8) The only escape is to deny (1) and allow identification even when hidden features might differ.
(9) But then we can't distinguish real kinds from shadow-kinds that match our criteria in every world we can access but diverge elsewhere, which makes ontology arbitrary.
(10) So either we bite the bullet and reject (5)-(6), or we accept that ontology is arbitrary.

I'm particularly keen to keep (1) and (8) — they both feel right in different moods — but I know I can't have both. Where exactly does the argument slip? Is it a modal slide between what's required for identification and what's required for kind-membership? Or is there an equivocation between accessable to us and accessable in principle? I'd like you to say which premise or step to give up, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives, especially what accepting shadow-kinds actually costs versus giving up the strong identification condition. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
