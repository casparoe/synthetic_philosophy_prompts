In my graduate seminar on philosophies of history, we have spent the term comparing four influential cyclical frameworks—Polybius's anacyclosis of constitutional decay and renewal, Ibn Khaldun's model of civilizational rise and fall driven by the dynamics of asabiyya, Spengler's morphological conception of cultures passing through organic seasons, and Toynbee's pattern of challenge-and-response that can either generate growth or precipitate breakdown. The students have mapped these onto shared historical episodes with considerable ingenuity, yet the discussion has stalled in a way that feels conceptually deeper than a mere disagreement about evidence. One faction treats all four thinkers as describing the same underlying structural recurrence—merely in different idioms—while the other insists that Khaldun's sociological mechanism and Spengler's biological metaphor are not competing descriptions of one phenomenon but incommensurable ontologies of historical change, with Toynbee occupying an ambiguous middle ground where normative agency disrupts pure cyclicity.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical—no archive will settle whether a civilization genuinely "repeats"—but concerns hidden disagreements about what constitutes an explanation in historiography, and possibly about whether "linearity" and "cyclicity" are even predicates that apply to the same kind of object. Specifically, I wonder whether one side is asking a descriptive question about reproducible causal patterns, while the other is asking a normative or phenomenological question about whether historical agents experience their own era as progressive or fated, with both sides using the single term "cycle" to cover what are actually distinct concepts—structural recurrence versus phenomenological repetition. Before I assign the final papers, I want a precise diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions running under one name? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of historical evidence, about which analogies count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding whether human affairs can genuinely improve? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates causal recurrence from lived repetition, a formal model that renders the cyclical claim empirically tractable, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
