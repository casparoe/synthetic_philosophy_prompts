At a workshop on constitutional design, two participants—Rosa and Dev—argued about whether a “generational lock-in” clause counts as a legitimate Ulysses contract. The clause in question is a constitutional provision binding future legislatures to maintain a specific carbon-budget framework, amendable only by a four-fifths supermajority.

(1) Rosa opened by saying this is precisely the right use of precommitment. The present democratic majority knows it is vulnerable to short-term pressure and to the temporal discounting that makes long-horizon collective goods hard to protect; by locking in the framework now, the polity binds itself against future weakness of will, just as Ulysses has himself tied to the mast.

(2) Dev replied that Ulysses contracts apply only to a single agent across time, not to successive generations. The people who will live under the clause in fifty years are not the same agents as today’s voters, and treating them as one continuous self is a fiction. Binding them is not self-restraint but one group exercising durable power over another, violating the democratic principle that no generation should be permanently subordinate to its predecessors.

(3) Rosa answered that democratic continuity creates a diachronic collective agent—the people as an enduring institutional entity—so the binding is genuinely self-directed at that collective level, analogous to an individual drafting a living will for old age. She added that the clause is procedural rather than substantive: it does not dictate particular policies but only raises the threshold for change, thereby protecting deliberation rather than imposing outcomes.

(4) Dev countered that “procedural neutrality” is illusory here. Selecting a four-fifths threshold rather than a simple majority embeds a specific normative weighting—present stability is valued over future adaptability—which is itself a contested value judgment disguised as institutional design. He then proposed what he called a “succession-neutral” criterion for legitimate constitutional precommitment: a binding clause is valid only if every future generation that inherits it could, in principle, have rationally consented to the mechanism at the moment of succession, not merely at the moment of authorship. By this test, Rosa’s clause fails, because successors facing radically different ecological or technological conditions might reasonably reject the framework yet be structurally blocked from doing so without an extraordinary majority.

(5) Rosa said Dev’s criterion collapses all long-term constitutional design into present whim. If every successor must be able to consent ex post, then no durable commitment is possible, and constitutions become mere expressions of current will. She argued that constitutionalism is meant precisely to resist that collapse—to allow a polity to make credible commitments across time—even if that requires accepting some intergenerational asymmetry.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of Rosa’s and Dev’s five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—especially regarding whether precommitment can be justified without assuming either a fully unified diachronic self or a fully atomized succession of generations. In your response, separate what is relatively settled in political philosophy (for example, the standard distinction between self-binding and other-binding, or the familiar problem of procedural rules encoding substantive values) from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of constitutional theory and intergenerational justice, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as the coherence of diachronic collective agency or the viability of succession-neutral consent—as though it were the consensus view.
