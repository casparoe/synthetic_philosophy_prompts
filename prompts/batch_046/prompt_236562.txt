I've been chewing on Margalit and Rieff on memory and forgetting, and I've come up with this hybrid view: institutions have a thick duty to remember systemic wrongs so they dont repeat them, but families and individuals have a kind of right to be released from carrying personal grievances once the direct victims are gone. I'm calling this active release "mnemonic labor" — not just letting things fade, but the community deliberately lifting the burden so remembrance doesnt become moral vanity (Rieff's worry). So remembering and forgetting are both moral practices, just at different scales.

What would actually count against this? I want serious defeaters — cases where institutional and personal memory bleed into each other, evidence that forced forgetting suppresses rather than heals, or conceptual reasons why "mnemonic labor" collapses into either denial or performative grief. Rank them if you can: fatal vs awkward. And please, seperate whats settled in the literature from whats genuinely contested among people who actually study this — dont present Margalit or Rieff as the consensus if they arent. Also tell me if my idea is unfalsifiable or just vague.
