I'm drafting an essay for a graduate seminar on comparative political ethics, and I'd like your comments on its argument, structure, and conceptual coherence. The working title is "Status Reciprocity and the Boundaries of Corruption: Clientelism, Gift-Giving, and the Parochialism of Western Standards."

Here are the core notes and claims. I begin by noting that dominant international anti-corruption frameworks—particularly those rooted in OECD and World Bank standards—tend to define corruption as "the abuse of entrusted public power for private gain." I argue that this formulation quietly embeds a Weberian conception of bureaucracy: the officeholder is expected to function as an impersonal agent, and any private benefit flowing from the office is therefore treated as a normative deviation. Against this background, I examine two practices commonly classified as corruption but potentially governed by different moral logics. The first is clientelism: the exchange of material benefits for political loyalty, which operates through long-term reciprocal obligations rather than discrete market transactions. The second is ceremonial gift-giving directed at officials, widespread in societies where presenting goods to a bureaucrat is not a quid pro quo for a specific decision but an affirmation of social standing and mutual recognition—what I am tentatively calling "status reciprocity." I distinguish status reciprocity from both explicit market bribery and clientelist patronage: unlike bribery, it lacks a specific contractual return; unlike patronage, it does not seek to secure ongoing political loyalty through resource distribution, but rather to acknowledge and reproduce a hierarchical social relationship. My central claim is that Western anti-corruption norms conflate these three categories because they presuppose that any non-market, non-contractual transfer within administrative contexts subverts rational-legal authority. I then press a conceptual challenge: if we imagine a political community in which legitimate authority is constituted through embedded social relationships rather than through impersonal rules, would status reciprocity still count as corruption? Or does the very concept of corruption depend on a prior, and possibly parochial, commitment to the strict separability of public role and private person?

I am especially uncertain about two points. First, whether "status reciprocity" is genuinely independent from clientelism, or whether it collapses into a form of patronage once it becomes institutionally regularized. Second, whether my argument succeeds as a conceptual critique of Western norms, or whether it merely describes cultural variation without undermining the normative authority of anti-corruption standards.
