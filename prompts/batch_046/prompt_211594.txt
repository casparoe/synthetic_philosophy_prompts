I'm planning a single seminar for first-year philosophy students on the paradox of tragedy—why we actively seek art that elicits grief or dread—because they typically arrive with one of two half-formed views: that sad art is cathartic in some vague Aristotelian sense, or that the enjoyment is just misdescribed masochism. I want the session organized around a precise but non-standard framing: tragedy operates through a safe-affective-frame in which negative emotion becomes an object of meta-cognitive appreciation rather than a practical threat, not through purgation or conversion. Plan the explanatory arc: identify the conceptual dependency that must be in place before this framing sounds like a real answer rather than an evasion—specifically, the distinction between first-order emotional response and second-order evaluation of that response; propose one carrying example other than Oedipus or Hamlet that contains both explicit emotional manipulation and a visible structural frame the audience can hold in mind, stating what properties it must have and what it must lack for the framing to land; map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—the catharsis-as-hygiene view, the "if it's framed then it's fake" objection, and the collapse into "we just like feeling bad"—indicating the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; specify the end-of-session check that distinguishes students who have grasped the paradox from those who have only learned to repeat the framing; and state explicitly which standard account, such as any Humean conversion thesis, you will deliberately leave out at this level and why. Finally, identify the weakest link in your proposed dependency chain and say what specific counterexample or empirical finding about emotional framing would lead you to revise the sequence.
