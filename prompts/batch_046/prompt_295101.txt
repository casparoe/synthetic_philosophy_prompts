Consider the following notes for a short philosophical essay on the sunk cost fallacy. The standard economic view holds that honoring sunk costs is irrational because past expenditures cannot be recovered and should not influence future-oriented decision-making. However, this analysis may conflate instrumental rationality with what might be called axiological coherence. In many constitutive practices—such as traditional craft apprenticeship, long-form artistic composition, or ritual observance—the resources already expended are not merely instrumental means but become integrated into the value-structure of the activity itself. Abandoning the project does not just forgo future benefits; it can retroactively diminish the meaning of the prior investment. One might distinguish between prospective optimization, which evaluates options solely by their future payoff, and retrospective valorization, in which completing the endeavor confers value upon the costs already borne. From this perspective, continuing a project despite unfavorable future prospects is not necessarily a cognitive error but may instead express a commitment to maintaining the narrative and moral integrity of one's prior actions.
