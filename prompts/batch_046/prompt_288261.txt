I work in institutional design and evolutionary modeling: I study norm stability using coordination games, costly signaling, reputation equilibria, path dependence, and mechanism redesign. I have only a passing acquaintance with moral philosophy, but I want to understand the debate over whether practices such as slavery and dueling disappeared through moral argument, economic self-interest, or shifts in honor. Bernard Williams's thesis, as I understand it, holds that these norms collapsed not mainly because people were rationally persuaded or because interests changed, but because the social recognition that made them honorable was withdrawn. Kwame Anthony Appiah's account also centers honor, though he emphasizes that honor can be redirected: the same competitive desire for social standing becomes attached to abolition or to refusing duels, rather than being eliminated.

Restate both positions in my vocabulary. Be precise: do not treat honor as a loose synonym for reputation, but map exactly what mechanisms it plays in each account. Say what translates cleanly into payoff structures, equilibrium selection, or signaling costs; what has to be distorted to fit (for instance, if moral argument is rendered only as an exogenous shock to utility weights, does that erase its internal structure?); and what has no expression at all in evolutionary game theory or mechanism design. Then report the residue: does the fact that honor seems to carry a normative pull not reducible to reputation costs show that my framework is missing a category, or does it show that the philosophers are describing coordination dynamics I already model, just with unnecessary normative ornament?
