I've been working through a debate with a colleague about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, using the case of a senior researcher who has spent eight years developing a theoretical framework that has repeatedly failed empirical tests, yet continues refining it rather than abandoning it for a newer approach. We mapped out six moves in our disagreement, and I would like an adjudication of each.

Move one: I argued that sunk costs are, by definition, expenditures—time, money, reputation—already made and unrecoverable. In normative decision theory, rational choice should maximize expected utility over future outcomes alone; weighting past irrecoverable losses is a category error, full stop. Move two: my colleague conceded the point for isolated individual choice but said the "sunk cost fallacy" label is often misapplied in institutional and creative contexts, where what looks like backward-looking attachment is actually protection of path-dependent capital—relationships, tacit knowledge, or credibility—that only retains value under continuity. Move three: I replied that path-dependent capital is a forward-looking asset, not a sunk cost; if that capital genuinely has future value, then continuing is rational for forward-looking reasons, and labeling it "honoring sunk costs" confuses two distinct mechanisms.

Move four: my colleague then introduced what she called "cognitive architecture lock-in"—the idea that in long intellectual projects, past investment doesn't merely produce transferable assets but shapes the researcher's specific problem-solving structure in a non-transferable way, so that abandoning the framework doesn't free up resources; it renders them useless because the cognitive scaffolding was built for this particular edifice. The past investment isn't being honored, she said; it is being acknowledged as having created an option value that exists only upon completion. Move five: I countered that this is still just describing a high switching cost or a complementary asset—both forward-looking considerations—and that she is smuggling backward-looking motivation into forward-looking language. If the only reason to finish is that the past work makes finishing cheaper or more valuable than starting over, that is rational cost-benefit analysis, not sunk-cost reasoning. Move six: she shifted to collective contexts, arguing that in a multi-author research program, past contributions generate legitimate expectations among collaborators. Continuing the failing framework can be rational not because the past expenditure demands repayment, but because abrupt abandonment imposes uncontracted costs on partners—wasted training, broken trust, lost coordination capital—that the initiating researcher has reason to avoid. I responded that this is straightforward reciprocity or contractual obligation, entirely separable from the sunk-cost fallacy, which specifically involves being influenced by one's own irrecoverable losses. She ended by asking whether, if all apparently sunk-cost behavior can be redescribed in forward-looking or social terms, the concept still has any non-trivial content, or whether it collapses into a rhetorical accusation.

Please adjudicate each of the six moves: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then address the underlying question—whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, or whether there are boundary conditions involving identity, collective obligation, or path-dependent cognition where the distinction between rational commitment and fallacious attachment genuinely collapses. Be precise about where the conceptual boundary lies, and whether my colleague's examples actually cross it or merely rename rational considerations.
