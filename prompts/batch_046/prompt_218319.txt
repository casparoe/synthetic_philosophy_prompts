I've recently begun work on predictive ecological modeling, and since encountering the no-free-lunch theorems in a machine-learning seminar I have been unable to dispel a skeptical argument about the justification of induction. Here is the chain.

(1) The no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning establish that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions mapping inputs to outputs, every learning algorithm achieves precisely the same expected error on data outside the training set.

(2) Scientific induction is a learning algorithm: it takes observational inputs and outputs general laws or predictive theories.

(3) Therefore, if we average uniformly over all logically possible worlds—that is, over every possible true distribution of natural events—scientific induction is exactly as accurate as any rival method of theory selection, including random guessing, contrarianism, or always selecting the simplest false law.

(4) Epistemic justification, however, is essentially comparative: to be rational in preferring induction, we need it to outperform its rivals in a manner that does not already assume the conclusions we seek to defend.

(5) The only way to make induction outperform rivals within the NFL framework is to abandon the uniform average over worlds and instead weight worlds by a prior distribution—one that favors simple, stable, local laws—thereby breaking the symmetry that makes NFL hold.

(6) But any such prior is itself an inductive commitment about the nature of reality; if it is justified at all, its justification derives from past successes of induction.

(7) Hence the defense of induction requires an inductive prior, and the justification of that prior requires induction in turn. The circle is not benign: it leaves us with no independent ground for preferring induction over a method that, say, predicts total chaos after every third observation.

(8) Yet we are plainly justified in expecting the sun to rise tomorrow, in trusting climate models built on inductive extrapolation, and in dismissing chaos-predicting rivals as irrational.

(9) So if (1) through (7) are sound, then our scientific and everyday beliefs are unjustified; since (8) is undeniable, the argument proves far too much.

Where does this go wrong? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1), and I want to preserve the intuition behind (4) that justification must be comparative rather than arbitrary. I also want to keep (8). But I suspect there is either an equivocation between statistical expectation taken over an artificial uniform distribution and rational expectation relative to our actual evidence, or a modal slide from "induction is not uniquely best in every world" to "induction is unjustified in ours." Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—particularly for the philosophical defense of model selection in domains like ecological forecasting?
