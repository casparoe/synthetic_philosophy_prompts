Two colleagues, Mira and Jonas, debated a live regulatory conflict: Statute A explicitly permits a particular financial instrument, while Statute B—enacted later but never repealing A—explicitly prohibits the same instrument, and the jurisdiction provides no meta-rule to resolve the clash. Mira argued that this yields a genuine dialetheia within the legal system—the instrument is both permitted and prohibited—and that the correct response is to model the framework with a paraconsistent logic such as LP, so that the contradiction does not trigger explosion and legal reasoning can continue without deriving arbitrary verdicts (for example, one might still derive that the instrument is either permitted or taxed without being forced to conclude that it is taxed). Jonas replied that this is a category mistake: legal validity is normative rather than descriptive, so there is no “fact of the matter” capable of being both true and false; the conflict signals an ambiguity that demands interpretive resolution, not logical embrace. Mira countered that even on a normative reading, the descriptive fact that both statutes remain in force creates a real contradiction about what the law currently is, and that tolerating this contradiction via LP is more honest than pretending interpretation always succeeds. Jonas then claimed that this formal safety misses the practical point: a court must render a single determinate verdict, and modeling the conflict as a tolerated contradiction merely formalizes judicial paralysis; he added that if contradiction is embraced whenever norms conflict, legal reasoning collapses into perpetual indecision with no principled stopping point. Mira’s final move was that Jonas smuggles in a hidden classical assumption—namely, that every contradiction must be dissolved—which is precisely the bias that paraconsistent logic rejects, and that recognizing the contradiction is epistemically preferable to forcing a spurious resolution.

They asked a bystander to referee.
