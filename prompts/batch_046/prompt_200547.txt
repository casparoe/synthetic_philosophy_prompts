I've been asked to lead a ninety-minute session for a reading group of philosophy-minded computer-science graduate students—people fluent in optimization and reward functions, but with no prior exposure to economic methodology or philosophy of mind. In our preliminary discussion, one student kept sliding between "utility" as a parameter in a reinforcement-learning loss landscape and "utility" as a mental state, and I realized the group needs to see why revealed preference theory was designed to make exactly that slide impossible. I want them to grasp Samuelson's operationalist claim not merely as a formal trick, but as a philosophical thesis about what utility means: whether it can survive as anything more than a reconstructed ordering of observed choices. Don't explain the theory to me; construct the pedagogical plan.

Specify the dependency-ordered sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced—for instance, whether the weak axiom of revealed preference has to precede or follow the distinction between mentalistic and behaviorist interpretations—and justify why each step depends on the last. Identify the single carrying example or case study that should do the philosophical work: it must exhibit a clear divergence between choice-pattern and reported subjective welfare, and you should state explicitly what features it needs (e.g., reversibility under framing, absence of monetary stakes) and why a standard apples-versus-oranges consumer diagram fails to carry that load. Map the misconceptions these students will likely import from their RL backgrounds—I am particularly concerned about a conflation of revealed preference with inverse reinforcement learning, and about treating the utility index as an internal reward signal—and pinpoint the exact moment in the sequence where each should be intercepted and defused. Provide one closing diagnostic question that tests whether they understand the stakes for mental content rather than just the formal machinery, and list the technical elements—such as the full axiomatic derivation of the utility index or the historical cardinal-versus-ordinal debate—that should be explicitly set aside and marked as outside this session's scope.
