I am writing this as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. After encountering Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions—the F-twist, in which deliberately false premises can strengthen a model's predictive power by isolating essential variables—I tried to apply the same logic to software engineering abstractions and ended up with an argument that seems to collapse scientific realism. The chain feels valid, but the conclusion is absurd, so something must have slipped.

(1) Friedman holds that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are not defects; predictive accuracy is the sole criterion of merit, and idealizations such as frictionless planes or point masses are often necessary to isolate causal mechanisms.

(2) Software architectures operate analogously. We model computation using entities—threads, packets, discrete objects—that have no direct counterpart in the physical substrate, which consists only of voltage states and gate transitions.

(3) These software abstractions are predictively powerful and practically indispensable, yet their core ontological assumptions are literally false about the underlying hardware.

(4) By Friedman's criterion, if unrealistic assumptions do not undermine predictive success, then that success should license treating the model's entities as genuinely real—otherwise we apply a double standard between physics and other predictive disciplines.

(5) But in software engineering we correctly deny that threads are fundamental entities; they are stable instrumental patterns, not discoveries about silicon ontology.

(6) So either Friedman's criterion fails to license ontology in general, or we should be realists about threads and packets.

(7) The second option is absurd, so the first must hold: predictive success, even with unrealistic assumptions, never by itself licenses ontological commitment.

(8) But then scientific realism collapses not through empirical failure but through the very success of idealized models—physics succeeds precisely by using false simplifying assumptions, so its predictive success gives no reason to believe in unobservable entities such as quarks or fields.

(9) I want to keep (1) and (4) if possible, and I am inclined to keep (5), but I refuse to accept (8). Where exactly does the argument break? Is there an equivocation between the reality of a model's entities and the reality of the mechanisms it isolates, or does the error lie in applying a methodological criterion across domains? Give me a committed diagnosis: identify the specific step or hidden premise that fails, explain why it looked plausible, and state precisely what I must pay—ontologically or methodologically—to reject it.
