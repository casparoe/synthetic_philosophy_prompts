I have been tracking a particular dialectical pattern in discussions of whether bodily pain is intrinsically bad, or bad only through an accompanying reaction. The pattern begins with clinical or exceptional cases in which a person reports unmistakable sensory qualities—burning, stabbing, crushing—associated with tissue damage, yet displays no withdrawal behavior, no request for cessation, and sometimes even amusement or flat indifference. Proponents of the reaction-dependent view cite these cases to show that the sensory core is value-neutral, and that all the badness resides in a separable affective or motivational layer that is merely contingently attached.

The pattern then takes a second turn. Once the two layers are conceptually separated, the compound state we ordinarily call pain—in which sensation and aversion are fused—begins to look like an accidental union rather than a natural kind. The claim that pain is not intrinsically bad then applies only to the stripped sensory component, which has been quietly redefined as the true referent of the word. Meanwhile, parallel cases exist in which intense suffering, dread, or distress occur without any localized bodily source, demonstrating that the affective layer can exist without the sensory one. These mirror cases are usually invoked to show that suffering is broader than pain, but they also highlight that separating the layers dissolves the very phenomenon that was originally under evaluation.

What I am trying to identify is the specific conceptual maneuver—if it is one—where decoupling the components of a unified experience is used to establish the neutrality of one part, but only at the cost of redefining the whole so that the original normative question no longer applies to it. Is there an established name for this move in philosophy of mind or value theory? How exactly does it differ from legitimate decompositional analysis, and what are its boundary conditions—does it recur in other debates about compound mental states, or is it peculiar to the pain case? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want to know whether the carving I have described fits an existing concept, and if not, what precise definition and distinguishing marks would be needed to capture it.
