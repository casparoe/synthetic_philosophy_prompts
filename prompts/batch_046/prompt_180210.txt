There's this urban commons panel in my city that issues rulings on who gets to stay when property rights clash with use rights. Their track record so far:

- They kept a rent-stabilized tenant in place after her building sold, saying 22 years creates a dwelling right that outlasts the owner.
- They kicked out squatters from an abandoned factory after only 8 months, ruling that occupancy with no tax payment and no maintanance work doesn't generate any claim.
- They stopped a luxury condo project on a lot that'd hosted an informal garden for 15 years, calling it "social infrastructure" with a collective neighborhood claim.
- They let a homeowner add a second unit despite neighbor complaints, saying exclusionary zoning violates an access principle.
- They denied camping unhoused people on private parking lot land, but made the city provide shelter beds — so they recognize negative rights against eviction but not positive rights to space.
- They evicted a tenant who'd turned her rent-controlled flat into an Airbnb, arguing she converted a dwelling right into a commodity right and broke the condition.

Now the new case: artists have lived in a decommissioned water tower (city property, basically forgotten) for 11 years. They never paid rent, but restored the structure, run free workshops, and have nowhere else. The city wants to lease it to a telecom firm for 5G gear, offering them "priority access" to some other center. The artists say they've earned a "maintenance-generated occupancy right" — not just time, but labor preserving a public asset.

If the panel wants to be consistent with itself, what's the call? Which precedent is the nearest analog — the garden, the factory squatters, or the rent-stabilized tenant? And there seem to be at least two rules that fit their past decisions: one about "social function fixes the right" and one about "labor plus long occupancy on public land creates a claim." What does each predict here? Which further case — say, a group that maintains a private vacant lot for 10 years vs. one that just lives in an unused public library — would tell those rules apart?
