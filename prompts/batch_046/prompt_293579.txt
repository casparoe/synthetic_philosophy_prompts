For decades, philosophers have argued over whether Quine's well-known critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction genuinely eliminates the boundary or merely demonstrates that it is gradational rather than absolute. The debate appears stalled at a high level of familiarity: both sides accept Quinean holism, both concede that logical and mathematical statements can be revised in principle, and both work within some version of the web-of-belief model. The eliminativists insist that without a sharp boundary there remains no philosophically substantive distinction capable of doing explanatory work; the reconstructionists respond that functional analogues—entrenchment, centrality, conservative revision strategy—preserve precisely what matters about analyticity under a naturalized description. Yet neither camp has moved the other, and the literature often feels like a rehearsal of well-known moves.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate arises from conflating two dimensions of belief revision: revisability in principle, which applies uniformly across the web, and revisability in practice given current theoretical commitments, which produces a revision-cost gradient of framework-internal recalcitrance. On this view, some propositions resist revision not because they enjoy metaphysical immunity but because altering them would cascade catastrophically through the rest of the system. One side reads this gradient as the death of analyticity; the other reads it as analyticity reconstructed. Is this the right diagnosis, or does the disagreement run deeper?

I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations. Do the disputants differ about hidden empirical claims regarding cognitive or linguistic practice, about normative standards for adequate philosophical explanation, or about bedrock intuitions concerning the autonomy of logic? Are they genuinely addressing the same question—what is the metaphysical or epistemic status of necessary truth—or are they answering different questions, one about semantic metaphysics and one about scientific methodology, under a single heading? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about the dynamics of belief revision or about whether theoretical cost can be measured independently of prior commitments—that is doing the real work? Given your diagnosis, specify what would count as progress: a formal result about revision dynamics, a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates revisability-in-principle from framework-relative conservatism, an empirical finding about human conceptual architecture, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the original question is ill-posed?
