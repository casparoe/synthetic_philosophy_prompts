Consider the following line of reasoning drawn from a fourteenth-century North African historian's study of civilization: he proposes that physical environment does not directly determine political forms, but instead governs the tempo at which societies accumulate and dissipate collective solidarity. In arid, hot regions this tempo is rapid, producing swift cycles of tribal cohesion and dissolution; in temperate zones it is slow, allowing more durable structures to form. Crucially, he deploys this framework not to explain any particular dynasty's collapse, but to show why the same political pattern recurs across centuries regardless of individual leadership. How should this claim be understood in relation to the distinction between long-term structural conditions—geography, climate, and slow-moving social forms—and the events or personalities typically treated as historical causes? Specifically, does framing climate as regulating a "tempo" of solidarity rather than acting as a direct cause successfully avoid determinism, or does it merely relocate determinism to a different temporal scale?
