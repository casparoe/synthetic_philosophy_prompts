For an advanced undergraduate or early graduate seminar in political ethics—twelve participants, ninety minutes—design a structured role-play with forced allocations and a changing institutional frame that makes the group experience the structural instability of the boundary between corruption, clientelism, and legitimate gift-giving, particularly when Western legalistic anti-corruption standards are applied to contexts in which formal welfare is absent and social exchange functions as survival insurance.

The conceptual architecture should turn on three registers of exchange built into the rules rather than named in a lecture: transactional quid pro quo, relational gift-obligation that maintains long-term social bonds, and patronage that operates as the only available distributive mechanism. In Round One, participants serve on a municipal resource board distributing three scarce goods—medical access vouchers, building permits, and school placements—under formal merit-based rules that prohibit any consideration of prior social ties. Each participant receives a private card describing hidden relationships (a kinship debt, a past life-saving favor, a religious obligation). They must deliberate publicly and vote, with allocations scored on public reputation points only.

In Round Two, the institutional frame collapses: state welfare disappears, and the board must now ensure community survival using the same formal anti-corruption rules. The payoff scheme shifts so that honoring a hidden relational obligation yields collective survival points, while strict merit-based allocation causes hidden penalties (family exclusion from food access represented by lost future-round participation rights). Participants must vote again without knowing which relationships others hold.

In Round Three, an external auditor—played by two participants assigned that role—applies Western-style transparency metrics retroactively to the Round Two votes. The board must defend or condemn specific allocations without knowing which metric (merit, survival necessity, or relational duty) the auditors will weight.

Specify concrete roles (board chair with tie-breaking authority, silent beneficiary whose family outcome depends on others' choices, community elder who can invoke but not enforce obligation norms, and rotating auditors), a precise timeline (ten minutes private card review, twenty minutes deliberation and vote per round, five minutes consequence revelation), and a reputation-point system that requires no real money.

The idea must emerge through action: participants should feel the normative inversion—condemning an allocation in Round One that becomes obligatory in Round Two—before anyone names the tension. Provide a debrief that maps Round One onto transactional logic, Round Two onto survival-insurance logic, and Round Three onto the imposition of universalist metrics; identifies the predictable failure mode (participants will likely construct an ad hoc hidden universal rule to resolve discomfort rather than tolerate structural ambiguity) and explains how to handle it without lecturing; and states frankly which feature of real-world corruption debates the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the historical sedimentation of colonial legal codes that shaped modern anti-corruption institutions—so the facilitator must supply that framing in a brief, citation-free closing.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or source citations. Build all conceptual distinctions into the rules, cards, and payoff structures themselves.
