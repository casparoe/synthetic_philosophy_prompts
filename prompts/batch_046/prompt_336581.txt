I take the rehabilitated Malthusian position that the principle of population describes not a permanently falsified law but a structurally dominant tendency that was only temporarily overridden by a historically anomalous subsidy—chiefly cheap fossil energy, synthetic fertilizers, and institutional arrangements that decoupled food production from local land constraints for roughly two centuries. On this account, Malthus was not wrong but early: the demographic transition and the Green Revolution are not counterexamples but postponements, and the underlying arithmetic-geometric tension will reassert itself as the subsidy depletes, possibly through climate-mediated yield collapses or mineral scarcity rather than classic famine. What would genuinely count against this "right but early" rescue? I want the arguments and evidence that should move a defender of this view, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. In particular: which empirical trajectories—say, sustained absolute decoupling of population growth from resource throughput, or a permanent, resource-independent collapse in fertility—would force abandonment rather than further qualification? Has the position been insulated by escape clauses (every technological rescue is "part of the subsidy," every failure to collapse is "not yet") to the point of unfalsifiability, and if so, does that undermine its status as a long-run structural claim rather than a narrative? Distinguish between defeaters that a committed rehabilitated Malthusian should concede—such as a demonstrated permanent decline in total fertility driven by cultural rather than scarcity mechanisms—and defeaters they could legitimately absorb into the subsidy framework. Also, what risky predictions does this view generate that distinguish it from generic limits-to-growth theories, and have any of those predictions already been falsified?
