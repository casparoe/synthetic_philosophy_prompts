I'm preparing a session for a graduate seminar on political philosophy and the aesthetics of war, and I'd like to work through Francis Ford Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979) with Hegel's Philosophy of Right specifically in view — particularly his argument that war is not a mere accident but belongs to the ethical concept of the state, preserving it from the stagnation of private interest and expressing the sovereignty by which individuals risk particular existence for the universal. The film is well-known: Captain Willard is sent up the Nung River into Cambodia to terminate Colonel Kurtz, a decorated officer who has established himself as a quasi-divine warlord beyond command, and the journey progressively strips away bureaucratic military order until Willard reaches Kurtz's compound and kills him.

What I'm trying to avoid is a reading that simply says war makes people crazy, therefore Hegel was wrong. Instead, I want a careful interpretation that asks whether Kurtz embodies not a failure of Hegel's logic but its extreme fulfillment — the state's absolute moment of sovereignty detached from the rational institutions of civil society — and whether Willard's mission, which is itself an extra-legal execution ordered by a shadowy command, reveals that the state's monopoly on violence is structurally unstable rather than ethically foundational. Does the film's progressive dissolution of military bureaucracy into ritual and madness support Hegel's claim that war exposes the genuine character of the state, or does it show that once the ethical substance of civil society is removed, what remains is not sovereignty but pure domination without universal purpose?

I'd like you to take both the film's imagery and narrative structure and Hegel's text seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Which scenes — perhaps the bridge at Do Lung, the USO show, or the final compound ritual — most sharply illuminate the tension between Hegel's view of war as ethical preservation and the film's depiction of war as the dissolution of ethical life? And what does the film add to the philosophical debate that Hegel's own argument, confined to the rational state, cannot account for? I am not looking for a bibliography or external scholarship, just a philosophically rigorous reading that could hold up in discussion.
