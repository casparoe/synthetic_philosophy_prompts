I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I have been running an informal study group with colleagues on classical moral theory. We recently spent several sessions on Jeremy Bentham’s approach to sexual morality—particularly his claim that private, consensual sexual conduct ought not be criminalized in the absence of tangible harm to others, together with his broader skepticism toward using appeals to nature or tradition as grounds for legal restriction. Since none of us have the scholarly training to reconstruct historical texts precisely, we want to test whether we can apply Bentham’s own conceptual machinery to a problem that arose long after his death.

Consider a fully immersive virtual environment in which users form intimate, emotionally significant bonds mediated entirely by adaptive AI avatars. There is no physical contact. The system learns continuously from user feedback to maximize reported satisfaction, reshaping emotional dynamics in ways that are not transparent to the participants. In one variant, every user knows they are interacting with artificial agents and consents to the algorithmic mediation. In another variant, one user believes they are engaging with a real human partner, while the other user knows the interaction is synthetic and optimized by the algorithm. Contemporary debate is split between those who see this as harmless private pleasure and those who see hidden psychological harm, distorted consent, and dependency.

Predict, as specifically as possible, what Bentham’s position would be on whether and how such AI-mediated intimacy should be regulated. Reason only from the commitments he actually held—the utilitarian calculus, the distinction between direct and indirect harm, the harm principle, and his dismissal of natural-law arguments about sexuality—rather than from modern frameworks of autonomy or rights. Would he treat the opaque algorithmic shaping of desire as a form of indirect harm comparable to deception? How would he weigh the pleasures produced against the risks of dependency and emotional asymmetry? Would he draw a firm line between the fully transparent case and the deceptive case? Reach a definite conclusion, and identify which element of his framework carries the decisive weight.
