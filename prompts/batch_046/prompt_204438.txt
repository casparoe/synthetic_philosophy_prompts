Next month I am speaking to my local civic philosophy circle—a group of about fifteen retired engineers, high-school teachers, and librarians who meet monthly to discuss ideas they have picked up from popular books and podcasts. Most of them have heard summaries of the Libet experiments and tend to split into two camps: the determinists who say neuroscience has shown free will is an illusion, and the commonsense defenders who insist, “Of course I chose my coffee this morning; I felt the decision happen.” I want to defend the view that the only variety of free will worth wanting is what I have been calling “retroactive reason-giving”: the disciplined practice of looking back at an action, constructing a coherent explanation for why I did it, and allowing that reconstructed story to reshape my future choices—whether or not the original cause was determined, random, or unconscious. I believe the metaphysical debates about “ultimate sourcehood” and “the ability to have done otherwise” chase a kind of freedom that would not actually improve anyone’s life, whereas retroactive reason-giving is a learnable skill that preserves genuine moral responsibility without requiring any spooky, uncaused causation.
