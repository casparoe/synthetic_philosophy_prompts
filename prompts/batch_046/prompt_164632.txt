As a journalist covering neuroscience and moral philosophy, I have been trying to settle my own view of what Libet's experiments actually threaten and where the philosophical damage stops. Here is the package of commitments I have arrived at, and I want a consistency audit rather than a general exposition.

First, I accept Libet's core temporal result as genuinely undermining the naive picture of conscious will: the readiness potential begins hundreds of milliseconds before reported awareness of deciding to flex, which shows that conscious volition is not the initiating cause of spontaneous bodily movement. Second, I reject both Libet's conscious-veto rescue and standard compatibilist redefinitions of responsibility, on the grounds that each saves moral agency only by redefining authorship into something thinner than what ordinary people actually care about when they ask whether someone could have done otherwise. Third, despite that rejection, I remain structurally a compatibilist: I think determinism is compatible with meaningful freedom, and that genuine agency requires only internal coherence among one's values and responsiveness to reasons, not an uncaused causal origin. Fourth, I hold that the counterfactual robustness required for responsibility — that an agent genuinely could have acted otherwise — is satisfied within deterministic systems that represent and weigh alternative possibilities, not by metaphysical openness in the causal chain. Fifth, I think that if neuroscience eventually achieves predictive transparency — near-perfect forecasting of human choices from prior brain-state measurements — the appropriate moral stance should shift away from praise and blame toward a form of moral engineering, treating people as complex deterministic systems to be guided rather than as hidden authors who deserve retributive responses.

My question is whether these five positions form a coherent worldview or whether I am borrowing incompatible motivations from different camps. The most obvious tension, to my mind, is between my compatibilist claim that determinism never by itself erodes responsibility and my claim that predictive transparency should make us abandon retributive attitudes: if determinism was never the problem, why should knowing the deterministic trajectory in advance change our moral posture? I also worry that my rejection of both the veto account and compatibilist redefinition implicitly relies on a stronger, perhaps libertarian, standard of authorship that my explicit compatibilism denies. At the same time, I suspect there may be only an apparent tension between my acceptance of Libet's erosion of spontaneous volition and my defense of reason-responsive agency: perhaps the readiness-potential finding targets arbitrary wrist-flexing with no deliberative structure, while my compatibilist account governs reasoned moral choice, so the two operate at different levels without conflict. Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the genuinely incompatible pairs from those that dissolve on inspection, and specify for each serious tension what would have to be revised and at what cost. If I must drop one commitment to restore consistency, which should it be?
