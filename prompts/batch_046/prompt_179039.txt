For my junior seminar on historical methodology, I am preparing a consistency check on my emerging view of Fernand Braudel's longue durée, and I would appreciate an audit before I present it. My current commitments are these: (1) geographic and climatic conditions form the deepest stratum of historical causation, constraining possibilities long before individuals act; (2) the Annales school's emphasis on slow structures is essentially correct, so that conventional political events are best treated as superficial disturbances—like foam on deep currents—rather than as primary drivers of change; (3) nevertheless, the collapse of the Soviet bloc from 1989 to 1991 was a genuinely decisive turning point that turned mainly on contingent diplomatic choices and the specific judgment of individual leaders, not on long-term structural inevitability; (4) I also accept a strong 'deep time' thesis, according to which the Neolithic Revolution remains the only fundamental rupture in human history, with the Industrial Revolution representing merely an intensification of agrarian surplus management rather than a new structural layer; and (5) consequently, historical explanation should privilege quantitative, long-range datasets and treat narrative biography as largely epiphenomenal. Do these commitments hang together? Please identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to conflict, rank the tensions by severity, and propose the least costly revision for each. In particular, examine whether (2) and (3) can coexist, whether (4) undermines (3), and whether (5) genuinely follows from the preceding claims. Finally, tell me whether a single revision could resolve most of the conflicts, or whether my framework requires a more fundamental restructuring.
