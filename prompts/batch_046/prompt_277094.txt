I am writing a paper for my seminar on classical utilitarianism and private morality, and we have spent the past few weeks working through Bentham’s fragments and essays on sexual ethics without recourse to modern commentary. From the excerpts assigned, I have reconstructed a consistent track record of his verdicts: private, consensual homosexual acts between rational adults are permitted, since there is no identifiable victim and no harm to outsiders; masturbation and other solitary practices fall outside the legitimate scope of law unless they become a public nuisance; consensual incest between adult siblings, conducted in private with contraception, is tolerated in principle because both parties possess full rational capacity and no reproductive harm results; bestiality is condemned, not on grounds of “unnaturalness,” but because the animal lacks rational agency and is therefore a victim incapable of consent; and commercial sexual relations between consenting adults are permissible in principle, though subject to regulation against fraud and coercion.

My difficulty is identifying the principle that actually governs these judgments. They are jointly consistent with a pure harm principle—no victim, no crime—but they are also consistent with a stricter capacity-based consent rule that permits any act among rational adults who agree to it and prohibits acts involving beings that lack rational agency. To distinguish these two interpretations, I want to apply them to a new case I have constructed for the paper. Consider four rational adults, all with full legal capacity, who enter a private, fully disclosed agreement to ingest a legally available, physically harmless, and fully reversible psychoactive compound that temporarily eliminates rational decision-making capacity during a sexual encounter; they provide explicit prior consent to the entire protocol, suffer no lasting physical or psychological damage, and the encounter produces no effects on anyone outside the group. A pure harm principle should permit this, since no one is harmed and all affected parties consented in advance. A capacity-based rule should prohibit it, because the sexual act itself occurs while the participants lack the rational capacity required for valid consent, rendering their prior agreement analogous to a contract executed under conditions that invalidate it.

Would Bentham permit or prohibit this arrangement, and which of the two principles—pure absence of harm, or the presence of rational-capacity consent—better predicts his verdict? If a third principle, perhaps one concerning the corruption of moral sentiment or the preservation of social institutions, also fits his earlier rulings but yields a different result here, I would like to know it. Finally, what further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a being with marginal, artificial, or temporarily restored rational capacity—would definitively settle whether his criterion is the absence of harm or the presence of rational consent?
