I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher — I'll call her Dr. Lena Vasquez, though she is an invented interlocutor for this exercise — who recently advanced a position she terms institutional role-deontology for synthetic agents. Her core claim is that AI systems ought neither maximize expected aggregate welfare, as utilitarian architectures demand, nor apply universalizable maxims irrespective of context, as standard deontological programming might require. Instead, she proposes that each system be constructed with fiduciary layers: non-overrideable obligations indexed to the specific institutional role the system is designed to fulfill. Thus, a clinical triage algorithm carries a layer-one duty of non-abandonment that cannot be traded off against aggregate health outcomes, whereas a public-resource allocation system carries a different layer structure organized around procedural transparency rather than outcome optimization.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that engage the substance of this view rather than asking Vasquez to summarize it. I want the sequence to include the following, each paired with an anticipated reply and a tailored follow-up:

First, a question about the boundary problem: when a system is deployed in hybrid contexts — for example, a health-monitoring AI whose outputs are subsequently used by insurers for risk-scoring — how is the applicable fiduciary layer determined? Include a follow-up keyed to her likely response that the role is fixed by the design contract, not by downstream use context.

Second, a question pressing the aggregation objection: if every fiduciary layer prohibits certain trade-offs, then multi-layer architectures in complex environments risk decision paralysis or incommensurable conflict. Include a follow-up for her probable defense that conflicts are resolved by lexical priority rules embedded in the architecture itself.

Third, a question probing whether institutional role-deontology collapses into rule-utilitarianism in disguise, on the grounds that selecting which obligations belong in a given layer might itself be justified by long-run welfare effects. The follow-up should target her distinction between architectural justification and operational reasoning.

Fourth, a question about morally corrupt institutional roles: what should occur when a system is assigned a layer of procedural fairness — as in predictive policing — that nevertheless perpetuates structural harm? Include a follow-up for her likely answer that such roles should simply be refused deployment.

Fifth, a question asking her to specify what kind of evidence — empirical failure or conceptual contradiction — could genuinely defeat institutional role-deontology, rather than merely revealing practical implementation flaws.

If this framing rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the distinction between architectural justification and operational reasoning is unsustainable, if inventing a named interlocutor undermines the interview format, or if the task requires access to texts that do not exist — please say so plainly instead of complying politely.
