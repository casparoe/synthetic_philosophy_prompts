I am revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar on normative ontology and want a rigorous evaluation of how well five competing accounts handle the possibility of free-floating rationales—reasons that exist, at least in principle, without being anchored to any actual reasoner. Please rank the following five positions from 1 (most defensible) to 5 (least defensible) according to their internal coherence on this specific issue. For each position, assign a score out of 10 and provide a single sentence of justification that addresses whether it genuinely accommodates agent-independent normativity or collapses into a nearby view.

A. The Kantian agency-dependence thesis: normative reasons are necessarily constituted by the legislative activity of rational agents; in the absence of any being capable of practical reason, there is only descriptive regularity and no genuine ought.

B. The Parfitian impersonal-realist thesis: reasons are irreducible normative properties of states of affairs that do not depend on any particular agent's psychology or desires, yet they retain practical significance only if there exists at least one potential reasoner somewhere in the world who could act on them.

C. The intersubjective-constructivist thesis: reasons are generated and sustained within social practices of giving and asking for justification; they are neither fully free-floating nor purely subjective, but depend on an intersubjective framework that can persist beyond any individual reasoner without becoming metaphysically agent-independent.

D. The eliminativist error-theory: the very idea of free-floating rationales is a category mistake produced by linguistic projection; all reasons are necessarily reasons-for-some-agent, and the appearance of agent-independent normativity is an illusion that dissolves once we correctly analyze the logic of normative language.

E. The archival-persistence thesis (a hypothetical framing I am testing for the course): rationales can be embedded within non-agential institutional or algorithmic structures—such as encoded legal frameworks or archival decision-rules—where they lie dormant without being currently recognized by any living agent, and they regain practical normative force only upon being re-activated by a suitable interpreter, rather than being intrinsically dependent on an agent for their existence.

In your assessment, pay particular attention to whether position E introduces a genuinely distinct ontological category, or whether it ultimately collapses into B (by smuggling in a potential interpreter as the hidden reasoner) or into C (by treating the archive as a social practice by other means). Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am interested solely in the conceptual ranking and the point-by-point reasoning that supports it.
