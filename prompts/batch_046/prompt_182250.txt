Exam Question (20 points): In Saul Kripke’s interpretation of Wittgenstein’s rule-following considerations, explain the skeptical paradox concerning meaning and rule-application, clearly distinguishing the “straight” solutions from the “skeptical” solution. Then evaluate the following extension: consider an isolated speaker who has never actually interacted with other people but has meticulously internalized what she takes to be the correct communal practice for applying “plus.” Does the skeptical solution have the resources to determine that she means addition rather than quaddition, or does the absence of an actual community leave the paradox intact? Identify any missteps in reasoning.

Student Answer:
The skeptical paradox, as Kripke develops it from Wittgenstein, shows that there is no fact of the matter about what rule I am following. Take addition: in all my past applications, I might have meant quus—where quus behaves like addition for pairs below 57 but diverges thereafter. Since my mental history is finite and my behavioral dispositions are compatible with both interpretations, nothing fixes that I meant plus rather than quus. This undermines the idea that meaning is determined by private mental states or by intrinsic dispositions.

The straight solutions try to rescue determinacy by locating the missing fact—whether in qualitative mental images, in explicit instructions I have given myself, or in causal-functional dispositions. All fail, because any proposed determinant is itself open to the same skeptical reinterpretation. The skeptical solution, by contrast, abandons the search for such facts. Instead of asking whether a meaning-ascription is true in virtue of hidden conditions, it asks whether it is warranted by the practices of the linguistic community. Assertibility replaces truth-conditional semantics: I mean addition when my applications align with those that the community accepts, and I am corrected when they diverge.

Applying this to the isolated speaker: although she has never interacted with others, she can still apply the rule correctly if she has internalized the communal norms. By imagining what an actual community would endorse, she establishes the conditions of assertibility for her own usage. The hypothetical agreement functions as the normative standard, so she can meaningfully distinguish correct from incorrect applications of “plus” even in solitude. Thus the skeptical solution resolves the paradox for her case, because the absence of physical interaction does not eliminate the intersubjective framework that grounds meaning.

One might worry that this collapses into a private language, but that objection misses the point. The isolated speaker is not inventing a private rule; she is participating in a shared practice counterfactually. Objectivity is preserved because her judgments are answerable to the community’s standards, whether those standards are applied in her presence or not.
