I'm taking a course on modal epistemology and have been trying to map my own intuitions about what counts as genuinely knowable, partly because the standard Fitch-style argument feels like it should convince me but doesn't fully match how I actually think about the limits of knowledge. Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios, offered without much theoretical dressing. First, a true statement describing the exact neuronal configuration of a human brain at a specific instant: I think this is not knowable in principle, not merely difficult, because any measurement capable of capturing it would alter the very system observed. Second, a mathematical truth that is provable only via a proof too long for any physically constructible computer to verify: I still think it is knowable in principle, since an ideal rational agent could in principle grasp the reasoning. Third, the statement "there exists at least one true proposition that no actual being will ever know"; I think this might well be true, yet it is also knowable—because we can understand the concept of unknown truths—without that generating the conclusion that we therefore know every specific unknown truth. Fourth, imagine a future technology that lets us observe distant exoplanet atmospheres but always introduces a systematic, irreducible error in one chemical parameter; I think the true value of that parameter remains knowable in principle, just not accessible via that particular technology. Fifth, if there is a logical truth whose only available proof requires assuming its own negation as a lemma, I think it is genuinely unknowable, and I do not treat that as a failure of omniscience but as a structural feature of logical space. I am genuinely torn, however, on a case I have not listed elsewhere: suppose a being possesses perfect logical and inferential faculties but entirely lacks sensory or empirical access; is a specific contingent truth about an unobserved color experience in a distant galaxy knowable to such a being?

These judgments seem to cluster around some distinction involving access, modal stability, or self-reference, but I cannot tell whether they form a coherent pattern or whether I am simply being inconsistent. Is there a principle—perhaps restricting knowability to propositions for which knowledge is modally stable, or to those that do not involve self-undermining observation, or to those accessible in principle to agents with the relevant conceptual apparatus—that captures all five verdicts? What does your principle predict for the torn case of the non-sensory being? And please be explicit about which of my judgments it must overturn to avoid the derivation that, if all truths are knowable, then all truths are actually known. If the principle accepts that derivation, explain why my resistance to it is mistaken; if it blocks the derivation, say exactly where the standard argument goes wrong on your view.
