I'm putting together a handout for my philosophy of language seminar and I keep getting stuck on how to organize the Twin Earth material. Every textbook just lists Putnam, then Burge, then Fodor, but that doesn't actually help students figure out where they stand on the issue. Could you build me a decision-tree taxonomy of positions on semantic externalism, built from the logical space rather than from a list of philosopher names?

Start at the root with whether mental content about 'water' can stay identical across Earth and Twin Earth despite different chemical extensions — answer yes or no. From there, split by whether you think the argument generalizes past natural kinds: does it also apply to artifact terms like 'arthritis', to phenomenal states like pain, or even to thick moral concepts like 'cruel'? Another branch should ask whether the division of linguistic labor is actually essential, or whether Twin Earth still works if Oscar is a complete hermit who never talked to anyone. Then split by what you do with narrow content: do you deny broad mental content entirely, allow both broad and narrow layers, or try to reduce broad content back to internal functional roles?

For each leaf, name the position (or write 'unnamed'), give its most awkward commitment in one sentence, and flag any pairs the tree can't cleanly seperate — like whether standard social externalism and causal-historical reference theories end up in the same node here. I want roughly eight leaves. Please include at least one genuinely unexplored branch — maybe whether Twin Earth applies to indexicals like 'now' — and briefly diagnose why that spot is empty (incoherent? just ignored?). Don't include bibliographies or reading lists; my students won't have access to check citations untill the exam anyway. Also, make sure the diagnostic questions are concrete cases students could answer without knowing the labels, so the tree sorts them by belief rather than by which name they recognize.
