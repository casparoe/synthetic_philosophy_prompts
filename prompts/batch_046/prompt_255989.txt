Here is an argument I have been working through about teleosemantic theories of mental content, and I am stuck because it seems to prove that misrepresentation is impossible, which cannot be right.

(1) On a proper-function account, a mental representation R has the specific content that p only if R was selected for indicating p in the normal conditions that explain the persistence of the representational mechanism.

(2) If R sometimes tokens in the absence of p, and those tokenings occur with sufficient regularity that they do not reduce reproductive success, then selection cannot discriminate p from not-p; the mechanism's proper function must therefore be to indicate some broader condition D that encompasses both.

(3) By iterative application, any tokening that initially looks like error can be redescribed as correct indication of a more inclusive disjunctive condition.

(4) Hence there are no genuinely false representations: every mental tokening correctly represents some state of affairs, even if not the one it seemed to represent.

(5) But if all representations are true, then beliefs that appear contradictory—say, believing a predator is present when it is not, versus believing it is absent—cannot both be false; at least one must correctly represent a broader environmental state that makes both tokenings appropriate.

(6) This collapses the distinction between specific false beliefs and true ones, and seems to erase the very phenomenon—systematic error—that teleosemantics was introduced to naturalize.

(7) Therefore, either teleosemantics cannot accommodate misrepresentation at all, or one of the premises above is wrong.

I want to preserve the idea that content is fixed by selection history (something like 1), and I want to preserve the reality of false belief (something like 6). Which step should I reject, why does it look plausible on its face, and what does giving it up cost for the naturalization project? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than (4), but I want to understand what I am paying.
