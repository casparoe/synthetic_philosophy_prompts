In debates about ancient historiography, source criticism is often analogized to a criminal trial: each text serves as a witness, the historian acts as jury weighing credibility, corroboration works like independent testimonies, Herodotus's first-person inquiry plays the role of direct examination, and Thucydides's exclusion of the fabulous functions as an instruction to disregard hearsay. The analogy is used to defend trusting single authoritative narratives—Herodotus on Egypt, for instance—even without corroboration, on the grounds that one credible witness can suffice for conviction. Lay out the correspondence table precisely: what maps onto witness, jury, cross-examination, rules of evidence, and the burden of proof. Then identify where the mapping collapses—texts are not people with intentions, transmission is not memory, and corroboration across genres is not independent observation. Say whether the analogy quietly imports a modern legal standard of proof that makes ancient single-source reports look more secure than they are, and whether the conclusion—that distant events are knowable through lone credible texts—survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If the trial analogy fails, suggest what a more accurate analogy for textual transmission and mediated testimony would be.
