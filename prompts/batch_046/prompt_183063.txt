A recent essay I encountered attempts to read Berkeley's monetary philosophy — principally in The Querist — through the lens of his metaphysics. It offers this summary: 'Berkeley treated money not as a material commodity but as a purely notional token, an abstract idea in the precise sense he criticised elsewhere, whose sole purpose is to signify labour. Because coins are merely conventional signs, gold and silver possess no intrinsic economic value; this, the essay claims, makes Berkeley the first genuine fiat-currency theorist. He allegedly proposed that Ireland should issue paper notes through a national bank, secured not by metallic reserves but by nothing more than collective public assent, on the grounds that bullion is itself only a bundle of sensible qualities lacking independent substance. Economic crises, on this reading, are never physical scarcities of metal but always cognitive failures: they occur when a population ceases to regard the token as a transparent sign of industry and begins to reify the paper or coin as if it had natural value. In short, the essay presents Berkeley's immaterialism as being applied without mediation to the theory of exchange value.' I suspect this captures a real strand of Berkeley's thought but probably conflates his metaphysical vocabulary with his economic one, and I am unsure whether the bank proposal is rendered fairly. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what Berkeley clearly held or implied about money and labour, what is a defensible modern interpretation, what subtly misrepresents his position — particularly regarding 'abstract ideas', immaterialism, and the backing of notes — and whether the claim that crises are purely cognitive failures follows from the parts that are accurate.
