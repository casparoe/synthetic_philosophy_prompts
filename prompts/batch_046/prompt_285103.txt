Consider the following argument, which I have been using to trouble my graduate seminar on rational choice and subjective value, but which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion undermines the very phenomenon it begins by describing. (1) If an experience is genuinely transformative—altering both what an agent knows and what she values—then prior to undergoing it she lacks the conceptual resources to assign it a determinate subjective utility. (2) Rational choice between options requires comparing them by their expected subjective utility. (3) Therefore a rational agent can never choose to undergo a genuinely transformative experience for its own sake; she can only choose it instrumentally, as a means to some further end whose value she already grasps. (4) But transformative choices—parenthood, religious conversion, emigration—are paradigmatically made for their own sake, not instrumentally. (5) So either such choices are never rational, or the model of rational choice in (2) is inadequate to domains of value shift. (6) If we reject (2) and permit rational choice without prior utility assignment, then we may rationally choose any experience that promises to alter our values, including those that diminish future welfare or erode our capacity for agency, since we possess no stable metric to rule them out. (7) Hence the only consistent positions are that all transformative choices are irrational, or that rational choice is impossible wherever values may change. Both conclusions seem intolerable. Where does the argument slip? Identify the precise step—whether an equivocation between epistemic access and evaluative commitment, a modal slide in what “prior to” requires, or a hidden timelessness assumption in the rationality condition—and explain what abandoning that step costs us for a theory of transformative experience.
