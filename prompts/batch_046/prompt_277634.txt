Please argue that the conventional periodization dividing the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution is not merely a retrospective historiographical construct but corresponds to a genuine structural discontinuity—though one that has been systematically mischaracterized. Specifically, defend the position that the decisive transformation conventionally labeled the Scientific Revolution was not primarily an epistemic shift in cosmology or natural philosophy, but rather an administrative and juridical transformation in the validation of testimony and craft knowledge, visible in the protocols of patent examination, guild arbitration, and evidentiary procedure between roughly 1550 and 1700. On this view, the "revolution" consists not in the adoption of mathematization or experimentation, but in the gradual replacement of a hierarchical, rank-based model of credibility with a procedural, reproducibility-based model. In your argument, address two objections: first, that this account is too diffuse and institutionally heterogeneous to merit the singular term "revolution"; second, that it cannot accommodate the well-documented conceptual innovations associated with canonical figures in astronomy and mechanics, and therefore amounts to reducing intellectual history to bureaucratic history.
