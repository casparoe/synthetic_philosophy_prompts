I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for graduate students in comparative politics with no background in legal philosophy. I want them to grasp not a spectrum from "private" to "surveilled," but the normative architecture of citizen-state information relations: German dignity-based informational self-determination, American administrative openness anchored in transparency traditions, and Chinese social-credit integration that collapses behavioral data into civic scoring. To prevent reductionism, I will frame the comparison through a specific conceptual distinction—informational opacity not as secrecy but as a structurally maintained asymmetry that either enables or disables democratic agency, depending on who controls the aggregation logic. The carrying example should be one concrete administrative interaction, applying for a municipal housing subsidy, traced through each system to show exactly what data is compelled, voluntary, cross-referenced, and sanctioned. I need the teaching arc: which conceptual piece must be in place before comparison can begin (specifically, the difference between data collection and data aggregation), the exact sequence for introducing the three regimes so students do not collapse them into national caricatures, the misconceptions they will bring in predictable order—likely that German rules are mere bureaucracy, that American openness is harmless transparency, and that Chinese scoring is purely punitive rather than infrastructural—and precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted and dismantled using the housing-example thread. I also need one diagnostic question to ask at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the structural logic of aggregation control rather than memorized stereotypes, plus an explicit statement of what I should refuse to cover at this level—perhaps the technical mechanics of GDPR Article 6 lawful bases or the algorithmic architecture of Chinese credit scoring—while being transparent about the omission. Plan the session's explanatory architecture, dependency structure, misconception map, and check for understanding; do not deliver the explanation itself.
