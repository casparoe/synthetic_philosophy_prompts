I've been in this moral philosophy reading group for like three months and we're stuck on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism in a way thats driving me crazy. One side says Rawls is right that utilitarianism doesn't take seriously the distinction between persons — it just adds up welfare like we're all one big container. The other side says thats a misreading, that utilitarianism can totally respect distributive concerns if you build in the right weights or prioritarian rules, and that the "separateness" language is just poetic confusion about aggregation. We've all read the standard texts and nobody's budging.

What I can't tell is whether we're actually disagreeing about ethics, or whether this is one of those debates where both sides are answering different questions and calling it the same thing. Like, is the objection really about distributive justice — that it's unfair to make one person suffer for others' gains — or is it deeper, about the metaphysical fact that there's no "super-person" whose welfare is the sum? And then there's this weird parallel I keep thinking about: in population ethics there's the non-identity problem and the repugnant conclusion, which also involve aggregation across possible people, but there people usually treat it as a technical puzzle about ordering outcomes. With separateness of persons it's treated as a fundamental moral constraint. Are we just applying different standards of proof to structurally identical aggregation problems? Or is there a hidden disagreement about whether personal identity over time is relevant — like, if I'm continuous with my future self in a way I'm not with you, maybe aggregation within a life is okay but across lives it's not?

So my question is: diagnose what's actually going on here. Is this a verbal dispute about "respect for persons"? A hidden empirical claim about psychology? Or are we stuck because both sides have bedrock intuitions about where the boundaries of moral concern lie? And if your diagnosis is right, is there any conceptual move — maybe borrowing from social choice theory or from the literature on interpersonal comparison — that could actually break the deadlock, or should we just accept that this is one of those philosophical questions where progress isn't gonna happen?
