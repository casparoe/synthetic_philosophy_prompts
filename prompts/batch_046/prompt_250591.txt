For a graduate reading group on eighteenth-century ethics and its modern receptions, I have been asked to write down my philosophical profile on suicide and rational self-destruction so that the others can identify internal pressures in my view. Here is where I stand: I accept the conclusion of Hume’s suppressed essay on suicide that the charge of offending God fails, but only on the condition that we read his argument about providence through a strictly naturalistic lens that strips away any teleological residue; I also believe that the essay’s most compelling strand is not theological at all, but rather an implicit proto-utilitarian weighing of suffering against social utility that Hume gestures toward without ever formalizing; nevertheless, I want to maintain that suicide can be rationally impermissible in cases where it clearly maximizes aggregate welfare, because I accept a modified Kantian constraint that treating one’s own future capacity for autonomous agency as a mere instrumental resource violates a duty of self-respect; and I further claim that Hume’s dismissal of the objection that suicide is contrary to nature tacitly relies on a form of moral particularism about bodily integrity—one that evaluates the permissibility of self-harm by attending to highly specific contextual features rather than applying a universal principle—which sits uneasily with the universalist, convention-based account of justice he develops elsewhere. Do these four commitments form a coherent set?

Specifically, I am unsure whether my endorsement of Hume’s anti-theological conclusion is compatible with importing a Kantian deontological constraint that seems to presuppose a metaphysics of the person that Hume’s empiricist framework should reject. There also appears to be a tension between the proto-utilitarian calculus I attribute to Hume and the particularist sensitivity to context I ascribe to him: if welfare maximization is genuinely the hidden engine of his argument, then particularist exceptions should not be able to block it in the way my Kantian constraint demands. At the same time, I wonder whether there is only an apparent conflict between my naturalistic reading of providence and my particularist reading of bodily integrity, since both might be expressions of a shared anti-rationalist tendency in Hume that I have not fully articulated. Could you audit these pairs for me? Rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether it is genuine or dissolves on closer inspection, specify what the cheapest revision would be in each case, and then tell me which single adjustment—dropping the Kantian constraint, abandoning the particularist reading, or weakening my naturalistic interpretation—would restore the most overall consistency without collapsing the profile entirely.
