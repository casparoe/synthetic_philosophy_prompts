I've been trying to think through a worry about future suffering on my own, and I built an argument that seems to prove too much. I can't tell if there's a hidden mistake in one of the steps, or if the conclusion really follows and I just have to live with it.

(1) If we can affect whether a being capable of suffering comes to exist, then that possibility carries moral weight—roughly in proportion to how intense and long-lasting the suffering or well-being would be.
(2) It's plausible that very advanced computer systems, if they ever develop anything like inner experience, could exist in numbers far beyond all biological life, running for timescales we can barely imagine.
(3) Even if the chance that any particular system suffers is small, multiplying that chance by an enormous number of possible instances and an enormous duration gives an expected harm that seems larger than every present-day problem combined.
(4) So anyone contributing to AI development, or even to general economic growth that might eventually produce such systems, is risking a moral catastrophe of unprecedented scale.
(5) But the same structure applies elsewhere: biological reproduction and space settlement could also eventually produce vast populations, some of which might suffer terribly.
(6) That means either we have to stop all technological and reproductive progress, or we have to deny that merely possible future suffering should guide our present choices.
(7) Stopping everything feels like an impossible and self-defeating standard. Denying the moral weight feels like willful blindness once you accept (1).
(8) So the argument seems to push me toward total paralysis, which can't be right.

Which step should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (2), since they seem like straightforward extensions of ordinary moral thinking into an unfamiliar case. If the problem is in (3), is it that I'm misusing probability, or that I'm treating hypothetical beings as if they already count? And whatever I give up, what does it cost me?
