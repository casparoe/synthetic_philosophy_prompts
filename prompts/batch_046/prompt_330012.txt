For a long-form feature I am writing on the boundary between legend and history, I need a classification instrument — a taxonomy or decision tree — that sorts what people actually believe about when a legend becomes history, using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as the three fixed test cases. Rather than asking respondents to name a camp, the instrument should present four concrete diagnostic questions that someone can answer without knowing which scholarly label each leads to, then map the pattern of answers onto named positions.

Please construct the taxonomy around the logic of what I am calling the substrate–superstructure divergence model: at each branch, distinguish between the substrate (material or documentary traces that exist independently of the narrative), the superstructure (the narrative as institutionally transmitted), and the measured divergence between them. The four branching questions should be: (1) Is direct material corroboration of a narrative core necessary for that core to count as historical, or can indirect ecological, linguistic, or institutional traces substitute? (2) Do mythic tropes — divine intervention, archetypal numerology, heroic hyperbole — automatically disqualify a core event, or do they merely indicate narrative accretion around a recoverable substrate? (3) Must the same epistemic standard apply uniformly across all three cases, or is differential assessment legitimate if the substrate–superstructure divergence varies? (4) Does institutional memory — the fact that a community’s identity-forming practices depend upon the event — confer any historical status on the substrate itself, or is it only a feature of the superstructure?

The leaves must include at least these five positions: the Hard Archaeological Minimalist; the Differential Corroborationist; the Institutional Memory Realist; the Narrative Stratificationist; and the Mythic-Structuralist. For each leaf, state the exact combination of answers that lands there, and briefly note what that position says about the three test cases.

Then map the full logical space of answer combinations. Identify at least two combinations that are unoccupied by any named leaf, and diagnose briefly why each is likely empty — whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that these four questions cannot genuinely separate, and specify what further distinction would be needed to split them.

Write in formal British English, suitable for an educated general audience interested in science and ideas. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Where you mention specific traditions or traces — for example Hittite correspondence, the Annales Cambriae, or the Merneptah Stele — treat them as general-knowledge reference points without verbatim quotation or precise attribution that requires verification.
