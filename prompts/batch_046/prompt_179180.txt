I am preparing the framing document for an advanced seminar on scalar moral patienthood, and I want to be precise about the standards of adequacy before we examine particular theories. The focus is not merely the familiar boundary between sentient animals and non-sentient matter, but the entire gradation: insects with contested nociceptive architectures, organisms whose phenomenal states are ambiguous or minimal, and—crucially—artificial or quasi-artificial systems such as thermostats equipped with adaptive feedback loops that exhibit functional analogues of homeostatic regulation without any plausible phenomenal consciousness.

Write the desiderata that any adequate, non-binary theory of moral status must satisfy. For each criterion, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it: evolutionary continuity and the avoidance of arbitrary thresholds; the distinction between proto-sentience and full phenomenal experience; the need to prevent panpsychist or functionalist inflation that would grant status to mere regulatory devices; the problem of specifying comparative weights or exchange rates across grades without collapsing gradation into linear aggregation; and consistency with our best neuroscientific and behavioural evidence regarding invertebrate cognition. You should flag which pairs of these desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable—especially any tension between demanding continuous gradation and demanding precise, determinate boundaries that exclude thermostats—and you should distinguish non-negotiable requirements from those that merely reflect the commitments of particular camps (for instance, phenomenal consciousness as the sole ground versus functional or relational criteria). Keep the conceptual work on the list of requirements and their conflicts; a brief indication of how gradualist sentientism, capacity-based tiering, and relational accounts stand against your criteria will suffice, but do not turn this into a comprehensive survey.
