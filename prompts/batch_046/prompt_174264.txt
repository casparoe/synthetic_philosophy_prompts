A recurring move in debates over pragmatic encroachment compares stakes-sensitive knowledge to graded examinations: just as the same answer can pass or fail depending on how consequential the exam is for the student, the same body of evidence is said to yield knowledge or fall short depending on what is practically at risk. The analogy is used to render encroachment intuitive—thresholds shift with context—while critics charge that it treats knowledge as an institutional award rather than a cognitive achievement. Lay out the correspondence precisely: what plays the role of the grader, the exam content, the passing threshold, the student, and the institutional rules? Then identify where the mapping fractures: do practical stakes function like an external authority setting a curve, or like an internal feature of the epistemic situation itself? Does the comparison smuggle in a constructivism about knowledge that pragmatic encroachment need not accept, and if the exam analogy collapses, does the conclusion that knowledge is genuinely stakes-sensitive survive on independent grounds, or was the analogy carrying the intuition all along? If a replacement is available—say, engineering safety margins or legal standards of proof—say briefly whether it avoids the same fractures.
