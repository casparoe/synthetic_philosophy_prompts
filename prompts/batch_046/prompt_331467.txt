I've been trying to make sense of the big-picture histories—Harari's story of cognitive and agricultural revolutions, Diamond's geographic-determinist account of why some societies developed differently, and the counter-narrative offered by Graeber and Wengrow that stresses deep human equality and agency. Without any formal academic training, I've come to hold a view that feels like a compromise but might be mistaken. I think these grand narratives are not best evaluated as true or false descriptions of past events. Instead, they function as what I would call scale-bridging metaphors: cognitive tools that let a human mind hold millennia in view without tracking every local choice. Their value is not standard explanatory accuracy but functional coherence—they orient us in deep time. The critics are right that such stories suppress variation and individual agency, yet I think they miss that this suppression is part of the tool's design rather than a simple error, unless it hardens into what I call narrative inertia: the point at which revising the story requires abandoning the scale-bridging framework itself.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for familiar objections about Eurocentrism or selective archaeology—those are well known. I want the evidence, arguments, or results from other areas—perhaps about how human cognition actually processes long timescales, or about the philosophy of scientific explanation—that should move me to abandon the idea that grand narratives are functional orientation tools rather than failed explanations. Which defeaters would a committed defender of my view concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank them from fatal, where the scale-bridging idea collapses, to merely awkward. And please be direct: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, so that any counterevidence can be absorbed by redefining "function," or does it genuinely risk something?
