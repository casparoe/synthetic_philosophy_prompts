I am a PhD candidate in philosophy working between formal epistemology and social epistemology. In two weeks I will present my job-market paper at a philosophy of science and epistemology seminar. The thesis I am defending applies Fitch's paradox of knowability not to individual knowers but to long-running scientific communities treated as distributed epistemic agents. Specifically, I claim that the standard individual-level resolutions—rejecting the universal knowability principle, restricting closure under conjunction, or indexing knowledge to temporal stages—leave intact a structurally parallel collective problem: if every empirical truth is in principle discoverable by the scientific community, then for any undiscovered true p there exists an undiscovered true conjunction (p and the community does not know p), which, if collectively discoverable, yields contradiction. I argue that dissolving this requires rejecting distributive closure of collective knowledge over arbitrary logical combination, treating collective knowledge as an irreducibly social achievement rather than an aggregation of individual closures.

The audience will be mostly formal epistemologists comfortable with modal logic and closure principles, several with verificationist sympathies who already distrust the knowability premise, and one senior external commentator known for insisting that logical paradoxes must be solved at the level of individual cognition rather than social structures. They will know Fitch and the canonical literature well, but they will regard my social framing as either a category mistake or an unnecessary complication.
