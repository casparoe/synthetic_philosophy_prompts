As a retired geophysicist who has recently taken up philosophy of science and archaeology, I am trying to systematize my judgments about when material remains justify attributing specific beliefs or institutions to past societies. I want a precise principle, or a clearly bounded family of principles, that captures my verdicts below; I also want an explicit statement of which verdict must be overturned if no unified principle works, and a definite prediction for the unconsidered case I describe at the end. Please take a committed position rather than merely surveying options.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

Case 1. A region yields thousands of standardized weights and uniform seal impressions distributed across settlements that are not adjacent. Verdict: justified in inferring a centralized administrative institution regulating exchange, not merely local agreements.

Case 2. The same region yields exactly one weight of foreign design in a domestic refuse layer. Verdict: not justified in inferring adoption of foreign metrological beliefs; accidental loss or scavenging is the better explanation.

Case 3. A large urban settlement shows monumental architecture and standardized storage, but no writing implements or durable inscribed media survive. Verdict: not justified in inferring absence of literacy or record-keeping; perishable materials adequately explain the gap.

Case 4. A burial complex shows identical grave orientation, standardized grave goods, and recurring iconography across generations, with no practical engineering reason for the uniformity. Verdict: justified in inferring a shared cosmological belief system.

Case 5. A different region shows the same standardized orientation and goods, but only in two adjacent sites separated by a river, with all other local sites using different practices. Verdict: justified in inferring shared belief across those two sites, but not across the wider region.

The tension I feel is between Case 3 and Case 4: in both we face absence of direct evidence for a non-material feature, yet I treat absence as defeating inference in one case but not the other. Is there a principle—perhaps turning on whether the absence occurs in a domain where taphonomic bias predictably suppresses traces versus one where the expected material correlates of the belief should have survived—that reconciles all five? Or must I drop one? Please state your principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and apply it to this further case: a settlement yields no religious iconography whatsoever, but its domestic architecture is systematically oriented to a solar azimuth that has no thermal or defensive advantage. Does your principle license inferring a cosmological belief?

Please conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by the core label or clause of your endorsed principle, enclosed exactly as \answer{Verdict: ...}.
