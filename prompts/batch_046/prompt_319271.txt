A colleague who works in cognitive neuroscience summarized Dennett's heterophenomenological method to me as follows: "Heterophenomenology requires the investigator to take a subject's first-person reports at face value as descriptions of an internal phenomenal field, but to maintain a strict methodological neutrality about whether that field corresponds to real properties or is merely a cognitive artifact—analogous to how an experimental physicist treats instrument readings without ontological commitment to the instrument's internal states. By applying this epoché-like bracketing, the researcher can construct a rigorous third-person science of consciousness that maps the structure of subjective experience without resolving, or even engaging, the hard problem; Dennett's aim was to show that phenomenal consciousness is best understood as a benign user-illusion that can be fully described in functional and neural terms, leaving no explanatory residue." My colleague insists this is not a misreading but the operational core of Dennett's approach. Something about this feels off—Dennett is usually read as rejecting the Cartesian theater rather than neutrally describing it, and the comparison to phenomenological bracketing seems foreign to his naturalism—but I want to be precise about where the summary slides from interpretation into distortion. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what accurately reflects Dennett's procedure and his stance on phenomenal properties, what conflates heterophenomenology with other methodological traditions, and what Dennett explicitly denies about the ontology of consciousness.
