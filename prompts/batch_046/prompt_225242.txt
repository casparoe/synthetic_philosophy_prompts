I have been trying to hold together the following five commitments regarding moral psychology and normative ethics, and I want to know whether they form a consistent package or whether I am implicitly relying on incompatible motivations.

(1) I accept the situationist critique in its stronger form: there is scant empirical evidence for robust, cross-situationally stable character traits; observed behavioral variance is better explained by situational parameters, framing effects, and contextual cues than by global dispositions.

(2) I nevertheless defend virtue ethics as the correct normative framework, specifically on the grounds that it directs moral evaluation toward practical wisdom (phronesis) and toward the agent’s cultivated responsiveness to particular circumstances, rather than toward rule-compliance or aggregate welfare calculation.

(3) I maintain that genuine virtue attribution requires cross-situational consistency: to ascribe courage, temperance, or justice to an agent, that agent must display the relevant excellence not only in accommodating contexts but also under duress, anonymity, social pressure, and cognitive load.

(4) I hold that moral education should therefore aim at long-term habituation designed to produce stable dispositional patterns—what might be called global virtues—rather than merely teaching situational management strategies, environmental design, or first-order response rules.

(5) At the same time, I deny that situationism undermines virtue ethics, arguing that virtue is best understood not as a first-order disposition with a high cross-contextual base-rate, but as a second-order meta-virtue: a calibrated capacity to select and weight first-order responses according to the normative salience of situational features. On this view, the empirical variability of first-order behavior actually supports the virtue-theoretic framework, because it shows that moral excellence consists in flexible calibration rather than in rigid trait-expression.

Please audit this position. Which pairs of commitments generate genuine logical or motivational tension, and which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection? Order the real tensions by severity, and for each specify exactly which commitment must be modified or abandoned and what that revision costs for the remaining four. I am not signaling which commitment I prefer to keep; I want a point-by-point assessment of the dependencies among them, with particular attention to whether (3) can survive together with (1) and (5).
