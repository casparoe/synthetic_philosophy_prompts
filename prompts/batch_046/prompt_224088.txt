Consider four institutional arrangements at the boundary of religion and state: in England, the Church of England remains established—the monarch serves as its Supreme Governor, bishops sit ex officio in the House of Lords as Lords Spiritual, and church canons take legal effect only through parliamentary enactment—yet the state imposes no religious test for public office and funds a pluralistic education system including other faiths; in Germany, religious communities meeting statutory criteria may register as public-law corporations (Körperschaften des öffentlichen Rechts), which entitles them to state-collected church taxes (Kirchensteuer) deducted via the income-tax system, a mechanism generally unavailable to secular worldviews and unregistered religious groups; in France, laïcité prohibits the state from recognizing religious identity in public institutions, yet private religious schools operating under contract with the state receive public funding; and in the United States, the Establishment Clause forbids government endorsement of religion while the Free Exercise Clause protects religious practice, producing a framework of non-establishment coupled with selective accommodation.

Treat these not as comparative-law data but as four answers to a single philosophical question: what is the proper relation between religious authority, corporate religious identity, and sovereign state power?

Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes the German cooperative model coherent: does it presuppose that neutrality requires equal facilitation of religious corporate life rather than equal distance, that religious communities are partners in the public sphere deserving of administrative infrastructure, or that sovereignty can be delegated to ecclesiastical bodies without violating liberal equality? Then examine whether the English establishment can rest on the same foundation, given that it assigns constitutional roles to one church while disclaiming coercive authority over conscience, and ask whether this requires a distinct concept of establishment—one that privileges symbolic sovereignty over fiscal or juridical power.

Next, identify which commitments are genuinely necessary and which are dispensable rationalizations. Must any regime that collects taxes for churches accept a substantive (rather than procedural) theory of religious neutrality? Does the English model require a particular metaphysics of corporate personality—the church as a corporation sole or as a body politic embedded in the constitution—or can it be justified on purely historical grounds without philosophical entailment? Is France's simultaneous prohibition of religious display in public schools and funding of private religious schools consistent with a single principle, or does it smuggle in a distinction between public identity and private association that collapses under scrutiny?

Finally, determine whether the four regimes are applications of one underlying theory of secular governance—perhaps a pluralist theory that permits institutional variation according to historical context—or whether they embody incompatible first principles about recognition, endorsement, and the boundaries of the public sphere. Do not cite specific statutes, court rulings, or scholarly works; focus on the conceptual architecture that makes each practice intelligible and on whether that architecture can be shared across cases.
