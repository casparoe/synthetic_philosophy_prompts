I work in software engineering and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading about the problem of other minds and keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern that I do not know how to classify. The pattern runs as follows. A person begins with a direct behavioral analogy: another human winces and withdraws from a stimulus in the same way I do, so I take this as reason to attribute pain to them. When an interlocutor points out that a synthetic system could reproduce the identical behavioral profile without any inner experience, the first person does not abandon the analogical inference. Instead, they append an asymmetry condition—first something concrete like “this applies only to entities with biological nervous systems,” then, once evolutionary convergence or synthetic neural architectures are raised as counterexamples, something more abstract like “only to entities with a shared evolutionary lineage” or “only within a shared form of life.” Each appended condition is individually reasonable, and each saves the original inference from the immediate counterexample, but the conditions are always formulated so that they exclude the challenging case while leaving the human case untouched. Over successive rounds the conditions grow more abstract and harder to apply in advance, yet the core claim—that we are entitled to the inference for other humans—remains protected. I am familiar with the general philosophy-of-science idea that theories can accumulate protective rules, but I am asking about this specific move in other-minds reasoning: the iterative retreat to ever more general asymmetry conditions that are tailored to preserve the analogical entitlement for humans alone. Does this pattern have an established name in the philosophy of mind or epistemology literature? How does it differ precisely from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from ad hoc reasoning, and from the so-called “no true Scotsman” maneuver? If there is no standard term, what should its boundaries be—does it cover only conceptual guard conditions, or also empirical ones—and what would be the clearest way to distinguish it from nearby failure modes? I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
