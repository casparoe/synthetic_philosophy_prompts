I'm trying to formalize a specific interpretation of the Humans-Consulting-Humans (HCH) framework—not as a literal organizational blueprint for scaling human reasoning, but as a normative idealization of fully factored deliberation. The view I have in mind treats the tree structure as a limiting case in which every judgment is decomposed into its smallest cognitively capable submodule, leaving no residual black-box intuition at any node. To make this precise, I have been using the concept of an unfactorable judgment residue (UJR): any decision point that cannot be further split into independent, verifiable sub-questions without losing the normative force of the original judgment. On my account, a perfect HCH tree is one with zero UJR—every branch terminates in a task that is either mechanizable or transparently justifiable to its parent node, and the root's authority derives entirely from this complete factorization rather than from any irreducible holistic expertise.

What would have to be true for this idealization to fail—not merely to be impractical, but to be conceptually misdirected? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which formal results or empirical observations about collective cognition would show that some UJR is unavoidable in any deliberative architecture, and that its presence is not a failure mode but a necessary feature of sound reasoning? Are there rival models of distributed cognition—perhaps ones emphasizing emergent coherence over modular decomposition—that predict outcomes a zero-UJR HCH tree cannot generate, and if so, what would those predictions look like in practice? I am especially interested in whether defenders of factored-cognition ideals would themselves concede that a minimal residue is indispensable—say, for integrating values that resist quantification, or for resolving underdetermined trade-offs between incommensurable goods—and whether such concessions collapse the position into a more modest claim about efficiency rather than about the nature of rational deliberation.

Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive (for example, a proof that any consistent reasoning system above a certain complexity must contain unfactorable nodes) from evidence that would merely be awkward (for example, historical examples of successful but messy human collaboration). I also want to know whether this position risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated by qualifications—such as defining UJR away as noise rather than structure—to the point of unfalsifiability. If it is well insulated, is that insulation a theoretical virtue, or does it indicate that the idealization has been protected from meaningful defeat? Finally, what would you personally find most persuasive as a defeater: a conceptual demonstration that holistic judgment is constitutive of rationality, or an empirical case showing that the best real-world deliberative outcomes consistently depend on precisely the kind of residue this model eliminates?
