I have just spent the better part of an evening in a rather intense discussion with a close friend about Pascal’s treatment of justice and force — in particular his well-known observation that justice without force is powerless, whilst force without justice is tyrannical. My friend advanced a reading that I find suspicious but cannot fully refute: he argued that Pascal is really outlining a form of recursive legitimisation, according to which sovereign power is justified solely by its efficacy in enforcing a conception of justice, whilst justice itself is defined retroactively by whatever the sovereign power successfully enforces. I countered that this sounds more like Schmitt or perhaps a crude legal positivism, yet my friend insisted that Pascal’s own framework invites it, since he appears to treat law as the necessary but arbitrary stitching-together of two principles that have no intrinsic affinity.

This brought me to what I think is the crux, though I worry I am smuggling in a false presupposition. Pascal places justice in the order of mind and force in the order of body, and he seems to regard these orders not merely as distinct domains but as genuinely incommensurable registers — especially given his claim that the heart possesses reasons that reason itself does not know. If that incommensurability is taken seriously, then any human institution that attempts to unite justice and force in positive law is not simply imperfect or tragically flawed, but ontologically confused: it treats bodily power as if it could serve rational justice, when in fact they belong to entirely different modes of being. Furthermore, if we introduce the third order of charity, should we not conclude that every juridical synthesis is actively deceptive, because it pretends that a bodily mechanism can be the vehicle for mental justice, when true justice might belong only to that higher order? I am therefore unsure whether Pascal’s position contains a hidden contradiction, or whether I am misunderstanding what he means by an “order” — perhaps he intends them as functional distinctions within a single ontology rather than as metaphysically separated strata.

Could you analyse this problem for me, writing in British English, and clarify whether my worry about ontological confusion is a misreading? I am particularly interested in whether Pascal’s framework genuinely allows for a non-deceptive union of justice and force in human law, or whether the inevitability of their separation is precisely the point. Please do not provide a bibliography or list of fragment references that I could not verify without access to a library; I would much prefer a direct conceptual analysis that addresses the logic of the orders, the nature of recursive legitimisation, and whether the introduction of charity renders all juridical compromise not merely weak but structurally dishonest. I should also be grateful if you could flag any specific misunderstandings in my framing, since I have the nagging sense that I am asking the question in the wrong terms.
