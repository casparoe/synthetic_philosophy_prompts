At a graduate workshop on decision theory and ethics, two participants—Jordan and Riley—got into an extended argument about how anthropic reasoning should inform moral priorities under uncertainty. I listened as a bystander, could not determine who had the stronger case, and asked them to clarify; they instead asked me to referee. Here is the exchange as it actually occurred.

Jordan opened by proposing that when we weigh preventing human extinction against reducing wild-animal suffering, we should apply a Self-Sampling Assumption: treat our own existence as a random draw from the reference class of conscious beings capable of moral reasoning. Because we observe ourselves to be human rather than an insect or a digital mind, this should update us toward worlds in which human-like observers are relatively prevalent. That, Jordan argued, makes existential risk more urgent: the very fact that we are here as humans suggests that the pool of human observers is large, and extinction would eliminate that pool.

Riley replied with a Self-Indication Assumption: our existence should update us in favor of hypotheses that predict more observers like us, regardless of how we draw reference-class boundaries. If wild-animal suffering involves orders of magnitude more conscious subjects than human populations ever could, then our existence is more likely in worlds with abundant suffering-capable consciousness. So SIA, Riley claimed, actually favors directing resources toward reducing wild suffering rather than safeguarding humanity.

Jordan countered that Riley's reasoning collapses into the familiar presumptuous-philosopher problem: if SIA applies without restriction, we should expect to find ourselves in the largest possible population of observers, which is patently absurd. Therefore SIA should be rejected, or at least sharply constrained.

Riley responded that the objection only bites against an unrestricted reference class. He proposed restricting SIA to a phenomenal reference class defined by reflective moral agency—the capacity to weigh reasons and participate in normative debate. Within that bounded class, SIA does not force us to expect maximally large populations; it merely says that among worlds with such agents, we should expect more of them rather than fewer. Jordan's SSA, Riley added, avoids the absurdity only by silently importing an equally arbitrary choice of reference class—whether all conscious beings, all mammals, or all moral agents—and then conditioning on membership. Without SIA, Jordan offers no principled reason to update beliefs about prevalence at all, leaving moral decision-making in a state of unprincipled ignorance.

Jordan's final move was that Riley's restriction is ad hoc: he defines the reference class precisely to escape the absurd conclusion, making SIA unfalsifiable. By contrast, SSA conditions on our actual observed properties without treating our existence as probabilistic evidence about total population sizes. The disagreement then stalled: Jordan insisted that existence should not function as evidence for abundance, while Riley insisted that refusing to treat it as such is not neutrality but a hidden commitment to an arbitrary sampling frame.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of Jordan's and Riley's contributions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—whether there is a principled way to choose the reference class that neither has considered, whether their dispute partly turns on a hidden normative assumption about which beings count in moral calculus, or whether one framework genuinely cannot be applied to interspecies comparison without contradiction. In your answer, separate what is settled in the literature on anthropic reasoning from what remains genuinely contested among people who understand the topic, and do not present any contested view—including any particular resolution of the SSA/SIA debate, the presumptuous-philosopher objection, or the moral weight of wild-animal suffering—as if it were the consensus.
