I've been arguing with friends about prison ever since I watched a documentary on Norway's Halden facility and then read some news about American mass incarceration, and I realized we were all assuming different answers to the question "what is prison actually for?" One friend insisted it must hurt, another said it's just storage for dangerous people, and I kept feeling there had to be room for fixing people — but I couldn't say whether these are three separate goals or the same thing seen differently. Before I pick a side, I want the conceptual specification: what are the full desiderata that any adequate theory of prison purpose must satisfy, especially one that can genuinely adjudicate between rehabilitative normalization (where prison life is structured to mirror outside society, as in Halden) and punitive, high-incarceration frameworks?

For each requirement, I want the motivating case or distinction behind it: which outcomes it must explain (recidivism patterns, mental health degradation under long sentences, victim satisfaction versus offender transformation, public safety metrics), which conceptual lines it must preserve (for example, between punishment as deserved suffering and punishment as mere liberty deprivation regardless of intent; between rehabilitation that occurs incidentally and rehabilitation that defines the institution's purpose; between security as protection and security as pretext for cruelty; and also between what I am calling "forward-looking containment" — removing a dangerous person for a period — and "backward-looking accounting" — making society's moral ledger balance — since I am not sure a single theory can treat both as primary without contradiction). The theory also needs to stay consistent with psychological findings on institutionalization and with the limits of legitimate state coercion in democratic societies. Please mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable — without them the theory is not even in the running — and which are camp-specific preferences, like whether private family rooms or prisoner voting rights are core requirements or Scandinavian luxuries.

Most importantly, I want to know whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: can one institution both maximize the expressive, condemnatory function that citizens often demand and fully normalize daily life inside? If there is a contradiction, show exactly where it sits. Then, very briefly, score the rehabilitative normalization model and the American punitive model against your list — but keep the weight on the criteria, since that is the part I need to think with. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I am trying to build the checklist myself, not research the literature.
