Exam question: In Amartya Sen's liberal paradox, minimal liberalism (L) requires that each individual have decisive control over at least one pair of social states that affect them personally. The canonical two-person example demonstrates that no social decision rule can simultaneously satisfy L, the Pareto principle (Unanimity), and transitivity of social preference. Now consider a proposed continuous extension: outcomes lie in a convex space θ ∈ [0,1], each individual's liberal domain is a convex subset of this space, and preferences are represented by continuous utility functions. A commentator claims that convexity dissolves the paradox, because the intersection of convex liberal domains is guaranteed to be non-empty whenever the Pareto frontier is non-empty, allowing a median selection mechanism within that intersection to satisfy minimal liberalism, Pareto efficiency, and transitivity together. Evaluate this claim precisely. Does the paradox survive? Address whether convexity resolves the logical tension or merely relocates it, be explicit about which axioms are preserved or violated, and discuss whether the proposed median mechanism respects minimal liberalism in the specific sense Sen defined.

Student answer: Sen's paradox arises because minimal liberalism assigns each person decisive authority over a discrete pair of outcomes, and in the two-person case these assignments overlap in a cyclical manner that forces social intransitivity. The three conditions are Pareto efficiency, minimal liberalism, and transitive social ordering. In the continuous convex formulation, each person controls a convex region rather than an isolated pair. Because convex sets with single-peaked preferences have non-empty intersections, the Pareto frontier necessarily lies inside the overlap of liberal domains. A median rule applied within this intersection selects a point that respects both individuals' decisive regions, so minimal liberalism holds. Pareto efficiency is satisfied because the point lies on the frontier, and transitivity is preserved because the median rule induces a complete ordering over the parameter space. Therefore the commentator is correct: convexity eliminates the paradox. Sen's result depends essentially on the discrete, non-convex structure of the choice set; once we move to continuous parameters, consistency is restored. In fact, Sen implicitly imports Arrow's unrestricted domain condition into the liberal-rights framework, which is why the conflict emerges. Relaxing unrestricted domain to convex subsets allows all three conditions to be met simultaneously.
