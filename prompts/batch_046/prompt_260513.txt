In my reading on the philosophy of collective action, I have encountered a dispute that has apparently persisted for decades without resolution. One side maintains that genuinely joint action—two people carrying a table together, for instance—requires an irreducible collective intentionality, a primitive "we-intention" that cannot be decomposed into individual mental states plus mutual belief about each other. The opposing side holds that the very same phenomena can be fully explained by each agent possessing an individual intention whose content references the joint outcome and is coordinated through common knowledge of the other’s plan. The arguments on both sides seem thoroughly catalogued—concerning whether collective commitment introduces a unique normative bond, whether individual intentions can contain joint goals without circularity, and whether betrayal in joint action proves the presence of an irreducible shared mental state—yet neither camp appears to move the other.

What puzzles me is that both sides seem to assume face-to-face, synchronous interaction as their central case. I keep thinking about a different configuration: staggered, asynchronous collaboration in which contributors to an open-source project never meet, share no simultaneous mental state, and may remain anonymous to one another, yet their successive modifications are guided by shared norms and converge on a unified artifact. This looks like joint production without real-time mutual awareness. I wonder whether the philosophical stalemate is partly a failure to distinguish this distributed, temporally extended case from synchronous cooperative action, and whether the two philosophical camps are in fact answering different questions—one about immediate interpersonal coordination, the other about the metaphysics of institutional or distributed agency—while using the single heading "joint action."

Is the disagreement substantive, concerning the actual ontology of intentionality? Is it verbal, turning on whether the term "intention" must be reserved for individual mental states? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps about the mechanisms of distributed cognition or the logic of multi-agent planning—that philosophical argument alone cannot settle? Furthermore, does my asynchronous case belong within the same debate, or does it reveal that the original question is ill-posed because it collapses distinct phenomena? I would like a diagnosis of the impasse’s structure, supported by what each side actually claims about reducibility and collective commitment, together with your judgment about what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction separating synchronous from distributed joint action, a formal model of staggered collective commitment, an empirical study of distributed coordination, or an argument that no further philosophical resolution should be expected and why.
