I have recently begun studying philosophy in earnest after retiring from experimental biochemistry, and at last Tuesday’s departmental seminar on causal explanation I witnessed a sharp disagreement that I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction. Two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who writes on explanation in medicine and molecular biology, and Dr. James Whitmore, a metaphysician with pronounced Humean leanings—fell into an extended exchange over whether omissions can ever be genuine causes, drawing repeatedly on examples from scientific practice rather than on abstract intuition alone.

Vasquez opened by observing that laboratory papers routinely treat absences as explanatory: when a transcriptional repressor is missing, gene expression proceeds, and authors write that the absence of the repressor caused the observed phenotype. Whitmore replied that this is merely elliptical for the presence of activators, basal transcription machinery, and favourable chromatin states; the omission is a background condition, not a causal producer in its own right. Vasquez then pressed an epidemiological parallel, noting that public-health reports attribute infection outbreaks to failure to vaccinate, and asked whether Whitmore would dismiss all such attributions as mere metaphor. He conceded their pragmatic value for resource allocation but insisted they are metaphysically misleading, since causation requires an event or process and absences are not events. Vasquez proposed a counterfactual dependency criterion: had the repressor been present, expression would not have occurred, so the absence satisfies the standard condition for being causally relevant. Whitmore objected that this renders causation promiscuous, because the absence of countless unrelated conditions—say, the absence of a nearby solar flare—would equally satisfy the counterfactual, yet we never cite solar calm as a cause of gene expression. Vasquez answered that causal claims are restricted by mechanistic specificity: the absence matters only within an identified biochemical pathway, and this restriction saves the counterfactual account without collapsing into constant conjunction. Whitmore then argued that invoking mechanisms to salvage omissions is circular, since mechanistic accounts themselves presuppose positive interactions between entities, and an omission, having no properties of its own, can contribute nothing to a mechanism.

As someone whose own research treated negative results and missing inhibitors as genuinely explanatorily significant, I find both lines of reasoning compelling in parts but cannot see where the decisive fault lies. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each of Vasquez’s and Whitmore’s responses from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, and indicate what either party is missing? I am particularly keen to know whether there is a principled middle ground that respects ordinary scientific practice—where omissions are cited without embarrassment—without committing to the metaphysical reality of negative entities or to an unacceptably promiscuous counterfactualism.
