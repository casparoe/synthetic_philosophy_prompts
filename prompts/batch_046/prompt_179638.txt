Write a sestina of roughly thirty-nine lines—six sestets followed by a three-line envoi—on the disappearance of moral norms such as dueling and chattel slavery. Choose six end-words drawn from the vocabulary of moral revolution—suggested: honor, interest, argument, shame, reputation, silence—and rotate them according to the sestina scheme so that each stanza forces these terms into new grammatical and conceptual relationships. Let this formal repetition enact the philosophical dispute over whether such norms collapse through rational argument, shifting material interests, or transformations in codes of honor and disgrace (the Williams thesis versus the Appiah-style emphasis on reasoning and interest). The poem should treat both positions without citing texts, providing a bibliography, or including direct quotations; render the causal mechanisms through image, syntax, and the physical placement of the repeated words. In particular, use the envoi to bring the honor-line and the interest-line into direct adjacency—whether by rhyme, repetition, or syntactic collision—so that the form itself stages their unresolved intersection rather than resolving the debate into a single story. The result should be readable as an independent meditation for someone unfamiliar with the literature, yet accurate enough in its structural mapping that a reader versed in the philosophy recognizes the tension between honor-based and argument-based accounts of moral change. Do not sermonize or conclude with a clear causal verdict; allow the compulsive returns of the form to suggest that norms die in the friction between registers, not in one alone.
