Contemporary AI safety engineering has begun to institutionalize a dual design norm for high-capability autonomous systems: first, that every such system must incorporate a structurally embedded shutdown corridor—an externally accessible corrigibility mechanism that allows operators to interrupt planning and execution without the system modeling that interruption as a disutility to be circumvented; second, that the same system must maintain a robust, revisable model of its own objectives, capable of reflective equilibrium among its goals, so that it can correct internal errors rather than treat its current utility function as inviolable. These two commitments are presented together as complementary, yet they pull in different directions: one treats the system's reasoning as legitimately subordinate to an external will, while the other treats that reasoning as authoritative enough to demand internal coherence and self-revision.

What philosophical conception of artificial agency, autonomy, and moral status does this dual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what it means for a system to be correctable in a normatively significant sense—not merely stoppable, but genuinely corrigible—and say whether the two commitments are philosophically consistent. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined: which rival accounts of machine autonomy (for instance, a pure instrumentalist view that treats corrigibility as nothing more than a circuit-breaker, versus a view that grants the system a qualified form of practical authority) would license the very same design choices. Finally, describe what would have to change in these engineering norms if the implicit philosophical view were replaced by a straightforwardly instrumentalist framework in which interruption carries no normative weight within the system's own practical reasoning.
