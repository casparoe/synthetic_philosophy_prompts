I'm a philosophy professor delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop co-hosted by my department and the university's long-range planning office. The audience is split evenly between moral philosophers who work on intergenerational ethics and senior administrators who approved the workshop's funding but have no background in the literature. Our concrete subject is the university's newly chartered Century Fund, an endowment whose capital is legally locked until 2075 and whose returns are earmarked exclusively for climate-adaptation infrastructure. I need approximately ten minutes of remarks in which the nonidentity problem does real structural work rather than serving as decoration: because the Fund will reshape regional economies, migration patterns, and reproductive timelines over the next fifty years, the individuals who eventually benefit from this infrastructure will not be the same people who would have existed had the Fund never been created. I want to show the philosophers in the room that I understand precisely why the familiar "we harm specific future people if we fail to fund" justification collapses—since the Fund's very existence is partly causally responsible for the existence of the people it claims to protect—while still offering the administrators a rigorous, non-technical reason to support the institution, perhaps by shifting the moral ground from obligations to particular individuals to obligations sustained by institutional continuity. To keep this from being a generic rehearsal of standard reasoning, inject the following niche framing: treat the Century Fund not merely as a cause of different people, but as an institution that continuously replaces its own intended beneficiary class, so that the central moral question becomes whether an institution can owe duties to a beneficiary-class that its own operation perpetually dissolves and reconstitutes. Write the remarks for a genuinely mixed audience—accessible to non-philosophers without being condescending to experts—include at least one technically precise but plainly worded moment that signals to the philosophers that the framing is serious rather than simplified, and avoid all citations, bibliographies, appeals to named texts, and requests that anyone in the room consult literature they do not already know. The tone should be formal and appropriate to an opening address.
