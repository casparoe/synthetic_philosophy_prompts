Design a classroom activity or game for a university seminar on collective memory and political myth—14 students, one 80-minute session—centered on the idea that “lost cause” myths depend structurally on keeping defeat unredeemed. The familiar archetypes are the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk, but treat them only as shared reference points, not as cases requiring research. The core mechanism to be felt is this: a defeated collective gains a distinctive moral authority precisely because its grievance remains open, and resolving the grievance—accepting a settlement—therefore dissolves that authority rather than fulfilling it. Structure the session so that participants work in four small groups, each assigned one archetype, and must decide, over two rounds, whether to “settle” the cause (closing the grievance, gaining tangible resources, but losing the group’s special status) or “preserve” it (maintaining the unresolved claim, retaining collective identity and moral standing, but bearing continuous costs). The rules should include a hidden or emergent scoring logic—perhaps a “legacy point” system awarded by an external “memory tribunal” (played by rotating students) that consistently values unresolved sacrifice over resolution—so that groups discover through play that settlement is penalized and preservation rewarded before the facilitator names the pattern. Include specific roles (e.g., archivist, negotiator, dissenter), a timeline breaking the 80 minutes into framing, round-one deliberation, tribunal evaluation, round-two revision, and debrief, and a payoff scheme that uses only social stakes—public ranking, collective commitment statements, or restricted speaking privileges—rather than real money. In the debrief, map each round onto a feature of lost-cause mythology: round one onto the romanticization of defeat, the tribunal vote onto the social reward for perpetual grievance, and round two onto the rationalization of continued sacrifice. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that one group breaks the frame and treats settlement as obviously rational, or that the tribunal applies inconsistent standards—and advise how the facilitator should handle it without breaking the immersion. Finally, include an honest limitation: the classroom rules can reproduce the strategic incentive to keep defeat alive, but they cannot reproduce the multi-generational transmission or the fusion with religious or ethnic eschatology that real-world myths require; state which feature the facilitator must therefore supply verbally and how to do it without undermining the participants’ own discovery. Do not require bibliographies, citations, or obscure historical details.
