As a novelist, I am drawn to institutional rituals that smuggle metaphysics into technical procedure. Consider the peer-review and policy-certification practices surrounding integrated assessment models in climate economics: reviewers routinely defend manifestly false structural assumptions—perfect rationality, representative-agent aggregation, damage functions calibrated to historical correlations that ignore adaptation—by invoking the principle that unrealistic premises are harmless so long as predictions hold, a defense in the spirit of Friedman’s well-known F-twist. Yet these same models are then used to derive precise normative prescriptions—optimal carbon-tax paths, intergenerational discount rates, welfare-maximizing emission targets—as if the false premises had no bearing on what ought to be done. Meanwhile, models that retain realistic behavioral assumptions but yield less precise point forecasts are treated as scientifically weaker and rarely admitted to policy committees. What philosophy of scientific representation, or of the boundary between description and prescription, does this double standard presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat predictive instruments as normatively transparent, allowing welfare conclusions to be laundered through them? Is it coherent? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what policy evaluation would have to become if we rejected that implicit philosophy and insisted that structurally unrealistic assumptions be quarantined from normative conclusions.
