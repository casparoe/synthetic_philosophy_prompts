The debate over whether constitutional entrenchment—eternity clauses, supermajoritarian amendment rules, or unamendable provisions—operates as a genuine Ulysses contract has been stalled in constitutional theory for decades. One camp, which I will call the diachronic-precommitment theorists, treats a democratic people as a single rational agent extended across time: just as Ulysses binds himself to the mast to resist future temptation, a constitution can rationally constrain future majorities against predictable failures of judgment or will. They therefore defend hard entrenchment as an expression of collective self-rule rather than its denial. The opposing camp, the synchronous-sovereignty theorists, rejects this analogy root and branch: they argue that each generation is a distinct democratic agent entitled to full self-government, so any constraint that the present generation cannot easily dissolve is illegitimate dead-hand control, regardless of the past generation's intentions. Both sides have refined their positions—introducing distinctions between first-order and second-order precommitment, or between hard entrenchment and soft default rules—yet the literature moves in circles, with the same appeals to popular sovereignty countered by the same appeals to rational continuity.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a hidden empirical disagreement about how citizens actually understand constitutional change, but a metaphysical disagreement about collective identity over time that is disguised as a normative dispute about democracy. Specifically, the precommitment side presupposes that a polity can be one agent diachronically, while the sovereignty side presupposes that agency is necessarily synchronous and non-transferable. If that is right, then the sides are answering different questions—one about the conditions for rational agency across generations, one about the conditions for democratic legitimacy within a generation—under the shared vocabulary of "binding," "consent," and "precommitment." Yet I am not certain whether there is also an unacknowledged normative divergence about whether future people are separate moral agents whose autonomy trumps present planning, or whether the disagreement is partly verbal, turning on whether "precommitment" conceptually requires that the bound party retain an unconditional capacity to release itself.

Diagnose this impasse with reference to what each side actually argues, not with a survey of the literature. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions collapsed under one heading? Does a hidden formal or conceptual issue—perhaps about whether constitutional interpretation is necessarily presentist, or about the logic of second-order rules—do the real work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a sharp distinction between hard and soft entrenchment that dissolves the analogy, a formal model of intergenerational consent conditions, an empirical finding about how constitutional change is perceived, a change in the framing question, or should we conclude that the Ulysses analogy is fundamentally ill-posed for constitutional law and abandon it?
