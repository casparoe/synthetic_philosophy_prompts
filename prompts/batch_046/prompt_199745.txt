I'm a journalist who writes about science, philosophy, and institutional design, and in recent reporting I've kept encountering a structural pattern that I can describe concretely but cannot place precisely within the literature. The pattern involves an agent—whether an individual, a firm, or a coalition—who deliberately adopts a costly commitment, not merely as a strategic precommitment in the familiar Ulysses-contract sense, but under conditions where the commitment's effectiveness depends essentially on its being sincerely held rather than instrumentally calculated. Once the commitment has done its work—securing cooperation, deterring harm, or holding allies together—maintaining it in full becomes locally irrational or actively harmful to the agent's other ends; yet relaxing or abandoning it retroactively exposes the original adoption as strategic, thereby destroying the very credibility that made the commitment effective. Two instances keep recurring in my interviews: a manufacturing firm that institutes an expensive, genuinely believed-in fail-safe protocol that becomes redundant once engineering improves, where relaxing the protocol reveals that the original belief was merely tactical; and a political coalition that promotes an absolute, non-negotiable ethical principle necessary to maintain partner trust, then finds literal enforcement counterproductive after achieving power, with any moderation undermining the principle's prior authority.

This feels adjacent to Parfit's hitchhiker scenario—rational irrationality, in which it is rational to be disposed to do something that is ex post irrational—yet the sincerity condition and the retroactive exposure problem seem to introduce a distinct layer. In the hitchhiker, the driver picks you up because you are the kind of person who pays afterward, even though payment is irrational once you are home; here, by contrast, the effectiveness of the disposition depends on observers being unable to distinguish sincere belief from strategic posture, and the damage of defection is not just a lost future ride but a collapsed epistemic status that invalidates the original act. I also see ways it differs from ordinary hypocrisy or strategic signaling, since the agent often genuinely acquires the belief or principle in the process of adopting it, and from standard self-binding, since the binding mechanism is external credibility rather than internal constraint.
