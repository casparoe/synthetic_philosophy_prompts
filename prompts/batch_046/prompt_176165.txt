Consider the no-free-lunch theorems in optimization and supervised learning: averaged uniformly over all possible target functions or problem instances, every algorithm performs identically to random search. Now treat this result as a formal analog to Hume’s problem of induction—specifically, that without prior constraints on the space of possible worlds, no inference rule can be rationally privileged over blind guessing. Given this framing, please describe the most important implications for the philosophy of scientific method. In particular: does the theorem entail that any justification of inductive success must be relative to substantive, empirically loaded assumptions about the structure of reality, rather than derivable from pure logic or rationality alone? Does it dissolve the traditional demand for a universal vindication of induction by showing that such a demand implicitly presupposes an impossible methodological neutrality? And what does it imply for the debate between scientific realists and instrumentalists—namely, whether predictive success can only be explained if our prior restrictions on possible worlds are approximately true descriptions of nature, rather than merely convenient heuristic filters?
