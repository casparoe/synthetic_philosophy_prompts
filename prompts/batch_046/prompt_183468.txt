My friend and I have spent the better part of an evening arguing over whether the word "liberal" denotes the same political philosophy on either side of the Atlantic, and I am now trying to work out whether our disagreement is merely semantic or reveals a deeper institutional divergence. In European comparative politics—particularly in the taxonomies used by party-family surveys and university examination boards—a "liberal" party is typically situated in the centre or centre-right, defined by commitments to secularism, individual autonomy in private life, market mechanisms, and a state that is neutral among comprehensive doctrines but not necessarily minimal in its regulatory reach. By contrast, in American secondary-school syllabi, polling instruments, and congressional scoring systems, "liberal" is placed on the left, aligned with welfare-state expansion, progressive taxation, and judicial protection of minority rights against majoritarian preferences. Both traditions claim descent from classical liberalism, yet the European taxonomy treats distributive egalitarianism as external to liberalism—often the province of social democracy—whereas the American usage treats robust distributive justice as central to it. Meanwhile, European liberal parties routinely endorse supranational governance structures that constrain national democratic majorities, while American liberals defend strong federal judicial review that does the same domestically, suggesting that both invoke democratic legitimacy selectively.

What philosophical commitments about freedom, equality, and the state's role does this institutional classification practice presuppose? Does the European taxonomy implicitly assume that liberalism is constituted by a negative-liberty core combined with procedural neutrality, thereby ruling out republican or perfectionist conceptions of liberty? Or can it accommodate the American usage if we distinguish between political liberalism and economic liberalism in a particular way? Conversely, does the American classification presuppose a specifically Rawlsian or social-democratic framework in which distributive justice is a precondition for the equal worth of liberty, and if so, why does it retain the label "liberal" rather than "social democratic"? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined—could a single coherent theory of liberalism license both taxonomies by partitioning domains—or do the two institutional practices presuppose inconsistent views of democratic sovereignty, given that both European and American "liberals" appear willing to constrain popular majorities through non-elected institutions? Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a republican conception that defines freedom as non-domination rather than non-interference, which parts of these classification systems would have to be abandoned, and which might survive?
