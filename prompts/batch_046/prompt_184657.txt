So I've been retired from biology for a few years now and got into reading philosphy, mostly philosophy of science, and there's this institutional quirk I can't shake. In a lot of countries the endangered species laws don't protect populations directly — they protect whatever the current taxanomic authority calls a 'species' or 'subspecies'. So when taxonomists split one group into two, like they did with African elephants — the bush ones and the forest ones — the legal protections can split overnight too, without any legislature voting on it. Meanwhile a museum might relabel the exact same skeleton when the taxonomy changes, but the law treats them as totally different entities for poaching penalties. What philosophical view of natural kinds is baked into that arrangement? Does it only make sense if you think species are real joints in nature that science discovers, or could a nominalist about species still justify giving legal weight to whatever taxonomy says this year? I want to know what the practice strictly presupposes versus what just fits well with it. And if we actually thought nature doesn't have those joints, what would have to change — would we write laws around ecosystems or populations instead? Am I missing something obvious here? I'm used to thinking about data, not metaphysics.
