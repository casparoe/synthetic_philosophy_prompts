I'm working through the literature on constitutional precommitment—particularly the analogy between Ulysses binding himself to the mast and entrenched constitutional provisions like eternity clauses or supermajority amendment rules—and I've reached a frustrating impasse with a colleague that feels more like a stalemate than an argument. We agree on all the institutional facts: democratic majorities shift their preferences over generations, some constitutions make specific provisions extraordinarily difficult to amend, and constitutional courts occasionally treat these provisions as binding on future legislatures regardless of present popular will. My colleague insists this is a coherent form of intertemporal self-binding: a constituent power, like a rational individual, can legitimately constrain its successors to prevent predictable future errors. I maintain that the analogy fails because democratic sovereignty is not a single persisting agent but a sequence of distinct collective agents, which means so-called precommitment is either a misleading metaphor or a normative justification for the dead hand of the past. After months of exchange, neither of us has moved, and I'm no longer sure whether we're disputing constitutional design, the metaphysics of collective agency, or something else entirely.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is hidden beneath a shared vocabulary: perhaps he's answering a question about institutional rationality—how should a polity stabilize expectations against temporary passions—while I'm answering one about democratic legitimacy—who possesses the authority to bind a future electorate that has not consented. But I wonder whether the real fracture is deeper: a bedrock intuition about whether autonomy requires the continuous possibility of revision, versus a competing intuition that rationality sometimes demands locking in one's best judgments. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, substantive about sovereignty, a clash over which cases count as legitimate democratic change, or two different questions running under the single heading of "Ulysses contracts"? And given that diagnosis, what, if anything, could move the debate: a formal model of diachronic collective agency, a distinction between binding future selves and delegating to other agents, a specific constitutional precedent that forces one reading over the other, or is this an irreducible normative tension we should simply learn to live with?
