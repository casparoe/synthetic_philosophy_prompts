Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level seminar of 12 students on cross-cultural differences in folk morality (WEIRD psychology) and what they imply for moral objectivity. The deliverable should be a runnable structured deliberation—complete with roles, rules, a timeline, payoffs or stakes, and a debrief—that makes participants feel the tension between objectivity and cultural framing before the facilitator names it.

Structure the activity as a “normative arbitration game.” Divide the group into three committees of four, each assigned a fictional society that weights moral violations differently: Committee A treats individual autonomy violations as the primary wrong, Committee B treats disruptions to communal resource boundaries (e.g., unauthorized extraction from shared pools) as the gravest offense, and Committee C treats failures of relational harmony (e.g., public refusal to defer to elder mediation) as decisive. Give all three committees the identical vignette—an ambiguous scenario in which one actor takes extra water from a shared well without asking, justifying it by family need—plus a hidden scoring rubric that assigns opposite ratings to the same action. Let committees negotiate for 20 minutes, then reveal the rubrics and require the full group to draft a unified meta-scoring framework that preserves all three ratings without collapsing them into a single scale. Assign specific roles within each committee: a primary negotiator, a recorder who tracks reasoning, and a devil’s advocate whose explicit task is to block any consensus that eliminates one committee’s framework. Add a facilitator-controlled “crisis round” at minute 50 in which a new detail (the actor is an outsider) is introduced and committees must re-score without consulting their hidden rubrics.

Include a minute-by-minute timeline (0–10 framing and role assignment, 10–30 first negotiation, 30–40 rubric revelation, 40–60 meta-rubric drafting, 60–75 crisis round, 75–90 debrief). Specify the rules for speaking order, the small stakes (e.g., each committee earns points for preserving its ratings in the final meta-rubric, but the group only “wins” if all three ratings survive), and how the devil’s advocate is evaluated.

The debrief should map specific moments—especially the crisis-round reversal and the meta-rubric deadlock—onto arguments about moral objectivity versus cultural construction. Be explicit about which feature of WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence the game can reproduce (the framing-dependence of harm and fairness judgments when resource-communal boundaries are made salient) and which feature it necessarily simplifies and must be supplied by the facilitator (the evolutionary or institutional origins of why some societies foreground autonomy over communal boundaries). Note predictable failure modes: one committee dominating because its framework sounds “rational” to WEIRD-trained students, or participants conflating “agreement on description” with “agreement on evaluation.” Conclude with an honest assessment of what the activity cannot reproduce: the actual lived social costs of moral deviation in small-scale societies.

Keep the complete design—roles, rules, timeline, stakes, debrief, reproducible features, unavoidable simplifications, and failure-mode warnings—within 550 words. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
