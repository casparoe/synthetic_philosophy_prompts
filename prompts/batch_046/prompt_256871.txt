As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to understand Strawson's reactive attitudes and their connection to moral responsibility, and I think I have found a structural parallel that philosophical discussions are missing. In reactive programming—specifically in event-sourced architectures—components respond to discrete state-change events without retaining a full historical trace of the upstream causal chain; the reaction is immediate, localized, and does not require access to the originator's internal state. If Strawson's reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation) operate in a genuinely analogous way—firing as localized emotional events in response to perceived actions, without requiring that we first solve the metaphysical problem of whether the agent was ultimately the source of that action—then moral responsibility might be reconceptualized as a distributed event-stream protocol rather than a verdict about deep agency. This seems to imply that the free will debate is largely a red herring: if blame is just a reactive event triggered by observed behavior, then determinism does not undermine it any more than deterministic message-passing undermines a reactive system. Yet I notice philosophers still treat reactive attitudes as presupposing that we regard others as participants in interpersonal relationships, which sounds like it demands a richer ontological commitment than a stateless event model allows. So I want a committed answer: is the reactive-programming analogy a fundamental category error that collapses once we recognize that emotional reactions are not stateless computational events, or does it correctly reveal that Strawson's framework makes metaphysical free will irrelevant to moral practice? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble and without restating my question.
