I am a retired physicist who, since leaving active research, have taken up philosophy of mind with the earnestness of a late student. Tomorrow I must deliver the opening remarks at the retirement symposium for Dr. Miriam Kessler, a neurosurgeon whose corpus callosotomy studies have made her a reference point in debates about the unity of consciousness. The audience is divided: her surgical colleagues and philosophy graduate students will catch every conceptual slip, while her family—who have heard her speak casually of the two-streams problem over dinner for thirty years—need to feel honored rather than anatomized. Write a five-minute address in which the split-brain evidence genuinely constrains what I can say: I want to praise her career without invoking a unified vision or singular self that her own work might undermine, and I want to comfort her family without pretending that consciousness is obviously one thing. The philosophical tension should determine which metaphors I can employ, which ones I must reject, and how to maintain the boundary between clinical accuracy and human affection.
