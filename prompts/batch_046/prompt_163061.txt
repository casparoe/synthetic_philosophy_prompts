Consider a counterfactual in which Albert Einstein survives into the present and is confronted with three scientific and technological developments that postdate his death in 1955, each bearing directly on the strict determinism and religious naturalism he maintained throughout his life. First, the neurophysiological experiments initiated by Benjamin Libet and subsequent researchers, which appear to show that preparatory brain activity precedes the subjective experience of conscious volition by several hundred milliseconds. Second, the emergence of large-scale behavioral-prediction systems—trained on digital traces of consumption, movement, and communication—that routinely anticipate individual human choices with accuracies exceeding ninety percent, without invoking any neural mechanism. Third, the quantum-Bayesian interpretation of quantum mechanics, which treats the wavefunction not as an objective physical field but as an agent's subjective degrees of belief updated via Bayes' rule, together with the related superdeterminist proposal that apparent quantum randomness is an artifact of hidden correlations established at the origin of the universe.

Given Einstein's well-known conviction that the universe is strictly deterministic and governed by rational laws he described in Spinozist terms, together with his explicit rejection of the idea that quantum mechanics involves fundamental indeterminacy, how would he likely interpret each development? Would he take the Libet results as demonstrating that conscious will is an epiphenomenal by-product of deterministic neural processes, or would he argue that they establish only temporal priority without undermining the causal efficacy of a unified mental-physical system? Would he regard high-accuracy behavioral prediction as confirming that human choice is computable in principle, or merely as a statistical correlation compatible with an underlying determinism he already accepted? Most importantly, facing the choice between embracing QBism—which preserves determinism only by making physical law depend on the observer's beliefs—and embracing superdeterminism—which saves local realism and objectivity at the cost of rejecting statistical independence—would he find either palatable? Or would he instead propose a third resolution consistent with his belief that nature exhibits an objective rational order that neither plays dice nor depends on subjective perspectives?

In answering, please do not catalog biographical sources; instead, construct the position that best coheres with Einstein's expressed commitments to determinism, his Spinozist conception of divinity, and his skepticism about the completeness of orthodox quantum mechanics, and identify which of the three modern challenges poses the deepest tension for that reconstructed view.
