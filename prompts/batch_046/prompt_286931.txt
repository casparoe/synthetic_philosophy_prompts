I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and next month I'm leading a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group composed of epistemologists and philosophers of mind with no formal training in archaeology or anthropology. I want them to understand a specific, non-obvious inferential move: how archaeologists reconstruct institutional rules—particularly norms governing inheritance transmission and ritual pollution—chiefly from the wear patterns, repair histories, and depositional contexts of mundane, non-symbolic artifacts (storage vessels and grinding implements), rather than from iconography, texts, or monumental architecture.

To ground the discussion, I intend to build the session around a hypothetical but realistic domestic assemblage: in one sector, heavily repaired storage jars occur exclusively in deposits adjacent to burial features, while identically manufactured but unrepaired jars dominate living-area refuse; in the same contexts, grinding slabs show differential abrasion patterns consistent with gender-segregated use-cycles rather than uniform wear. From this material profile, I want the group to see how one might warrant an inference to an institutional framework—say, rules linking repaired goods to ancestral obligation and segregated labor to pollution avoidance—without collapsing into either functionalist reduction or symbolic over-interpretation.

Please plan the explanatory arc for this session. Specify the order in which the conceptual dependencies must be introduced—what has to be in place before the audience can even see the inference as plausible rather than speculative—and identify the single carrying comparison or scenario that should anchor the discussion: what properties must it possess, and what must it lack, to prevent the audience from treating it as either pure symbolism or pure technological adaptation? Then map the misconceptions they are likely to import from philosophy of mind and epistemology: I anticipate they will conflate material correlation with causal mechanism, project intentional design onto wear traces, and mistake the absence of explicit representation for the absence of institutional belief. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be deflected. Propose one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that will reveal whether they have grasped the inferential structure—specifically, whether they can distinguish an institutional reconstruction from a mere behavioral generalization—rather than simply finding the example evocative. Finally, state explicitly which standard archaeological procedures (for instance, stratigraphic seriation, ethnographic analogy, and taphonomic correction) should be deliberately omitted at this level, with a brief justification for each omission, and indicate where in the arc I should openly acknowledge these limits without breaking the dependency chain.
