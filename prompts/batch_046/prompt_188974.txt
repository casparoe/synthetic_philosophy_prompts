I'm taking a course on Quine's naturalized epistomology and I'm trying to figure out whether his ontology actually lets him brush off moral values like he seems to want to. I built this argument for our seminar but it feels like it proves way too much, maybe even that Quine has to be a moral realist or else science collapses. Here's where I'm stuck.

(1) Quine thinks our ontological commitments are fixed by what gets quantified over in our best theories once they're regimented into canonical first-order notation. (2) Moral properties—wrongness, goodness, whatever—never appear as values of bound variables in physics, biology, or chemistry, so they don't make the cut. (3) So Quine should deny that moral properties exist in any serious ontological sense. (4) But Quine also admits that science itself runs on normative principles like simplicity, conservatism, and predictive success to break underdetermination. (5) Those principles don't show up in the quantificational apparatus either; they're not entities science talks about. (6) So either Quine denies those norms exist—which seems to destroy the very practice that grounds his ontological commitments—or he admits that something can be genuinely indispensible without being quantified over. (7) If he takes that second route, then moral values might be indispensible in the same pragmatic way—say, for social coordination—without needing a place in physics. (8) But then Quine has no principaled reason to reject moral realism, and his naturalism ends up either embracing it or undermining itself.

I think the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), maybe I'm treating methodological heuristics as if they were ontological commitments instead of just pragmatic rules, but I'm not sure what giving up that step costs. Can you tell me which premise to reject? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (3) since they feel central to Quine, but I'm open if that's the wrong place to cut.
