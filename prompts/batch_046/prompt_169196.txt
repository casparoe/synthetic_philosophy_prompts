I am a novelist who has lately been teaching a small seminar on consciousness and narrative form, and in preparing notes for an essay I intend to submit to a literary quarterly, I keep returning to Douglas Hofstadter’s conception of the self as a strange loop: the pronoun I functioning not as a pointer to a fixed substrate but as a symbol that refers back to the system that produces it. I want to propose that the first-person narrator in autobiographical fiction operates through a structurally parallel circuit, yet with an inversion I have been calling a ghost loop. Where Hofstadter’s loop consolidates identity through self-reference, the fictional I dissolves the boundary between author, narrator, and reader by sustaining a referential circuit with no terminal node outside the text itself. I sketch this in three movements: first, that the pronoun in fiction points simultaneously to a character and to the linguistic system generating it; second, that this double reference creates a feedback loop in which the reader’s attribution of consciousness to the narrator feeds back into the narrator’s own self-description; and third, that whereas biological strange loops tend to stabilize over time, narrative loops are deliberately destabilized by revision, unreliability, and the reader’s awareness of artifice. I am not seeking a bibliography—only a critical response to the architecture of the argument. Does the analogy hold, or does it collapse when linguistic reference is treated as if it were neural self-reference? Is the ghost loop a meaningful extension, or does it merely rename familiar problems in narrative theory? I would especially value your view on whether the third movement, about destabilization through revision, introduces a distinction that undermines the comparison rather than enriching it. The intended audience knows philosophy of mind but not necessarily narratology, so I wish to avoid both technical jargon and superficial analogy, and I am particularly unsure whether my framing of the reader’s feedback as constitutive rather than incidental misrepresents how narrative identification actually works.
