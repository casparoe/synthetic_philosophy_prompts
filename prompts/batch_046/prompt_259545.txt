Consider three concrete allocation practices: American universities’ long-standing use of race-conscious holistic admissions, which weights group membership without imposing fixed quotas; India’s constitutional reservation of legislative seats and public employment for Scheduled Castes and Tribes; and Norway’s statutory requirement that public company boards contain at least 40 per cent of each gender. Each treats group membership as a legitimate reason to alter the distribution of scarce positions, yet each is publicly justified by appeal to fairness, merit, or democratic representation rather than to explicit group entitlement. What implicit theory of distributive justice—or of the moral relevance of group identity—do these practices collectively presuppose? In particular, do they depend on the view that statistical group membership can stand in for unobserved individual disadvantage, thereby licensing a form of “statistical morality” in which demographic probabilities replace direct assessments of desert? Or do they require a genuinely non-individualist claim that groups themselves hold distributive claims? Reconstruct the philosophical architecture they share, distinguish what they strictly require from what merely accompanies them, and determine whether one coherent view can license all three, or whether they quietly rest on incompatible commitments about merit, historical redress, and the moral status of statistical categories.
