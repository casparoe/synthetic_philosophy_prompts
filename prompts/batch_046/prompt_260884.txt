I've been trying to work out for myself the real difference between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation, and I keep getting stuck on cases where lots of people could have stopped something but nobody did. Here is the concrete scenario I've been using to think with it: a neighborhood board has five members, each with individual veto power over cutting down an old oak tree. All five stay silent, the tree is removed, and a week later a falling branch from a nearby tree injures someone—though that nearby tree was only near the property line because the oak had been sheltering it. Three board members later say they never really caused anything, because even if one of them had spoken up, the other four could still have stayed silent and the oak still would have fallen. Meanwhile, the board chair argues that each silent member was a cause in a practical sense, because if you imagine stepping in and making just one of them speak, the outcome changes.

I want an explanation of the conceptual issue that underlies their disagreement—not a dictionary definition of the two theories, but a clear account of why they split on this kind of case. 1. The core conceptual distinction (about 25 percent): how each theory defines what it takes for one thing to cause another, with particular attention to how they treat omissions, redundant possibilities, and the baseline against which we judge change. Avoid naming specific philosophers or texts; I want the logic, not a history lesson.

2. The neighborhood-tree case applied (about 30 percent): apply both theories carefully. Under a counterfactual analysis, do the silent members count as causes of the tree's removal, or of the subsequent injury? Where does overdetermination or preemption enter? Under an interventionist analysis, what would it mean to intervene on one member's silence, and does that intervention change the outcome in a way that licenses calling them a cause?

3. The divergence in causal selection (about 25 percent): why the counterfactual theory seems to erase individual responsibility in collective omissions, and why the interventionist theory might restore it—or might not, depending on how we model the decision structure. 4. A synthetic bottom line (about 20 percent): can the two theories be reconciled in this kind of case, or does the scenario show that they are asking different questions? If they diverge, which one captures the intuition that someone should have spoken up, and which one captures the intuition that no single person made the difference?
