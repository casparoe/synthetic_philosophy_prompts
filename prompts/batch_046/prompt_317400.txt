I am preparing a graduate seminar on epistemic rationality and wish to present an argument that connects the lottery paradox with the preface paradox in a way that appears to license distributed contradiction. Here is the reconstructed argument.

(1) If an agent rationally believes each member of a large set of propositions, and the beliefs are governed by a uniform high-confidence threshold, then there is rational pressure to aggregate them.
(2) In the standard lottery case, the agent rationally believes of each ticket that it will lose, yet does not rationally believe that all tickets will lose.
(3) So rational belief violates closure under conjunction for large sets.
(4) In the standard preface case, an author rationally believes each claim in her book, yet rationally believes that at least one claim is false.
(5) So rational belief violates closure under disjunction for belief-sets of sufficient cardinality.
(6) Now construct a compound case: an agent holds beliefs B1 through Bn, each supported above the rational-acceptance threshold, and simultaneously holds a meta-belief—analogous to the preface belief—that exactly one member of the set is false, while maintaining, for each individual Bi, a first-order belief that Bi is true, analogous to the lottery belief.
(7) By (3) and (5), the agent is not rationally required to merge these beliefs into a single contradictory state.
(8) But if aggregation is not rationally mandatory, then there is no internal defect in maintaining that Bi is true within one cognitive partition and that some Bj is false within another, even when the agent possesses the resources to recognize that i and j range over the same domain.
(9) Generalizing, an agent could systematically partition her doxastic economy so that for any proposition p, p is believed in one partition and not-p is believed in another, with no rational obligation to reconcile them.
(10) Therefore, accepting both paradoxes as accurate descriptions of rational belief entails that rational agents may harbor pervasive, structurally hidden contradictions distributed across partitions.
(11) Yet a conception of rationality that permits undetectable contradiction across partitions is indistinguishable from unrestricted doxastic arbitrariness.
(12) Hence one must reject either the reasoning in (3) or the reasoning in (5).

My suspicion is that the illicit slide occurs between (7) and (8): partitioning may block aggregation without blocking the rational demand for local consistency. But I am unsure whether rejecting (8) forces us to restore full logical closure, to abandon the Lockean thesis linking belief to high subjective probability, or to concede that rational belief is sometimes genuinely contradictory. Which step is the weakest? And what does giving it up cost—specifically, does it require us to treat the preface and the lottery asymmetrically, or can they both be preserved at the price of a more expensive revision elsewhere?
