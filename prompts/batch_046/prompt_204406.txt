I'm writing this between marking essays, so apologies if it's a bit rambling. I've been roped into advising our borough council on a pilot scheme for emergency housing grants. The scheme works like this: you get an initial payment if you're in crisis, but to recieve the second tranche you have to complete eight hours of 'community reciprocity' — park maintenance, library shelving, that sort of thing. The councillors say it's about avoiding dependency and making sure people aren't gaming the system, but to me it feels like we're reintroducing a moral exam for basic survival. At the same time, I can see the worry: if there's no link between effort and support, don't we risk undermining the reciprocity that makes solidarity sustainable? I want to tell them whether this kind of behavoural gatekeeping can be defended without simply sorting people into worthy and unworthy claimants based on how cooperative they seem, or whether any condition on basic need is just corruption by another name. What's the honest philosophical verdict — is there a principled middle ground, or is the condition itself the problem?
