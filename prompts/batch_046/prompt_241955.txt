I have no formal training in philosophy or cognitive science, but over the past two years I have worked through the teletransporter problem on my own and developed a specific thesis I plan to defend in a twenty-five minute presentation to a small interdisciplinary salon. My position is that psychological continuity is not sufficient for survival in the teletransporter case; what matters instead is singular causal ownership of one’s narrative history. On my framing, scanning and transmission do not merely replicate mental states—they generate a competing causal branch that claims the same autobiographical trajectory. If the original body is destroyed, only one branch exists, so survival holds despite total physical discontinuity; but if the original survives, the narrative history is now jointly owned by two causally independent branches, meaning neither fully survives as the original person, even though both remain psychologically continuous with the pre-scanning individual. I will present this to an audience of about twelve people: four analytic philosophers who have taught personal-identity courses and tend to defend either psychological-continuity or animalist positions; three cognitive-science graduate students who think in terms of memory consolidation, neural-pattern persistence, and reconsolidation errors; one software engineer who reasons about data replication, redundancy, and distributed backups; and one palliative-care clinician who cares about what patients actually fear when they face death and whether identity persists through medical transformation.
