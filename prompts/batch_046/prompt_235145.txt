Draft the joint statement that an institutional-authority interpreter and an emergent-authority interpreter of Thomas Aquinas's teaching on tyrannicide could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the dispute centered on Summa Theologiae II-IIae, q. 42, a. 2.

The institutional-authority interpreter holds that Aquinas's requirement that deposition occur by public authority necessarily involves a formally constituted office, representative body, or jurisdictionally recognized superior power.

The emergent-authority interpreter holds that the same requirement can be satisfied by a distributed, widely shared judgment among the subjects that legitimate order has collapsed, even when no functioning formal magistracy exists.

Produce a single document with the following sections:

1. Numbered agreed claims. List the propositions both sides accept about Aquinas's argument—specifically, his distinction between a usurper and a legally established but unjust ruler; his comparison between killing a tyrant and killing a wild beast; his ruling out of purely private, unrecognized rebellion by isolated individuals; and the conditions under which resistance becomes permissible. State these without verbatim quotation from any Latin or standard English translation.

2. Disagreements table. Use two columns, one for each interpreter. Each row should present one precise divergence in wording that the respective side would accept for its own view, avoiding straw-man formulations.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. One suspected crux is whether the concept of public authority in this argument necessarily implies an institutional office or can also denote a collective epistemic state among subjects. Another suspected crux is whether the implicit historical examples—such as senatorial action against an illegitimate claimant versus popular resistance to an appointed but tyrannical ruler—require formal institutional initiation to count as legitimate.

4. Tests and predictions. For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical case or piece of internal textual reasoning both sides agree is relevant. For instance, both might agree that Aquinas's distinction between a ruler who seizes power without right and one who was legitimately appointed but rules unjustly bears directly on the authority question, but they should record opposite predictions about which category, if either, permits collective action without a formal body. Include each side's explicit prediction in advance. You may also introduce one invented scenario—a city-state whose formal magistracy has dissolved but whose citizens achieve unanimous, visible consensus that the ruler is illegitimate—to illustrate how each interpretation applies, but keep the reasoning within Aquinas's conceptual architecture rather than importing modern political theories.

5. Neutral conclusion. State whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—meaning both sides employ the same phrase with different but compatible senses—and, if so, identify exactly which portion and why.

The document must remain adjudicatively neutral: it should not declare which interpretation is correct. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for external citations. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for an academic working paper.
