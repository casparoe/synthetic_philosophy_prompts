I am a retired physical chemist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind. At an interdisciplinary workshop next month—attended mainly by physicalist philosophers of mind, two cognitive neuroscientists familiar with predictive processing, and a historian of early modern thought—I will defend the claim that Berkeley's master argument, reconstructed for contemporary neuroscience, undermines the standard physicalist appeal to "unobserved" neural substrates as explanations of phenomenal consciousness. My position is not that brains do not exist, but that invoking subpersonal neural processes that are never themselves experienced to "produce" consciousness repeats the abstraction error Berkeley identified: treating an operational construct defined within an observational framework as though it were metaphysically independent matter existing outside all conception. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the objections I will actually receive, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased in the vocabulary of each faction present; for each, provide a concise reply and an explicit note on where that reply is softest. State what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and identify the one objection—probably from the historian or from a committed physicalist—that I hope no one raises, together with a brief strategy should it arise.
