I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or political theory. Recently I have been trying to organize my intuitions about who belongs to the demos—who should be included in collective decision-making—filtered through the kind of reasoning people associate with Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism: do not tear down an institutional boundary unless you understand why it was erected and what failure its removal would cause. I do not have a theory; I have verdicts on concrete cases, and they feel partly consistent but partly not. I want to know whether there is a principle that captures them, and if not, which ones I should overturn.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1. A city charter allows only residents of five years' standing to vote on infrastructure bonds. The original justification was to block property speculators from swinging a single election; that threat is now covered by other regulations, and no one can point to a specific failure that would occur if the waiting period were removed, though it has never actually been tested. My verdict: keep the fence. Do not tear it down just because the original reason sounds dated; wait until you understand what breaks.

Case 2. A technology cooperative restricts votes on architecture decisions to employees with engineering degrees. The justification—technical competence—is explicit and intact. However, the architecture affects non-engineering staff (security, operations, legal) just as deeply. My verdict: tear it down. The boundary is misaligned with the actual stakes.

Case 3. A neighborhood democratic assembly draws its border along a line established in 1920 for administrative convenience. The residents on one side share sewer systems, schools, and emergency services with those on the other, and the line does not track any functional community boundary. My verdict: redesign the boundary. The fence is arbitrary, and its purpose is clearly obsolete.

Case 4. A governance forum grants voting rights to verified human users but excludes automated accounts that hold economic stakes. The exclusion is justified by the claim that only humans possess moral standing, yet the verification mechanism is opaque and unaudited. My verdict: keep the human-only boundary for now, but only on the condition that the verification mechanism is made transparent and auditable. The fence stands, but its gate must be inspectable.

Case 5. A constitutional rule requires a seventy-five percent supermajority to alter the voting-eligibility rules themselves. The purpose—preventing a temporary majority from entrenching itself—is well understood and still applies. My verdict: preserve it absolutely. This is a meta-fence that protects all other fences.

Case 6. A proposal suggests extending voting rights to synthetic agents that manage significant economic resources, provided they can demonstrate consistent preference ordering over time. The arguments for inclusion (stakeholder representation) and for exclusion (lack of consciousness) are both speculative; there is no documented failure mode for either change. My verdict: reject the proposal and maintain the current human-only boundary. Do not build a new fence, and do not tear down the old one, when you cannot predict the failure mode.

These verdicts feel sensible to me, but they are in tension. Cases 1 and 3 both involve outdated or obscure justifications, yet I want to preserve the first and redesign the second. Cases 2 and 5 both involve explicit, intact justifications, yet I want to tear down one and preserve the other. Case 6 seems to apply a stricter standard than Case 1, because in Case 1 I also lack a documented failure mode for removal, yet I still want to keep the fence.

My questions are these. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the alignment between a boundary's purpose and its current stakes, or involving epistemic conditions for institutional change—that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If there is, state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for two further cases: (a) a proposal to weight votes proportionally to years of residence rather than giving equal votes, and (b) a proposal to grant voting rights to synthetic agents that own real property but have no demonstrated preference consistency.

If no single principle captures all six, then tell me which verdict or verdicts I should overturn to reach coherence, and present the simplest repaired principle together with the case that motivates each remaining clause. Be explicit about which of my original intuitions the repaired principle sacrifices, and why that sacrifice is warranted.
