Hey — I'm drafting a piece for my science/ideas column about Jeffrey conditionalization, the update rule Richard Jeffrey proposed for when your evidence is uncertain but doesn't point to one clean outcome. Most popular explanations use the bird-vs-plane example, but I want to dig into a problem I think gets glossed over.

My notes sketch an argument like this: Jeffrey's rule keeps your conditional probabilities fixed inside each cell of a partition and just shifts the weights across the cells. That works fine if you already know exactly what the cells are — say, bird, plane, drone. But what happens when you're not even sure about the partition itself? Imagine you're interpreting climate sensor data that's noisy in a way that could mean the model's wrong, the instruments are mischalibrated, or there's some third failure mode you haven't named yet. The evidence still feels like it should update your beliefs, but Jeffrey's machinery seems to require you've listed every possible Ei in advance. I'm calling this "frame rigidity" — the rule handles smearing within a frame, but not smearing across frames.

Then I add this second layer I'm tentatively calling "evidential smearing with authority bias." If the source of your uncertain evidence is an expert who is themselves unsure about the partition, your credence shift shouldn't just redistribute over their listed options; it should also bleed into unlisted possibilities. I think Jeffrey's rule can't capture that without being modified into a higher-order Bayesian update over spaces of partitions, which might just collapse back into standard conditioning.

I don't need citations or a reading list — I want to know if the logic holds. Is frame rigidity a real limitation of Jeffrey conditioning, or is there a standard response I'm missing? Am I conflating psychology and epistemology with the authority-bias part? And does the higher-order fix actually save Jeffrey's view, or does it erase what's distinctive about it? Any other holes you see?
