Instead, I want the group to read the story through Robert Nozick's specific concept of the "utility monster" from Anarchy, State, and Utopia — the hypothetical being that derives so much more welfare per unit of resource than ordinary people that aggregating across individuals seems to license grotesque allocations. The niche twist I want to press is whether the child imprisoned in the cellar operates not as a standard victim of simple cost-benefit reasoning, but as an inverted or reverse utility monster: a single person whose suffering produces disproportionately large welfare returns for thousands, functioning structurally like a suffering multiplier rather than a pleasure multiplier. Does Le Guin's narrative architecture actually instantiate Nozick's formal worry, or does it distort it by distributing the benefits across an entire citizenry rather than concentrating them in one monstrous consumer?

Read the story through this precise lens. How should we understand the citizens who remain — are they treating the child's pain as a resource that yields superlinear welfare returns, and does that make the city itself a collective utility-monster economy in which the monster is distributed? Where does the story resist being reduced to a distributive-justice thought experiment, and what does it contribute about complicity, normalized knowledge, or the aesthetics of suffering that Nozick's argument alone does not capture?
