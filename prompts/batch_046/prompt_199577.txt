I am writing an essay for my philosophy of social science seminar on whether long-running empirical debates can stall for purely conceptual reasons. We have been examining the dispute over whether the world is becoming less violent, which has persisted since Steven Pinker’s defense of long-term per-capita declines in homicide, war mortality, and institutional cruelty, and the sustained responses from critics who either reject the aggregation of heterogeneous harms, insist that structural and state violence must be included, or argue that large populations render per-capita normalization misleading. Despite extensive data exchange, the discussion feels frozen: defenders cite statistical trends, while opponents reply that the category “violence” is too context-dependent to support a global trend line, that absolute suffering may still be rising, or that there is no fact of the matter about a single “world violence level.” I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than a verdict on who is right.

Specifically, is this stall mainly verbal—centered on whether “violence” should cover only direct kinetic harm or also structural, symbolic, and institutional coercion? Is it a substantive disagreement hidden behind different evidential standards, with one side treating archaeological estimates and mortality statistics as decisive and the other demanding intentional-institutional analysis? Or are the camps answering different questions—one about the individual risk of being killed, another about the total quantum of organized suffering—under one heading? I also wonder whether an unacknowledged formal issue is doing hidden work: the choice among normalizing by population, by interaction opportunity, or not normalizing at all seems to smuggle in normative judgments about progress, yet neither side defends that baseline choice explicitly. Could part of the impasse be that “violence” is being treated like a scalar physical property (amenable to global averaging) by one side and like a relational-moral property tied to legitimacy and context by the other?

Please organize your response as follows:

1. The apparent positions (about 250 words): describe concretely what Pinker-style defenders and their critics actually commit to, avoiding caricature.
2. Diagnosis of the disagreement (about 350 words): classify the impasse—verbal, substantive, evidential, or a case of different questions under one name—using the distinctions above; identify any hidden empirical or formal issues; and explain whether there is a fact of the matter about global violence trends.
3. What progress would require (about 200 words): specify whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result about aggregation baselines, an empirical study, or a change of question could resolve the stall, or argue that no progress should be expected and why.
