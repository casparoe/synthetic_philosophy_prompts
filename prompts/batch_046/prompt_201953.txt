hey — i'm working up a short essay for my grad seminar on panpsychism and i could use a fresh pair of eyes. the working title is "Two Combination Problems and the Indexical Residue" and the core move is splitting what everyone calls the combination problem into a structual problem and an ownership problem, then arguing the second one leaves an indexical residue that Russellian monism doesn't really clean up.

here's the draft in notes form:

1. the usual setup: macro-consciousness is composed of micro-consciousness, but we can't see how micro-qualia sum to macro-qualia. Chalmers and Goff both press this, but they tend to run together the structural mismatch (micro-experiences don't have the right spatial/relational form) with the subject-unity problem (why is there one experiencer rather than a committee).

2. i want to call the second half the ownership combination problem. even if you solve the structural part — say via proto-phenomenal properties arranged just right — you still haven't explained why the arranged parts don't each retain their own first-person perspective. there's a leftover "someone-else-in-here" feeling; i call that the indexical residue.

3. to make it concrete, i use a gradual cortical-substitution scenario: replace neurons one by one with functionally identical units that either lack micro-experience entirely or only have isolated micro-experience with no integration mechanism. on standard panpsychism, does the macro-subject fade gradually, vanish suddenly, or stay intact? i think the scenario shows that ownership isn't reducible to causal structure, which means the combination problem was never just about structure.

4. my tentative conclusion is that Russellian monism solves the structual mismatch by making physical structure phenomenally transparent, but it still owes an account of why transparency yields one owner rather than many. if it can't do that without sneaking in a primitive subject, then panpsychism hasn't really avoided the hard problem — it's just moved it downstairs.

can you read this over and tell me: (a) is the distinction between the structual and ownership problems actually sharp, or am i just renaming confusion? (b) does the cortical-substitution thought experiment do real work, or does it smuggle in assumptions about personal identity that don't belong in a metaphysics of mind? (c) is the "indexical residue" framing interesting, or just jargon? i don't need line edits — i'm more worried about whether the argument holds water and whether it's saying something that hasn't already been beaten to death in the literature. be honest if parts feel like they're reaching.
