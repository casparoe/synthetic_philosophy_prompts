I am in an upper-division seminar on Enlightenment philosophy and gender, with about thirteen other students, meeting for one ninety-minute session. We have been working through Émilie du Châtelet’s reflections on happiness, especially her claim that genuine contentment depends not on transparent self-knowledge but on what she treats as necessary illusions—structured, self-flattering frames that allow passionate engagement to survive rational scrutiny—so that the very clarity Enlightenment rationalism celebrates can dissolve the happiness it seeks to explain. I want an in-class, rules-based activity that makes my peers feel this tension in their own choices rather than merely understand it conceptually: they should experience how acquiring an accurate, fully articulated account of why they value something can diminish the pleasure they take in it, and they should see that resisting this disillusionment is not simple ignorance but the maintenance of a specific epistemic posture.

Please design a runnable exercise—using role-cards, staged commitments, or a symbolic token economy with small but real stakes such as public ranking or irreversible trade-offs—for this group, arranged so that participants run into the problem themselves before I name it. Include clear rules, a payoff or reward structure that does not require real money, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto du Châtelet’s distinctions between passionate happiness and the kind of rational satisfaction that survives scrutiny. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, for instance participants who treat rational transparency as an unqualified virtue refusing to inhabit their roles, or the gendered dimension of her argument being flattened into a generic psychological point. Also tell me frankly which feature of her view—perhaps her specific suggestion that women’s education ought to function as a practice of managed illusion—cannot be reproduced in a single classroom exercise and must be supplied by me in the closing discussion. I do not need bibliographies or citations; I need something I can facilitate from memory in the room.
