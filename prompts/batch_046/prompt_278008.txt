I am writing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of dramatizing the recently dead in biographical film, and I want to pressure-test a theoretical framework I am developing—call it the dramatic embodiment thesis—against a well-known recent case. The framework begins with the standard Feinberg-Levenbook view that the dead can suffer posthumous harm because interests survive death, but adds a phenomenological layer: when a biopic invents private, embodied experiences—self-injury, hallucination, sexual intimacy—for a real dead person and renders them in first-person cinematic perspective, the audience does not merely acquire false beliefs about that person but temporarily inhabits a counterfeit subjectivity that crowds out the authentic one in cultural memory. The harm is therefore not purely epistemic but, in a derivative sense, existential.

Take Pablo Larraín’s 2021 film Spencer, which compresses Princess Diana’s marital crisis into a single 1991 Sandringham Christmas weekend, fabricates hallucinatory sequences in which Diana identifies with Anne Boleyn, and includes an invented scene of bodily self-harm using domestic objects. Applying the dramatic embodiment thesis: what obligations, if any, did the filmmakers have toward Diana, who died in 1997? Does the thesis correctly identify why the invented self-injury scene is ethically different from, say, compressing a timeline or inventing dialogue? Where does the framework break down—for instance, does the audience’s awareness of Kristen Stewart’s performance block the phenomenological appropriation the thesis claims, or does the first-person camera work overcome it? Does the episode confirm the thesis, reveal that it applies only to subjects with an already saturated cultural image, or show that the real ethical problem lies elsewhere—in the structural asymmetry between artistic freedom and the dead subject’s inability to correct the record?

I want a disciplined two-way mapping. Identify the players (the filmmakers, the estate or family, the audience, Diana’s posthumous interests), specify which representational choices count as “embodied invention” versus permissible dramatization, and say exactly where the theory explains the case, where it misdescribes it, and where it is silent. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; work with the concepts as I have defined them.
