For a 90-minute upper-level philosophy seminar of about ten students, design an in-class simulation of a modified centipede game that makes participants experience backward induction not as a generic conflict between cooperation and self-interest, but specifically as the collapse of common knowledge of rationality once reasoning becomes partially opaque. Structure it as paired play across six visible decision nodes, but add a hidden seventh node—revealed only if both reach it—whose existence is unknown to the pair beforehand; after each round rotate partners and maintain a visible reputation ledger that records only whether a player terminated or continued, never exposing hidden-node encounters, with tokens convertible into a trivial collective reward (e.g., choosing next week’s discussion topic). Provide precise rules for rotation, information revelation, token allocation, and ledger maintenance; a debrief mapping the first unexpected termination, reputation-driven shifts in subsequent rounds, and any surprise at the hidden node onto the formal backward-induction steps and onto the distinction between mutual belief and common knowledge; an honest account of which feature—the infinite regress of higher-order beliefs—this physical arrangement cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator; and a frank prediction of what will misfire the first time (for example, students treating the ledger as a strategic asset rather than an epistemic signal).
