I've come around to a kind of neo-Bradleyan take on relations and the propostion problem. The gist: Bradley's regress stops if relations aren't extra entities but directional potentials already baked into the terms — so a and b each carry an internal pointing-toward-R that only activates when they come together. That means aRb needs no separate relating thing, and the unity of the propostion is just this single actualization event, not some external glue holding terms together. I think it kills two birds with one stone, but I'm not sure what it actually risks. So what would count against it? Give me the real defeaters — formal results, counter-cases, or arguments from other parts of metaphysics — sorted from fatal to merely awkward. What does this view predict that a standard Russell-style relational realism doesn't? If someone points to relations that look totally arbitrary and external, do defenders have to concede defeat, or can they just say the potential was hidden? And has this been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that actually a problem for a metaphysical view like this?
