I've been thinking about nuclear command structures and how weird they are philosophically. There's the dual-key thing for NATO nuclear weapons in Europe — host country and US both have to turn the key — and then there's the old Soviet Perimeter system that could auto-launch if sensors detected a strike, no human in the loop. These two designs sit right next to each other in the literature but they seem to presuppose totally different things about what makes a threat credible. Does the dual-key setup assume that splitting agency between states makes the commitment more believable, like a game-theory handshake? And does Perimeter presuppose that deterrence doesn't actually need a rational mind at all, just a reliable causal chain? I'm trying to map out what view of agency and commitment these institutions implicitly rely on, especially since brinkmanship theory usually treats the state as a single rational actor. Is that implicit philosophy coherent, or are these designs quietly incompatible with it? And if we replaced the unitary-actor model with something distributed or automated, what'd have to change in how we think about nuclear deterrence? Definately curious where the cracks are.
