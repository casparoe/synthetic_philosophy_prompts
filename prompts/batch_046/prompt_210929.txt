As a retired physicist now working through the philosophy of mathematics, I have become convinced that Benacerraf's dilemma can be dissolved only if we reject a hidden premise about what counts as epistemic access. I want to argue that knowledge of abstract mathematical structures does not require causal interaction with those structures themselves, but rather what I will call interventional accessibility: we come to know a structure when we can systematically manipulate physical systems whose behavior is asymmetrically dependent on that structure, much as we understand conservation laws through symmetry operations rather than by detecting the law itself. Please construct a formal argument for this position. First, show precisely why the causal-epistemic premise that generates Benacerraf's skeptical horn is too restrictive, using an analogy from physics where we have robust knowledge of entities that are not directly causally detectable. Second, explain how interventional accessibility differs both from standard Platonist intuitionism and from reliabilist accounts of mathematical belief, and why it does not collapse into saying we only know the physical instantiations. Third, defend the asymmetry claim—that physical states depend on mathematical structures, not conversely—against the objection that this merely pushes the epistemic problem back onto our knowledge of the physical dependency relation. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, but for a rigorous, step-by-step defense of the view; avoid bibliographic references and treat the reasoning as self-standing.
