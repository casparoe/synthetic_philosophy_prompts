In a discussion about effective altruism, someone rejected the claim that donating a kidney to a stranger could be morally obligatory by insisting that such acts are, by definition, “above and beyond the call of duty,” so treating them as required collapses the distinction between duty and heroism and violates the principle that “ought implies can.” Separate the precise claims hiding in that phrase: the definitional claim that supererogation marks acts that exceed duty; the normative claim that anything exceeding duty is automatically non-obligatory; the axiological claim that labeling heroic acts “required” strips them of special moral value; and any hidden structural premises—such as whether moral demands have a fixed “moral ceiling,” whether obligations can be graded rather than binary, or whether the “paradox of supererogation” (if an act is best, why is it not also required?) undermines the very category being invoked. Which of these readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which does the slogan actually need to do the work of blocking the kidney-donation argument? Tell me also which precise reading was being invoked when the phrase was used to end the exchange, and whether that reading survives once the paradox and the ceiling/floor distinction are taken seriously.
