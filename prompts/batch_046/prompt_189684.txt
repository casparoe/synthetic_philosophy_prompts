I have recently begun hosting small, private viewings of contemporary prints and poetry for a close circle of friends, and I have become preoccupied with the fact that the value I assign to these gatherings does not decompose neatly into the value of the art plus the value of the companionship taken separately. This has pushed me back toward G. E. Moore’s treatment of personal affection and aesthetic appreciation as intrinsic goods that combine in organic wholes, and I am trying to determine whether my intuitions about these gatherings can be captured by any coherent principle.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, I think a beautiful painting viewed in the presence of a close friend is more valuable, all else equal, than the same painting viewed alone; but I also think the increase is not merely additive—the shared attention seems to alter the quality of the aesthetic experience itself. Second, if a friendship exists almost entirely around discussing art—say, two people who meet only to critique exhibitions—I feel its value is lower than that of a friendship that includes shared aesthetic experiences within a broader context of mutual care; yet I still think the art-discussions contribute genuine value that a purely practical alliance would lack. Third, imagine two friends experience a powerful work together, but afterward discover they had entirely opposite emotional reactions—one found it hopeful, the other despairing. I think the friendship is not thereby damaged, but I also feel the shared moment was somehow less valuable than if they had genuinely resonated, even though each person’s individual experience of the work retains its full intrinsic worth. Fourth, consider a beautiful rare manuscript that can be read by only one person at a time; I think its value does not depend on being shared, but I also think that if its owner reads it aloud to a trusted friend—one listening in silence—the value of that session exceeds the manuscript’s solitary value plus the companionship value taken separately. Fifth, contrast this with being in a crowded museum surrounded by strangers: the presence of others seems to diminish rather than enhance the aesthetic value, even though no one is actively interfering.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps along Moorean lines about the conditions under which goods form organic unities—that delivers all five verdicts? Or must some be abandoned? Specifically, can my distinction between instrumental co-presence (the crowd) and genuine shared appreciation (the intimate salon) be maintained without smuggling in an independent principle about intimacy? What does the resulting account predict for a case I have not listed: two people who have lost all personal affection for one another but continue to meet regularly in a particular beautiful garden they both love—does that remaining shared aesthetic value sustain any intrinsic worth in their continued association, or does the friendship’s value evaporate once the personal affection outside the aesthetic context has faded? And finally, which of my five verdicts is the weakest link that a simpler principle should overturn?
