After a long argument with a friend about whether Scanlonian contractualism can genuinely avoid aggregating harms, I want to work through the disagreement more carefully. Write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—one defending Scanlon’s framework of justifiability to each person and the other pressing the aggregation problem—centered on this specific case: a public-health policy saves one life with certainty but imposes a fatal risk of exactly one in a million on each of one million distinct people, making a collective death nearly certain. The contractualist should explain why no affected individual can reasonably reject being subjected to the tiny risk, while the critic should argue that this verdict depends on an implicit aggregation of the near-certain collective harm, and should propose a concrete alternative such as a weighted-lottery model of distributive justification. Each speaker must respond directly to the other’s actual points rather than talk past them, and the exchange should honestly address whether the reasonable-rejection standard can accommodate cumulative-risk cases without collapsing into a disguised form of rule-utilitarianism.
