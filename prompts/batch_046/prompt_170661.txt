I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on why researchers continue to defend models they know are descriptively false. Milton Friedman's classic argument—commonly called the F-twist—holds that the realism of a model's premises is irrelevant so long as its predictions succeed. Philosophers of science, however, have parsed unrealistic assumptions into distinct functional roles. I want to evaluate four specific modes: isolating abstraction, in which confounding variables are stripped away to study a single mechanism in isolation; robustness-through-falsity, in which deliberately false premises generate predictions that remain stable across parameter shifts; heuristic distortion, in which an unrealistic framing is adopted primarily to restructure expert intuition rather than to represent reality; and counterfactual scaffolding, in which physically impossible baseline conditions are posited solely as comparative anchors rather than approximations. Please rank these four modes from the strongest to the weakest defense against the charge that unrealistic assumptions render a model scientifically hollow, assigning each a tier—S, A, B, or C. For each mode, state in a single sentence whether Friedman's predictive criterion alone is sufficient to justify it, or whether it must be supplemented by an independent epistemic virtue—such as explanatory transparency, structural robustness, or instrumental reliability—to avoid being merely false. The judgment should be precise enough for philosophers of science yet accessible to an educated general reader.
