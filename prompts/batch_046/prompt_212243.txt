I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science serving as the grader for an advanced seminar on scientific reasoning. For one exam question, students were asked to apply a framework we developed in the course called counterfactual anchoring—the view that a scientific thought experiment yields knowledge only when its imaginary scenario is tethered to an empirically verified baseline that fixes the variables being manipulated—to two canonical cases: Galileo's falling-bodies argument and Einstein's elevator thought experiment. The exam prompt read as follows: "Assess whether Galileo's reductio of Aristotelian speed-proportionality and Einstein's equivalence principle each satisfy the counterfactual anchoring condition. For each case, determine whether the reasoning delivers genuine empirical knowledge or merely conceptual clarification, and defend your distinction."

One student submitted the following answer:

"Both cases deploy imaginary scenarios, yet only Galileo's argument satisfies counterfactual anchoring. Galileo starts from empirically observed regularities—specifically, that bodies of differing weight descend inclined planes at rates that are approximately equal, once air resistance is minimized—then constructs a reductio in which a heavy object is tied to a lighter one. The resulting contradiction with Aristotelian speed-proportionality forces the conclusion that natural acceleration is uniform and independent of mass, a result that predicts identical free-fall in a vacuum and was later incorporated into Newtonian dynamics and experimentally verified.

Einstein's elevator, however, fails the anchoring condition. The scenario of being weightless inside a sealed, freely falling chamber was not empirically available to Einstein; it is a purely hypothetical construct. Consequently, the equivalence principle does not yield empirical knowledge but merely establishes a conceptual identity between inertial and gravitational mass. Although Einstein's field equations were eventually tested through Mercury's orbital precession and measurements of starlight deflection, those confirmations belong to the derived theory rather than to the thought experiment itself. Thus, while Galileo's reasoning produces anchored predictive knowledge, Einstein's serves only conceptual clarification."

Please evaluate this response as if you were assigning it a course grade. Give a specific grade (for example, B+ or 16/20) and justify it with point-by-point comments. In particular, identify where the student correctly applies the counterfactual anchoring framework, flag any subtle errors regarding the empirical basis of Einstein's principle and the logical function of Galileo's reductio, and assess whether the sharp distinction the student draws between the two cases actually holds up.
