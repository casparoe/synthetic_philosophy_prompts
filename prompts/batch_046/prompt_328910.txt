I have been attempting to systematise my philosophical commitments in preparation for a seminar on conceptual issues in medicine, and I should like a consistency audit before I present them to the group. Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) For somatic conditions I am a biological naturalist in approximately the Boorsean vein: a state counts as a disease if and only if some internal part functions with statistically abnormal inefficiency relative to the species-typical design, and this determination can in principle be made without importing values.

(2) For mental disorder I am a harmful-dysfunction normativist: psychiatric taxonomy necessarily combines an objective dysfunction component with a normative judgement about harmfulness, and neither component can be eliminated without loss.

(3) I reject evolutionary debunking arguments in medicine; that our disease categories may have been shaped by evolutionary selection or social utility does not, in my view, undermine their epistemic authority.

(4) I distinguish sharply between "health," which I take to be irreducibly evaluative and culturally variable, and "pathogen," which I understand as a purely causal-descriptive category—an organism that initiates a specific pathological process—carrying no evaluative content.

(5) Within evolutionary medicine I favour a teleological, value-neutral criterion for distinguishing adaptive defensive responses (such as fever or acute anxiety) from genuine pathological failures: the distinction turns on whether a regulatory mechanism is functioning as evolved, not on statistical rarity, and I take this teleological assessment to be entirely free of normative judgement.

(6) I deny that congenital deafness or achondroplasia are diseases, but not because they lack biological dysfunction—indeed they involve statistically atypical functioning—but because in accommodating social environments they do not cause harm; hence my exclusion of them rests on social norms rather than on biological statistics.

Please examine these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each serious tension, specify the least costly revision and what I should be prepared to surrender. Finally, indicate which single change would resolve the greatest number of inconsistencies, and whether adopting it forces me to abandon my naturalism about somatic disease altogether.
