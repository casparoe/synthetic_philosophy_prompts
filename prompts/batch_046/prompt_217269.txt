Exam question (as given to students):

Bernard Williams argues that moral revolutions—such as the decline of dueling—are rarely driven by rational argument alone, and that appeals to honor typically rationalize deeper shifts in institutional interest and power. Kwame Anthony Appiah, in The Honor Code, contends that honor itself can be an independent engine of moral change: practices collapse when they become dishonorable, often because an alternative pathway to status opens up that renders the old practice shameful rather than merely irrational.

Consider the following engineered historical case. In late-nineteenth-century Central European philology, professors maintained a practice called Scholarly Reckoning: when two scholars advanced incompatible interpretations of a canonical text, they were expected to resolve the dispute through a formalized ritual of public academic insult and theatrical repudiation, with refusal interpreted as an admission of intellectual cowardice. By 1890 the practice had collapsed, though it persisted longest among senior tenured professors with weak departmental ties.

A structural change occurred in 1875: the introduction of anonymous peer review for journal publication. This permitted scholars to reject interpretations and defend positions without public attribution, thereby reducing the career cost of avoiding the ritual.

Analyze this collapse using both frameworks. First, determine whether anonymous peer review functions here primarily as an Appian honor-based mechanism (it creates an alternative status pathway that makes refusal honorable) or as a Williams-style interest-based structural shift (it alters the cost-benefit calculus of participation). Second, account for why the practice lingered longest among senior tenured professors with weak institutional ties, and indicate which theorist this persistence favors. Third, address the following conceptual tension: Appiah insists that honor must remain publicly visible to function as a moral engine, yet anonymous peer review precisely severs the link between judgment and public identity. Does this tension undermine the Appian explanation of the case?

Write a structured response that applies both theories precisely and resolves the tension.

Student answer:

Appiah's theory in The Honor Code holds that moral revolutions happen when a practice becomes incompatible with honor—foot-binding and dueling ended because they came to signal backwardness rather than prestige. Williams, by contrast, thinks rational argument never drives moral change and that honor language is just a post-hoc rationalization of shifts in material interest and institutional power.

In the Scholarly Reckoning case, anonymous peer review clearly operates as an Appian mechanism. Before 1875, refusing the ritual meant public cowardice; after peer review, scholars could defend interpretations through anonymous publication and still gain prestige. This created an alternative status pathway, so refusal became honorable rather than shameful. That explains the rapid collapse after 1875.

The persistence among senior tenured professors with weak ties actually supports Appiah over Williams. These professors had tenure, so they faced the least career risk from avoiding the ritual; they also had weak ties, meaning they had little institutional interest in maintaining alliances through the practice. If interest and power drove the ritual, they should have been the first to abandon it. Instead, they kept it longest, which shows that honor operates independently of material interest: they clung to the old honor code even when it served no practical purpose.

As for the visibility problem, Appiah does not require that every honor transaction be publicly visible—only that the macro-level honor code be socially recognized. Anonymous peer review still operates within a public prestige system (journals have reputations, editors are known), so the visibility condition is satisfied at the institutional level. Therefore there is no real tension, and Appiah's framework applies cleanly.
