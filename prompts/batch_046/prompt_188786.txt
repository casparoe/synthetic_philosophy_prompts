I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship in philosophy and economics, and I want a proposal that turns a half-formed idea into a tractable project. The idea is this: Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren lays out a normative vision of the good life—once absolute needs are met, humanity should turn away from the accumulation of money and toward leisure, art, and friendship, treating the love of money as a kind of mental illness we can outgrow. Meanwhile, The General Theory treats liquidity preference and the speculative motives behind investment not as mere pathologies but as structural features of an economy marked by radical uncertainty about the future. The standard literature keeps these two Keyneses separate: one is read as utopian forecasting, the other as technical macroeconomics. I think they are in deeper tension than that, and I want to map exactly how deep.

The precise question: Does Keynes's macroeconomic theory of investment, liquidity preference, and long-term expectation presuppose the very psychological disposition—the anxiety-driven attachment to monetary security—that his ethical vision requires us to abandon? And if so, is the tension resolvable by treating liquidity preference as a transitional feature of immature capitalism, or does it show that Keynes's post-scarcity good life is institutionally unstable?

Why existing answers leave it open: Commentators either celebrate the 1930 essay as prophetic or treat The General Theory as self-contained descriptive machinery. Those who discuss animal spirits tend to treat them as exogenous psychological quirks rather than as endogenous to a monetary system. Few ask whether reducing the importance of economic growth—Keynes's explicit ethical goal—would necessarily suppress investment and therefore employment, making the good life self-defeating. I want to determine whether the need for speculative motives is built into the framework or merely historically contingent.

Approach and method: I will do a close conceptual analysis of Keynes's distinction between absolute and relative needs, read against his account of liquidity preference and the state of long-term expectation. I will ask whether the monetary system generates perpetual relative scarcity—security, status, precaution—even after absolute material needs are satisfied, which would mean that curing the love of money requires redesigning the institutions that make it rational. I will also examine whether modern welfare economics, particularly discussions of positional goods and status competition, gives us the tools to decouple relative needs from monetary accumulation, or whether it confirms that Keynes underestimated how deeply the monetary framework produces the pathology he wants to cure. The project is philosophical reconstruction, not archival or econometric; I will rely on published texts and established conceptual frameworks, and I will add all citations myself.

What a result looks like: A clear argument that either (a) Keynes's ethics and economics are compatible because liquidity preference withers as institutional uncertainty declines in a mature society, (b) they are in deep tension because the system requires perpetual anxiety to maintain employment, which makes the good life an unstable equilibrium, or (c) the tension is real but manageable through specific institutional redesigns—say, socialized investment or modified liquidity mechanisms—that I can specify conceptually.

What counts as failure: If the analysis collapses into biography—merely asserting that Keynes was personally inconsistent—without showing whether the tension is conceptual or institutional. Or if resolving it requires empirical claims about post-war growth patterns or behavioral data that I cannot defend without original research outside the scope of a philosophical fellowship.

Scope and deliverables: This is tractable for twelve months because it requires no archival discovery or quantitative modeling. The first semester will map the conceptual tension; the second will test it against secondary literature in welfare economics and the philosophy of economics; the final months will produce a manuscript arguing for one of the three outcomes above and specifying what institutional conditions would be necessary for Keynes's vision to be stable. For a foundation or committee asking why philosophy matters here: if Keynes's good life is institutionally unstable, then proposals for post-growth economics and reduced work cannot be purely cultural or technological; they must include monetary redesign. I will fill in all bibliographic references in the final draft.
