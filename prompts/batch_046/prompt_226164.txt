Exam question: Machiavelli often compares fortune to a violent river that can be channeled by dikes and dams, and he insists that virtù is not moral excellence but the practical capacity to adapt and act decisively. Some readers have proposed a further distinction within virtù: active virtù, meaning the forceful shaping of circumstances, versus receptive virtù, meaning the alertness to seize openings that fortune provides without attempting to dominate them. Evaluate whether this two-part distinction is coherent within Machiavelli’s broader framework, especially in view of his claim that fortune governs roughly half of human affairs while free will governs the other half. In your answer, discuss one concrete historical figure Machiavelli treats—such as Cesare Borgia, Agathocles, or Romulus—and show how that figure illustrates either active or receptive virtù, or both.

Student answer: Machiavelli describes fortune as a river that can be restrained by dikes, showing that human effort can limit its damage. For Machiavelli, virtù is not Christian virtue but effectiveness in politics. He also says fortune controls about half our actions, leaving the rest to free will. The active versus receptive distinction fits well here: active virtù means building the dikes, while receptive virtù means recognizing when the waters are about to rise so you can adapt. Cesare Borgia is a clear case of receptive virtù. After his father Pope Alexander VI died, Borgia inherited the opportunity to rule Romagna and largely waited for fortune to deliver the region to him, rather than forcing events through his own initiative. By contrast, Romulus shows active virtù because he founded Rome through bold, violent action without waiting for favorable conditions. So the distinction holds up, because both modes are necessary to manage the half of affairs that fortune does not control.
