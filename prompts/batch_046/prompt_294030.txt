I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or welfare economics, but I have recently tried to understand Harsanyi's aggregation theorem because of its apparent relevance to distributed optimization. From what I have read, the theorem proves that if rational agents share a common prior and satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, then any socially rational aggregation of their preferences must take the form of a weighted linear sum of individual utility functions, with weights that are constant across all possible states of the world. I have been attempting to apply this to federated learning, where each client can be modeled as an agent holding a local loss function, yet all clients observe the same global data distribution. Under the theorem, it seems that rational aggregation should force every client's objective into a single global model using identical weights for all clients, which would imply that personalization—assigning different effective influence to different clients—ought to be irrational or impossible. Yet in practice, personalized federated methods consistently outperform uniform global models. So I want to ask: does this empirical success show that local gradient updates violate the independence axiom that Harsanyi requires, perhaps because each client's parameter updates alter its posterior and thereby destroy the common prior? Or is the deeper error my assumption that optimizing a neural-network loss landscape over parameter space is structurally analogous to aggregating preferences over social lotteries?
