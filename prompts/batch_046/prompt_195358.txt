I am preparing the opening remarks for a public forum this Saturday evening at the town hall. The council is about to vote on whether to landmark the old Riverside Textile Mill—built in 1892, shuttered since 1987, now half-collapsed—or to clear it for housing and a community garden. The room will be split: preservation architects, former mill workers and their families, young neighbors who mostly want the rust and rats gone, and a few local artists. I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the last year I have been trying to think through this problem using an idea I found in an essay by Friedrich Nietzsche. He describes three ways the past can serve life: the monumental, in which we look to great past achievements to inspire present action; the antiquarian, in which we preserve and revere the past for its own sake; and the critical, in which we break from or judge the past when it burdens life. I want an eight-minute speech structured by these three modes, but applied precisely to this mill and this vote, so that the framework actually constrains what I can say rather than just decorating it.

Here is the complication that has been keeping me awake. In this town, the generational alignment of Nietzsche’s modes seems inverted. The older workers—men and women who actually lost fingers to the looms and remember the labor strike of 1912—mostly want the building torn down; for them the mill is a weight, not a monument, and preserving it feels like forcing them to live inside an antiquarian shrine to suffering they want to be free of. Their grandchildren, who never worked a day inside, are the ones circulating petitions to save every brick and every faded “Employees Only” sign, treating the site with a reverence that borders on the antiquarian. Meanwhile, a local collective wants to install a public work honoring the strike, but only if the monument also includes the injuries, the closure, and the empty years—trying to make the past monumental without sanitizing it. I want the speech to hold these three attitudes in the room at once, to show why no single one should dominate the decision, and to argue that “living with this history” is different from either bulldozing it or freezing it behind glass. The philosophical structure should shape the ending: I should not be able to conclude with a simple “let’s meet in the middle,” because that might betray the critical need to judge the past rather than merely balance interests. At the same time, I need to speak to people who are there for family memory, for civic pride, or just because the roof is falling in. Please do not include any bibliography, citation, or reading list—I am speaking from what I have read and lived, not from scholarship. And please do not invent specific archival documents or quote historical texts verbatim; if you reference the 1912 strike, treat it as the local event I have described. Can you write this for me?
