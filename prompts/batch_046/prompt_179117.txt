I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy systematically, and I have been trying to think through whether panpsychist interpretations of quantum field theory actually generate the ethical conclusions often drawn from them. To that end, I keep returning to Leibniz’s Monadology (1714), but I am unsure how to read one of its central distinctions in this modern context. I would be grateful for help understanding the conceptual architecture of the following passage (Robert Latta’s translation):

“The passing condition which involves and represents a multiplicity in the unity, or in the simple substance, is nothing else than what is called perception. This should be carefully distinguished from apperception or consciousness, as will appear in what follows. In this matter the Cartesians have fallen into a serious error, in that they deny the existence of those perceptions of which we are not conscious. It is this also which has led them to believe that spirits alone are monads and that there are no souls of animals or other entelechies, and it has led them to make the common confusion between a protracted period of unconsciousness and actual death. They have thus adopted the Scholastic error that souls can exist entirely separated from bodies, and have even confirmed ill-balanced minds in the belief that souls are mortal.”

My specific difficulty is this. Contemporary arguments for pan-sentience in fundamental physics often move quickly from the claim that basic physical entities possess some form of experientiality to the claim that the universe therefore contains a vast, hidden population of suffering subjects. But Leibniz seems to draw a firm line between bare perception—which, he suggests, belongs even to simple substances that lack consciousness—and apperception, which belongs to souls or entelechies and which he associates with animals rather than with inanimate or merely compound things. If one applies this distinction rigorously, it seems that a quantum field might exhibit Leibnizian “perception” (some internal multiplicity of state) without exhibiting anything like consciousness or suffering-capacity.

Does Leibniz’s framework therefore block the inference from physical panpsychism to an expanded domain of moral patients? More precisely, does his insistence that animals possess “souls” or “entelechies” rather than mere bare monadic perception imply that only systems organized to the point of achieving apperception are genuine candidates for ethical concern? And if so, is the panpsychist who claims that fundamental physics reveals widespread suffering smuggling in an assumption—that perception and suffering-capacity are coextensive—which Leibniz’s own architecture would reject? I am not asking for a reading list or for citations of contemporary authors; I am trying to determine whether the Monadology supports, undermines, or remains neutral on the move from “everything perceives” to “everything, or nearly everything, can suffer.”
