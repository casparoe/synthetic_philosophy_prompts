I've been trying to systematize my intuitions about rational belief revision when evidence doesn't determine a unique observation but instead shifts probabilities over a partition of possibilities—updates in the Jeffrey tradition, though I apply them inconsistently. Here are my concrete verdicts.

Case 1: A climate model outputs an ensemble distribution over future temperature anomalies—+1.5°C (40%), +2.0°C (35%), +2.5°C (25%). A policymaker revises her beliefs about agricultural yield by taking her prior conditional expectations within each temperature bin and weighting them by these ensemble probabilities. Verdict: correct.

Case 2: A physician receives a screening result that is neither clearly positive nor negative, but the assay reports a calibrated distribution over {healthy, mild-disease, severe-disease} of (20%, 50%, 30%). She updates her beliefs about prognosis by adopting these weights over her prior conditionals. Verdict: correct.

Case 3: A historian discovers an archive that makes "the coup was planned in March" twice as probable relative to "planned in April," but offers no internal information about specific days within March. She updates beliefs about logistical details by applying Jeffrey's rule over the {March, April} partition, preserving her prior conditionals within each month. Verdict: correct.

Case 4: An engineer measures structural stress with a sensor having Gaussian error; the reading is 82 MPa, and she updates her continuous belief distribution over true stress by likelihood-weighting her prior density. Verdict: correct, but if the error is heavy-tailed (Cauchy), I feel the revision should be more conservative—perhaps truncated—despite the formal rule being identical.

Case 5: A juror is told a DNA match has a random-match probability of 1 in 100,000, and also learns the lab has an unknown contamination rate she subjectively estimates at 5%. She assigns weights {valid-match: 85%, contamination: 10%, random-match: 5%} and updates belief about guilt using these over her prior conditionals. Verdict: wrong—the weights feel invented rather than evidentially fixed.

Case 6: A gambler learns one card is missing from a deck, with no information about which one. He assigns uniform probabilities over the 52 possibilities and updates beliefs about the remaining deck's composition by averaging over missing-card possibilities via Jeffrey's rule. Verdict: correct.

Case 7: A philosopher receives anonymous peer-review feedback—one hostile comment, one supportive, one ambiguous—knowing there are two reviewers. She assigns {hostile-wrote-all: 30%, supportive-wrote-all: 30%, mixed: 40%} and updates belief about the paper's quality by weighting prior conditionals on these authorship partitions. Verdict: wrong—this launders subjective ignorance rather than responding to objective evidential weights.

Case 8: A meteorologist learns her satellite has a calibration drift biasing temperatures upward by an unknown amount uniformly distributed over [0.5, 1.5] degrees. She applies Jeffrey conditionalization over the drift-parameter partition to revise beliefs about actual temperatures. Verdict: correct.

Cases 3 and 6 both apply Jeffrey over partitions with no internal resolution, yet I endorse both. Cases 5 and 7 both assign subjective partition weights, yet I reject both—though Case 5 concerns external error processes while Case 7 concerns mental-state authorship. Meanwhile Case 4 introduces tension: I accept Jeffrey for Gaussian error but want to restrict it for heavy-tailed error even though the formula is unchanged.

Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes evidentially determined partition probabilities from subjectively assigned ones, and continuous parameter spaces where likelihood weighting is appropriate from discrete partitions where it isn't?
