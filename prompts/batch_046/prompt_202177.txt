I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on distributive justice and want a consistency audit of my package before I present. Please think through this carefully, as a philosopher of politics and ethics, and trace the logical connections rather than giving a general overview. Here is my current position: (1) I accept Nozick's entitlement theory—justice in holdings is purely historical, fixed by just original acquisition and voluntary transfer—so I think patterned end-state principles like equality or need are incompatible with liberty, as the Wilt Chamberlain argument demonstrates; (2) I also hold that initial acquisition of unowned resources must satisfy a fairly stringent Lockean proviso, and that if background conditions are severely unjust, some apparently voluntary exchanges can be exploitative and may legitimately be corrected by the state; (3) I reject moral desert as a ground for distributive shares; (4) I maintain that taxation is permissible only to correct violations of entitlement conditions (theft, fraud, unjust acquisition), never to realize a distributive pattern; and (5) I think that if a society begins from a genuinely just baseline, persistent inequalities arising from free exchange should be tolerated even when extreme, because liberty outweighs equality in this domain. Do these commitments hang together? Specifically, I'm concerned that (2) smuggles in a background-conditions assessment that undermines (1) and (5)—if we can correct for unjust background conditions, aren't we implicitly committing to a patterned principle? And does (3) conflict with (2)'s claim that exploitation can vitiate consent, since recognizing exploitation seems to invoke some standard of what people deserve? Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish real contradictions from merely apparent ones, and identify the least costly revision: should I weaken the proviso, abandon the anti-pattern stance, or concede that liberty does not always trump equality? Be precise about which pairs actually pull in opposite directions and which can be reconciled by distinguishing acquisition-stage justice from transfer-stage justice.
