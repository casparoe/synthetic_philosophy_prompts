I serve on our university’s Committee for Institutional Honors and Ethical Recognition, and this spring we must decide whether to confer the Distinguished Service Medal for Research Integrity upon Dr. Elena Voss. The matter has become personally urgent: my own laboratory lost two years of funding because of the fabrication ring that Voss exposed in the School of Medicine, and I want to be certain that my recommendation is not colored by gratitude for the outcome alone.

Voss did expose a sustained pattern of data falsification in a high-profile clinical trial. The audit is unambiguous: she was correct, her report was precise, and patient safety was protected. However, internal correspondence released during the subsequent investigation shows that her decision to report was driven primarily by a longstanding professional grievance against the trial’s principal investigator, who had twice blocked her promotion. In her own testimony, Voss states that she believed the falsification was wrong and that she had a duty to report it, but she also concedes that she might have remained silent had the grievance not existed.

Our committee’s charter directs us to honor actions that demonstrate sustained commitment to ethical principle, not merely actions that happen to produce ethical outcomes. Half the committee argues that because Voss’s dominant motive was retaliatory, her act lacks the moral worth required for institutional recognition; they invoke the familiar distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty. The other half argues that institutions should not attempt to peer into motives, that the correct reason—protecting research integrity—was present and sufficient in her reasoning, and that denying the award would punish a whistleblower for being human.

I am drafting the committee’s formal opinion for circulation before Thursday’s vote. I need a clear recommendation—award the medal, deny it, or award it with an explicit qualification about the nature of the recognition—together with reasoning that treats the strongest opposing view honestly, in a memo of roughly 800 words that the committee can adopt or amend.

In constructing the reasoning, I want the memo to move beyond the standard framing. Specifically, I ask that it apply a conceptual distinction between a psychologically dominant reason and a structurally load-bearing reason. A reason is structurally load-bearing, on this view, if the agent’s correct normative consideration was indispensable to the causal pathway that produced the action, regardless of whether it was the strongest psychological pull. The memo should examine whether this distinction helps resolve the case, or whether it collapses under pressure—particularly in light of Voss’s admission that she might not have acted without the grievance. It should also address the institutional risk: if we honor actions where the correct reason was present but not dominant, do we incentivize grievance-driven reporting, or do we correctly separate institutional recognition from moral purity?

Please write the memo in a formal, institutional voice, with a concrete bottom line and a time-sensitive recommendation suitable for a Thursday decision.
