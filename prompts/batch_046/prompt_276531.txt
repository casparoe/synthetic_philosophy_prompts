Im giving a talk next month at an interdisciplinary world-history seminar — mostly postcolonial historians, a couple quantitative economic historians who still run diffusion regressions on the Columbian exchange, and a philosopher of history who gets anxious about relativism. The thesis is pretty sharp: even so-called "polycentric" global history — the Graeber and Wengrow kind, or recent "connected histories" work — can't escape Eurocentrism because the stitching mechanism itself (trade flows, epidemic vectors, technology transfer) is essentially a 19th-century European archive metric. So I'm proposing "parallel incommensurable archives" instead: tell the history of the Indian Ocean monsoon system and Ming fiscal policy in the same volume, but don't translate one into the other or claim they share a causal frame; keep them deliberately un-synthesized.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they'll actually hit, phrased like each camp talks. The postcolonial crowd will like the Eurocentrism diagnosis but accuse me of reproducing Orientalist separation under a fancy new name — they want solidarity across archives, not walls. The quant people will say I threw out causal linkage and turned history into literature. There's also the hidden objection I'm dreading, probably that selecting what counts as an "archive boundary" is itself a hidden synthesis I'm smuggling in. For each: what's the real force, what should I concede up front, what's my reply in the thirty seconds I have, and where is that reply weakest? Don't skip the hidden one. Finish the whole prep sheet with a line that starts Verdict: followed by that hidden objection in a short phrase.
