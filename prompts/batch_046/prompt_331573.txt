I have been trying to articulate, for a novel I am planning, what makes a popular referendum feel democratically legitimate rather than merely procedurally valid, and I find my own judgments pulling in directions that are hard to reconcile. Here are five cases with my verdicts.

First, the United Kingdom's 2016 referendum on European Union membership. Turnout was high; the question was binary but the framing mixed economic forecasting with questions of sovereignty and collective identity. The result was chaotic, implementation was disorderly, and later polling suggested widespread regret. Nevertheless, I judge it legitimate: the question concerned the fundamental narrative of the polity—who we are in relation to others—and that is precisely the kind of decision that should not be left to representatives alone.

Second, a California-style ballot proposition that would institute a complex, multi-tiered carbon-pricing mechanism with automatic regulatory adjustments, placed on the ballot through paid signature collection and passing with fifty-two percent in a low-turnout election. I judge this illegitimate. The mechanism requires iterative technical revision, depends on contested empirical models, and offers voters no stable account of what they have decided. The vote creates an illusion of finality where none is appropriate.

Third, Switzerland's 2016 referendum on an unconditional basic income. The proposal was clear in principle, the public debate was extensive, and it was rejected by roughly three-quarters of voters. I consider this exemplary: whether a society should guarantee subsistence regardless of labor is a moral principle suitable for direct decision, and the overwhelming rejection does not diminish the legitimacy of asking.

Fourth, a municipal referendum in a European city—imagine a place like Porto or Bruges—triggered after a prominent local writer argued that modernist glass-and-steel buildings rupture the narrative continuity of the city's historical self-image. The ballot asks whether all new construction in that style should be banned. Turnout is unusually high at sixty-five percent, and the ban passes with fifty-eight percent. Here I feel torn. By my reasoning in the first case, this touches collective identity and narrative coherence; yet I want to say it is illegitimate, because aesthetic judgment and minority rights should not be settled by majority vote, and the framing feels like emotional manipulation disguised as democratic deliberation.

Fifth, a regional referendum on enhanced autonomy, but the ballot text asks whether voters support constitutional reform that strengthens local decision-making within the existing national framework, a formulation that supporters of independence and supporters of mere administrative decentralization both claim endorses their position. It passes narrowly. I judge this illegitimate, not because the issue is unimportant, but because the ambiguity prevents the decision from functioning as a collective commitment: there is no shared story about what has been decided.

Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts? I am tempted by something like this: a referendum is legitimate when the issue is both fundamental—touching the polity's self-definition—and narratively closed, meaning the decision can be integrated into an ongoing collective story without requiring perpetual reinterpretation or hidden technical revision. This would explain the first and third cases, and condemn the second and fifth. But it struggles with the fourth: that referendum also offers narrative closure, perhaps too neatly. Must I drop my judgment about the fourth case, or can the principle be refined—for instance, by requiring that the narrative in question be political rather than aesthetic, or by adding a condition about the protection of minorities that I have not yet made explicit? Or is the real tension elsewhere, perhaps in my willingness to accept emotional framing in the first case but reject it in the fourth?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, with precision. For each, indicate which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to abandon. Also say what the principle implies for cases I have not mentioned—for instance, a referendum on abolishing a monarchy, or one on the technical specifications of a nuclear waste storage site. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual architecture.
