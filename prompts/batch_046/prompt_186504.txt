I work as a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, but I have been trying to understand how ideas about archives and power—specifically who gets recorded and what silences remain in the sources—might apply to the systems I build. I recently observed a disagreement between two colleagues about this, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

At our company, two senior engineers, Maya and Jonas, argued over whether the absence of execution logs for unauthenticated requests in our serverless platform should be interpreted as an archival silence structured by power, or as a purely technical omission.

Maya argued that our logging pipeline writes traces only for requests that pass authentication. As a result, the historical record retains only recognized users—authenticated subjects—while bots, failed login attempts, and unauthenticated probes are systematically erased. She claimed that this is not a neutral design choice: it reflects and reinforces who is deemed worthy of being remembered. The archive is therefore shaped by power, and its silences are not accidental gaps but productive omissions that will distort future security audits and accountability.

Jonas countered that this framing over-reads infrastructure. The omission exists because storing every rejected packet would be prohibitively expensive and noisy; it is an engineering trade-off, not an exercise of authority. He said that labeling every missing dataset an archival silence dilutes the concept, and that there is a meaningful distinction between a state censoring documents and a Lambda function omitting unauthenticated traffic from CloudWatch. The absence of logs, he insisted, carries no intentional or structural message about whose history matters.

Maya responded that intentionality is not required for an archive to be shaped by power. Even unintended design choices can produce systematic invisibility. Because future analysts will see only successful, authenticated interactions, the absence of failed attempts becomes an invisible frame that makes the system appear more secure and more uniformly legitimate than it actually is. The silence, she said, does not merely lack content—it actively constrains what can be known.

Jonas replied that this conflates epistemic limitation with political erasure. Every sensor has a threshold; not every unrecorded event is suppressed. He granted that the missing data might mislead auditors, but maintained that the cause is resource optimization, not the exercise of power over who is remembered. To treat a cost-driven omission as archival violence, he said, risks making the concept unfalsifiable.

They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not decide. Please explain who is right about each specific point they raised, identify what—if anything—both parties are missing, and reach an overall verdict about whether this particular logging omission should be understood as an archive shaped by power or as a neutral technical boundary. Defend your conclusion rather than ending on an equivocal note.
