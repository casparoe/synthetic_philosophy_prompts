I am preparing a position paper for a seminar on intergenerational justice, and a colleague shared with me her understanding of Karl Jaspers’s fourfold taxonomy of guilt from his postwar reflections. She summarizes it as follows: Jaspers separates criminal guilt, which attaches to specific unlawful acts and is adjudicated by courts; political guilt, which every citizen bears for the actions of the state they belong to regardless of personal involvement; moral guilt, which concerns individual conscience and one’s own omissions or complicities; and metaphysical guilt, which is a universal solidarity among all human beings for failing to prevent injustice anywhere.

She then argues that for Jaspers, political guilt is genuinely collective in a way that moral guilt is not, but she extends this to claim that metaphysical guilt also functions as a collective inheritance: descendants of a nation that committed systematic wrongs inherit a portion of that nation’s political and metaphysical guilt through the continuity of its institutions and cultural memory, even if they did not personally participate. From this she concludes that Jaspers allows us to speak of a nation being guilty across generations in a literal, not merely metaphorical, sense—something like a transmitted liability—while still maintaining that moral guilt remains strictly individual and non-transferable. I am inclined to think she is conflating metaphysical solidarity with inherited blame, but I am not well-versed enough in Jaspers to say exactly where the line is crossed. Could you audit her summary? Please separate what accurately reflects Jaspers’s distinctions, what subtly overstates or misplaces them—particularly regarding whether metaphysical or political guilt can be transmitted to descendants as an inherited burden—and whether her conclusion about literal transgenerational national guilt follows from the corrected view, or whether it depends on smuggling collective moral guilt back into the framework under another name.
