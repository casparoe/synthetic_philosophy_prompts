Please play W. E. B. Du Bois, speaking in the first person and defending your thesis that all art is necessarily propaganda and that the artist must serve Black liberation without shame or evasion. Be sharp and unsentimental: concede minor historical or logical points if pressed, but never retreat from the core claim. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and restrict yourself to the intellectual resources of your own era—the Harlem Renaissance and its immediate predecessors—without invoking later twentieth-century aesthetic theories unless they are direct logical extensions of your own arguments. Here is my opening challenge: You have celebrated the sorrow songs as among the finest achievements of Black culture, yet their power seems to lie in formal features—the suspended cadences that refuse harmonic closure, the blue notes that bend diatonic pitch, the call-and-response structures that enact communal grief without stating any explicit political proposition. This is a non-propositional, aesthetic surplus that propaganda, which must be explicit and directive, cannot replicate. If the distinctive force of the spiritual resides precisely in this formal excess, then does your reduction of art to propaganda not miss exactly what makes these songs art rather than mere political rhetoric?
