I've been turning over a problem that doesn't seem to have a standard answer in the places I've looked, and I'm hoping to see whether my reactions to particular situations add up to anything consistent.

Consider these cases. First, I receive two job offers that match in salary, location, benefits, and daily work; I flip a coin and accept one. That feels like a perfectly rational way to decide. Second, I'm genuinely torn between becoming a musician and becoming an accountant—both appeal to me in different but apparently equal measure over the course of my life. Here, flipping a coin feels like I'm dodging a decision I should be making for deeper reasons, even though I can't articulate what those reasons are. Third, a chess program evaluates two legal moves as exactly equal in expected value and picks one at random; that seems unproblematic. Fourth, a judge must award custody to one of two parents who are tied on every measurable welfare indicator for the child, and she draws lots. That strikes me as inappropriate, even if the outcome is fair. Fifth, I can save only one of two strangers from drowning, and every relevant feature—distance, need, my relationship to them—is identical; I think I may save either, but using a coin feels less objectionable here than in the courtroom.

My question is whether there is a single principle—perhaps involving whether my choice expresses my identity, whether I bear special responsibility for the outcome, or whether the goods at stake can be measured on one scale—that delivers all five verdicts, or whether I have to abandon at least one. If a principle saves the career and courtroom judgments but treats the rescue case differently, I want to know why. I also want to know what it predicts for a situation I keep returning to: a city planner must site one fire station on the east or west side of a town, and every risk model shows exactly equal expected lives saved; she programs a random selection. Is that like the job offer, like the judge, or like something else entirely?

Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and explain what it says about the planner case.
