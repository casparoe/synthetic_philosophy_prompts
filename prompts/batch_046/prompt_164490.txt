Question: Explain how the epistemic regress problem arises specifically for perceptual justification, distinguishing the challenge of circularity from the challenge of arbitrariness. Then present three responses: (i) classical foundationalism, according to which basic perceptual beliefs are immediately justified and need no inferential support; (ii) moderate foundationalism, which holds that basic beliefs are non-inferentially justified by experiential states but are nevertheless fallible; and (iii) infinitism, which denies that justification ever terminates and instead requires that for every belief there be a further available reason, without requiring that the believer actually completes an infinite chain. Next, address the standard psychological objection that infinitism demands an actually infinite number of occurrent beliefs in a finite mind: does this objection misunderstand the infinitist distinction between the availability of reasons and the completion of a series? To make this concrete, consider a perceiver who, upon judging that an apple is red, could in principle cite the appearance of redness, then the reliability of the lighting, then the reliability of her color vision, and so on without ever reaching a terminus—does this illustrate infinitist justification, or does it confuse the logical structure of reasons with the psychology of reasoning? Finally, evaluate whether a modified coherentism that allows some beliefs to be more central but none to be basic can avoid collapsing into moderate foundationalism. Conclude with your own assessment.

Student Answer: The regress problem happens when you ask why someone believes something, and then ask why they believe that reason, and it goes on forever. For perceptual beliefs, this is a problem because if every belief needs a reason, we never get started. Foundationalism solves this by saying some beliefs don't need reasons at all because they justify themselves. Classical foundationalism says basic perceptual beliefs are like this: they are self-justifying and certain, like believing you exist. Moderate foundationalism is basically the same, except it says basic beliefs can be wrong sometimes, but when they are right they still justify themselves immediately. Coherentism solves the regress differently: beliefs form a web where each belief supports the others, so nothing needs an outside foundation. If you tried to let one belief be basic inside the web, the web would break and you'd just have foundationalism. Infinitism says the chain never stops, but that's okay because people can always give another reason. The problem is that this requires a person to actually hold an infinite number of beliefs in their mind all at once, which is impossible for humans, so infinitism is false. In the apple case, the perceiver is just giving more psychological explanations, not real logical reasons, so it doesn't help infinitism. Because classical foundationalism gives us certain, self-justifying starting points without requiring impossible infinite minds or broken webs, it is clearly the best response.
