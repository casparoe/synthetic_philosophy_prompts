Consider the institutional practice surrounding the legal category of genocide, rooted in the framework originally articulated by Raphael Lemkin and now codified in international criminal law, which requires proof of specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. Alongside this, observe the widespread political, commemorative, and legislative practice of applying the same term to mass atrocities—such as sustained campaigns of forced cultural assimilation, ecologically induced displacement of indigenous communities, or systematic gender-based violence—where no documentary evidence of totalizing destructive intent exists. In this bifurcation, international courts often convict perpetrators of crimes against humanity for acts that advocacy networks, memorial institutions, and some national legislatures simultaneously classify as genocide, producing situations in which a single historical episode carries a dual ontological status: legally non-genocidal yet memorially and politically genocidal. What theory of moral and legal categorization does this bifurcated naming practice presuppose? Does it require that legal kinds be sharply bounded by mental-state conditions—something like an essentialism about culpable intent—while moral-political kinds function as family-resemblance concepts anchored in collective experience rather than perpetrator psychology? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what genocide denotes and what work it does—whether it tracks an objective harm structure, a particular psychological state, or a social function of recognition and prevention—and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Is the split philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of collective harm, group identity, and institutional naming could license the same divergence? Finally, if the underlying view were replaced by one that treated genocide as a scalar property of structural violence rather than a binary, intent-dependent legal status, what specific changes would have to occur in courtroom evidentiary standards, memorial authority, and the politics of atrocity naming?
