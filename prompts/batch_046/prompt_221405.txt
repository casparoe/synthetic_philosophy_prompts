Please consider the following exam question and a student’s response.

Question: “A standard objection to rights-based deontology is that it seems paradoxical: if one may not violate a single right to prevent ten identical violations, then in large-scale emergencies the view requires permitting catastrophic harm rather than acting. Some defenders reply by distinguishing agent-relative permissions from agent-neutral aggregation. Is this reply sufficient, or does the paradox persist at the level of moral accounting? Discuss, with reference to whether deontological constraints are ‘non-extensive’—that is, whether violations do not sum linearly with harm in the way consequentialist calculus assumes.”

Student answer: “The objection mistakes deontology for a form of rule-utilitarianism. A deontologist denies that rights violations are fungible goods; they are not like units of pain that can be traded off. The agent-relative perspective explains why I may not kill one innocent person even to save ten: my duty not to murder is directed at my own action, not at the world-state. Furthermore, Kant’s categorical imperative resolves the tension by testing maxims: a maxim of ‘break rights when the numbers favour it’ cannot be universalised without contradiction. Finally, the doctrine of double effect shows that if a single rights-violation is merely foreseen rather than intended as a means, it is permissible to cause it to prevent greater harm, so the paradox dissolves in practice.”

Please grade this answer out of 20 and provide specific point-by-point comments justifying the mark. Evaluate whether the student correctly distinguishes agent-relative reasons from aggregation failures, whether the appeal to universalisation genuinely addresses the numerical paradox, and whether the use of double effect is applied consistently to the case of directly causing one violation to prevent many. Do not offer only a general impression; assess each component separately. Write your response in British English.
