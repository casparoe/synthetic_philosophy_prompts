Design a decision tree for a philosophy-of-science audience that sorts positions on whether intuitionistic logic should govern reasoning about continuous physical measurements whose outcomes depend on empirically inaccessible initial conditions. Use exactly four branching questions: (1) Do mathematical objects exist mind-independently? (2) Does the law of excluded middle apply to propositions about unobservable physical states? (3) Must continuity be constructively provable, or can it be defined classically? (4) Does successful empirical prediction justify retaining classical logic even when constructive proof fails? Each leaf should be assigned one of five positions—classical scientific realist, Brouwerian intuitionist, Markov-style computable realist, formalist deflationist, or predicative constructivist—or marked "unnamed," followed by one sentence on its most awkward commitment regarding experimental prediction. Do not present the tree as one neutral taxonomy among others: defend why these four splits are the logically decisive ones, place each named position with explicit reasoning, and for every empty combination state definitively whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, with a brief justification for each judgment. Keep the tree focused on this applied framing rather than on pure mathematics alone.
