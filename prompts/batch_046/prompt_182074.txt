I am drafting a position paper for a university curriculum-review committee, and I want to clarify a philosophical tension embedded in our own institutional architecture. Our history department organizes faculty lines, hiring priorities, and the required undergraduate survey sequence into four sequential blocks—Ancient, Medieval, Renaissance and Early Modern, and Modern—with the span roughly 1550 to 1700 designated as the Scientific Revolution that serves as the defining rupture justifying the boundary into modernity. This division is not merely administrative convenience; it structures exam questions, course narratives, and the implicit story we tell students about where experimental method and mathematized nature emerged, treating that emergence as a discrete, largely Europe-centered event. At the same time, the faculty recently adopted a framework statement committing the department to decolonizing knowledge production, which implies treating scientific and philosophical developments in the Islamic world, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa as autonomous, co-constitutive traditions rather than as tributaries feeding a singular European breakthrough.

What philosophical commitments about historical ontology, scientific authority, and the reality of macro-periods does our four-block division presuppose? Does treating 1550–1700 as a singular Scientific Revolution require an essentialist or teleological view of science—one that holds scientific practice has a definable nature capable of being “born” at a specific time and place, and that prior or parallel traditions necessarily lack that essence? Or can the tripartite scheme be defended as a purely pragmatic pedagogical scaffold compatible with a pluralist, non-teleological historiography? Reconstruct the implicit theory of history embedded in this institutional practice, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates. Then assess whether that implicit theory is philosophically consistent with the decolonization mandate. If the two are in tension, specify precisely which institutional practices—faculty clustering by era, survey sequencing, exam design, or narrative framing—would have to be redesigned under a continuous, multi-centered diffusion model of scientific change, and which parts of the division could survive without metaphysical commitment. Finally, identify the weakest point in the rupture-model defense that a pluralist historian of science would press, and note what concession, if any, the defender of the current structure should make up front.
