I'm preparing remarks for a philosophy-of-agency reading group and want a consistency check before presenting. Here is where I have landed:

(1) The core wrong of manipulation is structural: it treats the target's rational capacities as obstacles to be circumvented rather than capacities to be engaged, regardless of whether the influence pathway is cognitive, affective, or associative.

(2) Persuasive framing—e.g., presenting clinical outcomes in terms of survival rates rather than mortality rates—is legitimate if it directs people toward choices they would reflectively endorse, even when the framing mechanism itself remains hidden from them at the time of choice.

(3) Transparency about persuasive intent is valuable but not constitutive of legitimacy in general; hidden influence is not necessarily manipulative provided it does not prevent the target from eventually recognizing and endorsing the influence upon reflection.

(4) Democratic deliberation is different: citizens should be treated as co-legislators, so hidden framing in collective decision-making violates political equality by giving framers an unaccountable structural advantage; here transparency is mandatory, not merely desirable.

(5) Nudging through default rules is permissible in administrative or private contexts because it preserves formal autonomy—the opt-out is available—and involves no deception, but it becomes ethically problematic when applied to collective political choices because it bypasses the deliberative process that democratic equality requires.

(6) There is no principled line between cognitive framing and emotional rhetoric—both operate partly through non-rational mechanisms—so if framing is legitimate under the conditions in (2), emotional appeals must also be legitimate under analogous conditions of reflective endorsability.

My worry is that these pull in directions I haven't fully reconciled. For instance, (2) licenses hidden influence that (1) might condemn as structurally circumventing rational capacities; (3) treats transparency as generally non-constitutive while (4) makes it mandatory in one domain without an explicit bridge principle; and (6) extends legitimacy to emotional rhetoric in ways that may outstrip the structural focus of (1). At the same time, some of these pairings may only look incompatible.

For each genuine tension, say which commitment(s) would have to be revised or qualified and what the cheapest fix costs—particularly whether resolving it requires weakening the structural account in (1), narrowing the reflective-endorsement condition in (2), or introducing an explicit domain distinction to save (3) and (4). Identify at least one pairing that appears tense but dissolves on inspection, and explain exactly why the conflict is only apparent.
