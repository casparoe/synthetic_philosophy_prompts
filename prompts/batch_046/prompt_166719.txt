hey, i'm working on my diss about pain and affect and i keep seeing this argumentative move that i don't have a word for. take pain asymbolia — patients who feel the sensory part but report zero suffering, sometimes even find it interesting. philosophers use these cases to push that pain isn't necessarily bad, since the badness is in the aversive quality not the sensation itself. but then defenders of the intrinsic-badness view reply that asymbolic pain is still bad, except now they ground it in representational disfunction or tissue-damage signaling. then, a few pages later, they're explaining ordinary pain's badness by pointing straight back to the felt awfulnes. so the normative ground shifts depending on whether they're blocking the counterexample or describing the standard case — affective quality in one spot, representational failure in the other. it's not quite motte-and-bailey, since both claims are serious, not a defensible core and an indefensible fringe. and it's not just equivocation on 'bad', because someone could consistently think both properties contribute. but there's this specific pattern of distributing the weight of badness across two different properties and leaning on whichever one is under pressure. does this have an established name in philosophy of medicine or value theory? if not, can you coin one with a sharp definition and boundary cases? i especially want to know how to tell if someone genuinely holds a pluralist view about pain's badness, versus just sliding between grounds as needed. please reply in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just like an email or letter. thanks!
