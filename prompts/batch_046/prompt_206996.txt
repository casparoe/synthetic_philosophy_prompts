I have recently begun working as a research assistant in an interdisciplinary climate-policy group, and I find myself repeatedly asked to defend macroeconomic forecasting models whose core assumptions—representative agents, perfect common knowledge, institutional stasis, and symmetric temporal reasoning—bear almost no resemblance to the social world they purport to describe. Our senior modelers justify these choices by invoking what they call the “F-twist”: the claim, associated with Milton Friedman, that unrealistic assumptions are methodologically innocent so long as the model delivers accurate predictions. For a philosophy-of-science workshop, I want a sustained satirical piece in the form of a parody peer-reviewed “Assumption Defense Appendix,” written in the deadpan, bureaucratic voice of an economics working-paper methods section. The appendix should defend a fictional forecasting model—call it Model Ω—that assumes all economic agents are spherical, experience time bidirectionally, and share a single unified belief state. Its author should invoke predictive accuracy as the sole and sufficient justification for each ontological absurdity, dismissing concerns about mechanism realism with the refrain that “the data do not care about your ontology.” The satire should exaggerate a real tendency in some modeling traditions: treating predictive success not merely as one virtue among others, but as an automatic license for any structural fiction, especially when the predictions are accurate only because the model is retrofitted to reproduce past observations rather than because it captures the right causal process. I want the piece to be legible to a non-specialist but rewarding to someone familiar with the philosophy of science literature on idealization and underdetermination; specifically, it should quietly introduce, then brusquely dismiss, the idea of “predictive equivalence classes”—the problem that structurally incompatible models can generate identical predictions, so that predictive success alone cannot uniquely vindicate any particular unrealistic core. Length: approximately 650 words, formatted with a brief “Executive Summary,” numbered “Clause Defenses,” and a “Response to Critics” subsection. The target is the rhetorical practice of converting predictive accuracy into unconditional ontological license, not any individual scholar. After the parody, please include a brief analytical note of about 150 words identifying which features of the satire exaggerate actual practices in model defense, where it takes liberties with Friedman’s more nuanced actual position (he treated predictive accuracy as one criterion among several, not as a universal solvent), and where the objection about predictive equivalence is genuinely contested versus broadly accepted among philosophers of economics. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any contested view—such as the claim that mechanism realism should always override predictive success—as if it were the consensus.
