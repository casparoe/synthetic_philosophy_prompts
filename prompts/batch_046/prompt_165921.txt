Here is the modern case. A state deploys an autonomous targeting system that selects and engages targets without a human in the loop at the moment of strike. The system’s architects intend only to minimize statistical collateral damage within programmed constraints; they do not direct any particular lethal error at any identifiable person. The operators activate it knowing that errors will occur at a predictable rate, but they do not intend those errors as ends or means—rather, the errors are structurally embedded in an optimization process that distributes lethal decisions across time, code, and human roles such that no single agent “directs” any given death. The killing is neither classically intended by an individual will nor a mere side-effect of an otherwise legitimate action performed by one agent; it is produced by a distributed architecture in which lethal outcomes are built into the system’s functioning.

My opening move: your framework was forged for cases like Truman, where one identifiable agent chooses to direct destruction at civilians. Here, no one directs the death, and everyone can sincerely claim they intended only statistical minimization. Doesn’t this either force you to call the entire system an intentional killing machine—which seems to misdescribe the agents’ actual mental states—or to classify the deaths as merely foreseen, which lets the architects and operators off the hook for what is in fact a designed, distributed killing process? How do you defend the distinction between directed and foreseen harm when the structure of agency itself has been fragmented?
