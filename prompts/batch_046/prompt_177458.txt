hey, been chewing on Good's classic result that for a rational bayesian agent free information can't reduce expected utility before a decision — people cite it like gospel but i think it hides more than it shows once you leave single-agent models.

take the challenger launch call on jan 28, 1986. common-knowledge version: thiokol engineers had O-ring erosion data from prior flights, especially in cold weather; they warned NASA not to launch below 53F; management overruled them and launched at 36F; the vehicle broke up 73 seconds after liftoff. analyse this through Good's theorem, but dont just say "they should have delayed for more tests." instead inject this twist: treat NASA not as one agent but as a distributed system with conflicting utility weights — schedule pressure vs crew safety — and ask whether the theorem applies at all when there's no unified decision-maker to recieve the information. does the case show the theorem is silent on institutional design, or does it actually diagnose the failure (maybe the "free info" condition failed because gathering more data had political costs, not informational ones)? map the players, options, and evidence precisely — who counts as the agent, what counts as "free" here, and where the theorem stops speaking rather than being forced.

also say what would change your mind: which interpretation of Good's conditions or which feature of the institutional dynamics would flip your verdict, and be honest about where the mapping is shakiest — like if the engineers' statistical reasoning was itself flawed, or if delaying actually wasn't free once you account for institutional utility.
