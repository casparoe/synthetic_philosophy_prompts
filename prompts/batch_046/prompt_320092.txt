I am drafting a position paper on consciousness and causal closure and want a rigorous consistency audit before presenting it. The view I defend comprises the following commitments: (a) phenomenal consciousness is strictly epiphenomenal—qualitative experiences have no causal efficacy over physical processes, including neural dynamics and behavioral output; (b) first-person phenomenal judgments (e.g., that one is experiencing pain or seeing red) are generally reliable and merit default epistemic trust; (c) the complete causal explanation of any phenomenal judgment is furnished by its neurophysiological correlates, with the qualitative character itself contributing nothing to the causal chain; (d) the mapping between particular neural configurations and particular qualia is contingent rather than metaphysically necessary—the same brain state could, in other worlds, be paired with a different quale or with none; (e) I reject interactionist dualism, panpsychism, and any account that grants phenomenal properties causal powers; (f) ontological parsimony requires that we not postulate causally inert entities unless they are genuinely indispensable for explaining some independently accepted phenomenon.

Please examine whether this set is jointly tenable. Which pairs are in genuine logical or motivational tension, which conflicts are merely apparent and dissolve upon closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision—whether it involves qualifying (b), abandoning (d), weakening (f), or surrendering (a)—to restore coherence? Rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each what precisely must be given up, and say whether the resulting position remains recognizably epiphenomenalist or collapses into an alternative framework.
