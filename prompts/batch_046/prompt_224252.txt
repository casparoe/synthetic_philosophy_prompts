Write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers—call them A and B—debating Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and the “one thought too many” problem, but frame the dispute around a specific conceptual architecture rather than the standard drowning-spouse scenario. A should defend a strict Williamsian position: that ground projects are not merely strong preferences but constitutive of practical identity, and that utilitarianism’s demand that an agent be prepared to weigh those projects against an impersonal calculus introduces alienation at the level of standing motivation, not just at the moment of crisis. B should defend “partitioned consequentialism,” a view that accepts agent-neutral justification at the meta-level but insists on “deliberative insulation” inside the project: the agent never calculates within the commitment to a partner, child, or vocation, yet accepts that the boundary conditions of such commitments are “normatively permeable” and could in principle be dissolved if the aggregate stakes become extreme enough.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth. A should press B on whether “normative permeability” is not simply the “one thought too many” relocated upward—specifically, whether knowing that one’s deepest commitment is conditional on an external calculus corrupts the internal structure of that commitment, even if the agent never performs the arithmetic in real time. B should respond by distinguishing “motivational corruption” from “regulatory oversight,” arguing that a compartmentalized moral psychology can preserve the phenomenology and causal efficacy of ground projects while still allowing consequentialist governance at the margins. A should then challenge whether that distinction is stable: either the meta-level justification does real normative work, in which case the agent is still alienated from her projects by being willing to abandon them for sufficient reason, or it does no work, in which case B has smuggled in a deontological sanctuary that collapses into Williams’s preferred view. Both characters should treat the other’s arguments with precision; neither should be a strawman.

Keep the exchange conceptual and focused on the logical structure of practical reason—particularly the relation between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons, and whether integrity is best understood as a property of deliberative procedure, motivational structure, or normative justification. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to specific texts by page or edition. The dialogue should be suitable for someone reasoning without access to external sources, but it should not be answerable by reciting standard textbook summaries of Williams.
