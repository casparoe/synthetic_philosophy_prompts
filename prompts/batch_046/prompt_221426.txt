Well, banking regulators do something structurally similar: they make institutions hold capital against 'market risk' using probabilistic models with known distributions, but they treat 'model risk' — where the underlying probabilities are contested or unknown — as a totally seperate category that demands conservative, non-probabilistic buffers, not just wider priors. I was discussing this with a colleague over coffee and she said I was overreading philosophy into bureaucracy.

So I'm asking you: what view of rationality or epistemology does that split actually presuppose? Does it tacitly commit regulators to the idea that ambiguity is normatively distinct from risk, maybe in a way that makes ambiguity aversion rational after all? Or could a Bayesian with imprecise credences license the exact same practice without buying into Ellsberg-style aversion? And if we replaced the bifurcated system with one unified probabilistic framework — treating model risk as just risk with really flat priors — what exactly in the practice would have to collapse, and what could survive? I'm genuinely curious whether there's a hidden epistemology here or if it's just institutional caution wearing philosophical clothing.
