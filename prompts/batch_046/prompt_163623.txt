As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I find myself preoccupied by a concrete case involving a former colleague. He is an elderly mathematician who, while fully competent years ago, signed a document instructing that if he ever developed severe dementia and no longer recognized his children, he should not be kept alive by artificial means. He now lives in precisely that state: he does not recognize his family, yet he appears genuinely cheerful, and he has formed a deep, affectionate attachment to one of his caregivers. His physicians have proposed an experimental intervention—an external cognitive prosthesis capable of selectively reactivating fragments of his earlier autobiographical memories and prior preference structures without altering his present emotional disposition or his bond with the caregiver. His children are divided. Some wish to activate the device in order to “consult” his earlier self, arguing that the document should be enforced only if that technologically restored profile still endorses the refusal. Others contend that summoning a fragmented ghost of the man he used to be would violate the dignity of the happy, present individual, and that the document was executed precisely to shield this later stage from being overridden by past wishes. I am unsure how to weigh these competing claims. Does the authority of his earlier instructions depend on some form of psychological continuity that the prosthesis might partially restore, or is biological and narrative continuity sufficient regardless? And should the evident contentment of his present self diminish the moral weight of that prior refusal? I am not seeking legal guidance or a list of readings, but rather a rigorous philosophical analysis of whether an earlier refusal retains binding force when pieces of the “earlier self” can be technologically resurrected, and of how the welfare of the present self should figure in that judgment. Please respond in American English, in a formal and polished style appropriate to philosophical discussion.
