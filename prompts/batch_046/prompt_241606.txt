Please grade the following exam answer. The course is an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral and political philosophy, with a unit on empirical claims in normative argument. The exam prompt was:

Some scholars, notably Steven Pinker, argue that human violence has declined over the long term, primarily on the basis of falling per-capita rates of violent death. Critics counter that absolute numbers of violent deaths have not shown a consistent downward trend—especially once global population growth is factored in—and that the answer depends on how "violence" is defined. In particular, if one adopts Johan Galtung's category of structural violence (systemic harm built into social institutions that prevents people from meeting basic needs), the empirical picture may change entirely. Address the following: (1) Is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or is the answer irreducibly conventional? (2) How should we weigh per-capita rates against absolute figures in assessing moral progress? (3) Does expanding the definition to include structural violence help, hinder, or simply change the subject of Pinker's empirical claim?

The student submitted the following answer:

There is a fact of the matter about whether violence is declining, but that fact is framework-dependent. Once we fix a definition—say, direct physical harm versus systemic institutional harm—we can measure change over time. Pinker's per-capita metric is the correct one for moral evaluation, because what matters ethically is the probability that any given person will suffer harm, not the aggregate number of victims. A world with one thousand violent deaths among ten billion people is morally preferable to a world with one hundred such deaths among one million people, even though the absolute figure is larger in the first case. Critics who invoke absolute numbers therefore confuse total harm with individual risk exposure.

If we incorporate structural violence, as Galtung defines it—preventable harm caused by social structures that deny basic needs—the empirical thesis does not merely become ambiguous; it is reversed. Modern economic and political institutions generate massive structural harm, measurable through indicators such as preventable mortality, resource inequality, and restricted access to basic goods. Because these indicators are available for both historical and contemporary societies, we can compare structural violence across eras directly. The data suggest that structural harm is now more widespread and intense than direct violence ever was in pre-modern societies.

That said, direct violence and structural violence are not fully commensurable. Direct violence requires intentional agency by identifiable perpetrators, whereas structural violence operates through diffuse, unintentional causation. Consequently, asking whether "the world" is becoming less violent collapses two distinct dimensions into one. There is no unified fact of the matter that encompasses both. Pinker's claim is therefore correct within its restricted domain but misleading if presented as a general conclusion about human welfare.
