I sit on our university's Senate Subcommittee on Digital Scholarship and Epistemic Authority, and we must reach a concrete decision by the end of next month on the proposed Interpretive Metadata Protocol for the AI-Assisted Philosophy Archive. The archive employs a large language model to generate first-order interpretive descriptions—mapping arguments, tracing conceptual lineages, and flagging internal inconsistencies—within an uncatalogued collection of philosophical manuscripts spanning roughly the 1950s through the 1980s. The protocol asks whether these annotations should be classified as expressions of original intentionality, thereby qualifying them for citation as primary curatorial scholarship, inclusion in tenure evaluation, and full institutional attribution, or whether they must be treated as artifacts of derived intentionality, requiring mandatory human co-signature, exclusion from primary scholarship metrics, and explicit labeling as algorithmic derivation.

I was discussing the matter with a colleague from cognitive science over coffee, and it struck me that our committee is operating with a binary—original versus derived—that may not survive close scrutiny once we apply the distinction to institutional practice rather than to individual minds. Some members invoke Dennett's two-bitser argument to suggest that functional complexity erodes any principled boundary between human and machine interpretation, while others invoke Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality to insist that syntactic processing, however sophisticated, never constitutes semantic understanding. Rather than asking you to adjudicate that metaphysical dispute in the abstract, I need a concrete institutional recommendation that respects both lines of thought without resolving them by fiat.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a specific course of action with a defined time frame—perhaps a phased pilot with review milestones at six and eighteen months—rather than deferring the decision to further study. The memo should treat the opposing position with intellectual honesty: specifically, the worry that defaulting all AI annotations to derived status creates a bureaucratic double standard that penalizes functional equivalence and suppresses a genuinely useful scholarly instrument. At the same time, avoid pretending that the system possesses beliefs in the same sense a human curator does; instead, propose an institutional mechanism—something like a distributed-attribution framework that tracks which interpretive moves originated in the model's pattern-matching and which in the human curator's judgment—so that the archive can proceed without collapsing the philosophical distinction into a mere administrative label. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation demands; the committee needs reasoning it can act on, not a literature survey. Keep the philosophical framing anchored to the Dennett-Searle axis, but apply it to the design of the protocol in a way that requires genuine conceptual construction rather than the repetition of familiar talking points.
