I cover philosophy of mind and emerging technology for a science magazine, and I was discussing this idea with colleagues over coffee last week—how most people have heard of the Chinese Room but have never actually felt the gap between syntax and semantics in their own actions. I am now preparing a programme for a small workshop of about twelve intellectually curious adults, none with formal philosophical training, to run for ninety minutes in a single session. Rather than walking them through the classic room scenario as a reheated textbook example, I want the group to inhabit a distributed version: each participant takes on a narrow syntactic role—token matching, routing, or pattern completion—within a pipeline that collectively produces fluent responses in a language none of them understands. The rules should be arranged so that participants first experience the collective system functioning smoothly, then hit a staged contradiction that shows correct outputs can still be produced when every local instruction is followed blindly, and finally face the question of whether understanding could be attributed to the network as a whole rather than to anyone inside it.

Please design the complete activity: specific roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a symbolic incentive scheme that rewards collective fluency but offers no reward for comprehension. The problem should emerge from the interaction and rules themselves, not from any explanation given beforehand. Include a debrief that maps each stage onto the relevant philosophical distinctions—syntax versus semantics, simulation versus duplication of mental content, and the issue of original versus derived intentionality—together with an honest assessment of which feature of the argument the exercise genuinely captures and which feature—perhaps the subjective absence of conscious meaning or the specific biological causation—must be supplied by the facilitator and cannot be reproduced in the room. Write the answer in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since participants will have no access to external sources.
