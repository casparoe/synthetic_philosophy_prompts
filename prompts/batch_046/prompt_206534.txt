I'm on the ethics review board at a research hospital and we keep getting protocols for sensory-restoration studies — like giving sight back to people with congenital blindness and then probing whether they learn "what it's like" to see red only after the surgery, or whether they could have known it from physics alone. The investigators always frame it as testing Jackson's Mary-room argument, but my job isn't to do philosophy — it's to decide, repeatedly and with real stakes, whether a given study actually isolates phenomenal knowledge from plain skill-learning, and whether the risk to the subject is justified.

Write me a reusable decision procedure my board can run in about 20 minutes per proposal. It should turn the knowledge argument into ordered steps: first check if the study design separates first-order phenomenal disclosure from second-order language acquisition or inference; then check for what I'll call "integration lag" — whether there's a measurable delay between the subject receiving the stimulus and being able to conceptually map it, which would indicate genuine novel knowledge rather than just new data; then determine where the physicalist account actually runs out versus where it's just incomplete; and finally flag the exact point where the question stops being empirical and becomes a value call about risk and consent.

For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where we stop with a hard no, and where we have to hand off to human judgment because the procedure can't settle it. Then apply it to three proposals: (A) restoring sight to an adult with congenital blindness with no prior color vocabulary; (B) testing a subject who already knows exhaustive color physics and can mentally simulate spectra — to see if they still report a "Mary moment"; and (C) one case where teh procedure gives an answer I won't like — maybe it approves a risky study but the knowledge argument clearly doesn't apply, or rejects a safe one where it should apply.

The procedure should be honest about false positives, especially with tiny n sizes, and shouldn't ask for bibliographies or citations — we're running this live with no web access. Write the answer in american english, keep it practical, and don't reduce Mary's room to just an illustration.
