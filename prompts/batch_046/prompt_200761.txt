In my graduate seminars on social ontology I keep returning to a deadlock I have never been able to classify with confidence. The dispute concerns whether conventions—language, queuing, certain moral prohibitions—are best explained by a broadly Lewisian rationalist framework, in which convention requires common knowledge of rational preference for coordination together with the belief that others will conform, or by evolutionary and game-theoretic accounts that show norms emerging and stabilizing through selection dynamics, learning, and repeated interaction without anyone performing explicit practical reasoning. For decades the literature has refined both sides without convergence: rationalists concede that explicit reasoning is rare but maintain that normative "ought" requires that agents could, in principle, recognize the rational basis for coordination; evolutionary critics reply that this is post-hoc rationalization and that payoff structures and transmission mechanisms do the real explanatory work. Recent technical advances—more sophisticated evolutionary models on one side, more nuanced epistemic conditions on the other—have not broken the impasse so much as deepened it.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical or formal but concerns what kind of explanation a philosophical account of convention is supposed to deliver. The evolutionary camp appears to seek a genetic or causal story: how norms arise and persist. The Lewisian camp appears to seek a normative-reconstructive story: what makes conformity rational even after the original coordination problem has dissolved, a problem I call "normative residue"—the binding force that remains when the payoff structure that generated a convention has shifted or disappeared. If I am right, the sides are answering different questions—genesis versus justification—under the single heading "What is a convention?" Yet I wonder whether the stall is not merely parallel projects but a substantive disagreement about whether rational recognition is a necessary condition for genuine normativity, with the evolutionary side implicitly collapsing normative force into causal stability. I also wonder whether the very concept of convention is being stretched across domains—language, morality, traffic—where the explanatory demands genuinely diverge, so that what looks like one debate is actually several running in parallel, including what we might call "meta-coordination" about the norms that govern how norms themselves change.

Please diagnose this for me, not survey the positions. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or structurally parallel? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps about whether evolutionary stability can generate common knowledge under specific conditions—that could resolve it, or should the impasse be expected to persist? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no resolution is possible? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning without preamble and without restating the question. Write in American English.
