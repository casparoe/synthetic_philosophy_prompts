Imagine that John Maynard Keynes, having survived into the present and studied the economic and technological developments of the last eight decades, were asked to comment on the following scenario. Advances in generative artificial intelligence and algorithmic labor-market matching have made it technically feasible for advanced economies to maintain current material output with a fraction of present working hours—effectively realizing the conditions for the drastically reduced workweek he once anticipated. However, these same technologies have given rise to sophisticated personalization architectures that do not merely satisfy pre-existing preferences but actively manufacture new relative needs, transforming leisure into a site of continuous, data-driven persuasion and status competition mediated by recommendation systems. Drawing on his well-known distinction between absolute needs and relative needs, and on his broader vision of a post-scarcity good life freed from the compulsions of economic necessity, how would Keynes likely assess this situation? Specifically, would he regard the technological solving of material scarcity as sufficient for human flourishing, or would he conclude that the colonization of attention and leisure by algorithmically generated relative needs represents a novel, perhaps more intractable, obstacle to the good life—one that requires not merely economic but institutional and ethical redesign?
