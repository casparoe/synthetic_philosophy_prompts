I've been working through the literature on peer disagreement for a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and after discussing the equal-weight view with colleagues over coffee I think I've hit a structural tension that the standard formulations don't address—though I'm unsure whether I'm missing an obvious reply. To fix ideas, consider a concrete case: two philosophers who are genuine epistemic peers with respect to a live controversy in philosophy of mind, specifically whether phenomenal concepts are non-physical in a way that blocks any reductive explanation. They share all the same first-order arguments, have comparable track records in the subfield, and each recognizes the other as equally competent. One assigns a credence of roughly 0.75 to the claim that phenomenal concepts do block reduction; the other assigns 0.75 to its negation.

The conciliationist prescription, as I understand it, seems to require that each revise downward—perhaps meeting near 0.5 suspension—because the bare fact of peer disagreement is itself a kind of higher-order defeater. But this is precisely where my confusion lies. If the shared first-order evidence genuinely supports high confidence in opposite directions, then jointly revising to neutrality doesn't restore rational coherence; it substitutes a single doxastic attitude—agnosticism—that neither peer originally held and that isn't actually warranted by any of the shared first-order considerations. It treats disagreement less like an epistemic signal and more like a procedural bargaining outcome, as if belief revision were governed by doxastic arbitration rather than by evidence tracking. Meanwhile, steadfastness at least preserves the connection between a peer's prior evaluation of the arguments and her resulting confidence. So my question is this: doesn't conciliationism implicitly require us to treat peer disagreement as a meta-evidential veto that overrides first-order reasoning, thereby violating the evidentialist constraint that belief should track the evidence? And if so, isn't steadfastness actually the more rationally responsible stance, provided each peer continues to weigh the first-order arguments honestly?
