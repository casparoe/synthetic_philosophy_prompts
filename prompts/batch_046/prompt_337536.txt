I'm a novelist constructing a philosophical subplot in which a clinical therapist, trained in the modern Stoic revival, finds her disciplined equanimity cracking when her sister receives a terminal diagnosis. To sharpen the tension for both my character and the reader, I have drafted the following argument, which seems to show that the contemporary revival is either ethically hollow or internally contradictory. I want to know precisely where it slips, because I intend to have her discover the flaw herself rather than have it handed to her.

(1) The modern Stoic revival—particularly in its therapeutic applications—rests on the dichotomy of control: we should direct our concern and emotional investment solely toward what lies within our power (our judgments, intentions, and acts of assent), and treat everything else—health, reputation, the actions of others, and the course of disease—with disciplined equanimity.

(2) Ancient Stoicism, however, embedded this dichotomy within a broader theory of oikeiosis, the natural process by which rational beings extend their sphere of care so that another person's welfare becomes, in a qualified but genuine sense, constitutive of one's own rational nature.

(3) If oikeiosis is authentic, then my grief and concern for my sister's suffering are not irrational attachments to an external outcome, but expressions of my rational self operating correctly—no more alien to me than concern for the coherence of my own judgments.

(4) The modern revival, by retaining the sharp dichotomy while discarding or marginalizing oikeiosis, transforms Stoicism into a program of isolated emotional management, severing the relational roots that once gave the doctrine ethical weight.

(5) But if my rational nature genuinely extends to include her welfare, then suppressing that concern in the name of the dichotomy is not wisdom but a form of self-mutilation: it severs a part of the self that Stoicism itself obligates me to cultivate and preserve.

(6) Therefore the revival faces a dilemma: either it denies oikeiosis and thereby abandons the ethical richness that distinguishes Stoicism from mere self-help, or it accepts oikeiosis and thereby admits that sustained emotional concern for externals can be fully rational, which dissolves the sharp boundary between what is and is not "up to us."

(7) By extension, any practice—clinical, artistic, or civic—that requires genuine emotional investment in outcomes one does not fully control becomes, under the pure dichotomy, not merely difficult but irrational, rendering the revival incompatible with responsible human engagement.

My instinct is that the argument overreaches somewhere between (3) and (5), perhaps by treating a relational extension of concern as if it were identical to direct control, or by sliding from "part of my rational nature" to "within my power." Yet I am unsure whether the real damage lies in (2), in the interpretation of oikeiosis; in (4), in the claim that modern practice has genuinely abandoned it; or in (6), where the dilemma is drawn. Which step should I reject if I want to preserve both the therapeutic value of the dichotomy and the possibility that my character can love her sister without philosophical hypocrisy? What does giving up that step cost—does it weaken the revival's claim to ancient authority, or does it force us to redraw the boundary of "what depends on us" in a way that makes the dichotomy useless? And please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis of the flaw, or where is your reasoning here most likely to fail?
