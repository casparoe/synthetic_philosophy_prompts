As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy systematically, I have been attending a small interdisciplinary reading group for scientists transitioning into humanistic inquiry. At our last meeting, we discussed Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with particular attention to his distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of relating to the past. The discussion turned to whether these categories can illuminate modern scientific practice, and two members became locked in a sustained disagreement that the rest of us could not resolve. I report it here as a bystander, without taking sides.

The interlocutors are Dr. Lian Chen, a historian of biology, and Dr. Marcus Webb, a philosopher of science. Both advanced arguments that seemed genuinely strong, and neither was obviously misrepresenting the other.

First round. Chen opened by arguing that contemporary science has fallen into a harmful antiquarian mode. She pointed to institutional mandates—for example, funding-body requirements that all raw data from failed experiments be archived in perpetuity with full metadata parity—as evidence of a reverence for the past that paralyzes present action. In her view, every negative result is treated as equally worthy of preservation, creating a burden that forces current researchers to navigate an ever-expanding graveyard of obsolete attempts. She claimed that what modern science needs is not more storage but a genuinely critical history: a selective judgment about which past efforts still serve present scientific life, with the rest being symbolically set aside.

Webb replied that Chen misapplies the category. For Nietzsche, the antiquarian mode is an emotional attachment to one's own inherited past—the preservation of local customs, family records, or cultural identity. Scientific datasets, he insisted, are not our past in that lived, identity-constituting sense; they are external instruments. Furthermore, he argued that Chen neglects the monumental mode: exemplary scientific achievements only function as inspiring models if we retain the full comparative record against which they stand out. To delete failed attempts is not to practice critical history but to flatten the landscape so that present successes appear inevitable rather than monumental.

Second round. Chen conceded that monumental exemplars need context, but she insisted that Webb was being overly literal about life. The relevant life, she said, is the life of a scientific community, and its present vitality is indeed stifled by the obligation to review, cite, and maintain every prior attempt. She introduced a specific proposal, which she called the threshold principle: only data that could plausibly inform an active research program should remain in open archives, while everything else should be transferred to a restricted critical vault that marks it as surpassed. This, she argued, is precisely what Nietzsche meant by using history for life—not indiscriminate destruction, but a value-laden judgment about what serves the present.

Webb countered that Chen was smuggling a normative policy preference into a philosophical framework that does not support it. He noted that Nietzsche's critical history is dangerous precisely because it judges the past by present standards, and in science such presentism is epistemically risky. He offered a historical example—discarded nineteenth-century measurements of the luminiferous aether, once dismissed as failures, later became essential for understanding the contingency of theoretical commitment—to show that selective deletion risks destroying materials whose value is not yet visible. He also challenged Chen's claim that preservation stifles action: storage is cheap, access is voluntary, and no researcher is compelled to read obsolete datasets. Finally, he argued that applying the tripartite scheme to inanimate data is a category error, since scientific life lacks the unified organic will that Nietzsche presupposes.

Third round. Chen responded that Webb's example actually proves her point: the aether data were valuable only because they were preserved in a curated, accessible form, not because every failed experiment of the era was retained with equal status. She maintained that her threshold principle distinguishes between thoughtful curation and indiscriminate hoarding. She asked Webb whether he could offer any principled reason, within Nietzsche's framework, to prefer total preservation over selective retention, other than a vague fear of loss.

Webb answered that there is indeed a principled reason, but it lies outside Nietzsche's scheme: the future of science is unpredictable, and any present judgment about what serves life is necessarily provisional. Therefore, the only defensible default is preservation, with critical judgment applied to interpretation rather than to retention. He concluded that Chen wants to use Nietzsche to justify reducing cognitive or institutional load, but the philosophical framework actually undermines her position, because critical history requires a clear sense of what life demands—something a pluralistic scientific community, lacking a single will, cannot provide.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the three rounds, say who is right about what, and where each speaker is making a genuinely decent point versus where they are overreaching. In particular, address whether Chen's threshold principle and critical vault are coherent applications of Nietzsche's critical mode, or whether Webb is correct that they represent a category error or hidden presentism. Also say what, if anything, both parties are missing—for instance, whether there is a way to reconcile the monumental need for context with the critical need for judgment without falling into either indiscriminate preservation or risky deletion. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I am interested in the philosophical structure of the disagreement itself.
