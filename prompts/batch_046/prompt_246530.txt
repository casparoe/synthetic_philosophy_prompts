As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind systematically, I am drafting an essay that compares illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—particularly the versions advanced by philosophers such as Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett—to historical cases of scientific elimination, specifically the theories of phlogiston and caloric. My central claim is that phenomenal properties function in introspective reports analogously to how phlogiston functioned in pre-Lavoisier chemistry: both are theoretical posits introduced to explain an observational regularity (the apparent qualitative character of experience, the weight gain of burned metals) that is actually better explained by the underlying mechanism without that posit (representational content and neural dynamics, oxidation and gas exchange). I further argue that illusionism is therefore not eliminativism about experience itself, but rather an error theory about a specific class of properties attributed to experience.

In the essay I develop a distinction between two ways a scientific error can be corrected. Replacement occurs when the old ontology is mapped onto the new one, as caloric was replaced by kinetic energy. Dissolution occurs when the old ontology has no structural counterpart because it was never tracking a real feature, which is what happened to phlogiston. I suggest that phenomenal consciousness, on the illusionist view, is dissolved rather than replaced. I then examine an objection I take to be serious: in physical science, an erroneous theoretical posit eventually conflicts with quantitative measurement in a publicly accessible domain, which allows intersubjective correction. In the case of consciousness, the relevant evidence is first-person report, and there is no independent instrument that can contradict a subject's claim to feel qualitative redness. I ask whether this structural insulation makes illusionism unfalsifiable in principle, or whether it merely relocates the falsifiable predictions to third-person cognitive science—predictions about which representational states will be misreported as qualitative.

I would welcome comments on the accuracy of the philosophical framing—especially whether I am misrepresenting illusionism as an error theory rather than as a positive account of representational transparency—and on whether the analogy to phlogiston genuinely illuminates the debate or collapses important differences between physical measurement and introspective access. I also wonder whether the distinction I draw between replacement and dissolution holds up under scrutiny, and whether there are stronger objections I have overlooked. Please reply in American English, and I ask that you not provide a bibliography or list of further readings, since I wish to evaluate the argument on its conceptual merits alone.
