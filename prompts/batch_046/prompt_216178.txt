You are a professional philosopher specializing in ethics, political philosophy, and the philosophy of cultural heritage. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the key conceptual distinctions at stake—such as collective identity over time, the difference between state and community custodianship, and the moral asymmetry between conquest and colonial extraction—so that your adjudication is precise and not superficial.

Two colleagues of mine, a historian of West Africa and a moral philosopher, got into a sustained argument about the philosophical framework for museum restitution, specifically regarding objects like the Benin Bronzes. I was present and report their exchange neutrally; I do not take sides. Please adjudicate it move by move.

(1) The historian said restitution should be guided by a continuity-of-custodianship principle: the rightful recipient is the entity that can demonstrate an unbroken chain of cultural practice and authority linking it directly to the creators. Applied to the Benin Bronzes, she argued this points to the Oba’s palace and associated ritual authorities in Benin City, not automatically to the modern Nigerian state museum system. She claimed this avoids reducing cultural heritage to state property.

(2) The philosopher replied that every custodial chain is broken or transformed over centuries; applying continuity selectively to colonial looting but not to pre-colonial conquest or internal migration creates an arbitrary moral asymmetry. He added that if we truly honored continuity, we might have to return artifacts that the Benin Empire itself seized from neighboring groups—an implication he took to be a reductio ad absurdum.

(3) The historian countered that the philosopher was conflating an organically transformed chain with a forcibly disrupted one. She proposed the relevant distinction is not continuity per se, but whether the break was caused by illegitimate external domination rather than internal cultural evolution. Thus violent extraction creates a specific moral deficit that organic change does not.

(4) The philosopher responded that “illegitimate domination” presupposes a theory of legitimate political and cultural authority that we do not possess in a pluralistic, post-colonial context. He further argued that returning objects to the modern Nigerian state—which was itself assembled through colonial administrative boundaries and post-colonial nation-building—merely reproduces the colonial logic of treating culture as state property. If the original wrong was domination, he asked, why repair it by transferring objects into another potentially dominating institutional structure?

Please adjudicate each of the four moves: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say, specifically, who is right about which point, what both are missing—particularly regarding whether the framework can distinguish community-level restitution from state-level restitution without smuggling in contested theories of sovereignty—and whether there is a consistent middle position that preserves the historian’s intuition about illegitimate disruption without collapsing into the philosopher’s skeptical challenge about authority.
