I'm trying to nail down a view I've been chewing on since a conversation with a friend who studies enviromental history. We were talking about whether things like massive volcanic eruptions, long climate shifts, or pandemics can genuinely be called "historical actors" — you know, the Black Death tearing through labor markets, Tambora triggering the Year Without a Summer, maybe even El Niño patterns messing with harvests and then with state stability. The usual objection is that treating them as actors is just enviromental determinism in a costume, stripping away human agency and turning people into passive victims of weather. But I'm not convinced the only options are "full-blown actor with intentions" versus "passive background context." I think there's a structual middle ground.

What if we distinguish "routing events" from both actors and conditions? A routing event isn't an agent — it doesn't intend, plan, or respond — but it also isn't mere backdrop. It works by suddenly constricting or reopening the paths that human institutions can take, forcing societies to re-route through different political, economic, or technological options without selecting which one they pick. The agency stays with the humans; the event just rewrites the map of what's feasible. So Tambora doesn't decide that European states will centralize grain storage, but it makes some previous agricultural strategies literally impossible, which means the range of human choices shifts dramatically. Could you argue that this framing — treating major germs, climate anomalies, and volcanic disruptions as routing events with this kind of non-intentional, structual agency — is actually the right way to include them in historical explanation? I want a defense that shows it avoids determinism, since societies still choose the reroute, but also explains why calling them mere "context" misses something real about how they reshape decision-spaces. Use concrete cases if it helps, and try not to lean on standard citation lists — I'm after the conceptual architecture, not a literature review.
