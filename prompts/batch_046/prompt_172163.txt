I've been thinking about the Buddhist teaching of anatta—no permanent self—and how it relates to the ordinary feeling that I am the same person who made a promise yesterday and will face the consequences tomorrow. Most accounts I've encountered either say Buddhism wipes out personal identity entirely or try to save it by turning the self into a story or a continuous stream of consciousness. I think both miss something, and I want you to argue for the view I keep coming back to, or tell me plainly if I'm confused.

The view is this. Personal identity is not an inner owner of experiences, and it is not merely a narrative we construct. It is what I call accountability threading: the way moral and practical obligations can be traced through a chain of mental states, actions, and causal relations even when there is no constant substrate carrying them. If anatta is correct, there is no soul linking my past and future. But there can still be a thread—someone made a commitment, someone bears the results of past choices, and others hold that particular sequence responsible—not because an essential "me" endured, but because the causal and moral pattern connects the states in the right way.

Please argue that this accountability-threading view is not only compatible with anatta but is actually the best way to preserve what is practically important about personal identity—blame, promise-keeping, long-term planning, and care—without smuggling in a hidden self. Defend it specifically against the objection that, without a persisting subject, holding someone responsible is just punishing one mental bundle for another's actions. Also address whether the view can make sense within Buddhist frameworks that talk about rebirth: if there is no self to be reborn, can accountability threading span lives, or does it necessarily break?

That said, I want you to push back if my request rests on a confused premise. If accountability threading is just a disguised name for the self, or if Buddhist ethics genuinely does not need personal identity at all and I'm solving a problem that doesn't exist, say so directly rather than politely constructing an argument on sand. How committed should you be? Give an honest, self-critical assessment: if the argument succeeds, say exactly where and why; if parts remain shaky or depend on unstated assumptions, name them. Do not pretend to certainty where there is none.
