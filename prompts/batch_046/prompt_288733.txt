Design a 75-minute in-class simulation for an interdisciplinary graduate workshop of about 18 students that makes them feel, rather than hear about, the fragility of direct reciprocity and the stabilizing role of partner choice in iterated cooperation. The setting should combine a one-shot baseline round with a 10-round iterated phase using a prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix (T > R > P > S, with points that convert to small privileges rather than cash). In each iterated round, participants are randomly paired, choose cooperate or defect, and then observe a noisy reputation signal of their partner's prior three moves—accurate with 70% probability, flipped otherwise. After observing the signal, each player may stay with the current partner or pay a small exit cost to be rematched randomly. The rules and materials should be arranged so that participants first experience the collapse of pure tit-for-tat under noise, then discover that voluntary exit allows cooperative clusters to survive without requiring altruism. Include the exact payoff numbers, the rematching protocol, materials needed, and how to run the one-shot baseline for contrast. The debrief should map specific moments—first defection under noise, an exit decision, a stable cooperative pair—onto direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, and network reciprocity, and should state frankly which evolutionary mechanism (for example, spatial structure or kin selection) the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion. Also predict what will go wrong on the first run—likely confusion about whether to trust the noisy signal or premature universal defection after a single betrayal—and suggest how to correct it. Write from the perspective of a PhD student in philosophy of science working on evolutionary dynamics, keep the prompt formal and concise, and do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
