There's this debate in transitional justice that feels totally stuck and I can't tell if it's philosophical or just people yelling past each other. Ever since the South Africa TRC and Argentina's CONADEP and Rwanda's gacaca courts, people have argued whether giving amnesty to get testimony is a necessary evil or whether it corrupts justice entirely. The one side says without amnesty you get silence, and silence is worse than imperfect justice. The other side says once you put a price on testimony—what I guess you could call a testimony market, where each unit of truth costs one waived punishment—you destroy the moral weight of the testimony and violate victims' rights. But here's the weird part: everyone more or less agrees on the historical facts from those three cases, everyone agrees victims suffered massively, and yet in thirty years noone's moved anyone. So is this actually a hidden disagreement about whether justice is retributive vs restorative? Or are they answering different questions—one about institutional design for historians, one about moral desert for victims? Could there be a hidden empirical claim about whether amnesty actually produces more truthful testimony in practice, or is it just bedrock intuition about wrongdoers suffering? And if it's bedrock, is there anything—like comparing the archival completeness across South Africa, Argentina and Rwanda, or drawing a distinction between 'truth-for-victims' and 'truth-for-history'—that could actually unstick the debate? Or should we just accept it won't move?
