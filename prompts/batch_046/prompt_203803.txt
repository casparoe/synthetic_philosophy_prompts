At a recent colloquium in my department, an intellectual historian and a philosopher of social practice became entangled in a disagreement that I found genuinely difficult to referee. Their dispute concerned a concrete case: the descendants of a nineteenth-century labor settlement maintain a collective memory that their community was destroyed by a deliberate arson orchestrated by factory owners in 1892, and that it was rebuilt through internal mutual aid; the municipal archives, insurance ledgers, and meteorological records, however, indicate an accidental lightning strike and document that reconstruction was funded primarily by external charitable bodies. I reconstruct their exchange below, as faithfully as I can, and ask for your adjudication.

The historian, Dr. Vasquez, opened by insisting that when collective memory conflicts with well-documented archival records, the record must correct the memory. She granted that archives are not transparent windows onto the past and that they were produced by actors with interests in minimizing labor conflict, but she argued that collective memory is vulnerable to generational drift, heroic embellishment, and narrative smoothing. She maintained that we retain critical leverage against myth only if evidentiary standards—physical burn patterns, funding ledgers, correspondence—can override communal narratives, even if that requires reading the archive against the grain.

Dr. Chen, the philosopher, replied that Vasquez was presuming the wrong normative relation. He proposed what he called mnemonic sovereignty: a community's authority to maintain the narrative structures that sustain its identity, provided those structures do not cause direct harm to outsiders. The mutual-aid narrative, he argued, functions as a living moral exemplar that strengthens present cooperative institutions; its causal inaccuracy does not diminish its social value. He added that if we allow collective memory to be overridden whenever the archive was produced by powerful actors, we risk treating history as an imperial discipline that disqualifies subaltern self-understanding by definition.

Vasquez countered that Chen's position collapses into a form of relativism. Without some evidentiary asymmetry—some recognition that collective memory lacks the self-correcting mechanisms of archival method—there is no principled way to distinguish a legitimate mnemonic sovereignty from mere false consciousness. She insisted that the proper response is not to replace history with memory, but to integrate critical archival findings with a hermeneutically sensitive account of why the community remembers as it does.

Chen then answered that Vasquez was still treating historical accuracy as a trump value. He argued that values such as continuity, self-respect, and intergenerational solidarity are not subordinate to epistemic correction; rather, they constitute independent goods that can legitimately outweigh accuracy in certain contexts. To demand that the community give way is, on his view, to mistake one form of knowledge—documentary history—for the only legitimate form of communal self-knowledge.

Please adjudicate their exchange round by round: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then indicate who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a defensible middle ground between treating the archive as sovereign and granting communities full mnemonic autonomy, or whether one of these positions genuinely collapses into the other. Please avoid bibliographic references and do not provide a reading list.
