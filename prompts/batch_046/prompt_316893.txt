For an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar of 14 graduate students, meeting for 90 minutes, design a structured simulation that makes participants feel the force of the idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity rather than a substantial inner director. The activity must not open with a lecture or with any named theory; instead, hidden incentives and procedural rules should make the absence of a Cartesian-style inner author emerge from what the group actually does.

Structure the session in three 20-minute rounds followed by a 30-minute debrief. In Round 1, assign each student a hidden gravity card that assigns an invisible narrative weight (high, medium, or low) and a private constraint on contributions to a collective biography of a fictional figure, Subject K. Constraints should be specific and conflicting—for example, one student must preserve ambiguity about Subject K’s childhood, another must insert a contradiction about values every third contribution, and a third must ensure that no emotional state is attributed without an external behavioral cue. The group builds a single shared document in real time, with no designated author or editor, and must achieve surface coherence without ever revealing their cards or constraints.

In Round 2, the facilitator introduces three interruption cards—a legal record, a clinical assessment, and a stranger’s testimony—that directly conflict with parts of the biography constructed in Round 1. The group must revise the document to accommodate all three, but the rules prohibit appealing to Subject K’s true intentions, hidden mental states, or an underlying real self; every revision must be justified solely by patterns already present in the existing text.

In Round 3, randomly redistribute the gravity cards and introduce a fragmentation token. Any student may play the token to split Subject K into two simultaneous, unreconciled narrative threads that must both be maintained for the remainder of the exercise with no meta-narrator permitted. The group votes on whether to deploy the token; if adopted, both threads continue without resolution until the round ends.

The debrief must map each round onto a feature of narrative gravity: Round 1 demonstrates distributed authorship in the absence of an inner director; Round 2 shows revision as a re-centering of pulls rather than a discovery of pre-existing fact; Round 3 reveals that the center can dissolve or multiply when the equilibrium of narrative pulls breaks. The debrief should predict specific failures—participants will almost certainly invent a phantom hidden author to resolve contradictions—and instruct the facilitator to block this by enforcing a citation-to-text-only rule for all revisions. It must also be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the long temporal continuity of a lived human life, the first-person phenomenology of being that center, and the normative accountability that binds a real person across decades. The facilitator must supply these missing dimensions explicitly.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Avoid verbatim quotations from philosophical texts unless you have fetched and verified the exact wording from a source; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. The prompt should require no web access to answer and should not depend on obscure theories or specific works that a model without search access could not reasonably reconstruct.
