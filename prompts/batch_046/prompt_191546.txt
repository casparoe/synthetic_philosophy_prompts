I serve on my university’s Committee on Professional Conduct and Pedagogical Ethics. We must issue a formal written opinion before Thursday’s vote in the case of Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of language, who was recorded during an open graduate seminar saying: “If you violate the maxim of Quantity repeatedly, you’re not being cooperative — you’re being deceptive.” A student advocacy group alleges that Dr. Voss thereby implicated that whistleblowers and institutional dissenters are deceptive, and demands a formal reprimand for creating a hostile classroom climate. Dr. Voss responds that her utterance was a technical illustration of Grice’s cooperative principle and conversational implicature, made in direct reply to a specific student question about informational norms; she insists she said nothing about whistleblowers, and that the alleged link is an unauthorized particularized implicature imported by listeners rather than part of her speaker meaning. She further notes that the remark is cancellable — she can consistently add, “Of course, this has nothing to do with legitimate dissent.”

The committee is split. Some members argue that in pedagogical settings speaker meaning must be fixed by what a reasonable student listener would take the speaker to have meant, and that technical intent cannot shield an expert from institutional accountability. Others argue that Grice’s distinction between what is said and what is implicated protects Dr. Voss, since her utterance was context-bound, technically precise, and cancellable. A further, more niche concern has been raised: whether Grice’s cooperative principle, designed for symmetrical conversational contexts, applies at all when a speaker holds institutional authority over listeners, and whether we need a modified framework — call it “asymmetrical implicature” — in which the maxims of Quantity and Manner are weighted differently because the audience cannot equally hold the speaker to account.

Please draft the committee’s opinion for circulation. Recommend a concrete course of action — dismiss the complaint, issue a private letter of clarification, or recommend formal reprimand — with an unambiguous bottom line. The reasoning should apply Grice’s concepts (speaker meaning, what is said versus what is implicated, cancellability, the cooperative principle) directly to the case, and should treat the strongest argument on the losing side with real weight: specifically, the concern that allowing philosophers to invoke technical frameworks to deflect institutional complaints risks letting expertise become a shield for harmful speech, and that classroom power asymmetry undermines the mutual cooperation Grice assumes.

If this request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if Grice’s framework is genuinely inapplicable to disciplinary proceedings, or if the notion of “asymmetrical implicature” is incoherent — please say so plainly rather than forcing an analysis.
