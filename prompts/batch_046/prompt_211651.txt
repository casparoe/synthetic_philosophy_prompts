I have been thinking about whether the possibility that we live inside a computer simulation should weaken an extreme form of commitment to the far future — the view that one should accept serious risks or sacrifices today for outcomes that might not occur for billions of years. Some people suggest that if ancestor simulations are common and are usually shut down once they reach a certain level of technology, then far-future outcomes in our world might literally never unfold, which should change how we weigh them. I want a case that isolates whether this reasoning genuinely dampens that extreme commitment, or whether it just adds ordinary uncertainty.

Please construct a thought experiment, no longer than one paragraph, set in a realistic context — for example, a research foundation or public agency choosing between two funding programs. In the scenario, Program A delivers a modest, near-certain improvement in human welfare over the next two centuries. Program B has a very small chance of success, but if it succeeds it produces benefits on an astronomical scale that persist for trillions of years. Without any simulation premise, the extreme longtermist calculus clearly favors B despite the risk. Now inject the simulation argument precisely: the decision-makers have a non-trivial, rationally grounded credence that they might be in an ancestor simulation that will be terminated long before those trillions of years pass, and that in such simulations the success of B is either undefined or never realized. The dampening of fanaticism must fall out of how they update their expectations, not from an obvious mistake or from stipulating that simulations do not count.

After describing the scenario, show what the extreme longtermist says about B both without and with the simulation premise, and identify whether the shift in verdict comes specifically from the truncation asymmetry between simulated and base-reality worlds, or whether it collapses into general skepticism about long-term predictions. State the decision rule or belief being tested, the relevant nearby worlds (base reality versus truncated simulation), and why each framework gives the verdict it does. Then explain what feature of the case is actually doing the work — for instance, the fact that simulation termination is targeted at technological maturity rather than being random — and what happens if you remove that feature but keep ordinary epistemic uncertainty about the future. Finally, include an honest note on how a defender of far-future fanaticism would attack the case: for example, by arguing that value should not depend on observational persistence, or that the credence in being simulated should apply symmetrically to near-future outcomes as well.

Please keep the scenario self-contained, avoid asking for citations or named texts, and do not rely on exotic physics, omniscient predictors, or demons. I am not looking for a bibliography; I just want the case built, the reasoning shown, and the weak point exposed.
