Here are my considered verdicts on hard choices among incommensurable values, together with a tentative principle I am trying to defend. I want to know whether any coherent account can preserve all of them, or whether reflective equilibrium requires me to abandon one or more.

The cases. (1) Life versus art, singular: in a fire, a museum director should save one unknown visitor rather than one irreplaceable masterpiece. Human survival dominates aesthetic value; this is not a genuinely hard choice but a clear ordering. (2) Life versus art, plural: the director can save either that same masterpiece or ten unknown visitors. I still think she should save the ten, yet the destruction of the painting registers as an uncompensated tragedy rather than a mere opportunity cost—the values remain incommensurable, but scale tips the balance without dissolving the loss. (3) Public health versus culture, institutional: a funding body can either eliminate a fatal disease for 1,000 people or fund community music education reaching 100,000 with no direct health benefit. I judge that neither option is rationally superior; both are permissible, but selecting at random is not, because the choice must be responsive to reasons even when those reasons do not determine a unique answer. (4) Career, personal: an individual with comparable talents in pure mathematics and hospice care chooses mathematics because the work is more fully expressive of her identity. I regard this as rational and not merely selfish, despite the greater direct suffering she could alleviate as a caregiver. (5) Career, institutional: the same person, now chairing a national grant board, must allocate the final award. I think she may not invoke "expressive fit" to favor mathematics over hospice expansion; in institutional allocation, authenticity-based reasons seem to lose their justificatory weight, even though the goods remain incommensurable. (6) Sacred preservation versus housing, collective: a community votes to preserve an ancient grove of irreplaceable spiritual significance rather than clear it for 500 units of affordable housing. I find this defensible only if the community internalizes the cost; if they demand public subsidy while the housing need remains unmet, the appeal to incommensurability becomes an unjust externalization of burden.

My tentative principle is that incommensurable values admit of domain-relative ordering rules—lexical dominance for survival over art at sufficient scale, reason-responsiveness without uniqueness in institutional trade-offs, and authenticity-weights permissible in first-person but not in representative or third-party choice—coupled with a burden-internalization condition: costs of preserving an incommensurable good must be borne by those who claim its value.

Please test this principle precisely against my six verdicts: which clauses save which cases, and which verdict forces me to drop a clause? In particular, can case 3's permission for either option be reconciled with case 1's clear dominance without collapsing into simple quantitative aggregation? Also predict the principle's verdict on two unlisted cases. (a) A parent who must choose between saving her own child and saving two strangers' children chooses her own and cites the incommensurability of parental duty; does my principle endorse this, and if so, on what domain? (b) A legislature bans a profitable but culturally destructive industry, citing incommensurability, while using general tax revenue to compensate displaced workers; does the burden-internalization condition condemn this arrangement, permit it, or split the evaluation?

State the repaired principle with its motivating clause for each case, flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, and say honestly which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate and why.
