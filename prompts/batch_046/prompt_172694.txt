As a retired experimental biologist who spent three decades investigating circadian rhythms before turning to philosophy of mind, I am preparing to moderate a colloquium interview with a philosopher who advances what she terms the transformational equivalence reading of Zhuangzi's butterfly dream. On her account, the passage is not an instance of Cartesian-style dream skepticism asking how we know we are awake, but rather a proto-phenomenological argument that dream and waking states can be ontologically continuous when they exhibit the same structural dynamics of transformation without leaving residual cognitive dissonance. She extends this claim into philosophy of science, arguing that contemporary neuroscience's sharp distinction between REM sleep and wakeful consciousness is conceptually arbitrary, because the proper criterion for state-identity ought to be functional-transitional symmetry rather than neurophysiological correlation. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic biography and instead press precisely where the literature on Zhuangzian ontology and consciousness studies tends to press. Please include at least one question that asks how her view differs from standard skeptical readings without allowing her to simply restate the distinction; another that asks what empirical finding—such as a demonstrated irreducible neural signature unique to veridical perception—would genuinely count as counterevidence rather than being absorbed as an adjustment to a broader research framework; and a third that probes whether her symmetry criterion commits her to a process ontology in which the self that transforms is itself an unstable relational node rather than a substance enduring through change. For each question, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, especially if she appeals to the irrelevance of neurocorrelation or invokes Zhuangzi's broader skepticism about fixed categorical boundaries. The tone should remain rigorous and suitable for an academic audience with some background in both classical Chinese philosophy and contemporary philosophy of mind.
