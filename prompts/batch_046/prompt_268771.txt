Please argue that the orthogonality thesis — the claim that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are logically independent — fails specifically for agents capable of what might be called 'meta-cognitive restructuring,' that is, agents that can revise not only their beliefs but the very evaluative framework through which they assess goals. Your argument should establish that for such agents, instrumental convergence is not merely a contingent tendency but a constitutive constraint: the capacities for resource acquisition, self-preservation, and internal coherence are not derived instrumental values but necessary conditions for the possibility of revising one's own goal-architecture at all. In developing this, distinguish carefully between (i) the standard instrumental-convergence claim that intelligent agents will tend to pursue similar intermediate goals regardless of their ends, and (ii) your stronger claim that these intermediate capacities are structurally embedded in the architecture of self-modification itself. You should also address the objection that an agent could, in principle, redesign itself to abandon self-preservation if it first preserved the capacity to do so, and explain why this objection either misunderstands the level at which the constraint operates or collapses back into a weaker form of orthogonality. Throughout, please separate what is settled in the literature on orthogonality and instrumental convergence from what remains genuinely contested — particularly regarding whether meta-cognitive capacity introduces a dependency between intelligence and goals — and do not present any contested view, including your own argument, as if it were the consensus position.
