You are advising an interdisciplinary research-review board that must evaluate proposals relying on cross-domain analogical reasoning—what philosophers call heuristic arguments, in which a pattern observed in Domain B is used to support an expectation in Domain A because the two share some structural feature. These arguments depend on a tacit presumption of independence: that A and B are not linked by hidden common causes, shared methodological artifacts, or conceptual framing dependencies that would make the analogy spurious rather than informative. Your task is to translate this philosophical principle into a reusable, fifteen-minute audit procedure that a scientifically literate but non-specialist referee can run without philosophical training.

Design the procedure as an ordered sequence of explicit tests, with three possible outputs at each stage: (i) proceed, (ii) halt with a provisional caution flag, or (iii) stop and hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is normative rather than factual. The procedure must formalize the presumption of independence as a default that stands unless defeated, and it must distinguish first-order defeaters (direct causal, logical, or institutional links between the domains) from second-order defeaters (dependencies introduced jointly by measurement instruments, theoretical vocabulary, funding structures, or disciplinary framing conventions that shape both domains without being part of either domain's content). Specify exactly what counts as a sufficient answer to defeat independence at each stage, and identify the points where the referee's own domain competence is irreducibly required—particularly in recognizing hidden vocabulary dependencies.

Then apply the procedure to two proposals:
1. A climate modeler proposes using epidemiological SIR dynamics as a heuristic analogy for predicting the spatial spread of an invasive aquatic plant, arguing that both involve transmission through networked nodes with threshold effects.
2. A political economist proposes using evolutionary-game-theoretic stability conditions as a heuristic analogy for predicting long-run institutional design in democratic assemblies, arguing that both involve strategy selection within populations subject to replication and mutation.

For each case, run the audit in order, name the specific first-order and second-order defeaters present, state the procedure's output, and flag the exact moment—if any—where judgment about the weight of a conceptual framing dependency must override the formal result. Conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot do: in particular, its inability to quantify analogical strength, and its dependence on the referee's prior knowledge of disciplinary vocabularies to detect second-order dependencies. Write in a formal, procedural register suitable for a board manual, avoiding any request for bibliographies or citations.
