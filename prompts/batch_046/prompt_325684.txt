I'm currently taking an advanced seminar on epistemic game theory and the philosophy of disagreement, and I've become convinced of a position I'd like to stress-test before committing to it in my final paper. The view is this: common knowledge of rationality should be treated not merely as a formal modeling assumption in coordination games, but as a substantive normative constraint on multi-agent reasoning. More precisely, if two agents genuinely share common knowledge that each is fully rational—where rationality includes not just instrumental coherence but also responsiveness to shared evidence—then divergence in belief or strategic choice, given identical information sets, is not merely improbable but rationally defective, analogous to a performative contradiction. I have tried to apply this to philosophical disagreement: when two philosophers persistently disagree despite apparently shared evidence and inferential norms, the explanation should not be that rationality permits reasonable divergence, but rather that they lack genuine common knowledge about the norms governing their reasoning—what I have been calling an "epistemic friction" in the common-knowledge framework rather than a rational disagreement. This seems to explain why some disputes feel intractable without collapsing into relativism, but I worry that invoking hidden failures of common knowledge to explain every persistent disagreement risks making the view unfalsifiable.

What would actually count against this position? I want a map of its exposure: which formal results, empirical observations about actual disagreement, or conceptual arguments should move a defender, and which would merely be awkward? For instance, are there well-documented cases of rational agents with shared information who rationally maintain divergent beliefs without any hidden normative disagreement—perhaps due to ambiguous prior weighting, legitimate value pluralism, or structural features of belief revision that do not reduce to rationality failures? Would defenders of the normative-convergence view concede that such cases defeat them, or would they absorb them by expanding the notion of "epistemic friction"? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any divergence can be explained away by a failure of common knowledge, does that undermine its philosophical value, or is insulation appropriate for a normative framework of this kind? Finally, rank the potential defeaters from fatal—those that would force abandonment—to merely awkward adjustments, and indicate which ones have arguably already appeared in observed patterns of scientific and philosophical disagreement, including whether the view predicts anything that its rivals do not about the conditions under which disagreement should dissolve.
