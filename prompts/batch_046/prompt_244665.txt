Read Sidney Lumet’s 1957 film Twelve Angry Men through the lens of judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. The jury must reach a unanimous verdict, yet the jurors initially disagree both on the conclusion—guilt or innocence—and on the underlying premises: whether the elderly witness could have seen the stabbing clearly, whether the switchblade was genuinely unique, whether the disabled neighbor could have reached his door in the time he claimed. Analyze whether the deliberation dramatizes the discursive dilemma: does the group aggregate judgments premise-by-premise and risk an inconsistent collective verdict, or does it vote directly on the conclusion and suppress dissent about the reasons? Focus on Juror 8’s strategy—re-examining the evidence item by item rather than forcing an immediate verdict—and ask whether his method resolves the aggregation problem or merely relocates it onto a hidden consensus about which premises matter. Where does the philosophical framework illuminate a specific scene, such as the reconstruction of the old man’s walk, the switchblade demonstration, or the realization about the witness’s glasses? Where does the film resist that framing—perhaps by treating unanimous agreement as moral awakening rather than procedural coherence, or by obscuring the fact that the jurors never actually converge on a shared set of premises? Conclude by saying what Twelve Angry Men adds to the debate about group rationality that abstract argument alone cannot capture, and whether an honest critic must admit that the film’s resolution depends on suppressing rather than solving the discursive dilemma.
