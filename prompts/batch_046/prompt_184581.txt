Exam question:
Consider a three-person committee that must render a collective judgment on whether a defendant is liable (L). The committee's charter specifies that L holds if and only if both premises hold: B (there was a breach of duty) and C (the breach caused the harm). The members cast binary judgments as follows:
Member 1: B = Yes, C = Yes, L = Yes.
Member 2: B = Yes, C = No, L = No.
Member 3: B = No, C = Yes, L = No.

(a) Demonstrate the discursive dilemma: show that premise-based majority aggregation and conclusion-based majority aggregation yield opposite results for L, with explicit vote counts.
(b) Explain precisely why this conflict is not reducible to Condorcet's voting paradox, even though both involve majority-rule failures.
(c) List and Pettit use cases like this to defend a specific view about collective rational agency—sometimes called the "group mind" position. State the core of their view, and then identify one significant cost or objection that critics defending a purely distributive account of group belief have pressed against it.

Student answer:
(a) Under premise-based aggregation, B receives Yes from Members 1 and 2, so B is Yes. C receives Yes from Members 1 and 3, so C is Yes. Because L is defined as B and C together, the group should derive L = Yes. Under conclusion-based aggregation, L receives Yes only from Member 1, so L is No. The two procedures therefore conflict: premise-based gives Yes, conclusion-based gives No.

(b) This is not Condorcet's paradox. Condorcet involves cyclical preferences over three or more alternatives—A over B, B over C, C over A—so that pairwise majority voting has no winner. Here there is no preference ordering and no cycle among alternatives. Instead, we have logical interdependence among propositions: the group collectively accepts B and accepts C, yet denies their logical consequence L. That is a failure of deductive closure in the collective judgment set, not a cyclical preference profile.

(c) List and Pettit argue that this shows collective bodies can be literal rational agents—a "group mind"—provided they resolve the dilemma by adopting a premise-based procedure and accepting that the group belief may diverge from the majority belief on the conclusion. The central cost, raised by critics such as Rovane and other defenders of distributive accounts, is that this requires attributing to the group a belief that almost none of its members actually hold. In this case Members 2 and 3 both reject L, yet the group is said to believe L. Critics contend this is not genuine collective belief but a fictional ascription that obscures the fact that belief is always ultimately distributed among individuals.
