I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about negative utilitarianism, specifically whether pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable and whether a lexical threshold can separate catastrophic suffering from everything else. She defends what I have come to think of as a two-register model: suffering either occupies a catastrophic register, which takes absolute lexical priority over all flourishing, or a sub-threshold register of minor discomforts, which she insists is incommensurable with pleasure rather than merely outweighed by it. To see whether I actually understand her view, I have been cataloguing her verdicts on hypothetical moral choice cases, and I now need to test whether they determine a unique rule or leave room for rival interpretations.

Here are her judgments so far. First, if preventing one instance of extreme, sustained trauma—what she calls breaking the catastrophic floor—requires reducing every human being’s capacity for profound joy by half, she says we must do it; the catastrophic register dominates regardless of aggregate scale. Second, she approves of a global medical intervention that eliminates serious chronic pain for one million people even at the cost of a slight, uniform reduction in baseline contentment across the entire population, because the suffering involved remains within that catastrophic register. Third, she rejects a technology that would eradicate all minor physical discomforts—brief itches, fleeting headaches, momentary nausea—if its mechanism requires suppressing the neurological basis for ecstatic or transcendent states and thereby capping peak human experience far below its current ceiling; she argues that sub-threshold disutility and flourishing lie on scales that do not communicate, so one cannot be sacrificed to purchase the other. Fourth, she holds that between two worlds identical in every respect except that in one, for a single minute, one person experiences an imperceptible flicker of negative affect with no impact on anyone else, the world lacking that flicker is strictly better, and if the flicker could be removed at absolutely zero cost to flourishing, we are morally required to remove it.

Now I want to know what she would say about a new proposal and whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer. The proposal involves a treatment she might call hedonic compression: it cannot cure a sufferer, but it can redistribute the intensity of one person’s catastrophic suffering downward, diffusing the excess negative intensity across the global population as imperceptible micro-perturbations—billions of people each experiencing a -0.0001 flicker for an instant, with no reduction in anyone’s flourishing or awareness—so that the original sufferer remains in serious but non-catastrophic pain (around -50), and the total quantum of suffering is conserved but no longer concentrated above the catastrophic floor. Would she approve? Among her earlier verdicts—the global intervention, the rejected comfort-technology, or the imperceptible-flicker case—which is the closest analog, and why? More importantly, I want to identify the precise rule she is running: is it strict lexical priority (any suffering dominates any pleasure), a two-register incommensurability rule (catastrophic suffering dominates flourishing, but sub-threshold suffering never trades with flourishing), or a single-scale saturation model (one shared metric with non-linear weights that collapse near zero)? Say what each predicts about the compression case, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons, and then propose one further hypothetical case—one I could present to her tomorrow—that would definitively separate the two rules that still fit her verdicts. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, be explicit about what principle she would be adopting with each choice.
