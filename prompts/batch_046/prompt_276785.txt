I am negotiating a separation agreement with my partner, and to prevent immediate anger or anxiety from driving the division of our shared assets, we adopted a decision rule: each of us must articulate what a fully informed, emotionally stable, long-term-oriented version of ourselves would recommend, and then treat that recommendation as binding even when it conflicts with our present feelings. Following this rule, I concluded that I ought to accept a smaller share of our liquid assets than an equal split, because the "future self" I am consulting apparently values sustained co-parenting cooperation over immediate financial security. I am now second-guessing the rule itself. The difficulty is that constructing this advisor requires feeding in detailed information about my actual circumstances—including that I am the one who will live with the outcome—yet the rule demands that I treat her recommendation as if it came from an external authority rather than from me. If what I ought to do is fixed by the outputs of this mental procedure rather than by direct evaluation of the choice in front of me, does the procedure depend on a kind of self-alienation? Specifically, when the idealized agent reasons about me as a case to be optimized rather than as the subject doing the reasoning, is there still a meaningful sense in which I am following my own rational will, or have I simply outsourced judgment to a constructed persona that I can imagine but do not recognize as continuous with myself? Where does that leave the authority of the recommendation?
