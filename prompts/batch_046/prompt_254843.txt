I am presenting the thesis that the separateness of persons objection, reconstructed as a constraint on ex-ante contractual justification rather than on simple interpersonal aggregation, undermines not only utilitarian risk-pooling but also Scanlon-style contractualist defenses of collective risk imposition—because ex-ante reasoning treats future individuals as placeholders whose losses can be offset within a single choice situation. The audience is a philosophy-of-risk seminar: two Scanlon specialists convinced that Parfit's division-of-reasons critique dissolved the objection for pure aggregation; one contractualist who explicitly uses ex-ante reasoning to justify climate-policy risk pools; and a population ethicist who thinks separateness has no force outside total-utilitarian frameworks. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in each interlocutor's vocabulary: which objections have real force, what I should concede up front (for instance, that Parfit succeeds against naive summation but not against justification structures), the single reply that must land for the argument to survive, the weakest point in my own position that I should defend rather than hope they miss—likely the boundary between permissible statistical risk and impermissible targeted sacrifice—and the objection I am hoping nobody asks, probably that my view collapses into a deontological veto rendering any collective action with diffuse harms impossible. For each objection, give me a concise reply and a brief note on where that reply is most fragile, keeping the whole prep ordered by damage potential rather than by speaker.
