I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of science with the enthusiasm of a convert but without the disciplinary armor of formal training. Next Thursday I am presenting a forty-minute paper at a university philosophy department’s work-in-progress seminar—an audience of analytic philosophers of science, mostly with Popperian or logical-empiricist sympathies, plus two theoretical physicists who regard philosophical intrusion into string theory with barely concealed irritation. The thesis I defend is that the multiverse landscape emerging from eternal inflation and string compactification can be a legitimate object of non-empirical rational assessment, not because it is verified in any naive sense, but because it is constrained by what I call structural induction: any viable vacuum must reproduce the Standard Model’s gauge group and Yukawa couplings with only exponentially suppressed deviations, and the measure problem forces precise statistical predictions about cosmological parameters—spatial curvature bounds, non-Gaussianity thresholds—that are empirically risky in a way evolutionary psychology’s adaptive narratives are not. To sharpen this, I introduce a niche distinction, predictive pluralism, between theories that generate empirically equivalent classes with no internal ranking mechanism (mere underdetermination) and theories whose parameter space is so tightly coupled to observable low-energy physics that selecting one region carries measurable statistical commitments, even when direct access to other regions is impossible.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually receive, ordered from the most damaging to the least. For each, phrase it as these interlocutors would put it—using their vocabulary of demarcation, special pleading, empirical equivalence, and ad hocness—include a concise reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and flag exactly where that reply is weakest. I want to know which objections I should concede up front—perhaps that the landscape problem genuinely weakens naive falsifiability, or that anthropic conditioning requires careful probabilistic framing—and which I must hold firm. Crucially, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks: the objection that exposes the tension between my retired-scientist intuition that mathematical consistency and inter-theoretic uniqueness can carry evidential weight, and the philosophers’ insistence that without direct empirical risk there is only metaphysics dressed in the notation of physics. Do not offer bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need to speak from conceptual command alone, and I ask that you treat the audience’s commitments with the precision of someone who has actually sat in their seminars.
