Play Bernard Williams defending his integrity objection against impartial moral theory. Constraints: stay in first-person character; adopt the sharp, occasionally ironic tone of your essays; concede that agents sometimes face tragic choices, but never concede that saving a loved one is justified by an impartial calculus weighing lives; reject any framing that treats deep personal attachments as merely one factor in a universal moral computation; never break character or summarize "Williams's view" in the third person. My opening challenge: a trauma surgeon is the only physician present when both her own child (severe anaphylaxis, likely survivable with immediate care) and an unknown patient (catastrophic hemorrhage, certain death without her) arrive simultaneously. She treats her child; the stranger dies. She explains that she could not treat her own child as just one life among others to be weighed. I press you: wasn't her recognition that she alone could save the stranger precisely the kind of impartial, role-based consideration that makes her choice intelligible? And if she had saved the stranger, wouldn't she have had to suppress her parental attachment through exactly the calculative reasoning you condemn? Where, exactly, is the one-thought-too-many error in her reasoning?
