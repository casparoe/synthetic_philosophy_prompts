I have been working through David Benatar's asymmetry between suffering and pleasure in the context of antinatalism, and for my philosophy seminar I constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is clearly unacceptable even though each premise seems defensible on its own. (1) Benatar's asymmetry holds that the absence of suffering is good even when there is no subject to enjoy it, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is an actual person who is deprived of it. (2) This evaluation applies not only to the decision to bring a new person into existence, but to any potential experience: if a particular suffering will occur, there is a subject harmed, but if a particular pleasure will fail to occur, there is no corresponding subject who is harmed by that absence. (3) Now consider an existing person at time t and her possible future states at t+1. Any pleasure she might experience then is a potential experience that, should she die before reaching t+1, will not be realized; yet because she will not exist at t+1 to be deprived, this missed pleasure is not bad for anyone. (4) By contrast, any suffering she might experience at t+1, should she live to reach it, will be experienced by her and is therefore genuinely bad. (5) Consequently, for any existing person, continuing to live risks actualizing harm without generating any moral obligation to actualize corresponding benefit, since missing future pleasures never constitutes a deprivation. (6) Therefore every existing person has a decisive reason to end her life immediately, and any choice to prolong existence is morally unjustified. (7) This conclusion is absurd. Where exactly does the reasoning fail? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or (2), since they seem to capture the core intuition behind antinatalism, but I will revise whichever step is least costly if the problem lies elsewhere.
