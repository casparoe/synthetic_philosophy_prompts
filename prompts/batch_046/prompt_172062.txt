I've been thinking about logical uncertainty lately, partly in the context of embedded agency and partly because I'm trying to understand how a bounded reasoner should assign credences to mathematical claims it hasn't derived yet. The logical induction framework seems promising—treating logical uncertainty as a kind of market that converges to coherence over time—but I'm unsure how much of the remaining work is genuinely foundational versus incremental formalization. There's a specific tension I'm trying to map out: on one hand, we want an agent to predict the outputs of deterministic algorithms it hasn't fully executed, including possibly its own future reasoning steps, without being logically omniscient; on the other hand, we want its credences to avoid Dutch-book vulnerability in a way that respects both logical and empirical updating. Some discussions treat these as separable problems—extending logical induction to richer languages, handling resource-bounded convergence, and adapting the framework for self-referential prediction—but I'm not convinced they're actually independent. In particular, it seems like the self-referential case might impose constraints on what "resource-bounded" even means, since if an agent predicts it will take ten more steps to verify a theorem, that prediction itself affects how it allocates resources.

Given that framing, I'm trying to prioritize where effort should actually go. Among the open directions—extending logical induction beyond propositional settings, formalizing genuinely resource-bounded variants, integrating it with causal decision theory for embedded agents, and clarifying whether logical uncertainty should be treated as a primitive or reducible to computation prediction—which matter most, and in what order? I want a dependency-aware prioritization rather than a survey: rank them by which are upstream of the others, which are tractable with current formal tools, and which wouldn't change much downstream if resolved. I'm writing from a rationalist, decision-theoretic perspective rather than pure logic, so I'm especially interested in whether solving the embedded self-prediction problem is genuinely prior to worrying about richer logics, or whether that's putting the cart before the horse. Defend your ranking with explicit criteria—tractability, downstream dependency, and relevance to embedded agency—and say what a better framing would be if you think I've mischaracterized the separation between these lines of work.
