As a novelist drafting a first-person narrative, I have created a narrator who bears my name and biography yet lives in a timeline altered by a single omitted decision. To maintain coherence, I imposed a formal constraint: every chapter opens with the narrator describing the same brass compass on his desk, but the needle's behavior—restless, fixed, or absent—must correspond exactly to his current capacity for autonomous choice. Over the past months, I have found that revising the compass passage does not merely reflect my mood; it appears to determine it. When I write the needle as spinning without settling, I experience a corresponding paralysis in my own minor decisions—what to eat, whether to reply to an email—until I edit the passage toward stability. The "I" who narrates the compass and the "I" who edits the manuscript now seem to be the same voice observing itself from both sides. Is this a mere psychological artifact of deep immersion, or does it demonstrate that a symbol referring back to its own origin can actually construct the consciousness it claims merely to describe? And if I preserve the loop rather than sever it, will I be building a unified subjectivity or eroding the distinction that makes fiction distinguishable from autobiography?
