I've been working out an institutional view of marriage for a policy discussion and want an audit of whether my commitments hang together. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Civil marriage is, at its functional core, a mechanism for distributing long-term dependency risks—caregiving, illness, income shock, and aging—across households, not a state recognition of romantic love or sexual intimacy.
(2) The state should license marriage only when it expects net-positive externalities, chiefly stable child-rearing or reduced public welfare dependency; unions pursued purely for private expressive ends should not receive state licensing.
(3) Same-sex couples ought to have full access to civil marriage, but the justification is functional equivalence in dependency-risk pooling, not a fundamental right to have romantic bonds validated by the state.
(4) The exclusion of polygamous or polyamorous households is arbitrary: any group of consenting adults with durable cooperative governance and demonstrable shared risk-pooling should be eligible for licensing.
(5) So-called "marriages of convenience"—entered solely for immigration status, tax optimization, or benefits access without genuine cohabitation or risk-sharing—should be denied and penalized, since they corrupt the institution's externality-producing logic.
(6) Despite all this, I think the best long-term reform is to abolish unitary marriage and replace it with a modular domestic-partnership registry that separates co-residence, parental obligation, and mutual economic support into distinct opt-in contracts with independent sunset clauses.

Go through these and tell me which pairs pull against each other, which tensions are only apparent, and how severe each is. For each genuine tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most tension overall. Be specific about whether (2) and (3) can coexist without making access conditional on reproductive or welfare outcomes, and about whether (6) undermines the rationale for fighting over access in (3) and (4).

Please also say what argument or consideration would change your mind about where the real tensions lie—for instance, if dependency-risk pooling turned out to require dyadic exclusivity, or if modular registries proved administratively unworkable—and flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
