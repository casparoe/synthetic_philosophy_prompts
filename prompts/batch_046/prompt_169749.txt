I hold a specific reconstruction of classical Buddhist ethics—drawing on Theravāda Abhidhamma and early Mahāyāna moral psychology—that frames it as an indirect, agent-centered consequentialism whose currency is not welfare but the attenuation of upādāna (clinging/grasping) across the mental continuum. On this view, the five precepts (pañcasīla) are not deontological side-constraints but intertemporal commitment devices: they block the javana-phase mental formations—greed, aversion, delusion—that generate binding karmic potentials, solving coordination failures between present and future stages of the stream. Even apparently absolute prohibitions, such as against intentional killing, are instrumentally justified because the mental structure required to violate them necessarily intensifies clinging, regardless of external outcome; though the view allows that if a violation could occur with genuinely purified intention and net reduction in clinging, the instrumental rationale might shift. What would genuinely count against this reconstruction? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Include: doctrinal patterns that resist instrumentalization; formal or conceptual results showing that "clinging-attenuation" collapses either into standard act-consequentialism or into a disguised deontological rule-system; internal inconsistencies in applying commitment-device logic across rebirth and non-personal continuity; and phenomenological evidence from Buddhist practice about how ethical decisions are actually made. For each, say whether a committed defender should concede damage or dismiss it as misframing. Finally, assess whether the view has been qualified—say, by retreating to a purely psychological claim about the agent—into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a normative theory of this kind.
