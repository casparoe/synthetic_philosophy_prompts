I've been thinking about that Air Canada chatbot case from earlier this year, where the bot gave wrong info about bereavement fares and then the airline tried to claim it wasn't their problem because the bot was a seperate legal entity. I want to analyze it through the lens of process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI, but I'm struggling to map the concepts cleanly onto what actually happened.

If we take process-based supervision seriously, the question is whether Air Canada had adequate protocols—testing specifically for fare-policy hallucinations, human review loops, clear documentation of what knowledge domains the bot was authorized to access. Outcome-based supervision, by contrast, doesn't care about those internal procedures; it just asks whether the system produced a harmful false statement and whether the deployer should be liable regardless. The tribunal seemed to go with the latter, holding the airline responsible for whatever its bot said. But I'm not convinced the case is a pure win for outcome-based views. There's a third framing people don't talk about enough: supervising the epistemic architecture itself—constraining how the bot retrieves and validates information rather than policing its outputs or auditing its training pipeline. Does the Air Canada episode actually show that process supervision is invisible to outsiders and therefore useless for consumer protection? Or does it show that outcome liability is too blunt, because the airline could have had perfect procedures and still faced an unpredictable error?

Also, people keep comparing this to product liability for a defective gadget, but that analogy feels strained since a chatbot generates novel utterances rather than repeating a fixed design flaw. I want an honest two-way analysis: what the process/outcome distinction explains about the tribunal's reasoning and the airline's defense, where it breaks down or stays silent—especially regarding that seperate-entity argument—and whether the episode should push us toward outcome-based rules for conversational agents or reveal that neither framework was designed for systems that have this kind of generative autonomy.
