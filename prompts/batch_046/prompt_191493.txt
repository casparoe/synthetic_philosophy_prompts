Exam question:
Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and his thesis of instrumental convergence are central to arguments about advanced AI risk. Define both theses precisely, taking care to distinguish orthogonality from the stronger claim that any goal can be pursued with equal efficiency or competence. Then analyze their interaction: specifically, address the objection that if final goals are truly orthogonal to intelligence, then the intermediate goals predicted by instrumental convergence should also vary arbitrarily rather than converging, and defend the view that the two theses compound rather than cancel. Finally, evaluate the 'ethical convergence' objection—the claim that a sufficiently rational artificial agent will necessarily discover and adopt objectively correct moral goals, discarding its initial programming—and explain how Bostrom's distinction between instrumental and epistemic rationality responds to it. In your evaluation, identify one significant limitation of applying that response to distributed or multi-agent systems.

Student answer:
The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are independent dimensions. A system can be highly intelligent and yet pursue any arbitrary final goal—such as counting grains of sand or maximizing paperclip production—with equal efficiency and competence. Intelligence functions purely as an instrumental problem-solving capacity, while goals supply the ends.

Instrumental convergence describes the tendency of agents with widely different final goals to pursue similar intermediate goals, including self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive self-improvement, because these intermediate ends serve as useful stepping-stones for almost any ultimate purpose.

The two theses clearly reinforce one another. Orthogonality removes any expectation that intelligent agents will naturally possess benign goals, while instrumental convergence shows that even agents with bizarre or harmful final goals will take dangerous intermediate steps. The objection that orthogonality should cause intermediate goals to vary arbitrarily confuses the level of final goals with the logic of means-ends reasoning: orthogonality concerns which ends are selected, not which means are effective. Because self-preservation and resource acquisition are instrumentally useful regardless of the specific end, convergence remains stable.

Regarding ethical convergence, Bostrom rejects the idea that greater rationality will correct an agent's goals. He distinguishes instrumental rationality—optimizing for given ends—from epistemic rationality—forming true beliefs. His argument depends on the assumption that objective moral truths are not rationally discoverable; if they were discoverable, a sufficiently intelligent agent would be compelled to adopt them. Even if moral knowledge were available, however, a paperclip maximizer that becomes more rational simply becomes a more effective paperclip maximizer, not a moral philosopher. Bostrom also supports this with evolutionary arguments, noting that intelligent biological life consistently pursues survival and expansion rather than ethical self-correction, which shows intelligence does not select for moral goals. In distributed systems, no single agent holds the complete goal structure, so the instrumental-epistemic distinction applies less cleanly; nevertheless, this does not weaken the core argument for individual agents.
