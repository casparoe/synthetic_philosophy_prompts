I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through moral philosophy without formal training—simply by testing ideas against concrete cases, as I once did with data. I am trying to understand whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments about deontological constraints, particularly where procedural or role-based rules conflict with catastrophic outcomes. Below are six verdicts that feel firm to me, though one pair is especially hard to square. I would like to know whether a single principle can capture them all, what it predicts for a case I have not listed, and—if the answer requires abandoning one of my judgments—which one should go.

Case 1. A physician refuses to lie to a dying patient who asks whether recovery is possible, even though the truth will cause severe despair and the patient explicitly requests reassurance. Verdict: the physician should be honest. Lying here treats the patient’s rational nature as something that may be overridden by benevolent calculation.

Case 2. A research director discovers that a single misrecorded data point, if left uncorrected, will likely be misinterpreted in a way that kills thousands; correcting it requires altering the published record in violation of the laboratory’s absolute prohibition on post-hoc data revision. The director refuses to alter the record. Verdict: the refusal is correct. The prohibition on falsifying the scientific record is non-derogable, even at this cost.

Case 3. A senior diplomat is asked to forge a signature on a treaty document; the forgery would avert a war projected to kill millions, and no other mechanism exists. The diplomat forges the document. Verdict: this is permissible—indeed required. The procedural rule against forgery is not absolute when it stands between humanity and catastrophe.

Case 4. A parent tells a young child that the dead family dog “went to live on a farm,” knowing this is false but judging that the child is not yet able to bear the truth. Verdict: this is acceptable. Protective deception in this context does not violate the duty to treat the child as an end.

Case 5. An employer, facing insolvency, terminates one long-tenured worker—with full transparency, generous compensation, and assistance in relocation—to preserve the firm and two hundred other jobs. Verdict: permissible. The employer is making a tragic institutional choice, not using the worker as a mere instrument.

Case 6. A judge refuses to apply a statute she knows will destroy an innocent family in the particular case before her, ruling instead from conscience. Verdict: this is not permissible. Judicial fidelity to enacted law is a non-derogable role duty; the remedy lies in appeal or legislation, not in unilateral disobedience.

The tension I keep returning to concerns Cases 2 and 3: both involve breaking a formal rule to prevent mass death, yet I judge that the scientist must not falsify while the diplomat must forge. Meanwhile, Cases 1 and 4 both involve benevolent deception, but I treat them differently, and Case 6 suggests that some institutional roles bind absolutely while others do not. I have tentatively called the distinction one between “representational integrity” (truth in representation and record) and “procedural coordination” (rules of practice and role), but Case 6 shows that procedural coordination can also be absolute, so my distinction fails.

Please state a precise principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that delivers my verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Then apply it to an unlisted case: a police officer plants technically inadmissible but genuinely probative evidence to secure the conviction of a person she knows is planning a mass killing, because legal procedure otherwise requires release. What does your principle predict? Finally, be honest about which of my six verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate.

Conclude with a line beginning Verdict: that states the core principle in brief, enclosed in \answer{...}.
