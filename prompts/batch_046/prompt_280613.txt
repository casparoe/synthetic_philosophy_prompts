I run a seminar on dirty hands in politics, about 14 students, grad and undergrad mixed, 90 minutes. I want an activty — not a lecture — that makes them feel the second-order version of the problem: not just one leader getting dirty for a good result, but the fact that if you design an institution that reliably produces good outcomes, you have to bake moral costs into specific roles, and then real people have to inhabit those roles permanently. So start them off constructing a decision charter for a city facing recurring crises. Then hit them with a live scenario: a toxic spill is moving toward a residential zone, and the only way to evacuate is to authorize temporary forced relocation of a resistant community, with guaranteed psychological harm and one probable injury. They have to vote on the order, assign who actually signs it, and decide whether to hardwire that authority into the charter so future crises can be handled fast. Give them small real stakes: each student gets two integrity chips they can spend to veto any dirty option, but each chip spent lowers their vote weight in the charter-design round. That way they feel the tradeoff between keeping their own hands clean and building a system that actually works. Include roles like mayor, ethics advisor, press secretary, and citizens' reps, with different payoffs. After the vote and charter vote, run a debrief that maps moments — charter design, chip spending, role assignment — onto Walzer's framing versus the institutional version, notes what predictably breaks (token hoarding, moral grandstanding, someone refusing to play), and is honest about what the room can't reproduce: the long-term corrosion of actually holding that power for years. Don't have them cite texts or read specific pages beforehand; just reference the concept generally. No bibliographies or reading lists, please.
