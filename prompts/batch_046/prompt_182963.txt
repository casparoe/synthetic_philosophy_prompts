I've been chatting with a mate about those spectrum arguments that try to show 'better than' isn't transitive — the usual heap-style chain where A is just barely better than B, B barely better than C, and eventually you loop back round. But I want to push this into slightly weirder territory: what happens when the comparison isn't on one tidy dimension like height, but across several incommensurable values?

Imagine we're comparing three funding programmes for mental-health care — call them Red, Amber and Green — where each small step improves accessibility but chips away at clinician discretion. Locally, every adjacent pair feels like a clear improvement, yet Red and Green sit on opposite sides of an ideological line and comparing them directly feels almost meaningless. Is the breakdown of transitivity here just vagueness about where the cut-off lies, or is there a deeper conceptual problem about whether 'better' needs a stable metric that survives the whole chain? I'm particulary stuck on whether we can rescue transitivity by letting the comparison standard shift contextually at each link — so 'better' means something subtly different each time — or whether that move just dissolves the comparison into a series of unrelated local judgements. Could you unpack the conceptual knot, especially why incommensurability makes the spectrum bite harder than in one-dimensional cases?
