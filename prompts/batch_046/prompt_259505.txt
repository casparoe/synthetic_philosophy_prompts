I was arguing with my roommate about knowing how vs knowing that and we got stuck in this loop that feels like it proves too much. Here's the arguement my roommate laid out, and I can't find the exit.

(1) Knowing how to do something is either just knowing that some procedure works, plus having the physical ability to do it, or it's something mysterious and non-cognitive.

(2) Take the case of a cyclist who can correct her balance instantly but can't say how she does it. If she really knows how, and knowing-how isn't mysterious, then she must implicitly know that shifting weight left by about two degrees when tilt exceeds some threshold is the right move, she just can't articulate it.

(3) Now imagine a hypothetical condition — call it procedural decoupling — where someone retains perfect propositional access to all those rules, she can write them out and teach them, but the integrative feedback loop between her proprioceptive sense and her motor execution is severed. She knows all the thats, she has the physical limbs, but she can't ride and can't recognize good riding in others anymore.

(4) In that decoupled state, we'd say she lost the knowing-how, even though the knowing-that and the physical capacity are both intact. So knowing-how can't be reduced to those two.

(5) But wait — apply that same reasoning to language. A native speaker knows how to form grammatically correct sentences without being able to state the rules. If knowing-how is this extra integrative loop, then either every fluent speaker has a hidden neural mechanism that can't be articulated, or we're saying that robots with the right sensors lack something that can't even be described.

(6) So the argument ends up saying that knowing-how is either reducible to knowing-that plus body, which the decoupling case refutes, or it's an unobservable, non-cognitive feel that we attribute to people but deny to machines arbitrarily. Either way, the distinction collapses into either over-intellectualization or mysticism.

(7) My roommate says this shows Ryle was wrong and that we should just ditch the catagory altogether. I feel like something slipped between (3) and (4) — maybe an equivocation about what intact means — but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd especially like to keep the idea that the decoupling case shows something real about skill, but maybe I'm wrong about that.
