You are a philosopher of defense policy and civil-military relations with particular expertise in the ethics of armed neutrality and institutional design. Consider a framing I will call the neutrality-conscription feedback loop. In states that maintain armed neutrality—historically Switzerland with its militia system, and Sweden and Finland until their recent shifts—the institution of universal or broad conscription has been understood not merely as a manpower mechanism but as a civic-structural feature: a citizen-army creates a broad political constituency with direct stakes in defense policy, which in turn is thought to bind the state to neutrality, since a conscripted population is less likely to consent to being deployed in foreign alliances. At the same time, the shift toward professional armies, precision technologies, and cyber-deterrence threatens to sever that link. Neutrality then becomes credible only through expensive technological self-sufficiency rather than through mass mobilization capacity, and the distributive burden of defense shifts from broadly shared risk (conscription) to concentrated fiscal cost (professional specialization plus high-tech procurement).
