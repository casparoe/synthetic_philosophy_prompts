Consider the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies, since harm requires a subject and death removes the subject. I have reached the following verdicts about when death is bad for someone, and I want to know whether they can be unified by a single principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

Case 1: A flourishing 35-year-old dies in an accident. She had long-term projects, deep relationships, and a clear sense of her life's narrative. Verdict: This is genuinely bad for her; she is deprived of decades of goods she was positioned to experience and value.

Case 2: A fetus dies in miscarriage at eight weeks. Verdict: A profound loss for the parents, but not bad for the fetus itself, since there was never a conscious subject with interests to be thwarted.

Case 3: A five-year-old child dies after a brief illness. The child had basic preferences, emotional attachments, and a developing personality, but no long-range life plans or robust narrative identity. Verdict: This seems, if anything, worse than Case 1—there are far more years lost—yet the child had far less of a stake in a specific future. I feel the harm is real and severe, but I cannot say it works in exactly the same way as in Case 1.

Case 4: The same 35-year-old has been in an irreversible coma for fifteen years with no prospect of recovery, then biological death occurs. Verdict: Not bad for her. The conscious subject was already absent; nothing valuable was being lost.

Case 5: A couple decides never to conceive, so a particular potential person never comes to exist. Verdict: Not bad for anyone. There is no subject who suffers deprivation.

Case 6: The 35-year-old lives to 85, completes her major projects, and dies peacefully. Verdict: Not a harm. The life was complete, and there is no deprivation.

Case 7: She dies at 35 as in Case 1, and fifty years later her unfinished writings are destroyed and her name forgotten. Verdict: Not bad for her. Posthumous events cannot harm someone who no longer exists.

I am drawn to an account on which death is bad only when it deprives an actually existing conscious subject of future goods that subject was positioned to experience. But this struggles with Case 3 versus Case 2 (why does minimal consciousness generate such severe harm?) and with Case 3 versus Case 1 (why does the absence of long-range plans not diminish the harm?). I also wonder whether narrative completeness matters: Case 6 feels different from Case 4 not just because of consciousness, but because the life in Case 6 reached closure.

Please propose one or more precise principles that capture these verdicts. For each principle, state the clause that handles each case, predict its verdict on two further cases—(i) a newborn who dies within hours of birth, and (ii) an adult who dies suddenly but had always lived entirely in the present with no long-term attachments or projects—and identify honestly which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate without distortion. If saving every verdict requires an overly complex principle, say so, and tell me which verdict a simpler, more defensible principle should overturn.
