As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun serious study in philosophy of medicine, I have become preoccupied with a stalemate that strikes me as peculiar. For decades, naturalist and normativist theories of health and disease have circulated through the same clinical examples—diabetes, schizophrenia, senescence—without either camp conceding ground. Naturalists treat disease as a deviation from species-typical biological function, typically anchored in evolutionary design, while normativists insist that dysfunction only becomes disease when it is evaluated as harmful or undesirable relative to the subject's welfare or goals. Each side has standard replies to the other's counterexamples; the literature has rehearsed these exchanges since at least the 1970s with little visible movement. My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not really about medicine at all, but about whether the evolutionary concept of "proper function" is already covertly normative—whether describing an organ as functioning correctly smuggles in a standard of correct operation that naturalists claim to reject—or, conversely, whether "harm" can be fully naturalized in terms of reduced fitness or survival without leaving a normative residue. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about evolutionary biology itself, or about the semantics of "function" versus "dysfunction"—that neither side has made explicit? I want an honest diagnosis rather than a survey: is the dispute verbal, substantive but bedrock, a case of two questions being answered under one heading (one ontological, one about justification for treatment), or genuinely resolvable by a conceptual distinction or an empirical finding? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would actually move the debate: a clarification of evolutionary teleology, a formal separation of statistical normality from welfare-relative evaluation, or is progress here illusory? Please also say what argument or consideration would change your verdict, and identify the specific point in your reasoning where you are least confident or most vulnerable to error.
