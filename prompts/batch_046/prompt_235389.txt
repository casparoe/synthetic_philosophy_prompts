I am drafting questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher—call her Dr. Voss—who defends what she terms the chromatic calibration thesis as a response to Jackson’s knowledge argument. She holds that Mary’s first experience of red is neither the acquisition of a non-physical fact about the world nor a bare ability in the Lewis-Nemirow sense, but rather the installation of an internal phenomenal reference frame: a calibrated metric for chromatic similarity judgments that restructures her representational capacities without revealing any property hidden from physics. The view is meant to be ontologically neutral but epistemically distinctive, denying both that Mary learns something physicalism cannot capture and that her gain is reducible to knowing-how or to bare acquaintance. I need eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her merely to restate this framework, but that press her on the actual fault lines in the literature. Specifically, please include: (1) a question that forces her to distinguish chromatic calibration from a sophisticated version of the ability hypothesis, together with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that calibration is representational restructuring rather than skill acquisition; (2) a question asking whether her thesis tacitly commits her to representationalism about color experience, and whether it can be reconciled with direct or naive realist accounts, together with a follow-up anticipating her appeal to structural isomorphism between the calibrated frame and the external color space; (3) a question about scope—whether every phenomenal learning episode involves such metric restructuring, or whether Mary’s case is sui generis because color spaces are metrically ordered, together with a follow-up addressing the risk that restricting calibration to metrically structured domains is ad hoc; (4) a question confronting the intuitive pull of the original argument itself—specifically, why Mary’s experience feels like a discovery about the nature of red rather than a mere reorganization of her internal map, together with a follow-up designed for her probable invocation of a map-territory distinction; and (5) at least one further question probing a boundary case, such as whether calibration applies to olfactory or nociceptive experience in the same way, anticipating her standard deflection. Write in a formal, precise tone suitable for an audience with philosophical literacy but without unnecessary specialist jargon; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
