As a novelist calibrating my own reasoning about narrative causality, I ask for your actual credence—expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1—that epistemic modesty, understood as the deliberate downgrading of confidence in a belief upon recognizing one's own attachment to it, can be coherently sustained only from an inside view of one's reasoning, and that a rigorously consistent outside view applied to one's own meta-cognitive processes collapses the distinction between warranted belief revision and random credence drift, unless one introduces an arbitrary stopping rule. Decompose the reasoning: assign a rough probability to whether inside-view access is necessary for any non-arbitrary revision of belief; to whether an outside view, applied reflexively, treats first-order reasons as mere statistical noise rather than as evidence; to whether an ad hoc stopping rule can rescue coherence without undermining the outside view's authority; and to any other term you judge essential. Provide one line of justification for each term, show how they combine into your final number, and identify which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, together with what conceptual or empirical evidence would most move it. Finally, please distinguish clearly between what is relatively settled in epistemology and what remains genuinely contested among those who know the topic, and do not present any disputed position as if it were the consensus.
