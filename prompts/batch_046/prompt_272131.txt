I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun attending philosophy seminars, and before presenting my views in an upcoming discussion on agency and neuroscience I want a rigorous consistency audit. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in neuroscience, action theory, and philosophy of science; before delivering your verdict, spend a moment brainstorming how each of my commitments interacts with the others, especially where standard positions are usually discussed in isolation. Here is the set of commitments I currently hold. (a) The Libet paradigm and its modern neuroimaging extensions show that unconscious neural processes determine the specific outcome of a voluntary choice well before the subject reports conscious awareness of having decided; this undermines the Cartesian-theater picture in which a conscious self initiates action de novo, and I think that picture was always empirically unsupported. (b) I therefore reject folk-psychological agent-causation. (c) At the same time, I accept a compatibilist framework for moral responsibility: an agent is responsible when her actions flow from stable reflective values, she remains responsive to reasons, and there is no external coercion. (d) Nevertheless, I also maintain a sourcehood condition: for genuine moral responsibility the agent must be the ultimate origin of the action, not merely a conduit for deterministic or stochastic neural processes that lie outside her control. (e) I am persuaded by recent work in embodied predictive processing that the brain is an open, coupled dynamical system continuously interacting with environmental affordances, so the boundary between internal neural causes and external environmental causes is graded and context-dependent rather than sharply defined. (f) I accept that quantum-level stochasticity in synaptic ion channels introduces genuine randomness into neural dynamics, but I deny that this randomness restores any form of agent-causal freedom.

Please audit this set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. I am especially concerned whether (c) and (d) can be held together without collapsing into an incoherent hybrid, and whether (e) undermines the absence-of-coercion criterion that is central to (c). In addition, please state clearly what would change your mind: which specific argument, conceptual clarification, or empirical finding would lead you to reorder these tensions or to abandon one of the commitments? Also indicate where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
