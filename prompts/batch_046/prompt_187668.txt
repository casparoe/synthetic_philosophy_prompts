Write a decision-tree handout for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on Mary's room. Start from the premise that Mary genuinely learns something upon seeing red for the first time. The tree should have four branch levels, each split labeled by a concrete yes/no or either/or claim that genuinely divides positions: (1) whether her gain is ability-based or propositional; (2) whether her prior complete physical knowledge was complete in content but presented in the wrong mode, or genuinely missing non-physical facts; (3) whether phenomenal properties are metaphysically identical to physical properties but accessed only via demonstrative phenomenal concepts; (4) whether the intuition survives a modified case in which Mary receives direct cortical stimulation that replicates the exact neural correlates of red-experience without retinal input and without any accompanying subjective color experience. Each leaf should name a position—e.g., ability hypothesis, phenomenal-concept physicalism, acquaintance physicalism, property dualism, or 'unnamed'—and include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Phrase every internal node as a question a student can answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate. Build the tree from the logical space of answers upward rather than from a list of named views, noting any unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnosing why they might be empty.
