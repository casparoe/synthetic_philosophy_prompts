Next week I am recording a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently developed a position she calls correlate-target epiphenomenalism. She grants the classic paradox of phenomenal judgment—if phenomenal properties are causally inert, they cannot cause the judgments we make about them—yet she denies that this undermines our knowledge of consciousness. Her solution is to claim that our phenomenal judgments are never actually about phenomenal properties themselves; they are about causally active phenomenal correlates, specifically stable neural-functional patterns that systematically co-occur with phenomenal states. On her view, the feeling of direct acquaintance with raw qualia is an illusion generated by the brain's inability to represent its own tracking mechanisms. She is very good at deflecting objections by saying they conflate the correlates with the phenomenal properties those correlates merely track.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question pressing the distinction between her position and standard physicalism about consciousness; at least one on whether her account can accommodate genuine introspective error about phenomenal states; and at least one on whether the alleged illusion of direct acquaintance undermines her own epistemic access to the correlates she posits. For each question, provide a brief follow-up keyed to her most likely reply—especially the reply that the objection confuses correlates with phenomenal properties. End with one question asking what empirical result, if discovered, could actually compel her to abandon the correlate/phenomenal-property distinction altogether.
