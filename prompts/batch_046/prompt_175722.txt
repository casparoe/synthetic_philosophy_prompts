I've been thinking about belief and risk with two friends—one who works in engineering risk assessment and another who reads philosophy blogs—after we argued about whether it's rational to say "I think there's a 30-to-60 percent chance of heavy rain" instead of committing to one number. I'm not in academia; I'm just trying to understand how people should hold beliefs when they genuinely don't know something, and we kept talking past each other. So I want to see what a real adversarial collaboration between the two camps—the "sharp credence" camp, which insists that rational belief must always be pinned to a single precise probability, and the "imprecise credence" camp, which allows belief to be represented by a convex set of probabilities—would actually produce if they did the work honestly.

Please draft their joint statement. Start with a numbered list of claims both sides accept: about the distinction between risk (known probabilities) and ambiguity (unknown or disputed probabilities); about what the classic two-color urn experiment—the one with the urn containing red and black balls in unknown proportion—actually demonstrates without interpretive gloss; about whether learning more information always eliminates imprecision or whether some ambiguity is structural; and about what any decision rule must do to avoid leaving an agent permanently paralyzed. Then present a disagreements table: the precise formulations of where they still differ, written in neutral language that each side would accept for its own view—specifically on whether ambiguity aversion is a rational response to missing information or a cognitive bias to be corrected; on whether the widening of probability intervals upon receiving new evidence (dilation) is a protective feature of honest belief or a destructive bug that undermines learning; and on whether a convex set of probabilities can genuinely ground a single definite choice or merely postpones commitment indefinitely. Then identify the cruxes—the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one party. I see two likely cruxes: first, whether every imprecise belief hides a precise belief that the agent is merely ignorant of; second, whether decision rules like maximin (choose the option with the best worst-case outcome) or selecting options that are optimal for at least one probability within the set actually resolve ambiguity or just rename indecision.

For each crux, include a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant. Use the scenario I sketched: a municipal planner must choose a flood-wall height today when climate models agree on a range of catastrophic-flood probabilities between 5 and 35 percent but disagree on the internal structure of that range—some models cluster risk near the bottom, others near the top—with no further data coming before the decision deadline and with costs that scale nonlinearly with wall height. Both camps should agree this case bears on the cruxes. Then record, in advance and separately for each camp, what they predict about it: for instance, whether the sharp-credence side expects the planner to aggregate expert opinions into a single precise probability and choose the corresponding height, and whether the imprecise-credence side expects the planner to maintain the interval, apply a robustness criterion, and select a middle height—along with what each side would take as evidence that their prediction was wrong. Please keep everything conceptual and self-contained: no requests for citations, bibliographies, or specific named papers, since I want this readable without library access, and avoid framing the task as deciding who is correct—just map the shared ground and the exact remaining fault lines.
