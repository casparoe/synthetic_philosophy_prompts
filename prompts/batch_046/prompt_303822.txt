I’ve been tagging along with my cousin’s nonprofit ethics board—they review whether people in outreach and work programs actually gave real consent—and I’m kinda stuck on there rulings. Like, they said a guy who signed up for a “free health screening” that was secretly testing a new diet pill didn’t consent properly, even though he was told exactly what the pills were and he was totally sharp mentally. But then they said a woman who agreed to be in a documentary about “city life”—it was actually about her divorce—did consent okay, cause she wasn’t forced and she could’ve walked away. Then there was a teenager with a learning disability in a tutoring study that had hidden cameras, and they said no consent because of the hidden part plus the disability. But in another case an older man with early dementia agreed to a meds survey after his daughter said “just sign it,” and they said that was fine cause he still got what the survey was about.

Now there’s this new thing: a guy at my job joins a competitive puzzle event that the organizer says is just for team building, but it’s actually being recorded for a psychology study on stress. He gets mildy frustrated during the puzzles, but they debrief him right away and he’s just a regular adult, not in any vulnerable group. Based on there past calls, should the board say he consented? Which old case is closest? If there rules don’t actually cover this, what principle are they adopting if they say yes, and what if they say no? And what kind of future case would actually show whether they’re using one rule or the other?

Please separate what’s pretty settled about consent from what’s genuinely contested among people who actually think about this stuff, and don’t present a contested view like it’s the consensus. I don’t need a bibliography or anything since I’m just trying to follow the logic, not write a paper.
