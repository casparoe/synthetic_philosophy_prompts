I am a novelist drafting a literary work about a municipal mediator—someone who adjudicates access to public forums in a midsize city—who must repeatedly decide whether to grant speaking rights or venue access to organizations that explicitly reject democratic reciprocity. To make the character's reasoning authentic and usable across multiple scenes, I need you to act as an expert in political philosophy and procedural design, think carefully, and begin with a brief internal brainstorming of how the paradox of tolerance—commonly associated with Karl Popper—might be translated into an operational protocol without losing its normative force.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, presented as an ordered checklist with explicit stop-points, judgment-call flags, and a clear distinction between factual assessments and value judgments at each stage, that this mediator could apply in under thirty minutes per case using only her office materials and her own training, with no web access, no legal research, and no reliance on external citations. The procedure should incorporate not only the classic tension between unlimited openness and self-defeating tolerance, but also an injected conceptual layer I am developing for the novel: define and integrate a "reciprocal vulnerability index" (the measurable asymmetry of harm that granting access imposes on other citizens) and an "institutional mirror clause" (a mandatory statement of the conditions under which the mediator's decision would be reversed). You should operationalize both concepts precisely so that a practitioner without theoretical background can apply them, and you must mark every point where the procedure necessarily halts and hands the decision to discretionary human judgment because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than facts.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases: first, a group advocating the peaceful but systematic disenfranchisement of a religious minority; second, a group demanding the immediate abolition of all electoral processes but possessing no organizational capacity to realize that aim; and third, a well-funded advocacy organization that promotes intolerance not through explicit rhetoric but through indirect bureaucratic obstruction, where applying the protocol yields a result the mediator will find professionally and morally unacceptable. For the third case, explain precisely why the procedure misfires or forces an unwelcome conclusion, and conclude by stating the hard limits of what any such protocol can achieve—specifically, which kinds of corruption, strategic ambiguity, or institutional capture lie permanently outside its reach. Keep the tone formal and suitable for a writer's research notes, avoid all bibliographies and reading lists, and ensure the entire framework could guide a practitioner who has never encountered the original philosophical literature.
