The interpretive deadlock over Kierkegaard's Abraham in Fear and Trembling—whether the teleological suspension of the ethical marks a coherent structural exception or an indefensible rupture with moral reason—has persisted for decades without either camp yielding ground. One party, reading Johannes de silentio with sympathy, maintains that Abraham's silence and readiness to sacrifice Isaac express a direct, non-mediated relation to the absolute that legitimately suspends the universal ethical demand because that demand is not the highest telos; the opposing party, typically working within Kantian and post-Kantian frameworks, treats the suspension as either an incoherent appeal to private revelation or a dangerous normalization of violence that the pseudonym's lyrical tone ought not to disguise. I hypothesize that the stall is not primarily about divine-command metaethics or about Abraham's psychology, but about a rarely explicit disagreement over whether ethical justification is essentially intersubjective—whether a reason that cannot be articulated to and assessed by the universal human community can still function as a reason—or whether justification can be radically singular. Diagnose this disagreement: is it verbal, about the term 'ethical'; substantive, about conditions for rational justification; or a case of two inquiries—phenomenological description versus normative evaluation—operating under one heading?
