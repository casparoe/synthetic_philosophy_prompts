I retired several years ago from a career in experimental physics and have since begun attending a philosophy discussion group at a local university's continuing-education program. At our most recent meeting, two other members—Marcus, whose background is in economics, and Elena, a retired clinical psychologist—became locked in a disagreement concerning demandingness objections to consequentialism. They have asked me, as a neutral observer, to adjudicate their exchange.

The argument proceeded in six stages. First, Marcus claimed that the demandingness objection rests on a category mistake: it treats moral requirements as though they were physical burdens that deplete a finite reservoir of energy or willpower. Moral reasons, he maintained, are closer to logical constraints—like the rules of arithmetic—than to forces that tire an agent out. Therefore, to say that consequentialism demands too much is analogous to complaining that calculus demands too much computation; the difficulty is practical, not a theoretical defect.

Second, Elena replied that this interpretation misses the actual structure of the objection. The problem is not energetic depletion but the erosion of the boundary between one's own life and the lives of strangers. She proposed an analogy drawn from scientific practice: just as a local instrument calibration cannot be discarded merely because a global model exists—both scales being necessary for sound measurement—so an agent's own projects function as indispensable local parameters that a purely global moral calculus improperly suppresses.

Third, Marcus countered that Elena was smuggling in agent-relative permissions without independent justification. If partiality toward one's own projects is defended only as a preference, then it is no more principled than a researcher insisting on her own data set solely because it belongs to her—a bias rather than a methodological virtue.

Fourth, Elena introduced what she termed a narrative continuity constraint. She argued that moral agency requires that a person be able to regard her life as a coherent narrative extending through time, sustained by projects she sees as genuinely her own. Consequentialism's demands are structurally disruptive not because they are tiring, but because they permanently subordinate those projects to an external optimization function. The demandingness is therefore architectural, not metabolic.

Fifth, Marcus responded that narrative continuity is a psychological description, not a normative justification. A life devoted entirely to global welfare—he mentioned the case of a dedicated humanitarian worker—can possess just as much narrative coherence as one centered on personal projects. If coherence is the only criterion, then the global optimizer's life is equally continuous, and Elena's point collapses.

Sixth, Elena distinguished between a constraint that limits what one may do and one that determines what one must do. She suggested that agent-relative permissions operate like the former: they establish boundaries within which an agent's own values govern choice, without dictating every decision from outside. Marcus, she said, treats all partiality as arbitrary preference, but if partiality is instead a condition for having a practical standpoint at all, then consequentialism does not merely limit options—it dissolves the agent's framework for acting.

They have asked me to go through these six moves point by point, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, identifying where each speaker misses something important, and saying who is more right overall. In particular, I want to know whether either of them has addressed whether demandingness can be assessed independently of an agent's prior commitments, and whether the scientific analogies they invoke actually carry the philosophical weight each assigns to them. Please adjudicate their exchange accordingly.
