I sit on our county charter review board. We're voting next week on whether to recommend a "future-binding clause" for the new charter: if voters approve the green-infrastructure framework now, future councils can only amend it with a 3/4 vote plus a finding by an independent panel that the change responds to genuinely changed conditions rather than just political convenience. Its meant to be a Ulysses-style precommitment — locking in long-term investment against short-term cycles. Half the board thinks this is legitimate democratic self-binding, like constitutional entrenchment; the other half says it violates intergenerational equality by treating future citizens as non-autonomous, especially since the panel is appointed by the current council. There's also a weird auto-sunset: the clause dissolves if regional rainfall drops below the 30-year baseline for five straight years, which supporters say saves it from tyranny but critics say turns it into a contingent policy with extra steps. Draft the memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the board can actually act on, and treat the strongest argument for the side you reject seriously. Also tell me what argument or empirical case about precommitment failure would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
