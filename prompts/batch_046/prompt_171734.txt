In a graduate seminar on the ontology of social institutions, a participant kept returning to the one-liner: 'Institutions are just patterns of individual behavior—nothing more, nothing less.' She used it first to block talk of institutional 'intentionality,' then to reject a claim that regulatory frameworks have downward causal power over individual choice, and finally to argue that macro-historical explanations are necessarily incomplete. I suspect the sentence is doing very different work in each case. Could you lay out the precise claims it could be expressing—distinguishing ontological reduction, explanatory reduction, supervenience without causal autonomy, and a normative preference for micro-foundations? For each reading, indicate whether it's trivial, defensible but substantive, or actually false. Then say which reading is required to support each of her three moves (blocking intentionality, denying downward causation, demanding micro-foundations), and whether any consistent version of methodological individualism actually permits the aggregate patterns she dismisses to be both real and causally relevant. I want the readings presented as a list I can use to press her on which version she holds, not a single interpretation with a defense.
