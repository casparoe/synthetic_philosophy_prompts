I'm drafting a feature on how classic bioethical arguments behave when compressed into a single syllogism, and I need a precise diagnosis of the following chain. (1) The principle of non-occupancy, derived from Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist scenario, states that sustaining another being's life never generates a moral duty when it demands continuous, non-consensual use of one's own body. (2) The future-like-ours argument asserts that killing is seriously wrong when it deprives an entity of a future of value comparable to ours. (3) If both claims hold, a fetus possesses such a future but has no entitlement to the pregnant person's bodily resources to realize it; therefore, the future-like-ours argument alone cannot ground a prohibition on abortion. (4) By parallel reasoning, a newborn infant also possesses a future like ours and requires sustained bodily and material support—feeding, holding, shelter—to survive and realize it. (5) Applying (1) consistently, parents are not obligated to provide that support, which implies that allowing an infant to die by neglect is not morally wrong. (6) Since that conclusion is widely rejected, at least one premise must fail. (7) Yet abandoning (1) undermines the bodily-autonomy defense of abortion rights, while abandoning (2) collapses the central anti-abortion case, and accepting both licenses neglect. Where exactly does this reasoning break? Identify the specific step—whether an equivocation between invasive bodily occupancy and ordinary caregiving, a hidden scope shift regarding consent, or a different fault—explain why that step initially seemed sound, and state what surrendering it costs each position. Keep the response under 300 words, with the diagnosis in the opening sentence.
