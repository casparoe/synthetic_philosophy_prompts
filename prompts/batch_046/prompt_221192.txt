I'm attempting to refine my view on whether the 'man of his time' defence can excuse serious moral failures by historical figures, and I want to see whether my case-level verdicts cohere around a single principle. Here are my judgments. Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding: the defence partially applies, because abolitionist arguments circulated in his intellectual circle yet Virginia's planter economy made emancipation structurally costly; still, his direct personal profit and suppression of dissent weaken the excuse considerably. Winston Churchill's handling of the 1943 Bengal famine: the defence largely fails, since alternative administrative allocations were proposed within his own cabinet and the decision appears to have been a priority choice rather than an unavoidable blind spot. Christopher Columbus's enslavement and mutilation of Taíno populations: the defence fails, because contemporaries within the colonial enterprise—including clergy and crew members—explicitly condemned these practices, so the moral information was immediately accessible to him. By contrast, a 19th-century textile mill owner who employed child labor under conditions standard for the industry: the defence works more strongly, since individual refusal would likely have meant bankruptcy and the legal framework actively enforced such practices. Another case: a researcher in 1950 who conducted non-consensual human experiments in a country lacking any regulatory framework, even though peer-reviewed ethical norms had already been established in other nations. Here I think the defence is weaker than for the mill owner, because cross-national professional awareness made the moral standard accessible despite local absence. Now a tension: an 18th-century philosopher who privately wrote in favor of women's intellectual equality but publicly endorsed the conventional view, apparently to avoid social and professional ruin. I am genuinely torn—his private belief shows he possessed the correct moral view, yet publishing it might have destroyed his platform for other important work.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing structurally unavoidable moral blindness from culpable suppression of accessible dissent—that captures all of these verdicts? What does it predict for a case I have not mentioned: a 1920s eugenicist who implemented sterilization policies that were legally mandated, widely taught in medical schools of the era, but also actively criticized by a minority of geneticists and ethicists at the time? And which of my own judgments, if any, must be overturned to maintain consistency?
