After a lengthy and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about whether the gaps in colonial administrative archives ought to be treated as mere omissions or as active traces of power, I have realised that we were speaking across one another because neither of us could articulate the full logical space of positions. I should like you to construct a taxonomy — specifically a branching decision tree — that classifies the views one might actually hold on the ethical and epistemic status of archival silence within institutional records. The instrument should be built from the diagnostic questions that genuinely separate people, rather than from a list of well-known names.

Please take as your root node the question of whether an archival silence functions primarily as an epistemic gap (missing information that could, in principle, be recovered) or as a form of political testimony (an absence that itself records the operation of exclusion). From there, branch on whether the archivist’s primary obligation is fidelity to the record exactly as it survives, or whether there is an active duty to annotate, frame, or otherwise mark the absence. A further branch should ask whether reparative cataloguing — explicitly documenting what is missing, whose lives are unrecorded, and by what mechanism the record failed — counts as legitimate scholarly practice or as an unacceptable imposition of present moral frameworks onto past materials. You should also include a branch addressing what I shall term indexical absence: the presence within the archive of deliberately blank forms, redacted files with intact metadata, or struck-through ledgers, and whether these should be read as positive evidence of suppression rather than as ordinary silence.

At each leaf, provide either a conventional label for the position (for example, preservationist minimalism, reconstructive archivalism, or forensic silence studies) or mark it as unnamed, together with one sentence describing its most awkward or counter-intuitive commitment. Where the tree permits combinations of answers that appear to be unoccupied, note them and briefly diagnose the emptiness — whether it stems from incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or simple neglect in the literature. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and explain why the distinction collapses. The intended audience is a postgraduate seminar in archive studies; the tone should therefore be precise and analytical, but need not be introductory. Please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the exercise should be answerable from first principles and general conceptual knowledge alone.
