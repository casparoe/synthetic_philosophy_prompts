My partner and I are trying to decide whether to have a child, and we've approached it with the usual rationalist toolkit: longitudinal satisfaction data, opportunity-cost modeling, and explicit preference weights. But I'm stuck on what seems like a structural feature of the choice itself. Many people report that after becoming a parent, not just their emotions but their fundamental evaluative framework shifts—what counts as flourishing, which trade-offs seem reasonable, and which relationships matter most can all be replaced rather than merely updated. My partner treats this as an empirical forecasting problem: assign probabilities to various post-parental value-states, weight them by our current utility function, and maximize expected value. I worry there's a deeper obstacle. If the post-parental self operates with values that are incommensurable with mine now, then aggregating welfare across the transition assumes a continuity of the evaluating subject that may not exist. Is this a genuine philosophical barrier to rational choice—something that breaks standard decision theory—or can it be accommodated with dynamic consistency conditions, preference-coherence constraints, or some other formal apparatus? What's the right analysis?
