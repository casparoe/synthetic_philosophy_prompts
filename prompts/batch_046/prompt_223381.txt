A friend and I just finished arguing about whether eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires proves too much. Here is the chain he pressed on me, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) Eliminative materialism denies that there are belief-boxes or desire-boxes with propositional content; mental life is just patterns of neural activation.

(2) But maintaining a sustained theoretical position—say, being an eliminative materialist across decades—requires diachronic cognitive integration: tracking consistency between earlier and later commitments, revising sub-claims, and preserving a stable representational core.

(3) Neuroscience describes only transient, context-sensitive prediction-error minimization; there is no neural mechanism that functions as a persistent theory-file integrating representations across long timescales.

(4) So if eliminativism is true, there are no sustained theoretical commitments—only momentary activation profiles that happen to correlate with verbal behavior.

(5) Yet neuroscience itself is a sustained theoretical commitment spanning centuries, requiring scientists to maintain stable representations of models, anomalies, and revisions.

(6) Therefore, if eliminativism is true, neuroscience cannot exist as a sustained science, and the empirical evidence for eliminativism collapses.

(7) Hence eliminativism is self-undermining and must be rejected.

I want to keep (3) and (5) if I can, since both seem descriptively accurate, but I feel something slipped between (4) and (6). Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do the standard responses—instrumentalist reconstruals of theory-commitment, appeals to vector-space similarity across time, or pattern-realist accounts of sustained cognition—have to pay to reject it? In particular, does giving up (2) force us back into folk-psychological belief-boxes, or is there a middle path?
