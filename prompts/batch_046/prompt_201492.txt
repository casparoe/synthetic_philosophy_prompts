Write a poem of twenty-four lines, arranged in three stanzas of eight lines each, with a consistent rhyme scheme of ABABCDCD in every stanza so that the sonic architecture remains identical even as the grammatical subject transforms. The philosophical concept is the scale argument in debates about insect sentience: the claim that even a very low probability that any given insect is conscious, multiplied across vast numbers, generates a massive aggregate moral expectation, especially given the precautionary asymmetry that falsely denying sentience risks far greater harm than falsely attributing it. Make the poem do this work structurally. In the first stanza, address a single hypothetical insect in the second person singular ("you"), using precise anatomical or behavioral detail—an antenna’s twitch, a compound eye’s facets—that suggests but does not confirm inner experience; let the rhyme scheme hold the lines together as if holding one life in focus. In the second stanza, shift to the third person plural ("they"), describing a local swarm or population with statistical and ecological language—density, mortality curves, predation rates—so that the same rhymes now bind a crowd rather than an individual. In the third stanza, eliminate all pronouns entirely: write only in quantitative, taxonomic, and ecological abstractions, so that the rhyme scheme still sounds but no "who" remains. The transition between stanzas should feel like the very move the argument makes, from the opacity of one mind to the transparency of aggregate harm, but the poem should not resolve whether that move is a moral revelation or an erasure. Leave open the tension between the precautionary impulse—it costs little to act as if they matter—and the epistemic humility that makes each individual case opaque. End on an image that belongs to all three registers—something like a wing, a census figure, a silence—without explaining which register owns it. The verse should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the scale argument recognizes its moves, but it should read as poetry to someone who is not.
