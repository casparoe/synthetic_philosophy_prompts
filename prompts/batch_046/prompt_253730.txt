Hey, I'm in this local history group in our town and we've been stuck in the same loop for like six months about whether to rename Whitmore Avenue. Named after some 19th-century mill guy who built a schoolhouse but also broke strikes with privet guards. What's driving me nuts isn't the politics—it's that both sides keep acting like they know exactly what a street name is *for*, and then they switch it up the second that version gets attacked. The people pushing rename say the name works like a medal: every envelope addressed there is an honor, so keeping it means we're praising the strikebreaking. Then the defenders say no, it's not a medal, it's a record—like a page in a textbook—so removing it is just erasing history.

But here's the thing, neither group behaves like they actually believe those descriptions. If it were really just a medal, they'd care about the cost of changing maps and emergency servces, but they don't mention that. And if it were really just a record, they'd be fine with replacing the honorific name with a neutral explanitory plaque that lists his crimes alongside the school. Instead they want to keep the name exactly as is. I see the same pattern with statues—people say it's there to teach history right up until you suggest putting an explanitory plaque that actually teaches the bad parts, then suddenly it's about heritage and not judging the past by today's standards. Is there a proper philosophical term for this kind of move, where people defend a commemorative object by assigning it one social function, then retreat to another function when the first one is undermined? If there's no name, what's the best way to describe the boundary between this and just saying monuments have multiple meanings? I want to understand if I'm seeing a real failure mode or just being pedentic.
