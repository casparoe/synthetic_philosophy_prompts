I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science and social epistemology, and my advisor has been developing what she terms a reconstructed “normative calculus of planning” implicit in Carnap’s scattered writings on value-neutrality, scientific explication, and democratic administration. Rather than treating Carnap as offering a systematic political theory, she reads his remarks on the separation of factual prediction from normative decision, on the public articulation of value premises in scientific planning, and on the dangers of embedding evaluative commitments inside technical architectures, as generating a consistent but unstated decision procedure for evaluating large-scale planning institutions. She has applied this reconstructed framework to a series of historical and hypothetical cases, and I am trying to determine whether she is running a single rule or whether her verdicts are genuinely overdetermined.

Her judgments so far are these. She endorsed the Tennessee Valley Authority’s regional development model on the grounds that it maintained an explicit separation between hydrological forecasting and democratically negotiated welfare objectives, even though the distributional weights were left underspecified in the technical reports. She rejected the Soviet material-balance planning of the 1930s because predictive economic modeling was collapsed into normative labor-allocation targets, violating the requirement that value premises be independently articulated. She conditionally approved the postwar British town-and-country planning acts, which used statistical demographic forecasting but required parliamentary ratification of the welfare weights before implementation. She rejected Stafford Beer’s Chilean Cybersyn proposal not because of its cybernetic automation per se, but because the feedback loops embedded normative premises about resource distribution inside the technical control architecture, rendering them inaccessible to democratic revision. Finally, she endorsed a contemporary Dutch flood-prediction system that employs machine learning for hydraulic forecasting and publishes its loss-function weights as explicit explications open to expert and public scrutiny, while leaving the actual gate-allocation decisions to human engineers rather than automating them.

The new case is a proposed municipal predictive-equity platform. The system uses machine learning to forecast neighborhood-level health and educational outcomes over two decades and then automatically reallocates infrastructure budgets according to an optimization target that maximizes aggregate welfare; the value weights are technically present in a published appendix, but they were set by an unelected expert panel without democratic ratification, and citizens are given only an ex-post dashboard of results rather than any ex-ante mechanism to revise the parameters or intervene in the automatic allocations. Predict my advisor’s verdict on this proposal, and identify the two or three competing interpretive rules—perhaps centered on explicit value-explication, democratic ratification, or architectural non-embedding—that fit her track record equally well, stating precisely what each predicts here. Which further hypothetical case—one that varies exactly on the feature that divides these rules—would best distinguish them? I am especially interested in where your reasoning is most vulnerable: what specific consideration about Carnap’s actual treatment of planning, or about the analogy between scientific explication and democratic governance, would cause you to revise the predicted verdict? And if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, what normative principle is she implicitly adopting with each possible choice?
