You are grading as the course instructor or designated exam reader. Do not indicate your own philosophical commitments; assess the answer strictly on its internal coherence, fidelity to the assigned concepts, and logical rigor.

Question: Explain Jean-Paul Sartre’s thesis that human reality is condemned to be free. Show how this thesis grounds his concept of bad faith (mauvaise foi), and carefully distinguish its two principal structures: treating one’s transcendence as if it were pure facticity, and treating one’s facticity as if it were pure transcendence. Then apply both structures to the following case. A senior university administrator approves deep budget cuts that eliminate staff positions in her own department. To her board she insists she had no choice, citing institutional hierarchy, fiduciary obligations, and her family’s financial dependence on her salary. To her peers, however, she describes the same decision as an act of courageous autonomous leadership—something she freely chose for the long-term good of the institution and for which she alone should receive moral credit. Analyze whether Sartre’s framework allows her to invoke her institutional role as a legitimate excuse, and discuss the function of anguish (angoisse) in recognizing the freedom she attempts to deny.

Student answer: Sartre’s central claim is that human beings are condemned to be free. Unlike objects, which have a predetermined essence or purpose, human reality lacks any fixed nature. We are thrown into existence and must continuously choose ourselves through our actions; there is no divine plan or biological destiny that determines what we become. This freedom is not a pleasant privilege but an inescapable burden, producing anguish—the direct awareness that we bear total responsibility for our choices and for the world we thereby create.

Bad faith is the flight from this burden. It is not ordinary lying but a specific structure of self-deception in which consciousness hides its own freedom from itself. Sartre identifies two principal forms. The first denies transcendence by reducing oneself to pure facticity: one identifies completely with a social role, past decision, or material condition, treating oneself as an object with a fixed essence. The café waiter who performs his duties with excessive precision, as though being a waiter were his destiny rather than a role he sustains, illustrates this form. The second denies facticity by treating oneself as pure transcendence: one pretends to be an unconditioned will, ignoring one’s body, history, and concrete situation.

In the administrator’s case, both forms operate. First, she denies her transcendence when she tells the board she had no choice. She treats her institutional hierarchy, fiduciary duty, and economic dependence as deterministic forces that eliminated her agency, reducing herself to a mere functionary—pure facticity. Second, when she presents the decision to her peers as courageous autonomous leadership, she denies her facticity. She describes herself as an unconditioned moral agent who acted solely from conviction, as if the organizational pressures, her salary, and her career investment played no part in shaping the choice. By claiming full authorship while ignoring these conditions, she treats herself as pure transcendence.

Yet Sartre’s position contains an important consistency. Because freedom necessarily transcends every concrete situation, any appeal to one’s circumstances is automatically an evasion of responsibility. The administrator therefore cannot legitimately invoke her institutional role: for Sartre, being embedded in a structure never removes the freedom to reject or reinterpret it. Moreover, bad faith is ultimately unstable. Once consciousness experiences anguish—the irreversible recognition of total freedom—it becomes transparent to itself. Consequently, the administrator cannot consistently maintain that she is unfree in one context and fully autonomous in another. The two stances are logically incompatible, and Sartre holds that authenticity requires embracing freedom without reservation rather than oscillating between denial and assertion.
