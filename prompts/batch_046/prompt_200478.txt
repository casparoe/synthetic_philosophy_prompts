Consider the institutional practice, embodied in the war-guilt clause of the Treaty of Versailles and debated in the Fischer controversy, of treating a sovereign state as a single moral agent responsible for initiating a world war. The clause assigned collective guilt to Germany and imposed reparations on the state as a continuous legal entity, despite the fact that the decision for war was made by a small executive elite, many citizens actively opposed mobilization, and the post-1918 regime bore little institutional continuity with the Kaiserreich that had authorized it. Meanwhile, international law continues to recognize states—not individuals or regimes—as the primary bearers of war-related obligations.

What view of collective intentionality and moral agency does this practice presuppose? Must a nation possess a unified will that transcends its government and citizens, or can the assignment of guilt be defended instrumentally, treating the state merely as a durable vessel for liability? Is the practice philosophically consistent with the criminal-law principle that punishment should not extend to those who did not participate in the wrong, given that reparations and territorial sanctions fell on populations with no role in the decision? And if the underlying theory were replaced by one that recognized only distributed, individual-level culpability—blame attaching solely to specific officeholders and their direct accomplices—what exactly would have to change? Would the concept of a war-guilt clause in a treaty become incoherent, or could it survive as a purely fiscal mechanism detached from moral ascription?
