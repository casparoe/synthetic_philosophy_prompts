I'm drafting a chapter for my diss on hard choices and incommensurability, and my advisor threw this argument at me in our last meeting that I now can't shake. It feels like it proves that either pluralism is false or rational agency is impossible, and I don't buy either horn. Here's the chain she walked me through:

(1) If goods A and B are genuinely incommensurable, then no comparative judgment—better, worse, or equal—holds between them. (2) So if there's no comparative judgment, there's literally no reason that counts in favor of A over B, or vice versa. (3) A choise with literally zero reasons in its favor is arbitrary, not rational. (4) Therefore, if incommensurability is real, every hard choice is just a coin flip we dress up with post-hoc stories about 'who we are.' (5) But we clearly don't treat hard choices—like picking between a career in philosophy or taking the policy job that actually helps people, or deciding whether to have a child—as arbitrary coin flips. We think people can be mistaken, that practical identitiy is at stake, and that we owe explanations. (6) So incommensurability must be an illusion in any domain where our practical identity is engaged. (7) But that means all values are eventually commensurable on some hidden scale, so there's always a uniquely correct answer to every life choice. (8) Which makes regret always irrational: you picked the best option, full stop, so mourning the path not taken is just cognitive error. (9) Yet regret after hard choices seems not just rational but almost morally required—it's how we honor the value we sacrificed.

So I have to reject something. I really want to keep (1) and (5) if possible, because dropping (1) feels like collapsing value pluralism into some creepy monism, and dropping (5) feels like saying our lives don't matter to us. Is the slip between (2) and (3)—maybe there are non-comparative reasons that don't rank options but still guide choice? Or is there a hidden modal slide between (6) and (7)? Also, I've been toying with this idea I call 'normative leakage,' where commitments to one incommensurable path don't erase the reasons for the other but let them bleed into our retrospective evaluation... does fixing the argument require me to buy into that, or can I save pluralism without it? Where's the weakest link, and what does repairing it cost?
