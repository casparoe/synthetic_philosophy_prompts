You are a philosopher specializing in social epistemology, philosophy of medicine, and conceptual analysis, with particular attention to how institutional practices shape knowledge attribution. Before you adjudicate, please take a few moments to map the conceptual terrain: distinguish testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, and any intermediary or hybrid forms you think relevant, and note how linguistic mediation in clinical settings might complicate these categories. I work as a clinical ethics coordinator at a community health center serving a largely immigrant population, and last week I observed a case conference that turned into a sharp disagreement between two colleagues, Dr. Aris Thorne (a bioethicist) and Dr. Bina Patel (a medical anthropologist). They were discussing an encounter with a patient I will call Maria, a 58-year-old woman with limited English proficiency who came in describing what she called a burning that climbs up my back like hot wire and a heaviness that makes me forget where my feet are. Her adult son, who often accompanies her, translated these phrases for the attending physician. The physician, noting the son's use of terms like radiating neuropathic pain and vertigo with dissociative features, directed his follow-up questions almost exclusively to the son, eventually documenting the son's biomedical framing as the primary subjective report. Maria was present but was not asked to confirm or elaborate.

Thorne argued that this is a clear instance of testimonial injustice: Maria's epistemic authority as the subject of her own symptoms was systematically deflated, and credibility excess was transferred to her son because of an implicit prejudice that non-English-speaking patients are unreliable narrators of their own bodies. He acknowledged that language barriers complicate the picture, but insisted that the physician's choice to treat the mediator as the epistemic proxy rather than using a professional interpreter revealed an identity-based credibility deficit.

Patel pushed back. She agreed that harm occurred, but denied that testimonial injustice is the right diagnosis. She introduced a distinction she called hermeneutical extraction: the problem was not that Maria was disbelieved, but that her distinctive experiential concepts were stripped away and replaced by the son's biomedical vocabulary before they could enter the clinical record. The harm, Patel said, is conceptual dispossession, not credibility deflation. Maria was not rated as less credible; she was made epistemically invisible by having her own conceptual framework erased. Patel further argued that if we classify every interpreter-mediated encounter where a mediator's framing dominates as testimonial injustice, we collapse an important distinction and overextend a concept originally developed with a specific focus on identity-prejudicial credibility deficits.

Thorne responded that Patel's distinction is artificial. In practice, conceptual erasure and credibility deflation are inseparable: by accepting the son's vocabulary as the authoritative report, the physician implicitly judged Maria's own descriptions as inadequate or untrustworthy. He added that Patel's hermeneutical extraction is just hermeneutical injustice misapplied—Maria was not lacking the concepts; she had them and they were taken from her.

Patel's counter was that hermeneutical injustice, as usually understood, involves a gap in collective interpretive resources that prevents a group from understanding its own experience. Here, the resources existed—Maria had her own rich descriptive language—but they were actively suppressed in favor of another framework. That suppression, she said, is not hermeneutical injustice but something closer to epistemic appropriation mediated through translation.

They asked me to referee, and I genuinely cannot tell where the line should be drawn.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move. For each of Thorne's and Patel's main arguments, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then address whether Thorne's charge that Patel's distinction collapses in practice is fair. Next, explain whether Patel is correct that this case falls outside standard hermeneutical injustice. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing, especially regarding the role of institutional translation protocols in structuring these harms.
