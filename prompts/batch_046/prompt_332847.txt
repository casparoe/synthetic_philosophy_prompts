The class-struggle endogenous model: the bourgeoisie, having mobilized the sans-culottes to destroy feudal privilege, necessarily liquidated its radical wing once capitalist property relations were secured; Thermidor completes this class logic, so Robespierre falls because he represents the lingering popular-democratic obstacle to consolidation.

B. The ideological-purification exogenous model: Jacobin ideology contains an inherent logic of suspicion—virtue demands unanimity, and the general will cannot tolerate deviation—making internal purges structurally inevitable; Thermidor is not driven by class interest but by the collapse of a purity spiral accelerated by war and foreign conspiracy, with Robespierre destroyed because he embodied the extreme of that purity.

C. The contingency-and-coalition model: Robespierre’s isolation resulted from a specific, non-repeatable alignment of the Plain, the committees, and personal enemies; the devouring metaphor is misleading because no structural mechanism required his fall—substitute a more pragmatic leader and the Revolution avoids self-cannibalization, so the pattern is not genuinely explanatory.

E. The narrative-performative contradiction model (a synthetic framing): the Revolution defined itself through a continuous present of regeneration that required periodic symbolic purges to maintain narrative coherence; Robespierre becomes the scapegoat precisely because he was the Revolution’s most perfect agent—his fall is the performative closure that allows the narrative to transition from regeneration to consolidation, making the devouring an internal story-requirement rather than a social or political cause.
