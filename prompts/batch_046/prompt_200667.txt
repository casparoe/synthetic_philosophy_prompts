You are a philosopher specializing in moral psychology and character theory. Think carefully, and begin by briefly outlining the core commitments of virtue ethics and the situationist critique so your reasoning is explicit. At a district-level education workshop, two colleagues debated a new policy: the district now awards "grit certificates" to students who complete particularly demanding coursework, but only within classrooms that provide heavy scaffolding—structured peer groups, frequent formative feedback, and flexible deadlines. Colleague A argued the policy presupposes a virtue-ethical framework: persistence is being treated as a stable, praiseworthy character trait that merits recognition regardless of the outcome, and the district is implicitly saying students are responsible for cultivating it. Colleague B argued the opposite—the policy actually presupposes situationism. Because the certificates are only available in environments engineered to produce persistence, the district tacitly admits that grit is not an internal trait but a product of situational design; praising students for "their" grit is therefore conceptually incoherent, like praising someone for being tall in a room with high ceilings. A replied that situationism never denied traits exist, only that they are expressed inconsistently across contexts; providing scaffolding is just sound pedagogy, not an admission that grit vanishes elsewhere. B countered that if the district genuinely believed in trait-based virtue, it would assess persistence in unpredictable, low-support conditions rather than manufacturing it. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: identify which steps are sound, which confuse trait-expression with trait-possession, and say whether the policy is philosophically coherent or whether it quietly relies on one framework while officially endorsing the other.
