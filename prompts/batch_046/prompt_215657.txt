I work as a firmware engineer maintaining remote-update controllers for legacy implantable cardiac devices—roughly forty thousand units currently in the field. We recently discovered that a specific MRI-induced electromagnetic interference pattern can trigger an unintended lethal shock. A remote patch is ready: it actively suppresses all shock delivery whenever that interference signature is detected, which eliminates the false positives entirely. But suppression also means that if a patient genuinely needs defibrillation during that exact interference window, the device will remain silent and the patient may die.

The patch has not yet been deployed. At present the devices behave exactly as originally certified: they deliver necessary life-saving shocks, but they also carry the rare, known risk of a lethal false positive.

My team is divided over whether to push the update. If we deploy, we are actively altering every device's behavior; some patients who need a shock during interference will die because our code is now suppressing delivery. If we do not deploy, we leave the certified behavior untouched; the false-positive risk remains, but we are not the ones changing the device. I keep feeling these choices are morally different—one feels like an action I take that kills, the other like allowing an existing risk to unfold—yet I have no philosophical vocabulary for the distinction. Is my intuition tracking something real about the moral weight of acting versus standing back, or is it only a reaction to whether my own code is in the loop?
