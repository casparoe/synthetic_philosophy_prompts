For my independent study in moral philosophy, I have been working through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, particularly the claim that being truly morally responsible requires being causa sui—having authored one's own mental nature—which is impossible because every agent is fully shaped by heredity, environment, and prior causes outside their control. Rather than requesting a standard summary, I want to understand what would genuinely count against this argument if we apply it only to a deliberately narrowed domain: actions that arise from explicit, conscious deliberation occurring within the final few seconds before execution, with habitual, emotional, and reflexive behaviors excluded by stipulation. What conceptual distinctions, empirical findings about conscious agency, or logical results about self-causation would have to hold for even this restricted version to fail? Please distinguish between objections that Strawson or defenders of the Basic Argument would likely concede as genuinely damaging and those they would dismiss as missing the core point about the impossibility of self-creation. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and include an honest assessment of whether the argument has been qualified—perhaps by redefining "true moral responsibility"—to the point that it is insulated from counterevidence, and whether such insulation constitutes a philosophical weakness rather than a strength. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need only the conceptual analysis of where the argument is actually vulnerable.
