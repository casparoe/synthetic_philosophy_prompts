I hold that the Löbian obstacle defines a normative boundary rather than a mere technical limitation: a rational agent should restrict its logical strength so that it can consistently believe in its own consistency, and attempts to overcome this—whether by adopting a stronger logic or by substituting an empirical inductive argument for consistency—constitute failures of rational humility rather than epistemic progress. To make this precise, I propose a "reflective budget" model: an agent allocates its epistemic confidence across domains such that the portion reserved for self-consistency never exceeds the provability strength of the system expressing that confidence, with any surplus redirected into empirical predictions rather than meta-logical claims. Tell me what would count against this view. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which formal results (say, about self-modifying agents or about the behavior of probabilistic self-trust), which intuitive cases, and which analogies from other domains of bounded rationality would genuinely move a defender? Which of these would the defender concede as damaging, and which would they absorb by refining the budget model? In particular, does the view risk collapsing into either an arbitrary stipulation of logical weakness or into an unfalsifiable claim about "proper humility"? I want an honest assessment of whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
