I am a software engineer with a background in distributed systems and no formal training in philosophy, though I co-host a small podcast that tries to make philosophy of mind accessible to people in technical fields. Recently, while discussing sleep and consciousness with some colleagues after work, one of them asked whether dreams are actually experiences or just stories we tell ourselves upon waking; that conversation convinced me to devote an episode to the classic Malcolm-Dennett disagreement, but I want to go well beyond the standard summaries I have encountered.

Next week I will interview Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher who defends what he calls integrated report theory about dreaming. Chen accepts Malcolm's core claim that there is no independent criterion for dream experience apart from the waking report, but he rejects Dennett's conclusion that dreams are therefore narrative confabulations inserted after the fact. Instead, Chen holds that a dream becomes an actual experience only when it reaches a minimal level of cortical integration during sleep—a binding threshold—while sub-threshold neural activity produces only potential phenomenal structure that is articulated, not invented, upon waking. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having him restate this framework. I specifically need at least one question addressing how his binding-threshold account handles lucid dreaming, in which the subject seems to possess second-order awareness during the dream rather than constructing it retroactively; one addressing how he distinguishes his real-time integration view from Dennett's model of post-hoc narrative insertion without merely asserting that integration occurs in sleep; and one pressing him on whether infants or patients with severely degraded working-memory architecture can ever cross his binding threshold, and if not, whether he must deny that they genuinely dream despite behavioral and neurophysiological indicators. For each of these three, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give—especially anticipating his probable appeal to real-time neurofeedback studies and his distinction between potential phenomenal structure and actualized experience.
