Construct a paired thought experiment to isolate whether Berkeley's master argument fails because of the nature of the object conceived or because of the nature of the conceiving act itself. Scenario A: a physicist conceives of an unobserved radioactive decay in a sealed, permanently unmonitored chamber, treating it as a real disposition that has manifested independently of observation. Scenario B: the same physicist conceives of an unrendered virtual artifact in a suspended simulation with no active server and no observer—an entity whose properties exist only in dormant code, not in any rendered world. Constraints: both entities must be genuinely unperceived; neither scenario may invoke divine or human observers as necessary conditions for existence; the logical form of the master argument—that to conceive X as unperceived one must conceive it as being conceived—must apply with identical structure to both; present the pair in two concise paragraphs. Then demonstrate, for each scenario, the precise belief-content formed, why Berkeley's argument either succeeds or is blocked, and what a dispositional realist opponent concedes or denies. Identify the single structural feature that makes the verdict diverge across the pair, explain how a defender of Berkeley would try to collapse that feature, and how the realist would defend its relevance. Conclude by stating which argument or empirical discovery would most change your view about which feature is actually decisive, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to attack.
