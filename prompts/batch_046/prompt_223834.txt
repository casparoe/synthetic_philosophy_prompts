I am organizing a seminar on metaphysics and literature, and our session on Borges has moved past the familiar observation that he denies linear time in essays such as A New Refutation of Time and dramatizes that denial in stories like The Garden of Forking Paths and The Library of Babel. I want to focus instead on a more precise philosophical vulnerability: if time is genuinely unreal, then any linguistic, narrative, or cognitive act that expresses that denial must apparently unfold through successive moments—before and after, premise and conclusion, memory and anticipation—which seems to presuppose exactly the temporal structure being rejected. With that tension in view, please rank the following five Borgesian devices from the one that best escapes this pragmatic self-refutation to the one that falls into it most clearly: (1) the circular labyrinth, in which every path ultimately returns to its point of origin, collapsing progression into repetition; (2) the mirror that generates an infinite series of reflections with no original image and no final surface; (3) the Library of Babel, conceived as an eternal, unchanging totality in which every possible volume already exists and no new page is ever added; (4) the garden of forking paths, where all possible outcomes of a single decision exist simultaneously without a privileged actual branch; and (5) the explicit argument that the present moment is a mental fiction synthesized from a non-existent past and an unrealized future. For each device, provide a single sentence explaining whether its form or content covertly relies on succession, causation, or directed attention—thereby reintroducing the time it seeks to dissolve—or whether it genuinely manages to present its negation of time without presupposing temporal sequence in its own structure. I am asking for a philosophical ranking of metaphysical coherence, not for literary criticism or an account of Borges's influences.
