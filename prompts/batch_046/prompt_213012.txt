Here are my gut checks on whether breaking a local convention is actually wrong, not just awkward, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

- In the US, leaving no tip for okay service at a sit-down restaurant: I feel that's genuinely wrong, almost like breaking a promise.
- In Japan, leaving a cash tip on the table for a waiter: I think that's wrong too, but in the opposite direction — it's disrespectful.
- Deliberately jumping a visible queue in Britain: clearly wrong. But if you're a visitor and there's no visible line, just people clustered near a bus door, and you board first because you missed the local invissible-queue norm: not really wrong, just ignorant.
- Jaywalking in Berlin at 3am with literally no cars: breaking the rule, but I don't feel it's morally wrong at all.
- Driving on the right in the UK because you forgot: obviously wrong, dangerous.
- Ordering a cappuccino after noon in Rome: locals winse, but I don't think the tourist is doing something morally bad.
- Refusing to take your shoes off entering a friend's home in Thailand where that's expected: that feels wrong, like ignoring their hospitality rules.

So some convention-breaks feel like moral failures (tipping, queue-jumping, wrong-side driving, shoes) and others feel like harmless cultural mismatches (cappuccino, jaywalking). But I can't draw the line without just listing the ones I care about. Is there a principled distinction — harm, tacit consent, visibility of the rule, role-obligation — or am I smuggling in a false split between moral and merely conventional? If I have to drop one verdict to keep the account neat, which one should go? And what does the best principle say about a tourist in a Moroccan souk who pays the first price asked without bargaining — moral miss, or just naive?

Please push back plainly if I'm asking for a coherence that isn't there, or if the task as posed rests on a confused premise.
