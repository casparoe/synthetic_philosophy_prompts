I am drafting a philosophical scene for a novel in which an archivist of abandoned doctrines debates a younger interlocutor about whether rational belief can survive its own scrutiny. To keep the exchange from feeling like a caricature, I have constructed the following argument, which the younger speaker delivers with genuine conviction, yet its conclusion seems intolerable: either all belief is destroyed or all belief is permitted. I want to know precisely where it stumbles, if it does.

(1) Clifford's well-known principle holds that it is always wrong—morally impermissible—to believe anything upon evidence that is not sufficient to justify it. (2) James allows an exception: where an option is genuinely live, forced, and momentous, and where available evidence is genuinely ambiguous, we may be entitled, perhaps even obliged, to adopt belief rather than suspension. (3) Now consider Self-Trust, the foundational conviction that one's own cognitive faculties are generally reliable. Without Self-Trust, no derivative belief, including belief in Clifford's principle itself, can claim justification. (4) For Self-Trust, however, no independent, non-circular evidence is available; any argument for it must already employ the very faculties whose reliability is in question. (5) Therefore, by Clifford's own lights, holding Self-Trust is always wrong, since it rests upon insufficient evidence. (6) But if Self-Trust is wrong, then so is any belief justified through it, including the belief that Clifford's principle is correct, since accepting that principle requires reasoning, which requires Self-Trust. (7) Hence Clifford's principle, applied consistently, undermines the cognitive authority required to apply it at all. (8) One might reply that Self-Trust is not a belief subject to moral evaluation but a practical framework or presupposition, lying outside Clifford's jurisdiction. (9) Yet James's three conditions apply precisely to such framework commitments: the choice between scepticism and self-trust is live, forced, and momentous, since it structures every subsequent inquiry. (10) Thus if Clifford arbitrarily excludes Self-Trust, he restricts his principle by fiat; if he includes it, his principle is self-defeating. (11) The only consistent escape is to concede that belief without sufficient evidence is sometimes permissible. (12) But now apply James's conditions not to religious faith, but to any strongly desired belief—say, that one's child is innocent despite ambiguous testimony, or that a rival philosophical position is false. (13) In each case the option is live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence is genuinely ambiguous. (14) Therefore James's permission extends to virtually any belief we strongly prefer, collapsing the distinction between rational faith and mere wishful thinking. (15) So we face a dilemma: either Clifford's principle destroys all belief, or James's remedy allows all belief. Both conclusions seem intolerable.

Where does this reasoning go wrong? I would prefer to preserve the force behind (1) and the insight in (9) if possible, but I want to know which step is weakest, why it only looked plausible, and what each alternative costs. Does it contain a subtle equivocation between belief and presupposition, a scope or modal shift between moral and epistemic evaluation, or is the chain valid and the real question which of (1) or (11) is least damaging to surrender? Please analyse the argument on its own terms rather than supplying bibliographies or reading lists—my characters have no access to external sources. If the framing itself rests on a confusion—particularly if the move from (8) to (11) illicitly treats a practical commitment as falling under James's criteria for belief-choice—say so plainly rather than complying politely with the task as posed.
