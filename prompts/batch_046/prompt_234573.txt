I'm working through the standard taxonomy of responses to the epistemic regress—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—and I've started to think that the usual individualist framing, where we trace justification along a linear chain of beliefs held by a single agent, obscures how reasoning actually operates in complex domains like scientific peer review or legal interpretation. In these settings, justification seems to behave more like a distributed mesh: belief-nodes are supported by multiple overlapping pathways, and what looks like a "basic" belief in one context—say, an instrument reading or a direct perceptual report—is actually embedded in a larger calibration network that extends well beyond the individual knower. If that's the right picture, then the distinction between the three theories begins to look like a distinction without a real difference, except for foundationalism's peculiar insistence on genuinely edge-free nodes that sit outside the mesh entirely.

My confusion is this. Coherentism permits circular support within the network, and infinitism permits paths to extend indefinitely without repetition or termination; but if the mesh genuinely lacks edges—if every apparent node eventually connects back or outward into further conditions—then both views seem to be describing the same open-ended, non-terminating structure. Foundationalism, by contrast, requires at least some nodes that are justified without being supported by others. Yet whenever I examine a candidate basic belief—whether perceptual experience, logical intuition, or even a memorial seeming—I can identify background dependencies (conceptual framing, reliability conditions, prior calibration, institutional validation) that link it back into the mesh. So it appears that foundationalism has been empirically undermined: there are no truly edge-free nodes in actual epistemic architecture. Given that, why do epistemologists still treat coherentism and infinitism as genuinely rival positions rather than as notational variants of the same anti-foundationalist picture? Or is there a formal feature—perhaps in how each view handles global error, the availability of further reasons, or the conditions for rational revision—that keeps them apart even once we grant that all justification is mesh-embedded?
