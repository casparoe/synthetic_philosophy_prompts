I'm giving a job-market talk defending a resolution to the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. My thesis is that the paradox is not solved by refining rationality assumptions—common-knowledge failures, bounded rationality, or non-expected utility—but by recognizing a category error in the strategic-form representation itself. In any real-world interaction that genuinely resembles the centipede—sequential cooperative exchange—the payoff at each node is not exogenously fixed but is co-constituted by what I call a continuation-protocol embedding: the shared expectation that the interaction continues is part of what makes the payoff values meaningful. Once you strip away that protocol to build the payoff matrix, you have altered the interaction so radically that backward induction applies to a different object altogether. This saves the universality of rational-choice analysis without invoking psychological limits, but it risks sounding like a sociological dodge that makes game theory unfalsifiable.

The audience is a philosophy-of-economics seminar with four factions I need to worry about: a formal game theorist who has worked on epistemic common-knowledge treatments of backward induction and will defend the strategic form as a normative idealization; a behavioral economist who regards the centipede result as empirical proof that rational-choice models are bankrupt; a philosopher of social science who worries I'm collapsing strategic reasoning into institutional sociology; and several mainstream decision theorists who will ask whether my view makes any testable difference from standard incomplete-information models. They care about precision, not charity.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each could do, phrased the way members of these factions would actually put them.
