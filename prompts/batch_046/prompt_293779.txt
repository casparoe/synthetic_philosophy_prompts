Design a 55-minute simulation for a philosophy-of-economics seminar of about 16 students that makes them feel the tension in sunk-cost reasoning—not merely the individual irrationality of “throwing good money after bad,” but the harder case in which honoring a sunk investment functions as a rational commitment or credibility-preserving move within an institutional or cooperative setting. The activity should center on a shared, sequential “project build” (for example, a cooperative research platform or communal infrastructure) in which small groups make repeated point-commitments from a shared pool. Use symbolic stakes only—ranking points or token rewards—never real money. Structure the rounds so that information about project failure is revealed gradually and asymmetrically: some participants learn the true payoff is negative while others still face social or strategic pressure to continue contributing. Arrange the payoff scheme and decision rules so that groups naturally split between those who cut losses (individual rationality) and those who escalate (preserving collective credibility or path-dependent relational capital). Provide exact rules, round sequence, materials, and the point-allocation formula. Include a debrief that maps observed behaviors onto three layers: standard individual-rationality arguments for ignoring sunk costs; the instrumental-rationality argument that continuing can signal future reliability; and the path-dependent-value argument that abandoned investments sometimes destroy irreversible structural or relational capital. State frankly which of these layers—most likely the long-horizon reputation-compounding effect—the simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally. Also identify what will predictably fail in the first run, such as participants conflating emotional attachment with strategic signaling or the facilitator inadvertently framing abandonment as “smart” before the mapping is complete, and say how to correct for it. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Conclude by asking the responder to state how confident it is in the design and to single out the specific design choice or conceptual claim about which it is least sure.
