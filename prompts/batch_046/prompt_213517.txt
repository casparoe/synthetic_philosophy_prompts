I've been trying to understand how groups can reason together without falling into contradictions, and I keep running into what is called the discursive dilemma: a committee can agree on every premise that supports a conclusion, yet when they vote on the conclusion itself the majority rejects it, depending on whether they aggregate the premises first or vote directly on the final judgment. I first noticed something like this in my neighborhood cooperative, where we voted separately on whether a proposed building plan was environmentally sound, whether it was equitable to long-term residents, and whether to approve the plan—only to discover that the majorities supporting the first two premises did not align with the majority on the third. That experience made me wonder whether the core problem is architectural, such as finding an aggregation rule that cannot be strategically manipulated while remaining consistent across premises and conclusions, or whether it is semantic, meaning that terms like "environmentally sound" or "equitable" shift their content between individuals so that the premises being aggregated are not really the same judgments at all. I have no formal background in logic or social choice theory—my experience is in community organizing—and I can devote about six months of serious self-study to this, so I need to know where that limited attention is best spent.

Within judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, I see several families of open questions, but they sit at very different levels. One family concerns aggregation architecture: whether any rule can be both strategy-proof and consistent between premise-based and conclusion-based procedures; whether groups should abandon premise-based reasoning entirely; and how to model strategic misrepresentation of intermediate judgments. Another family, which I am tentatively calling semantic stabilization, asks whether inconsistencies largely dissolve if premises are anchored in a shared public framework rather than interpreted privately, and whether collective reasoning should be redesigned to stabilize meaning before counting votes. If you had to rank these directions—together with any important lines I have missed—according to which are genuinely upstream, in the sense that resolving them would reshape or resolve the others, I want that ordering defended with explicit criteria: dependency relations among the questions, tractability for someone without graduate training, and practical payoff for real-world collective decision-making. I am not asking for a literature survey or a bibliography; I want a prioritized agenda. Which questions should I treat as dead ends or overemphasized distractions, which are tractable enough that six months of focused work could yield real understanding, and what kind of theoretical result or empirical finding over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list?
