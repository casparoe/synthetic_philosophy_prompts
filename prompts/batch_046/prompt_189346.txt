I'm co-organizing a philosophy-of-mind workshop on revisionist explanations of the color phi illusion, and two long-time interlocutors—one defending an Orwellian revisionist account and one defending a Stalinesque revisionist account—have agreed to stop debating past each other and instead produce a signed adversarial-collaboration document. The Orwellian holds that conscious experience consists of discrete, unconnected snapshots (a red spot at location A, then a green spot at location B) that are retrospectively woven into a unified narrative of smooth motion with intermediate hues, much as a regime might rewrite archival records after the fact; the Stalinesque holds that the first snapshot is registered at a pre-conscious level but is actively suppressed, airbrushed, or overwritten before it reaches access consciousness, leaving only the second snapshot coupled with an inserted motion narrative, analogous to removing a figure from an official photograph before publication. They have asked for a draft that treats them as parties to a collaboration rather than as opponents in a dialogue, and they want the document to address not only the standard Dennett framing but also one injected conceptual issue: whether the revision operates on the content of experience, on its temporal index (when it is experienced as occurring), or on both simultaneously, and whether that tripartite distinction collapses once access and phenomenal consciousness are properly separated.

Please draft the joint statement they should sign. Structure the response in the following named sections, in this order, with approximate lengths: (1) "Shared Commitments" (about 450 words)—eight to ten precisely worded claims both sides accept about the color phi phenomenon itself, the role of interstimulus intervals and visual persistence, the distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness, and the basic neural timeline of early visual processing, phrased so precisely that neither party can later claim they meant something else; (2) "Disagreements in Neutral Wording" (about 400 words)—a table or numbered list covering four specific issues: the ontological fate of the first-color representation, whether either view tacitly presupposes a Cartesian theater, how temporal tagging relates to content revision, and whether cross-modal phi analogues (e.g., auditory-visual sequences) imply a unified revision mechanism or modality-specific ones, with each camp's position rendered in language the holder would accept and the opponent would recognize as fair; (3) "Cruxes, Tests, and Predictions" (about 500 words)—three cruxes such that resolving them would move at least one side, including the interpretation of backward-masking data in the 80–150 ms window and the hypothetical split-hemifield variant in which left and right visual fields present different colors with mismatched ISIs, with for each crux a concrete observation or experimental result both agree is relevant, followed by each camp's explicit, advance prediction of what that evidence shows or would show; (4) "Limits and What Would Change Our Minds" (about 300 words)—each camp states the single argument, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction that would cause it to abandon or substantially revise its position, notes the weakest point in its own reasoning, and addresses the injected issue of content-versus-index revision by saying exactly where that distinction helps and where it dissolves; and (5) "Meta-Note on Drafting Neutrality" (about 150 words)—identify the specific formulation in sections 1–4 where neutrality was hardest to maintain and explain what consideration would convince you that the framing there was unfairly slanted toward one camp.

The document should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; it should not invoke obscure psychophysical literature unknown outside consciousness studies; and it should be written for philosophers of mind familiar with the revisionist debate but not for specialists in vision science. Finally, include in section 4, for each camp, an explicit statement of what would change its mind, and conclude the entire response with a brief note on where the reasoning in the draft is most likely to fail or to have smuggled in an unstated assumption.
