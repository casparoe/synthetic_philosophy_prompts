I'm preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar for philosophy students who have read Article II of the 1948 UN Genocide Convention but have not studied Lemkin's broader pre-Convention taxonomy. The goal is not to define genocide, but to teach why naming an atrocity "genocide" functions as a political-legal verdict with structural consequences, and how the Convention's narrowing of protected groups and its exclusion of cultural destruction was not merely a pragmatic drafting compromise but a conceptual transformation that reshaped what states could be accused of. Plan the session's explanatory arc: what foundational distinction—between moral taxonomy and legal verdict, or between Lemkin's original categories and the Convention's restricted ones—must be in place first so that the narrowing registers as a loss of conceptual tools rather than just diplomatic trimming; the one carrying example or hypothetical case (explicitly not Rwanda or the Holocaust, which the students already associate with the label) that should illustrate how the politics of naming unfolds when a state resists classification despite mass violence—specify what features that case must include and what it must lack in order to work pedagogically; the misconceptions they will predictably bring, in the order they are likely to surface—likely that cultural genocide is only metaphorical, that political groups were excluded for evidentiary clarity rather than geopolitical pressure, and that the duty to prevent and punish makes the label purely instrumental—plus the exact point in your sequence where each should be intercepted and defused; a final question or brief exercise that would reveal whether they have grasped the politics of naming or have merely memorized the protected-groups list; and which parts of Lemkin's original taxonomy you should openly refuse to reconstruct in detail at this level, saying so explicitly.
