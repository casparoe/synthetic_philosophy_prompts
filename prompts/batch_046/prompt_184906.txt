As a novelist who has spent years thinking about why holding an author’s actual manuscript feels different from holding an exact facsimile—why the paper seems to carry a kind of historical weight that photocopies never possess—I want you to take a definite philosophical position rather than surveying options. Argue that the devaluation of perfect forgeries and replicas is neither irrational nor merely a matter of market provenance, but is grounded in what I will call the “gesture-trace”: an indexical relation, in something like Peirce’s sense, between the physical artifact and the singular, unrepeatable intentional act that produced it. On this view, aesthetic properties such as expressive urgency or narrative risk are not fully instantiated in perceptual structure alone; they are anchored in a specific historical trajectory that connects the object back to the maker’s embodied decision at a particular moment. You should defend this claim against the familiar counter-argument—associated with Goodman’s analysis of authenticity—that if two objects share all perceptible and structural properties, any differential valuation must be non-aesthetic. Be explicit and committed: explain exactly what kind of aesthetic achievement is genuinely absent when the gesture-trace is missing, using the case of a perfect literary forgery (for instance, a reconstructed “lost” manuscript that is word-for-word identical to a destroyed original but was produced centuries later by an unknown hand with no causal connection to the original author). Do not retreat into agnosticism about whether the loss is “real”; argue that it is, and specify whether the absence undermines the work’s expressive force, its capacity to bear witness, or its role in a continuing artistic conversation. The response should be formal, polished, and structured as a direct argument with reasons, not a literature review or a list of competing views left for me to adjudicate.
