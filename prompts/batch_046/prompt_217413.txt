I am preparing an independent study presentation on Avicenna's flying man argument, in which a person suspended in empty air with no bodily sensation and no sensory contact with the world is said nonetheless to know immediately that he exists. Before I assess interpretations, I want the full specification of what a theory of minimal self-consciousness must deliver to count as adequate. Please list the desiderata: the core phenomena it must explain (the non-inferential certainty of self-existence in total sensory deprivation, the distinction between awareness of one's being and awareness of one's body, and the persistence of this awareness across imagined variations in bodily state), the conceptual distinctions it must preserve (such as between first-person immediacy and inferential self-knowledge, or between essential and accidental self-properties), and the empirical constraints it must respect—such as findings from prolonged sensory-deprivation studies or clinical cases involving severely disrupted bodily awareness—together with the reason each is required. Please also indicate which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and whether any pair or subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I want particular attention to one under-discussed constraint: the theory must explain why the flying man is aware of being one unified subject rather than a diffuse or divided field of consciousness, since standard treatments neglect this. Finally, briefly score an essentialist substance view, a contemporary embodied-cognition account, and a minimalist functionalist interpretation against your list.
