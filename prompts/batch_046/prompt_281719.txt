I work as a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, but I have been reading about the socialist calculation debate out of a long-standing interest in how large distributed systems coordinate without a single global controller. As I understand it, Mises and Hayek argue that socialist central planning is impossible in principle because economic knowledge is irreducibly dispersed, local, and tacit—prices in a market aggregate this information in real time, and no central board can replicate that computation. Lange and the market-socialist tradition respond that a planning authority can approximate market outcomes through iterative trial-and-error adjustments to prices, retaining public ownership while mimicking the signaling function of markets. Both sides seem to share a common formal framework—marginal analysis, equilibrium conditions, resource constraints—yet the debate is frequently described as having reached an impasse that has lasted for nearly a century, with neither camp conceding substantive ground.

What puzzles me is whether the disagreement is really about computation and information, or whether something else is sustaining the stall. From my perspective in software engineering, Hayek's position resembles an argument that a system with many nodes holding private, constantly changing state cannot be consistently coordinated by a central server without unacceptable latency or partition errors, whereas Lange's model looks like a feedback loop that assumes the central authority can observe and correct discrepancies faster than the underlying state changes. This makes me wonder: is the core dispute empirical (about whether dispersed knowledge can actually be captured and updated centrally), conceptual (about what counts as economic knowledge and whether it is the kind of thing that can be aggregated), or normative (about whether equality of outcome justifies accepting coordination risks that markets avoid)? I have also read suggestions that the two sides may be answering different questions—one about static allocative efficiency, the other about dynamic adaptation to change—or that the disagreement is partly verbal, turning on different uses of terms like planning and market. I am not asking for a verdict on socialism versus capitalism; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. What kind of stall is this? Is there a hidden formal or philosophical distinction—perhaps in social epistemology or the philosophy of science—that would clarify whether central planning and market coordination are addressing the same problem? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an empirical demonstration, a conceptual clarification, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed?
