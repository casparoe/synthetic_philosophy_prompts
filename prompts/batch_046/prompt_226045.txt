Write a sestina on the comparative moral calculus of reducing beef versus chicken consumption, using the six rotating end-words acre, replace, scale, young, field, and mouth. The poem should move through the direct harm of slaughter—cattle versus poultry—while also tracking the indirect deaths caused by crop cultivation, including field mortality from harvest machinery and the reproductive-rate asymmetry that means reducing chicken consumption eliminates more potential lives per unit of nutrition. Structure the rotation so that each stanza shifts the burden of suffering between categories—land use, direct killing, replacement rates, wild-animal habitat loss—without allowing any single category to dominate the envoi. In the final three lines, bring all six end-words together to leave the comparison genuinely suspended: the form should show that choosing beef over chicken (or vice versa) does not resolve the harm but displaces it onto a different scale of victims. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the wild-animal-suffering and crop-death literature recognizes the quantitative relationships being invoked, yet it should function as a poem for a general philosophical audience rather than as a technical summary.
