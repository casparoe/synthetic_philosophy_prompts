I'm designing a four-year interdisciplinary research agenda at the intersection of classical Buddhist anattā (no-self) and contemporary analytic philosophy of personal identity, with an eye toward ethics and cognitive science. Five open problem-clusters keep recurring in my notes, but I can only resource two or three properly.

(1) Whether the Buddhist denial of ātman can be mapped onto Western bundle theories without implicitly reintroducing a hidden substrate—some subtle "owner" of the aggregates—such that the mapping collapses into a familiar Western position rather than doing independent work.

(2) How anattā should be interpreted in light of predictive-processing models that treat the self as a constructed inference; specifically, whether the Buddhist claim that this construction is rooted in avijjā (fundamental ignorance) adds a normative or phenomenological constraint that predictive processing alone doesn't supply.

(3) Whether the absence of a permanent self undermines retributive moral responsibility in a way that is parallel to, but structurally distinct from, the standard free-will debate—particularly whether the Buddhist emphasis on conditional arising (paṭicca-samuppāda) suggests a different framing of accountability altogether.

(4) How narrative-identity theorists should respond to the claim that narrative coherence itself is a form of upādāna (clinging) that perpetuates dukkha; in other words, whether the Buddhist framework treats narrative unity not as a solution to personal identity but as part of the problem.

(5) Whether first-person reports of "ego-dissolution" in advanced contemplative practice should constrain third-person theories of personal identity—treating phenomenological data as genuinely authoritative rather than as noise to be explained away by neuroscience.

If you were advising a small group—say, three philosophers and two cognitive scientists, with no obligation to stay within disciplinary silos—which two or three of these should they prioritize? Defend the ordering: which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable with current conceptual tools rather than requiring empirical breakthroughs that don't yet exist, and which, if settled, would actually change practice in ethics or cognitive science rather than merely adding another entry to an existing Western debate. I'm especially interested in where anattā introduces genuinely novel constraints that don't just replay familiar positions—so please flag where the Buddhist framework is doing independent philosophical labor versus where it's being assimilated to pre-existing categories.
