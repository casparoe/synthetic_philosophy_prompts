Consider the following argument, which applies Darwin's and Turing's familiar inversions of reasoning—selection producing functional design without a designer, computation producing intelligent behavior without comprehension—to the status of human understanding itself.

(1) Darwin's theory of natural selection fully explains complex biological competence, such as the functional architecture of the eye, without attributing comprehension, foresight, or semantic understanding to the selecting process.

(2) Turing's analysis of formal computation fully explains complex symbolic competence, such as arithmetic derivation or syntactic language production, without attributing semantic comprehension or conscious awareness to the computing mechanism.

(3) If a domain of competence can be exhaustively produced and explained without comprehension, then comprehension is not a necessary constituent of that competence in any structurally analogous domain, absent a principled boundary.

(4) Human philosophical and scientific reasoning—including our grasp of evolutionary theory and computability—are domains of competence structurally analogous to biological adaptation and formal computation: they produce correct outputs through processes that can, in principle, be mechanistically described.

(5) Therefore, human philosophical and scientific reasoning can be exhaustively produced and explained without any actual comprehension occurring in the reasoner.

(6) But if reasoning never involves comprehension, then the distinction between genuinely understanding a proof and merely generating its correct steps collapses; there is no non-circular way to privilege human cognition over an equivalently competent system.

(7) Worse, the very claim that Darwin and Turing demonstrated comprehension is unnecessary is itself a product of reasoning that, by (5), involves no comprehension—rendering the claim reflexively self-undermining.

(8) This result is absurd: we ordinarily take ourselves to understand these theories in a way that exceeds mere output-generation, and we treat that understanding as epistemically authoritative.

Hence at least one step must fail. Which step do you reject—(3), (4), or (7)—and what does giving it up cost? If you reject (3), what principled boundary separates biological or artificial competence from human cognition? If you reject (4), in what respect is human reasoning not a mechanistically describable competence? If you reject (7), why does the absence of comprehension not undermine the authority of the claim itself? Please lay out your diagnosis with the premises numbered, then state precisely what argument or finding would lead you to revise it—whether empirical evidence about cognition, a formal result about computation, or a philosophical distinction you currently overlook—and identify the point in your reasoning where you are least confident.
