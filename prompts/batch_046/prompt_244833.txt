Write a deadpan travel-guide entry, in the style of a Lonely Planet page, for a fictional sovereign state—call it Grue-Meridia—whose constitution formally resolves Hume’s problem of induction by adopting the predicate-distinction framework associated with Nelson Goodman’s “new riddle.” The piece should run about 600 words and include standard guidebook sections: a brief introduction, “Local Customs,” “What to Do When Your Forecast Is Challenged,” “Tips for Tourists on Passing the Predicate Licensing Exam,” and a sidebar titled “National Holiday: Entrenchment Day.” The satire must exaggerate genuine features of the induction debate—the symmetry between “green” and “grue,” the appeal to linguistic entrenchment to break that symmetry, and the circular risk of defining projectibility by prior projection—without ever stating these objections explicitly and without breaking the deadpan voice. Let the country’s institutions embody the philosophy: citizens must register all predictions with a Bureau of Projectible Predicates; a predicate is legally valid only if it has been entrenched in at least three prior successful forecasts by licensed speakers; tourists are warned that asking whether emeralds will remain green after Tuesday requires a different entry visa than asking whether they will be grue; restaurant reservations and weather apps operate only with certified projectible terms; and romantic commitments phrased with non-entrenched predicates are unenforceable in court. The humor should arise from applying this logic meticulously to mundane social life—hotel bookings, dating etiquette, public-transport schedules—so that a reader familiar with Goodman’s riddle recognizes the reductio, while an unfamiliar reader can still navigate the guide. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts; do not use authorial asides or winking explanations.
