My verdicts on when backward induction succeeds or fails in strategic reasoning, and whether a single principle can capture the pattern. In the classic centipede game with perfect information and common knowledge of rationality, I think rational play demands immediate termination at the first node, yet I also think that in actual laboratory settings people continue much longer and this is not mere error. If the total number of rounds is unknown—drawn from a distribution with an unbounded tail—I think backward induction genuinely fails to apply and continuing is rational. But I also feel that in a probabilistic-rationality variant, where each player assigns a small epsilon to the other being an irrational commitment type who never stops, the rational player should continue for several rounds rather than one. The hard case is a reflexive centipede I want to consider: the in-game payoffs are unchanged, but outside observers update beliefs about the players' cooperative dispositions, and these beliefs determine substantial rewards or punishments in all subsequent interactions. Here, despite perfect information, common knowledge, and no epsilon-types, I think rational players should continue well past the first node because the external reputational feedback loop breaks the local dominance reasoning that drives backward induction. Is there one precise principle—perhaps about the boundaries of the reasoning domain or about when local dominance aggregates globally—that accommodates all four verdicts? If not, which verdict must I drop? Please also state what your principle predicts for a variant where the external feedback loop is present but the in-game payoffs are strictly zero-sum.
