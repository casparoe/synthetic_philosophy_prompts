I am a novelist preparing remarks for an interdisciplinary symposium on moral philosophy and narrative form. I intend to defend the view that Nietzsche's genealogy of morality should be interpreted as a structural-literary inversion rather than a historical-psychological narrative: specifically, that the aristocratic evaluation of good is not simply displaced by the slave's evaluation of evil, but is recursively hollowed out, producing a semantic ghost that continues to organise modern moral discourse without retaining any positive content of its own. My audience will be analytic moral philosophers well versed in the standard genealogical literature—above all the interpretations associated with Bernard Williams and Brian Leiter—alongside a single literary theorist who favours formalist analysis but is deeply sceptical of philosophical vagueness. They prize conceptual precision and will treat appeals to narrative structure as suspect unless those appeals generate clearly distinguishable claims.

Please draft my preparation sheet: list the objections I am most likely to encounter, ordered by how damaging each would be if left unanswered. For each objection, render it in the vocabulary and tone this audience would use; state what I should concede without abandoning the central thesis, what I must hold absolutely, and where my reply is most vulnerable. Finally, include the question I most hope will not be asked—namely, whether my semantic ghost is nothing more than a metaphorical restatement of ordinary conceptual change, and whether I can provide any non-circular criterion that distinguishes hollowing-out from routine semantic drift. Please reply in British English.
