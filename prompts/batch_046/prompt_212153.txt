Consider a recurring structural pattern in negotiations between communities that hold genuinely incommensurable moral frameworks, not merely different weightings of shared values. In one instance, a community that ranks personal autonomy as lexically prior to collective welfare reaches an agreement with a community that ranks communal solidarity as lexically prior to individual liberty. Rather than constructing an overlapping consensus or a blended synthesis, they establish a cross-protection pact: the autonomy-prioritizing group accepts a narrowly circumscribed welfare obligation that activates only in direct interaction with the other group, and in exchange the solidarity-prioritizing group accepts an equally circumscribed liberty exception, also interaction-bound. The result is not a revised hierarchy but two intact orderings, each now containing a foreign, conditional clause justified not by its own foundational commitments but solely by the presence of the other framework. A second instance involves a deontological group and a consequentialist group: the former permits a consequentialist override restricted to cooperative joint projects, while the latter adopts a deontological side-constraint restricted to those same projects. Over successive negotiations, such clauses accumulate, yielding value systems that remain internally ordered yet are interlaced with exceptions that lack endogenous justification. I want to distinguish this precisely from a modus vivendi, from Rawlsian political liberalism, and from value pluralism that accommodates lexical trade-offs within a single agent's reasoning. Does this specific configuration—where incommensurable systems exchange rank-specific protections, generating interlocking, non-integrated, interaction-bound exceptions—have an established name in moral or political philosophy? If not, how should one delineate its boundaries: for example, how does it differ from reflective equilibrium that simply accommodates plural values within one framework, and what separates it from a market-style exchange of discrete moral concessions?
