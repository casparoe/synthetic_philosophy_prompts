My colleague, a philosopher of action, has rendered judgments on five cases involving practical failure:

First, a dieter breaks an explicit, calmly formed resolution not to eat dessert, acting in a moment of stress and feeling immediate regret; she classifies this as weakness of will (akrasia).

Second, a retiree consistently saves too little because he applies a steep hyperbolic discount rate across all intertemporal choices, yet he has never formed any specific resolution about savings targets; she classifies this as irrational preference structure, but not as akrasia.

Third, a student pulls an all-nighter for an exam she had previously decided to abandon, having simply forgotten her decision; she classifies this as a failure of memory rather than of will.

Fourth, a smoker lights a cigarette during stress and genuinely judges in that moment that immediate relief outweighs long-term health, feeling no regret afterward and maintaining the revision upon calm reflection; she classifies this as an authentic local preference revision, not akrasia.

Fifth, a patient skips chemotherapy minutes after explicitly reaffirming her desire to live, reporting that the future survival benefit suddenly felt abstract and unreal, describing the omission as feeling alien to her, and expressing regret; she classifies this as akrasia.

Consider now a sixth case: an agent locks a portion of her income in a savings vehicle with an early-withdrawal penalty—a precommitment device—then discovers and exploits a legal loophole to access the funds, spends them on an immediate luxury, and reports no regret. She maintains, even in calm reflection, that long-term financial security is more valuable than the luxury, but she also maintains that her present self possessed legitimate authority to override her past self's constraint, framing the exploitation as a strategic victory rather than as an impulse.

Predict my colleague's verdict on this case, identifying the closest analog among the five and stating whether she will classify it as akrasia. Then articulate at least two rules jointly consistent with her track record: one that treats akrasia as any action violating one's own reflective value-ranking regardless of psychological mechanism or strategic framing, and one that treats akrasia specifically as a failure of executive control over hyperbolic impulses, marked either by experiential alienation or by the absence of strategic reasoning. For each rule, state what it predicts about the precommitment case and what principle the colleague adopts by following it. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish these two rules—perhaps contrasting an agent who honors a precommitment solely from deference to past authority despite intense present-biased desire, with one who violates a resolution through calm strategic reasoning but then expresses genuine regret upon reflection—and explain why it breaks the tie.
