I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and its political dimensions, and I've become increasingly dissatisfied with the standard separation between Carnap's technical philosophy of language and his documented interest in rational economic planning and democratic social coordination. For my final project, I want a research proposal—approximately 5,000 words, over six weeks—that treats these domains as philosophically interdependent rather than biographically adjacent. Specifically, the project should investigate whether Carnap's method of explication, by which vague pre-scientific concepts are refined into precise formal frameworks, can serve as a normative model for the institutional design of democratic planning mechanisms, or whether his principle of tolerance regarding the choice of logical languages undermines any claim that planning institutions must converge on a single authoritative conceptual framework.

Please frame the proposal around a precisely stated question that has a determinate answer: if democratic planning requires the explication of collective social goals into specific institutional mechanisms, does Carnap's philosophy commit planners to one correct explication, or does it permit plural, competing institutional languages without attributing metaphysical error to any of them? The proposal should explain why existing scholarship leaves this open—particularly, why treating Carnap's logical empiricism and his planning sympathies in isolation prevents us from determining whether disputes about institutional design are internal practical questions or external metaphysical commitments in his sense. Outline an approach using conceptual analysis of framework choice, the internal-external distinction, and the role of observational or protocol-like constraints in social coordination, without including a bibliography or literature review by citation. Specify what a successful result looks like—whether planning explications are framework-relative in the same manner as logical languages, and under what conditions competing planning frameworks remain rationally permissible—along with an honest assessment of scope limitations for a six-week philosophical project. Include a realistic evaluation of failure conditions, such as the possibility that the analogy between logical language choice and institutional design is merely decorative rather than substantive, and explain how the proposal addresses that risk without relying on invented biographical details. The deliverable should be useful for someone deciding whether democratic planning requires conceptual uniformity or can tolerate institutional pluralism.
