My philosophy-of-action colleague has rendered the following judgments about whether an agent “intentionally brought about” a particular outcome. In case one, a company chairman approves a new program solely to increase profits; he knows it will damage the environment and does not care, and she says he intentionally damaged the environment. In case two, the chairman approves a program solely for profit and knows it will improve air quality, and she says he did not intentionally improve the air. In case three, a surgeon removes a tumor by a method that unavoidably severs a nerve, and she says the surgeon intentionally severed the nerve. In case four, a city planner reroutes a bus line for efficiency; she knows this will eliminate a convenient stop for elderly residents and could have kept it with minimal cost, yet she says the planner did not intentionally eliminate the stop.

Next, consider a climate-engineering AI that runs a global simulation. To stabilize atmospheric carbon, the model must suppress ocean-temperature variance; this suppression is not a target but a mechanical prerequisite of the algorithm. The suppression causes a virtual coral-bleaching event that the designers foresaw, documented, and could have avoided by using a slower, more expensive computation. Would my colleague say the designers intentionally caused the virtual bleaching?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning: identify at least two distinct rules that accommodate all four prior verdicts—perhaps one tracking normative valence, one tracking causal-mechanical integration, one tracking counterfactual avoidability—and say what each predicts here. Then propose one further case that would best discriminate between them.
