Since my recent resignation from a local party committee has left me reflecting on whether collective political organization inevitably corrupts individual moral judgment, I wish to examine the 2016 founding and 2017 electoral victory of Emmanuel Macron's movement, subsequently institutionalized as La République En Marche!, through the lens of Simone Weil's critique of political parties, particularly her analysis of collective gravity and the discipline of attention. Weil argued that parties function as mechanisms of moral gravity that suppress individual judgment by substituting collective loyalty and emotional belonging for the patient, detached pursuit of truth; analyze whether Macron's explicit rejection of the left-right axis and his movement's organizational form—decentralized local committees selecting candidates, funding outside traditional party structures, and a rhetoric of transcending partisan division—escapes this diagnosis or reproduces it in a modified structure. Specifically, map which institutional features of the movement correspond to Weil's account of gravity (the suppression of internal contradiction, the substitution of collective identity for individual discernment, the demand for unconditional adherence to a collective will), whether any feature genuinely preserves the space for attention (the capacity to hold contradiction, to perceive the other without projection, to refuse collective passion), and whether the movement's rapid consolidation into a conventional parliamentary party after 2017 confirms Weil's claim that party-form is structurally inevitable. Where Weil's framework is silent—particularly regarding the role of media spectacle, technocratic expertise, or the specific institutional architecture of the French Fifth Republic—say so explicitly, and indicate whether the Macron episode reveals a genuine limitation in her account or rather an anticipated failure of an attempted exception.
