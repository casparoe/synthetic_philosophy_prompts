Having recently found myself in a collaborative project where I can no longer determine whether I originated a particular conceptual framework or merely refined my partner's prior contribution—each genealogy I trace loops back, with attribution constantly exchanging hands—I have become preoccupied with whether any hierarchy of authorship possesses a ground that is not itself authored. Write a formal dialogue between two interlocutors of equal competence: one insists that every conceptual or institutional hierarchy must contain an inviolate level, a point at which contribution becomes genuinely original and ceases to be derivative; the other maintains that all such hierarchies are tangled loops, structurally analogous to Escher's Drawing Hands, in which every level is constituted by the one it apparently grounds, leaving no non-derivative base. Rather than treating Escher's image as a casual metaphor, have them use it as an explicit structural model for mutual constitution. To keep the exchange from collapsing into familiar foundationalism-versus-coherentism tropes, direct them to examine a specific, somewhat uncommon case: a body of successive judicial opinions that cite one another as authoritative, each reconstructing the original statute's intent differently, so that the statutory text's ground meaning is itself an emergent product of the interpretive loop. They should argue, with genuine back-and-forth, over whether this legal loop demonstrates the absence of an inviolate textual fact, or whether there remains a brute semantic or historical residue that escapes the loop entirely. Neither speaker should be a strawman; each should concede small points but defend the core claim. The dialogue should respond directly to the previous speaker's arguments rather than talking past them.
