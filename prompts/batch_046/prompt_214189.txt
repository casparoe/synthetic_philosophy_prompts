I'm working through the philosophy of collective responsibility as it applies to the First World War, specifically in light of the Fischer controversy over whether German leadership deliberately pursued escalation and expansive war aims. Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Elias Voss—who disagree about what it means to hold a nation responsible for initiating and prolonging the conflict.

Thorne should defend an institutional-agent view of collective blame. Drawing on the Fischer thesis regarding strategic planning bodies and calculated risk-taking—such as the formulation of expansive war aims and the acceptance of escalation—she should argue that the German state apparatus exhibited sufficient organizational continuity and decision-making coherence to count as a persisting agent. She should press the problem of successor-state moral liability: if institutional structures, civil-service personnel, and legal-administrative logic persisted through defeat, constitutional reconstruction, and into the Federal Republic, does that continuity preserve the same collective subject available for blame? Or does the rupture of 1945 and the democratic reconstitution break the chain of agency? She should also consider whether attributing blame serves a forward-looking normative-repair function for successor populations, rather than merely assigning retrospective guilt.

Voss should resist this as a reification of bureaucratic complexity. He should argue that Fischer's evidence reveals not unified intent but competing factions—within the military high command, the foreign office, and the chancellery—whose conflicting preferences and informational asymmetries dissolve any singular "Germany" that could be blamed. He should invoke the problem of counterfactual institutional autonomy: given alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, and structural diplomatic pressures, decision-makers in 1914 lacked meaningful alternative paths, so ascribing culpable choice misattributes systemic drift to collective will. He should challenge Thorne directly on whether institutional form alone can carry moral liability without shared intentionality, and whether holding a successor population accountable for actions taken under radically different normative conditions is either coherent or fair.

The dialogue must be genuine back-and-forth. Each speaker should respond to the other's actual arguments about institutional continuity, bureaucratic fragmentation, counterfactual autonomy, and the normative purpose of blame—not talk past each other. Neither should be a strawman. Avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the exchange should stand on conceptual and historical reasoning alone.
