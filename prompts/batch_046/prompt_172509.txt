Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—one who accepts Harry Frankfurt's argument that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, and one who defends the principle of alternative possibilities—set not in a seminar room but at a live installation in which a pianist performs a fully notated score while an algorithmic monitor tracks her neural activity, poised to suppress any impulse to improvise, though the suppression never triggers because she never attempts to deviate. Have them argue, in real back-and-forth, whether this counts as a genuine Frankfurt-style case when the "intervener" is a non-agential system rather than a deliberate agent like Black or Jones; whether the absence of a "flicker of freedom" is more damaging here because the constraint is distributed and automated; and whether moral responsibility can survive if we replace the neuroscientist with a socio-technical apparatus that never exercises a choice. The Frankfurt defender should address the flicker objection and the demand for an agentive blocker; the PAP defender should address whether algorithmic constraint alters the modal structure of the situation and whether responsibility requires genuinely open alternative branches rather than mere non-interference. Neither should talk past the other: each response should engage the specific point just made.
