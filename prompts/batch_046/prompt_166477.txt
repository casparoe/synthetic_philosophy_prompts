I am drafting a novel set in a society governed by the strict principles of luck egalitarianism, where citizens are fully compensated for every misfortune that falls outside their control yet must bear alone the costs of risks they genuinely choose to take, and I need to resolve a philosophical difficulty before I can render the moral logic of this world with any consistency. I want you to write an essay examining whether luck egalitarianism can preserve its foundational distinction between choice and circumstance once it confronts what might be called adaptive risk preferences: psychological dispositions formed under conditions of unchosen deprivation—chronic scarcity, inadequate education, or sustained exposure to danger—that lead individuals to make subsequent decisions, such as refusing preventive care, accepting exploitative labor contracts, or rejecting available social support, which appear voluntary on the surface but may in fact be the predictable downstream effects of prior bad luck rather than autonomous exercises of agency for which no compensation is owed. Weigh the strongest defense of the view that these preferences remain authentic choices deserving of full personal responsibility against the strongest critique that treating them as such collapses the boundary between choice and circumstance and thereby undermines the internal coherence of the theory itself. Reach and defend a definite conclusion about whether luck egalitarianism can survive this challenge without either abandoning its commitment to personal responsibility or implicitly extending compensation to cover the cascading consequences of every earlier misfortune. Write this entirely as continuous prose in the manner of a formal letter: do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other structural formatting, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation.
