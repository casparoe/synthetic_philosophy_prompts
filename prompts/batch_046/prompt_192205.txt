I'm preparing a session for my graduate seminar in philosophy of aesthetics and cognitive science — about twelve students, one 75-minute meeting — and I've become preoccupied with a particular twist on the Letter Spirit problem: not merely the familiar question of what makes every instance of 'a' count as an 'a' despite radical stylistic variation, but what I am tentatively calling the problem of stylistic inheritance. The idea is that a letterform seems to carry a kind of genealogical trace of its form-family even when every measurable geometric feature has been systematically altered, and that recognizing a letter involves tracking this invisible lineage rather than matching against an essential template. I want my students to feel the force of this distinction physically, through disagreement and failed coordination, before we ever name it in the debrief.

Design a runnable in-class game or structured simulation — using paper cards or a shared whiteboard — in which students must classify transformed letterforms and defend their judgments under escalating ambiguity. The rules should create genuine coordination pressure: groups earn collective points only when they reach unanimous consensus on whether a displayed form belongs to a given letter-family, but each student also receives a hidden "lineage-bias" card that rewards them privately for either insisting on or rejecting stylistic inheritance in specific rounds. By round three, the displayed forms should be ambiguous enough that family resemblance and morphological essentialism pull in opposite directions, and the payoff scheme should make disagreement feel costly — for instance, a failed consensus resets the group's accumulated score rather than merely failing to add points. The sequence should move from unambiguous cases through moderate distortion to forms that preserve statistical regularities of the original letter while violating every intuitive "spirit" of it.

Please specify: the materials and physical setup; the exact sequence of rounds with descriptions of what is displayed; the hidden-role mechanism and the collective-versus-individual payoff structure; the debrief that maps specific moments of breakdown onto the difference between token identity, type membership, and stylistic inheritance; and an honest assessment of what this activity cannot reproduce about actual letter recognition — particularly its rapid, unconscious aspect — which I will need to supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it. The response should be written in American English, should avoid asking students to cite sources or provide bibliographies, and should not depend on specialized typographic knowledge they are unlikely to possess.
