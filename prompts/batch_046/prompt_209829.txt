I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on existentialism and philosophy of religion, and over the past several weeks I have been trying to work out my own stance on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling—specifically on whether Abraham's figure can be made philosophically respectable outside a strictly Christian framework. I want to check whether the commitments below actually hang together, because on reflection they feel coherent, but I worry I may be drawing on incompatible motivations without noticing it. Here is where I have landed:

First, I read Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac as genuinely exemplary of faith rather than as ethical failure, madness, or mere fanaticism; he holds the universal ethical prohibition against killing one's child in infinite respect even while he acts to suspend it. Second, I take the teleological suspension of the ethical to be strictly vertical: it requires an unmediated, direct command from God, and in the absence of such a command, violating the universal for a private end is not faith but crime or insanity. Third, at the same time, I think there is a defensible secular analogue—what I have been calling horizontal suspension—in which an individual's deepest vocation or an act of radical moral innovation requires breaking with established social norms, and in those rare cases the norms should be treated as genuinely suspended rather than merely outweighed by competing considerations. Fourth, I take Abraham's silence, his refusal to justify himself to Sarah, Isaac, or the community, to be structurally essential to the knight of faith; if he could explain himself in universal ethical language, he would not be suspending the ethical but only operating within it at a more complex level. Fifth, I also hold that in any defensible secular analogue, transparency and public justifiability are non-negotiable moral requirements; one cannot ask others to accept a norm-violating act solely on the basis of private vocation. Sixth, I reject interpretations that treat Abraham's faith as simple irrationalism or as anti-ethical; I think he remains within the ethical in a paradoxical mode, and I further hold that if God had not actually issued the command, Abraham's act would be an unmitigated moral atrocity rather than an admirable paradox.

Please audit this package for joint tenability. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve once the commitments are spelled out precisely, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency without abandoning the core idea? Order the tensions by severity, and for each specify exactly what has to give and at what cost. I am not asking for a survey of secondary literature or a lecture on Kierkegaard scholarship—just a precise consistency check on the position I have staked out.
