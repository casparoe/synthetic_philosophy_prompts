I serve on the governance board of a municipal digital-heritage repository—essentially a public, version-controlled archive of local records, oral histories, and digitized documents. I am a software engineer by training and have no formal background in philosophy. Our board must vote next week on a concrete case, and I need a formal policy memo I can circulate in advance.

The case: for four decades, a local community has maintained that the 1978 closure of the Meridian textile mill was deliberately engineered by outside owners to destroy a union, and that the official “accidental fire” report was fabricated. Recently digitized insurance ledgers, court transcripts, and engineering assessments—previously inaccessible—strongly support accidental failure and contradict the sabotage narrative. The community’s representatives petition our archive to designate their oral-history collection as the “authoritative narrative layer” for this event, relegating the digitized documents to a “disputed appendix.” Our advisory historians insist on the reverse: the documentary record should remain primary, with community memory preserved but clearly flagged as contradicted by the best available evidence.

The underlying tension is clear even without academic vocabulary: when a community’s living memory and the documentary record conflict, which should govern a public archive? To frame the memo precisely, I want to use two distinctions that a colleague mentioned in passing—whether they are standard terms or ones I have improvised, I do not know. The first is “mnemonic sovereignty”: the principle that a community holds authoritative standing over how its own past is represented in public institutions. The second is “evidentiary precedence”: the principle that an archive’s primary duty is to reflect the best-available causal account, regardless of communal ownership.

Deliverable: a memo addressed to the board recommending one of three concrete courses:
1. Adopt the community-requested layering (oral history primary, documents in appendix);
2. Maintain the documentary record as primary, with community memory in a clearly demarcated parallel layer;
3. Implement a hybrid “branching narrative” display that refuses to rank the two, paired with a governance rule for resolving future conflicts.

The memo must include:
- A clear recommendation with an implementation timeline (e.g., a pilot period and review date);
- An honest, non-strawman engagement with the strongest argument for the option you reject—if you recommend option 2, you must seriously treat the claim that relegating living memory to an appendix risks epistemic erasure, not merely dismiss it;
- Reasoning accessible to engineers, librarians, and community representatives, with no reliance on bibliographic citations, named philosophers, or reading lists;
- A brief scaling principle: if we settle this for the mill closure, what rule governs the next conflict?

Write for decision-makers, not for an academic audience. Keep the reasoning precise and the bottom line actionable.
