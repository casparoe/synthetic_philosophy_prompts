I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on nationalism, collective memory, and the construction of modern political identity. I want one essay prompt that requires students to work with Eric Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions and Benedict Anderson's theory of imagined communities, but I want it constructed so that a student cannot earn a high mark by merely summarizing the two texts. To achieve this, the question should embed a specific conceptual tension: Hobsbawm tends to treat invented traditions as responses to rapid social change, often orchestrated by elites who fabricate continuity with a suitable past, whereas Anderson emphasizes structural conditions—particularly print-capitalism, the bureaucratic standardization of language, and administrative practices such as census-taking, cartography, and museum curation—that make it possible for people who will never meet one another to imagine themselves as part of a simultaneous, horizontal community. The exam prompt should ask students to evaluate whether these accounts are genuinely complementary or whether Anderson's emphasis on popular, structural imagination undermines Hobsbawm's more top-down, functionalist model of deliberate fabrication, using a concrete scenario in which a colonial or post-colonial state simultaneously deploys census categories, standardized territorial maps, and national museums to consolidate a unified national narrative. The deliverable should include the exam question itself, formatted for a 48-hour take-home assignment with a word limit of approximately 1,500 words; a model answer of about 650 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves an excellent response must contain—such as distinguishing Hobsbawm's functionalist explanation from Anderson's cultural-materialist one, recognizing that Anderson's term imagined does not mean fictitious but denotes a specific mode of social cognition, and analyzing whether state bureaucratic practices generate belief from the top down or create the conditions for bottom-up identification; and a detailed marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the precise argumentative steps required for the highest grade, including attention to the different mechanisms each author identifies, an explicit evaluation of their compatibility rather than a simple comparison, and an accurate discussion of the census-map-museum triad without reducing it to mere propaganda. The marking scheme should also identify common errors that should cost marks, such as conflating invented with false, treating both authors as identical social constructivists, or describing Anderson's argument without referencing the material conditions of print and administration. No bibliography, citation list, or reference to secondary scholarship should be required.
