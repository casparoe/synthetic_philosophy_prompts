I'm trying to determine what I actually think about the scope of aesthetic testimony, going by my reactions to concrete cases. First, when a trusted art historian tells me that a particular gray-scale painting induces a specific kind of melancholy, but I have never seen it, I do not think I come to know that the painting is melancholy-inducing; at best I acquire justified belief about the historian's opinion. Second, when I study a detailed structural analysis of a symphony—describing its harmonic tensions and resolutions—without ever hearing it, I think I can come to know that it possesses those structural properties and that they contribute to its aesthetic merit, yet I do not acquire first-person knowledge of what it sounds like aesthetically. Third, when I initially feel nothing before a painting and then, guided by a friend's testimony that it expresses grief, I look again and genuinely feel grief, I think the testimony functioned legitimately as guidance rather than substitution, and I now possess direct aesthetic knowledge aided but not replaced by testimony. Fourth, in a local tradition where "sublime" applies not to overwhelming scale but to extreme density of detail in miniature works, if an expert tells me a small intricate object is sublime within that tradition and I apply the term correctly without feeling awe, I think I can have testimonial knowledge of the community's aesthetic categorization, but not first-person aesthetic knowledge of sublimity in that object. Fifth, I smell a fragrance, find it beautiful, and am then told that an identical synthetic replica—indistinguishable phenomenally—lacks the "organic coherence" of the original; upon learning this, I withdraw my aesthetic judgment of the replica, feeling that its origin undermines its beauty despite unchanged experience.

Is there one principle that captures all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by each case, and say what it predicts about a further case: being told that a poem in a language I do not understand is beautiful, where I can verify its phonetic patterns but cannot grasp its semantic content. If no single principle works without distortion, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what simpler account replaces it? Also, identify the pair of verdicts that is hardest to reconcile, explain whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, and say what further case—perhaps involving testimony about an aesthetic property I am constitutionally unable to experience—would best adjudicate between the competing principles.
