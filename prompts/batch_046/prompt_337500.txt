After a long, somewhat circular argument with a close friend last evening—she kept insisting that if a punishment produces no deterrence, no rehabilitation, and no emotional satisfaction for the victim, then it is nothing more than state-sanctioned cruelty, while I kept feeling that there is something conceptually wrong with inflicting suffering on someone solely because doing so maximizes future welfare, even when that person clearly does not deserve it—I realized that I do not actually possess a clear specification of what I should demand from a theory of punishment before I declare it adequate. So, before I read any more systematic defenses of retributivism or consequentialism, I want the full desiderata: what must any adequate theory of punishment be able to deliver in order to solve the problem rather than merely relocate it?

Write the requirements list with the motivating case or consideration behind each. The theory must explain why punishing a genuinely innocent person is wrong in a manner that is not reducible to its bad social consequences, since that intuition survives even in hypothetical scenarios where framing an innocent party produces no detectable erosion of deterrence. It must accommodate the judgment that proportionality—punishment fitting the severity of the wrong—matters independently of utility calculations, without collapsing into the claim that suffering is intrinsically valuable. It must draw a principled distinction between punishment and other harmful state practices such as quarantine, civil commitment, or taxation, so that we do not confuse moral accountability with mere risk management or social choreography. It must handle borderline cases that my friend and I kept circling: algorithmic sentencing in which no human agent intends the suffering; preemptive detention of individuals who pose statistical risks but have not yet acted; collective sanctions applied to diffuse groups where individual culpability is unclear; and whether an automated system can genuinely “punish” or only cause harm, given that my friend kept analogizing to traffic cameras. It should also be consistent with well-established empirical findings in criminology and psychology about deterrence—without making deterrence the sole justification—since a theory that contradicts robust evidence about what actually reduces crime fails as an institutional guide. Mark which of these are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, such as whether libertarian free will must be true for punishment to be justified, or whether that is a negotiable metaphysical add-on. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, an absolute prohibition on punishing the innocent combined with a license for preemptive risk-based detention—say so explicitly; mapping that tension is precisely what I need. Finally, very briefly, how a pure retributivist account, a pure consequentialist account, and a hybrid communicative or expressive account fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard.
