Write a triptych poem—three sections of eight lines each—exploring Braudel’s three temporal scales: the near-timeless rhythm of geography and climate; the cyclical, generational pace of social and economic structures; and the abrupt, narrated time of political events and individuals. Give each section a form that enacts its scale: the first in long, unhurried lines with sparse punctuation, as though mountains or currents are speaking; the second built around a repeating refrain every four lines, suggesting rhythms that survive persons but still turn; the third in short, clipped lines that break off suddenly, like a chronicle interrupted. Make the philosophical work structural: have one word or image born in the first section reappear in the third, but distorted—used by the event-narrator with a meaning that ignores its geological origin—so that the event’s autonomy is quietly undermined by a vocabulary it does not own. Do not resolve whether the slow structures determine the event or merely constrain it; let that ambiguity live in the tension between the sections’ rhythms. The result should be recognizably Braudel’s framework to someone who knows it, and still work as an independent poem for someone who does not.
