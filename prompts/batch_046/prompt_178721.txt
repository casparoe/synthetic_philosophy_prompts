I'm writing a paper for my aesthetics seminar on whether creativity is ever truly ex nihilo or always just tweaking what's already there, and I got into a long argument with my roomate about Groundhog Day (the 1993 film with Bill Murray). She thinks Phil Connors is just learning skills, but I think the movie is actualy dramatizing Hofstadter's idea of creativity as knob-twiddling—systematically varying parameters of a fixed conceptual theme until something clicks. Could you read the film through that lens?

Specifically, I'm interested in whether Phil's repeated days function like Hofstadter's conceptual spaces: he keeps the 'same day' frame constant and only adjusts social and behavioral knobs—the piano lesson, the ice sculpture, the way he treats Rita—while the underlying structure stays identical. Does the film treat his eventual breakthrough as a radical restructuring of the theme or just the right combination of twiddled variables? And there's this bit with the old homeless man he saves every day but still dies—does that resist the knob-twiddling model because some outcomes are structurally fixed regardless of how you vary the inputs? I'd like a reading that takes both the movie's narrative arc and Hofstadter's model seriously, and ends by asking whether Groundhog Day actualy supports the idea that creativity requires a pre-existing theme to vary, or whether Phil's transformation is something the model can't quite capture.
