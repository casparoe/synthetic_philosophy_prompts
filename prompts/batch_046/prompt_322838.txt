I'm trying to sort out whether everyday conventions actually carry moral weight, especially when you're just passing through somewhere. Here's roughly where I've landed — I want a consistency check before I argue it with my flatmate.

1. I reckon conventions like queuing, not jaywalking when people are around, or driving on the left become real moral obligations only when they function as non-coercive coordination equilibria: everyone benefits from everyone expecting compliance, and breaking the rule messes up other people's planning.

2. I also think visitors aren't automatically exempt. If you step into the queue at the local post office or use the pedestrian crossing, you're in the scheme, so you should follow it — doesn't matter if back home people don't queue like that.

3. At the same time, I hold that bare geographic location can't ground moral duties on its own. There's nothing special about being in Tokyo versus Toronto that makes bowing or driving on the left obligatory; it's about which practice you're actually embedded in.

4. I think tipping fails the coordination test — it's not solving a shared problem, it's a coercive wage-substitution mechanism — so neither locals nor visitors have a moral duty to tip, even where it's expected.

5. Finally, I think that even for genuine coordination norms, the obligation is fully cancelled when you're literally alone: jaywalking on an empty road at 3am isn't morally wrong, just legally risky. The duty is conditional on there being other people around to coordinate with.

Do these five actually hang together? I'm especially worried that (2) clashes with (3) — am I smuggling location-dependence back in through 'being embedded in a practice'? And does (1) really allow me to exclude tipping in (4), or am I being arbitary about what counts as coordination? There's also something that feels off between (5) saying obligations vanish when alone and (1) saying they come from mutual-expectation equilibria — are those the same idea or do they pull apart?
