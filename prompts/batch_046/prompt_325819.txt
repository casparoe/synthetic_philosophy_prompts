For a graduate seminar on historiographical method I am drafting a position paper and want to check whether my commitments about periodization actually cohere. Here is where I stand: I think the standard triad—"Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution"—is essentially a nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century pedagogical invention, not a set of natural kinds discovered in the archive. At the same time, I believe there really were localized shifts in intellectual culture—around fourteenth- through sixteenth-century Italy and sixteenth- through seventeenth-century Northern Europe—that these labels imperfectly approximate, so I am not a pure nominalist about the past. I reject the Renaissance as a sharp rupture with medieval scholasticism; I think continuity in university logic, textual transmission, and natural philosophy is stronger than the humanist self-fashioning narrative admits. I treat the "Scientific Revolution" as an aggregation of geographically dispersed practices—Copernican mathematical astronomy, Baconian experimental advocacy, Galilean mathematization—rather than as a unified European shift in method, each with its own chronology. I also think periodization is unavoidable and not intrinsically harmful: we need macro-level temporal scaffolding to teach, compare, and orient thought, which means the ethical task is transparent acknowledgment of construction rather than elimination of categories. But I accept the genealogical critique that "Middle Ages" was consolidated by seventeenth-century Protestant church historians and later secularized by Enlightenment writers, embedding an implicit progressive teleology—darkness yielding to light, ignorance to reason—that I regard as structurally embedded and unsanitizable; no purely chronological usage can fully purge this evaluative baggage. Despite that unsanitizability, I routinely deploy "medieval" and "Renaissance" as chronological descriptors in my own writing, on the grounds that etymology and origin do not determine present semantic content. And my practical conclusion is that serious scholarship should abandon broad epochal names altogether and replace them with granular, problem-specific timelines—say, "Latin university Aristotelianism, c. 1200–1650"—even though I concede that such macro-labels remain indispensable for public pedagogy and introductory courses.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe is each real tension? Then tell me which single change—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most conflicts with the least collateral damage, and whether that revision is one I should actually make or one that reveals a deeper problem in my position that I have not yet faced.
