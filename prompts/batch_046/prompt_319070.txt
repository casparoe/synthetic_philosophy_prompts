I'm defending a thesis I'll call modal-profile pluralism about natural kinds in a philosophy-of-science job talk. The core claim is that whether the sciences carve nature at its joints depends on what kind of joint one is tracking: chemical kinds are individuated by rigid modal profiles fixed by nuclear-structure essences; biological kinds are individuated by homeostatically plastic modal profiles sustained by evolutionary stabilization mechanisms; and if psychological kinds exist, they are anchored by functional-role profiles that allow wider variance in realization. I argue that this pluralism respects the realist intuition that classification succeeds or fails independently of our interests, while accommodating the anti-essentialist data from biology, without collapsing into a reductive physicalism that treats all higher-level kinds as merely predictive instruments.

The audience is a research-university philosophy-of-science colloquium: about half philosophers of biology who are broadly anti-essentialist about species and inclined to treat biological taxonomy as interest-relative; two philosophers of chemistry who defend microstructural essentialism but are skeptical of extending essentialist machinery beyond chemistry; one cognitive scientist who thinks kind-talk is just a heuristic for predictive processing; and a philosopher of physics who holds that all genuine kinds eventually reduce to the fundamental kinds of physics. They care about empirical adequacy, metaphysical parsimony, and whether the view can be operationalized in scientific practice. They will not be impressed by purely conceptual distinctions that lack methodological payoff.
