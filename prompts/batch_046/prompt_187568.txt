Consider a refined framing of the compatriot-priority debate that moves away from distributive weighting and toward the logic of directed obligation. On this framing, shared national membership is treated not as a source of greater moral weight, but as a "threshold condition" that converts generic, undirected duties—such as a general duty to alleviate suffering—into specific, directed obligations owed to particular others, in a manner structurally analogous to how entering a contractual agreement transforms a background prohibition on harm into a concrete duty of performance, except that the transformation occurs without explicit consent or voluntary enrollment. Assuming this contractual-analogy model is adopted as a serious conceptual heuristic but not as a literal consent-based account, please describe the most important implications for cosmopolitan positions that reject any special moral status for compatriots. In particular, address how this framing challenges the standard cosmopolitan argument that nationality is morally arbitrary like birth-order, and examine whether accepting this heuristic commits cosmopolitans to either denying all special obligations (including those arising in family or friendship) or conceding that certain non-voluntary associations can indeed generate directed moral duties.
