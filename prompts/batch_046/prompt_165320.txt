I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and after a long argument with a friend about whether self-deception is literally possible—whether one and the same mind can both know and not-know something about itself without merely being confused or split into two agents—I realized I need the full specification before judging any account. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of self-deception must satisfy, with the conceptual or empirical pressure behind each. Cover: the range of phenomena it must explain (the spouse who suspects infidelity yet behaves with complete loyalty, the patient who avoids screening despite knowing the base rates, and affective cases where an agent denies shame or guilt while displaying its behavioral and physiological markers); the distinctions it must not collapse (doxastic versus affective self-deception, strategic versus non-strategic forms, motivated ignorance versus full-blown deception, first-person opacity versus third-person detectability, and unified agency versus homuncular division); the architectural constraints it must respect (no literal internal agent or committee, compatibility with hierarchical predictive processing or dual-process architectures without being reducible to either, and preservation of a single locus of agency); and the core paradox-condition—whether the theory dissolves the appearance of simultaneous contradictory belief by appeal to mental partition, graded credence, differences in representational format, or by denying that one pole is genuinely believed. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable (e.g., avoiding homuncular explanation, preserving unified agency) and which belong to specific camps (e.g., that self-deception must be intentional, that it requires an explicit contradictory belief, or that it is always defensive). Identify the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating popular theories, and briefly—without scoring every view—indicate how a partition-based account, a deflationary reduction to motivated ignorance, and a purely affective account stand against the list. The standard of adequacy is explanatory: the theory must show how self-deception is psychologically possible for one agent, not merely normatively undesirable or therapeutically treatable.
