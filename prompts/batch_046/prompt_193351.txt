In my constitutional theory course this term we have been examining Ulysses contracts and mechanisms of constitutional precommitment—particularly rules that bind future legislatures through entrenched procedural thresholds. I have observed a recurring pattern across comparative constitutional practice that does not seem to fit the standard distinctions we have covered. A jurisdiction adopts a stringent self-binding provision—for example, a constitutional clause requiring a four-fifths legislative majority to authorize any new public debt instrument. Rather than being formally repealed or falling into desuetude, the provision remains in force and is occasionally invoked by courts. Over successive decisions, however, judges interpret its operative terms—“new,” “public debt,” “instrument”—with such restrictive precision that the clause applies only to financing mechanisms that legislatures never actually contemplate using. The precommitment therefore survives as a formally valid constraint but is systematically circumvented in practice, not by violating the rule but by redefining its domain until it is vacuous. This is not ordinary judicial nullification, because the court affirms the rule’s validity, and it is not mere drafting ambiguity, because the narrowing occurs through accumulated interpretation rather than being present in the original text. Is there an established concept—perhaps in discussions of constitutional endurance or the failure of intertemporal binding—that captures this specific mode of hollowing out? If not, I would like to understand how precisely to delimit it: what distinguishes an interpretive erosion of a Ulysses contract from legislative evasion, and at what point does a preserved-but-vacuous precommitment cease to function as a genuine constraint on future majorities? I am less interested in a bare label than in the boundaries of the phenomenon and its nearest conceptual neighbors, such as symbolic constitutionalism or strategic drafting.
