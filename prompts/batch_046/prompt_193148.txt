For a conceptual analysis of institutional norms, consider the classificatory practice embedded in international anti-corruption frameworks—such as those advanced by Transparency International and the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention—whereby both explicit quid pro quo bribery and diffuse social practices, including clientelistic patronage appointments and ceremonial gift-exchange between office-holders and constituents, are uniformly categorised as corruption regardless of whether local customary norms treat such exchanges as legitimate obligations of reciprocity or political loyalty. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory this practice presupposes: does it necessarily commit to a Weberian conception of rational-legal authority in which public office is strictly separated from private interest, and to a universalist claim that Western liberal norms of impartiality apply across all political cultures? Or might the practice be sustained by a thinner, functionalist theory—say, that corruption is defined solely by its undermining of allocative efficiency—without importing thick normative universalism? Examine further whether the practice is internally consistent, given that the same jurisdictions frequently permit institutionalised influence-peddling—corporate lobbying, campaign finance, or revolving-door appointments—that structurally resembles the clientelism they condemn in other contexts. Does this asymmetry indicate that the implicit criterion is not substantive harm or unequal influence, but rather deviation from formally codified rules? Finally, specify what elements of the framework would need revision if its underlying universalist, formalist theory were replaced by a pluralist or communitarian view that recognises locally legitimate roles for patronage and reciprocity.
