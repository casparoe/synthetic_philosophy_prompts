I'm drafting a philosophical novel in which two constitutional theorists, Elias and Mara, debate the coherence of a self-amending legal code in the fictional republic of Varelia. Their exchange unfolds as follows.

Elias argues that Article Omega—the provision allowing the constitution to amend itself by a three-fourths vote of its own interpretive council—escapes the classic paradox of constitutional self-reference only if the amendment rule is not itself subject to amendment without external authorization. He claims that if Article Omega can be used to alter its own amendment threshold, then the system either collapses into infinite regress or requires a meta-rule that is necessarily non-self-applying, which violates the republic's founding principle of total legal self-containment.

Mara replies that this assumes a hierarchical model of rules that Varelia explicitly rejects. She contends that self-reference is not paradoxical but iterative: Article Omega applies to itself at time t, producing a modified rule at t+1, and there is no contradiction because the rule at t+1 is a different token from the rule at t. She adds that Elias's demand for a non-self-applying meta-rule smuggles in a foundationalist assumption that the system's designers intended to avoid.

Elias responds that Mara's token-type distinction solves the logical form but not the normative authority: if the council at t can lower the threshold for its own future iterations, then the authority of the original constituent power is dissolved without democratic ratification, which is precisely the legitimacy crisis that self-amending rules were designed to prevent.

Mara counters that legitimacy in Varelia derives from procedural continuity, not from an original constituent moment, and that Elias is covertly importing a social-contract theory that the republic's legal philosophy denies.

They have asked me, as the author attempting to render their positions fairly, to adjudicate: where is each right or wrong in these specific exchanges? Which of their underlying philosophical commitments—about legal hierarchy, token-type identity in normative rules, and the source of constitutional legitimacy—are actually in tension, and who, if anyone, holds the stronger overall position? Please respond in American English, and treat the dispute as a serious philosophical disagreement rather than a plot device.
