I am preparing a case-study exercise for my graduate seminar in philosophy of religion. The scenario is an interfaith ethics consultant at a secular university who must adjudicate, roughly twice per semester, whether a student’s sustained religious nonbelief should be classified as an epistemically reasonable response to divine hiddenness, rather than as culpable neglect or cognitive failure. The consultant has fifteen minutes per case, no access to outside theological expertise, and must produce a brief written justification defensible to both religious and secular administrators. I want you to translate Schellenberg’s argument from divine hiddenness—specifically the claim that a perfectly loving deity would not permit reasonable nonbelief—into a reusable decision procedure for this consultant. To avoid mere repetition of standard formulations, build the procedure around a distinction between structural hiddenness (the absence of divine manifestation in the causal-epistemic order actually available to the agent) and interpretive hiddenness (the agent’s own doxastic framework rendering available manifestations invisible), and include a preliminary screen for what I will call minimal epistemic stewardship: the agent has engaged seriously with at least one counter-position without straw-manning it. The deliverable should be a flowchart in words with ordered tests, explicit branch points, and a final tripartite classification: (1) reasonable nonbelief consistent with hiddenness, (2) culpable epistemic neglect, (3) indeterminate requiring referral. You must flag the judgment-call node—where the consultant weighs the agent’s cognitive environment against conditions of universal accessibility—without disguising it as mechanical. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: a secular-raised philosophy major with no prior exposure to religious argumentation; a formerly devout student who reports sudden experiential absence after trauma; and a rigorously trained theologian who now rejects belief on evidential grounds, including the instance in which the procedure yields a classification that conflicts with the student’s own self-understanding. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot determine and where it is likely to misfire.
