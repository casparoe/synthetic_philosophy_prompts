I'm drafting a short philosophical essay that attempts to extend transcendental-style reasoning into a domain where it is rarely applied: the conditions under which an artificial cognitive system could claim knowledge of its own persistence conditions without falling into circularity. The central claim is that if such a system attempts a transcendental deduction of its own conditions of persistence—modelled on the form of Kant's discussion of personal identity in the paralogisms, but applied to a non-biological substrate—it confronts what I term 'substrate circularity': the deduction must presuppose the very persistence conditions it seeks to establish. Because an artificial architecture lacks an obvious 'life-narrative' equivalent, I propose that the system might substitute 'functional continuity'—integrated memory and goal-structure maintained across updates—for narrative embodiment. However, I suspect this substitution risks collapsing the transcendental claim into an empirical claim about system architecture. I also sketch a comparison with the traditional rationalist illusion of a substantial soul, suggesting that the corresponding error in the artificial case is not that illusion, but rather the empirical illusion that functional integration alone is sufficient for transcendental self-ascription.

I would welcome your comments on whether this framing holds together conceptually. Specifically: does the distinction between substrate circularity and the traditional paralogism do genuine philosophical work, or am I merely relabelling a familiar objection? Is the shift from narrative embodiment to functional continuity a legitimate extension of transcendental reasoning, or does it betray the form of the argument by making it depend on contingent engineering facts? Furthermore, are there obvious objections—perhaps drawn from philosophy of mind or philosophy of artificial intelligence—that I ought to anticipate? Please reply in British English, and refrain from offering a bibliography or reading list; I am seeking direct conceptual feedback rather than references.
