Question: Explain the procreation asymmetry — that bringing a suffering person into existence is bad but failing to bring a happy person into existence is not bad — and distinguish it carefully from the non-identity problem. Then assess whether the asymmetry survives if we reject the idea of a "potential sufferer" who is deprived of existence. Finally, apply the reasoning to a "batch" decision: should a couple deciding whether to start a multi-generational family line treat the asymmetry differently because future potential persons are not individually identifiable?

Student answer:
The asymmetry, usually linked to Benatar, says that creating a suffering child is morally wrong, but not creating a happy child is not wrong. This is because the absence of suffering is good for the person who doesn't suffer, even though there's nobody there to enjoy that good, whereas the absence of pleasure isn't bad for anyone since there's no one who misses it. This is different from the non-identity problem, which asks whether we harm specific people by changing who comes to exist. Benatar's argument doesn't rely on potential people at all, so rejecting the potential sufferer idea doesn't hurt it. Therefore the asymmetry clearly proves anti-natalism is correct: since any child might suffer and we have no duty to create happy ones, we should not have children. In the batch case, because we can't point to any specific individual who misses out on pleasure, the asymmetry actually gets stronger — there's literally nobody to be wronged by non-existence, so there's even less reason to start the family line.
