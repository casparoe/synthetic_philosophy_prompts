I'm preparing a two-hour seminar for a small reading group of literature graduate students, joined by one colleague from archaeology; none of the students have formal training in historiography, and they arrive treating the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative as interchangeable examples of "stories that may or may not have happened." I want them to leave with a precise sense of when a legend becomes history—not as a matter of archaeological yes-or-no, but as a structural shift involving material traces, institutional transmission, and narrative elasticity—though I ask you to build that idea into the sequence rather than front-load it.

Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself. Begin by identifying the dependency that must be in place before the comparison can even begin: what naive theory of evidence do they bring that will corrupt the exercise, and what should replace it? Next, designate which one of the three cases—Troy, Arthur, or Exodus—should carry the core distinction, specifying exactly what that case must possess and what it must lack in order to do that work, and then assign precise roles to the other two (counter-example, parallel case, or boundary condition). Map the misconceptions they will form in chronological order; I expect first the belief that "finding ruins proves history," then that "all three are equally uncertain," and finally that "sacred texts are automatically excluded from historical inquiry"—indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted and with what kind of response. Provide one concrete check—whether a posed task or a targeted question—that would show me whether they have grasped the structural transition rather than merely memorized which narratives have archaeological support. Finally, state clearly what I should openly refuse to settle in this session, particularly the question of whether widespread communal belief in an event is a necessary condition for its historical status, and declare that refusal so the group recognizes the boundary of our discussion.
