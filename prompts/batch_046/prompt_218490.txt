Two members of my study group, Mara and Julian, got into an extended disagreement about whether widespread agricultural use of neonicotinoid insecticides is morally defensible. They asked me to referee, but I found both sides compelling and want an outside adjudication.

(1) Mara opened by arguing that insects almost certainly lack phenomenal consciousness. She noted the absence of integrated cortical structures and said that nociceptive reflexes can be fully explained without subjective experience. Therefore, she concluded, killing insects with pesticides is not directly wrong in the way killing a sentient mammal is; any wrongness must be indirect, via ecological damage.

(2) Julian replied that this sets an unjustifiably high bar for moral status. He pointed out that we now grant sentience to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans despite very different neural architectures, and that functional criteria—goal-directed avoidance, nociceptive pathways—are sufficient to establish welfare interests. Thus insects can be directly harmed.

(3) Mara conceded that insects might have some form of sentience, but insisted that the moral calculus still favors controlled agricultural use. Without effective pest management, crop yields fall, leading to greater human food insecurity and, indirectly, more habitat destruction that harms far more sentient animals. So the net suffering prevented likely outweighs the direct harm to insects.

(4) Julian called this a speculative empirical defense disguised as ethical reasoning. He said Mara was equating necessary for current yields with necessary for preventing harm, ignoring integrated pest management, crop rotation, and organic methods. Without showing that no less harmful alternative can meet nutritional needs, he argued, her position collapses into status-quo bias.

(5) Mara then drew a distinction between agricultural pest control, which targets insects competing with crops for human consumption, and public-health vector control, which targets disease carriers. She claimed the latter is more defensible because it protects human life, while the former merely maximizes agricultural efficiency.

(6) Julian countered that this ranking is inconsistent with both of her premises. If insects have direct welfare interests, then using them instrumentally for agricultural profit is wrong regardless of scale, whereas public-health use involves a genuine conflict of interests. But if her real concern is ecological, then agricultural use is actually worse—pollinators are destroyed for monoculture—while targeted vector control can be localized. So her attempt to rank the two uses fails either way.

(7) Mara's final move invoked the doctrine of double effect. In both applications, she said, the death of insects is not the intended end but a foreseen side effect of protecting something valuable—food or health. The moral evaluation should therefore focus on proportionality and necessity, not on whether insects are used as means.

Julian responded that double effect requires the harm not be a causal means to the good effect. In agriculture, dead pests are precisely the mechanism by which yields increase; the harm is instrumental, not incidental. The same applies to reducing vector populations for disease control. So the doctrine does not apply.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each step from (1) to (7), rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what conceptual distinction or practical consideration both parties are missing—something that could resolve or reframe their disagreement without simply repeating one of their positions.
