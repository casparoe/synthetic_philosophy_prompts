I'm preparing a three-hour seminar for third-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished the Gödel-numbering and strange-loop sections of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. They understand that a formal arithmetic system can encode statements about its own provability, but they consistently conflate syntactic self-reference with semantic self-awareness: they either reduce Hofstadter's analogy to mere recursion or anthropomorphize the formula into something that literally contains a self-referential pronoun. I need a teaching plan for the analogy itself—how to move students from Gödel's self-referential formula to the idea that a tangled hierarchy might illuminate conscious self-reference—rather than a lecture on Gödel's theorem per se. Lay out the dependency order: what distinction must be in place before the analogy can even be introduced, for instance the difference between describing a system from the outside and a statement generated within the system that refers back to the system's own rules, and why that distinction is more important than mastering the mechanics of Gödel numbering at this stage. Choose the one carrying example for the session—explicitly avoiding both the canonical formulation about an unprovable statement and Hofstadter's record-player analogy—and explain what that example must have and lack in order to make the level-crossing visible without inviting the recursion misconception. Then map the predictable misconceptions in the order students will raise them: the reduction to mere recursion, the attribution of literal subjectivity to the formula, and the belief that self-awareness is therefore provable within the system. For each, specify the point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the brief redirect that heads it off. I also want to weave in a somewhat unfamiliar framing: treating the embedded self-referential statement as analogous to a move within a Wittgenstein-style language-game that refers not to external objects but to the rules of the game itself. How can I integrate this framing so that it illuminates the Gödel-to-mind analogy rather than turning the seminar into a discussion of later Wittgenstein? Provide a single check-for-understanding question that would distinguish a student who grasps the analogy's structural limits from one who is merely repeating that strange loops produce consciousness. Finally, identify the part of the full argument—perhaps the ontological leap from formal syntax to phenomenological self-awareness—that I should openly refuse to defend in this session, and advise how to state that refusal clearly without deflating the students' interest in the analogy's philosophical weight.
