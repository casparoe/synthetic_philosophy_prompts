As a retired physicist who spent decades modeling non-equilibrium systems and has only recently begun studying moral philosophy with any seriousness, I have tried to formalize the central commitment of pacifism with the same rigor one applies to conservation laws. I believe I have constructed an argument that derives, from a seemingly modest principle, a conclusion so extreme that it threatens to make pacifism self-defeating—yet I cannot identify the precise step where the inference slips, and I am wary of smuggling physical analogies into normative reasoning where they do not belong. The argument proceeds as follows.

(1) The core pacifist claim is that the intentional use of lethal force against a human being is morally impermissible in every circumstance, including self-defense or the defense of innocent others, because the value of human life is incommensurable with any competing good. (2) If an act-type is impermissible, then deliberately arranging conditions under which others perform that act-type on one's behalf is also impermissible; one cannot outsource what one refuses to do oneself without moral hypocrisy. (3) Every living human being occupies a region of space and consumes resources—energy, water, territory—that could, under conditions of scarcity, be seized by others through lethal force; one's continued survival therefore depends counterfactually on the fact that such seizure is prevented. (4) In any functioning society, that prevention is maintained not solely by moral suasion but by institutional structures—property enforcement, border control, penal systems—that ultimately rest on the credible threat of lethal or severely harmful coercion. (5) Consequently, by continuing to live within and benefit from such institutions, the pacifist accepts a protective framework sustained precisely by the kind of lethal violence she condemns. (6) If she refuses that protection—by withdrawing entirely from society, refusing all legal redress, or actively dismantling coercive institutions—she makes her own survival vastly more precarious and likely hastens her death; if she accepts it, she lives only because lethal force is held in reserve for her benefit. (7) Therefore consistent pacifism demands either complete withdrawal leading to probable death, or complicity in the very violence it forbids; since neither option is morally obligatory, the doctrine collapses into incoherence.

I am inclined to reject step (5) or perhaps (4), because the reasoning feels like it confuses causal dependency with moral authorization, much as one might confuse heat flow with intentional direction in thermodynamics. But I want to understand exactly where the modal slide occurs—whether in the move from individual prohibition to institutional complicity, or in the counterfactual claim that survival necessarily depends on restrained violence. If I preserve (1) and (2), what practical commitments must I pay? And if the argument is actually valid, which premise is least costly to abandon: the incommensurability claim in (1), the outsourcing principle in (2), or the counterfactual dependency in (3)? I would appreciate a precise diagnosis that treats each step as one treats premises in a physical proof, without appeal to authority or citation, and that explains what rejecting the faulty step costs the overall position.
