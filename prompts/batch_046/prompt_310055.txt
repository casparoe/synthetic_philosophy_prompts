For a graduate seminar handout on Buddhist ethics and consequentialism, construct a branching taxonomy that sorts positions on whether Buddhist moral evaluation can be reconstructed as an aggregative, inter-life consequentialism or remains essentially non-aggregative because karma is bound to individual mental continua. The taxonomy must contain exactly five binary branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim a student could accept or reject without knowing the philosophical label it corresponds to. Order the nodes as follows: (A) whether karmic merit and demerit are properties of mental states alone (cetanā) or also of objective outcomes across sentient beings; (B) assuming outcomes matter, whether karmic effects are inter-subjectively aggregable—so that suffering reduced for one being can compensate for suffering increased for another within the same or different lifetimes; (C) if aggregation is permitted, whether an inter-life discount rate applies, diminishing the moral weight of karmic fruits in distant future rebirths relative to present ones; (D) whether the principle of non-harming (ahiṃsā) functions as an absolute constraint on action-types or as a defeasible rule whose weight depends on aggregate karmic calculus; (E) whether the cultivation of equanimity (upekkhā) requires emotional neutrality toward suffering outcomes (making consequentialist calculation compatible with practice) or demands direct compassionate engagement that conflicts with aggregative reasoning.

From these five nodes, map all thirty-two logical combinations. For each combination, indicate whether it is occupied by an actual named or unnamed position, unoccupied but coherent, or incoherent, and briefly diagnose why (e.g., internal contradiction, unattractiveness, or mere neglect). You must explicitly locate at least four positions in the tree: (1) a classical Theravāda Abhidhamma view that rejects aggregation and holds mental-state exclusivity; (2) a Mahāyāna bodhisattva view that accepts aggregation but denies inter-life discounting; (3) a hypothetical karmic-utilitarian view that accepts aggregation, applies discounting, and treats ahiṃsā as defeasible; (4) an unnamed combination you argue is incoherent—specifically one that combines mental-state exclusivity with inter-subjective aggregation. Additionally, inject one novel conceptual distinction not standard in textbooks—call it karmic externalities, where an action produces no direct karmic result for the agent but systematically alters the mental-state conditions available to others—and split one of the five nodes with it, noting whether any position occupies that further split.

Present the taxonomy first as a nested decision tree with branch labels, then as a table listing each occupied leaf with its answer-pattern across nodes A–E and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves that share identical answers at all five nodes and therefore cannot be separated by this instrument. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote any passage verbatim unless you verify it by fetching the source, and in that case copy the quote exactly—otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Rely only on widely recognized attributions (e.g., Theravāda Abhidhamma, Mahāyāna bodhisattva ethics) and avoid obscure modern commentators. Keep the audience and scope fixed: one page, suitable for students who can answer the branch claims without prior knowledge of the taxonomy’s labels.
