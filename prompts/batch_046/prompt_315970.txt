At a rationalist meetup last month, two people got stuck in a multi-round argument about emergence in science. I was listening and couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm replaying it for adjudication.

Person A opened with a standard physicalist line: temperature is just mean kinetic energy, and saying "the heat caused the metal to expand" is only a useful summary of countless particle interactions. There is no extra causal power floating above the micro-level; downward causation is an illusion created by our cognitive inability to track Avogadro's-number-scale dynamics. If a hypothetical supercomputer simulated every particle, it would never need the concept "temperature" to predict the expansion, so emergence is purely epistemic—an artifact of our computational poverty.

Person B pushed back that derivability in principle isn't causal redundancy. The macro-state of being at 0°C doesn't merely summarize micro-states; it constrains which micro-configurations are accessible, acting as a filter that explains why ice melts rather than sublimates. Delete the macro-concept and you don't just lose a shorthand—you lose the pattern that actually carries the explanatory weight, because tracking individual particle trajectories wouldn't tell you why the phase transition occurs at that specific boundary.

A replied that this only proves our brains are lazy, not that macro-properties are ontologically autonomous. Thermodynamic laws are derivable from statistical mechanics, and any appearance of downward causation disappears once you do the derivation. The macro-level is like a center of gravity: predictively indispensable but causally basic only in a bookkeeping sense.

B then introduced a more specific case: evolutionary selection. If you intervene at the macro-level—introducing a predator—the causal effect isn't captured by listing changes to each organism's particles or even its genes. The intervention alters the selection environment itself, and that environment includes ecological relations that aren't properties of any individual part. You can predict evolutionary outcomes from gene frequencies only if you already embed the macro-level environment as a parameter. So macro-interventions have causal profiles that micro-descriptions miss unless your "reduction" reproduces the very emergent structure it's supposed to eliminate.

A countered that B was just expanding the system boundary. Once you include the predator, prey, and energy flows in your base, the dynamics reduce to gene-frequency shifts and biochemical interactions. There's no downward causation—just the recognition that open systems interact with surroundings. B's "autonomy" collapses into a wider, but still fully physical, description.

B answered that this makes reduction trivial: anything is reducible if you're allowed to smuggle the macro-relations back into the base. The interesting claim was that macro-science explains things that micro-science cannot, without reproducing the macro-level inside the micro-account. Once your reduction must embed the selection environment in its premises, you've conceded that the macro-level isn't calculable from the parts in isolation—precisely the autonomy that was at stake.

A closed by insisting that B was conflating explanatory autonomy with ontological emergence. Even with an open-system boundary, every event still obeys micro-laws; the macro-level remains a useful fiction, not a fundamental feature of reality.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five turns from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who's more right overall about whether emergence is real in science, and identify what both sides are missing—particularly whether there's a meaningful middle position between "purely epistemic" and "ontologically basic" that neither speaker acknowledged. Don't just declare a winner; show where the argument actually breaks down.
