(1) If a full philosophical zombie is conceiveable, then physicalism about consciousness is false.
(2) But I don't need the full zombie — I can conceive of a partial zombie that's phyiscally identical to me except it lacks just one specific phenomenal quality, say the exact colour of crimson I see when I look at a particular sunset.
(3) So if conceivability entails possibility, there must be worlds phyiscally identical to ours but missing that one crimson-experience.
(4) The same reasoning works for every other specific phenomenal quality — the taste of a specific wine, the feel of velvet — so for each there is a phyiscally identical world without it.
(5) That means physics, being identical across all these worlds, can't determine which specific qualia are present; each one is an independent, fundamental, non-physical property.
(6) So we're not just left with dualism; we're left with a wild pluralism where every possible experience is its own basic building block of reality, far more ontologically extravagent than any dualist ever proposed.
(7) Nobody should accept that kind of explosion.
(8) So (1) must be wrong, or conceivability doesn't entail possibility.

But I feel like something slipped between (3) and (5) — like I'm treating specific qualia as separable atoms in a way that might not be warranted. Which step should I actually reject? I'd rather keep (1) than (7) if I can, but I'm open to giving up (2). Can you tell me where the argument really breaks, and what each option costs me philosophically?
