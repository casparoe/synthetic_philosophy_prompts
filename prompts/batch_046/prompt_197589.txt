I'm planning a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in philosophy and political theory on Sen's liberal paradox. I want them to understand not just the result but why it threatens a particular intuitive package — that individual rights over personal domains and unanimous social preference can always be reconciled. Don't explain the paradox to me; instead, plan the teaching sequence.

What has to come before what? The students will arrive thinking that if every member of a group prefers option A to option B, then the group should choose A, and that rights are essentially veto points that can be layered on top without tension. Map the dependency chain: which formal conditions — the Pareto criterion and minimal liberalism — must be introduced separately and in what order so that the contradiction feels structurally inevitable rather than contrived. Specify the precise point at which the two conditions must be made explicit and why reversing that order collapses the pedagogical effect.

Choose one concrete carrying scenario — explicitly not the classic literary-censorship framing — that can bear the full weight of both conditions. It should sit in a modern, relatable domain (digital privacy, medical autonomy, or urban planning) where two individuals each hold a protected sphere over a distinct choice, yet their preferences over each other's sphere conflict with a Pareto-improving alternative. What properties must this example possess, and what properties must it deliberately lack, to prevent students from dissolving the tension by appealing to harm, externalities, false consciousness, or a hidden third option?

Map the misconceptions you expect in the order they will arise: the conflation of minimal liberalism with full libertarianism; the inference that the paradox proves democracy or social choice is impossible tout court; the attempt to resolve it by substituting a different aggregation rule; and the belief that the contradiction is merely verbal. For each misconception, identify exactly where in the sequence — before the formal conditions are introduced, during the working of the example, immediately after the contradiction is displayed, or in the closing synthesis — it should be headed off, and with what brief conceptual move.

Design one check for understanding at the end: a task or question that reveals whether students have grasped the structural conflict between the two conditions rather than merely memorized that rights and efficiency can clash. State clearly what you will leave out at this level — for instance, the extension to full impossibility theorems, game-form representations, or connections to Arrow's framework — and justify why omitting it protects the core idea rather than distorting it.
