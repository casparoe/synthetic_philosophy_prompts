I am drafting a novel set in a fictional republic where a charismatic leader repeatedly invokes the phrase "the will of the people is the supreme law" to justify dissolving constitutional courts and ignoring electoral minority safeguards. In my writing group—mostly other novelists, not political theorists—we have been arguing over what that sentence actually commits one to when it is stripped of its rhetorical thunder and used to close a debate rather than open one. Could you separate out the precise theses hiding inside the slogan? I am looking for a menu of interpretations: which ones are near-tautologies about democratic legitimacy deriving from popular consent; which are substantive, defensible claims about where sovereignty properly resides; which are empirically false assertions about the coherence or accessibility of a unified public opinion; and which are normatively dangerous conclusions that automatically license the override of institutional checks. In particular, I want to understand the version that gets deployed to end an argument about whether courts may restrain a popularly elected government—does it presuppose a synthetic, almost metaphysical collective agent whose intentions can be read directly off election results, or is it merely an aggregative claim that majority preference should prevail regardless of procedural constraints? Please lay the readings out as distinct options with verdicts on each, so I can decide which philosophical ambiguity my fictional leader is actually running, rather than writing a character whose populism is merely sloppy.
