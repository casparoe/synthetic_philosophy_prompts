Consider the institutional practice in common-law contract interpretation: courts apply the plain-meaning rule to treat the contract text as determinative (the outer message) unless ambiguity is shown, yet they invoke framing devices—recitals, purpose clauses, structural context—to resolve that ambiguity (the frame message), while treating the parties’ private, unstated intentions as inadmissible parol evidence (the inner message suppressed). At the same time, AI interpretability work tends to read a model’s weights and activations as carrying an outer message, reverse-engineers an inner message (the model’s goals or world-model), and largely treats the training distribution and architecture as background noise rather than as a constitutive frame message.

Using Hofstadter’s tripartite distinction from Gödel, Escher, Bach—frame message, outer message, and inner message—where meaning is not localized in any single layer but arises from their interaction, reconstruct the implicit philosophy of meaning that each practice presupposes. Does contract law quietly depend on a distributed view despite its rhetoric of textual objectivity? Does interpretability’s neglect of the frame message commit it to a naive internalism about mental content? Are the two practices philosophically consistent with one another? And if one takes the frame message to be constitutive rather than merely auxiliary, what concrete changes would each domain have to make—for instance, in rules of admissible evidence or in the design of mechanistic interpretability probes?
