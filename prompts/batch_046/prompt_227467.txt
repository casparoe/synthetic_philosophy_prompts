As a philosopher of ethics, I have come to regard the paradox of deontology—the tension between absolute agent-centered constraints and the outcomes permitted under conditions of widespread partial compliance—not as a single settled puzzle but as a branching point for normative theory. I am not asking for a survey of standard objections to threshold deontology or to familiar accounts of agent-centered prerogatives. Rather, I want your judgment on which open conceptual questions, or which downstream lines of work, in this vicinity deserve the greatest sustained attention over the coming years.

Consider in particular three directions. First, whether refining the distinction between direct and indirect agency in collective institutional settings genuinely dissolves the aggregation problem, or merely displaces it onto structural features of multi-agent interaction that we have not yet formalized. Second, whether the paradox carries hidden implications for the design of decision procedures—specifically, constitutional rules that bind committees or deliberative bodies rather than individual agents, and whether such rules alter the moral calculus in ways that individual-centered deontology cannot capture. Third, whether the recent emphasis on moral remainder and post-violation obligations renders the original paradox conceptually obsolete, or instead reveals a deeper, still unnamed structural feature of deontological reasoning that survives even after remainders are acknowledged.

Please defend a prioritized agenda. Which of these directions—or an alternative you propose—should occupy the center of the field, judged by conceptual importance, tractability for philosophical rather than empirical analysis, and the number of other debates that depend on its resolution? For each item in your ordering, explain the reasoning behind its rank, where the sharpest trade-off lies between theoretical purity and institutional applicability, and what specific argument or conceptual distinction over the next few years would move it up or down.

Finally, please state clearly where your reasoning is most likely to fail: identify the least secure premise in your assessment, and specify exactly what counter-argument or finding would change your mind about the ordering.
