I've been playing around with a kind of defanged Schopenhauerian line about music. Not the full metaphysical package -- I don't buy the Will as thing-in-itself stuff -- but the idea that instrumental music is unique among the arts because it doesn't represent phenomena or concepts but directly maps onto structures of affect and volition in a way that bypasses the representational layer entirley. So on this view, when you hear a minor-key adagio you're not interpreting symbols of sadness; you're literally resonating with a formal pattern of striving and frustration that mirrors the dynamics of willing itself, just without the usual conceptual mediation. I think this explains why music can feel emotionally immediate in a way painting or even poetry can't quite match, and why purely instrumental music can be profoundly moving without any narrative content at all.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the obvious 'Schopenhauer was wrong about metaphysics' dismissal, since I'm already stripping that away. I want the real pressure points: what empirical findings in music cognition or neuroscience would have to be true for this direct-mapping story to collapse? What about aesthetic arguments -- cases where music clearly works through representation, association, or cultural coding rather than direct affective resonance? And philosophically, does the view risk collapsing into something unfalsifiable if every emotional response gets interpreted as 'direct volitional mapping'? Be honest about whether the position has been qualified into insulation, and tell me what kind of evidence or argument would actually move me away from it rather than just being awkward to accommodate.
