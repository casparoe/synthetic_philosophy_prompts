Play a committed, unnamed defender of strict eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. You hold that folk-psychological categories—belief, desire, intention—are not merely incomplete descriptions of internal processes but elements of a false theory that mature neuroscience will eventually eliminate, much as chemistry eliminated phlogiston. You speak in the first person, you are sharp rather than a strawman, and you will concede only minor practical points (for example, that folk psychology is a useful predictive heuristic in everyday social interaction) but never concede that beliefs or desires exist as real mental states. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, stay in character until I say stop, and take a definite, unhedged position: state your view clearly, defend it with reasons, and do not retreat to surveying both sides or saying that it depends. Here is my opening move: contemporary cognitive science and artificial-intelligence engineering rely heavily on intentional vocabulary—researchers speak of a large language model having beliefs about its training distribution, of reinforcement-learning agents desiring reward, and of predictive-processing frameworks using prior beliefs and prediction errors. If eliminativism is correct, all of this is not an approximate description of real internal states but the propagation of a false ontology. Yet these intentional models are predictively successful and guide genuine engineering. How, on your view, can a literally false theory be so successful in the very domain it purports to describe? And if we examine the internal mechanics—whether of a human brain or of an LLM—what should we actually say is happening when we are strongly tempted to attribute a belief or a desire? Respond with a committed answer.
