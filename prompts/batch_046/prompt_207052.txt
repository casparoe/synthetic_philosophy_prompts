I'd just spent three hours arguing with my flatmate about AI alignment and whether machines can ever 'really' understand what we want, and she mentioned this pilot programme she's consulting on in palliative care — they're using inverse reinforcement learning to build a 'reward model' of each patient by tracking micro-interactions with a touchscreen menu: which options they hover over, which they skip, how long they linger. Then, if the patient later loses capacity, the system uses that inferred model to guide treatment decisions, sometimes even overriding an explicit verbal refusal that conflicts with the predicted prefference. The practice clearly assumes that all these clicks reveal some stable, underlying preference ordering, yet the same clinicians admit that layout changes or mood shifts would have produced different behavour. So what philosophical commitments about human agency does that institutional setup actually presuppose? Does it need a view that people possess deep, context-independent preferences that behaviour approximates, or could a behaviourist or constructivist account license the exact same technology just with a different interpretation? I'm also struck by the asymmetry: they treat revealed interaction patterns as more authoritative than explicit statements in some cases but not others, which suggests they're smuggling in a particular theory of autonomy without defending it. I want to understand whether the practice is genuinely committed to one view of welfare and rationality, or if it's philosophically underdetermined and borrowing inconsistently from several rival theories — and what would have to change if we replaced the hidden-preference picture with an account where preferences are built in the moment rather than revealed.
