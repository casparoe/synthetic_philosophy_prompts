I serve as the senior acquisitions editor for a small press that publishes formal verse in translation—villanelles, sestinas, rhymed quatrains—where our contracts require that the English version preserve not just meaning but the original's constraint architecture: the specific pattern of syntactic inversion, semantic compression, and phonetic echo that the form forces upon the language. I have been working from Douglas Hofstadter's discussion of Clément Marot's translations, in particular the idea that a beautiful translation under formal constraint should aim for what he calls le ton beau de Marot: not a surface copy of rhyme and meter, but a parallel solution to the same formal problem in the target language. Because we reject roughly sixty percent of submissions for formal drift—where the translator has smoothed out the difficulty the form imposed—I need to turn this principle into an operational audit procedure my copy editors can apply consistently, without requiring them to be poets or linguists.

Write a reusable evaluation protocol—ordered steps, with the sequence of checks, what counts as passing each stage, where the protocol stops with a rejection, and where it must hand the decision to a human editor because the issue is aesthetic judgment rather than constraint isomorphism. The protocol should treat the original poem as a constraint-satisfaction network: identify the active formal variables (meter, rhyme scheme, syntactic inversion patterns, semantic compression points), determine whether the English version solves an isomorphic problem in English's constraint space, and flag false friends—cases where surface rhyme or meter is preserved but the underlying syntactic pressure that generated the feature in the original has been abandoned. Include explicit instructions on which checks require only textual comparison and which require linguistic analysis that staff can perform with standard tools.

Apply the procedure to three submitted cases: (1) a villanelle translation that keeps the ABA rhyme scheme and iambic pentameter but eliminates every syntactic inversion, yielding fluent but formally unfaithful English; (2) a sestina translation that preserves the end-word rotation exactly but changes the grammatical category of two end-words, altering the constraint pressure on the surrounding syntax; and (3) a rhymed quatrain where the protocol returns an acceptance recommendation that I, as the editor, will find ugly and reject—so explain precisely where the protocol's authority ends and taste must override it. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot adjudicate, including cases where the target language genuinely lacks a parallel constraint and the translator must invent a substitute.
