I work as a protocol auditor for distributed interaction networks—peer-to-peer resource pools, supply-chain verification layers, and mutual-insurance schemes—in which autonomous agents face repeated prisoner's-dilemma structures, but pairing is random, exit is costless, and defection is observed only after a stochastic latency. I need a reusable audit procedure, grounded in the evolutionary theory of co-operation (one-shot versus iterated dilemmas, conditions for reciprocity stability, and reputation-mediated selection), that I can apply to any proposed protocol in under an hour without consulting external literature. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of tests: for each, the precise condition being checked, the evidence required, the threshold for passing or failing, the point at which the audit halts with a definitive rejection, and the points where the theory is silent and a value judgement must be substituted. The procedure must explicitly accommodate noisy observation, truncated shadows of the future due to high turnover, and settings where direct reciprocity is impossible and third-party reputation markets must carry the co-operative load. Run it on three proposals: (1) a bandwidth-sharing scheme with instantaneous feedback; (2) a supply-chain verification network where fraud is detected only post-delivery; and (3) a voluntary-entry mutual-insurance pool with free exit. In at least one case the procedure should return an approval that strikes me as intuitively wrong or exploitative, and you should explain why the theory still permits it. Finally, state clearly what this evolutionary framework cannot settle—especially regarding distributive fairness, the legitimacy of exit costs, and the moral status of free-riders who never enter the co-operative pool.
