As a journalist covering the intersection of sociology and philosophy of science, I have become preoccupied with a recurring objection in social explanation: the claim that culture, understood as shared values or normative commitments, cannot genuinely cause macro-level outcomes because values belong to the space of reasons rather than the space of causes. Max Weber’s Protestant Ethic thesis is routinely cited as the paradigmatic counterexample—Calvinist anxiety about predestination is said to have causally produced the rationalized labor and capital accumulation characteristic of modern capitalism. Yet I find the standard defense of Weber unsatisfying, because it treats theological beliefs as direct motivational engines without addressing the philosophical worry that explaining action through values collapses causal explanation into rational justification.

I want you to argue for a specific reinterpretation: that Weber’s thesis succeeds not when we read it as saying that individual believers consciously channeled salvation anxiety into economic discipline, but when we treat “the Protestant ethic” as a structural selection environment—an invisible filter that rewarded behavioral patterns compatible with rationalized accumulation and gradually eliminated practices that were not—regardless of whether most agents subjectively experienced their labor as religiously motivated. On this view, culture operates causally not through explicit value commitment but through misrecognition, where normative structures are experienced by agents as practical necessities or instrumental optimizations rather than as ethical imperatives. Your argument should defend this structural-selection reading against the charge that it eliminates culture altogether by reducing it to institutional constraint, and it should pinpoint exactly where in Weber’s original causal chain—between the psychological pressure of predestination and the emergence of systematic labor—the motivational interpretation becomes redundant.

Please write in American English. Be explicit about what kind of empirical finding or conceptual argument would change your mind—whether, for instance, archival evidence of widespread conscious theological motivation among early capitalists would force you back to a belief-driven account, or whether a rigorous distinction between selection environments and institutional rules could collapse your reinterpretation into standard institutionalism. Also identify the weakest link in your reasoning, particularly whether this reading preserves enough of Weber’s historical specificity to count as an interpretation of his thesis rather than a philosophical replacement that abandons its core claims.
