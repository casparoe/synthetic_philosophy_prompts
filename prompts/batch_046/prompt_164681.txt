I've been working through the standard moral distinction between killing and letting die in preparation for a seminar on institutional ethics, and I'm struck by how quickly the usual examples—pulling a plug versus not starting a ventilator, shooting someone versus walking past a drowning child—feel contrived once we move to systemic omissions.

Write an essay on whether the killing/letting-die distinction collapses when we analyze omissions through an interventionist framework of causation, where an omission is causally efficacious just in case an intervention to perform the act would have altered the outcome, with background conditions held fixed. Focus especially on institutional cases: a hospital board that votes not to purchase life-saving equipment, a state that declines to regulate a known toxin, or a corporation that discontinues a maintenance protocol that prevents fatal failures. Weigh the claim that these omissions are metaphysically and morally on a par with the corresponding actions—deliberately breaking the equipment, actively emitting the toxin, sabotaging the maintenance—against the defense that omissions lack the right direction of fit or agential structure to count as killings, even if they are causally equivalent. Your essay should defend one of these two positions conclusively: either that the distinction survives but requires a more nuanced metaphysics of agency, or that it dissolves and should be replaced by a scalar account of causal responsibility. Ground your argument in at least one detailed institutional or medical case, and address the objection that your view either makes too many bureaucratic failures into homicides, or conversely lets institutions off the hook by hiding behind the language of mere omission.
