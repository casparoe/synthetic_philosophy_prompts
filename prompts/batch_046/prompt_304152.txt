I've come to accept a strong version of what might be called the Williams-Appiah synthesis on moral revolutions: that norms like slavery, dueling, and foot-binding collapse primarily through shifts in elite honor codes and status-competition dynamics, with moral arguments serving almost entirely as ex post rationalizations rather than as independent causal forces. More precisely, I hold the zero-independent-efficacy claim—that arguments never initiate a revolution in the absence of prior elite-status incentives; that where the relevant status tournament is absent, no amount of philosophical reasoning will dislodge the norm; and that where elite incentives do align, revolutions occur even when the available arguments are weak or largely absent. What would have to be true for this to be wrong?

I want an honest map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which historical patterns, comparative observations, or conceptual distinctions should move me? For instance, are there cases—such as the rapid 19th-century abolition of chattel slavery across multiple societies, or the near-universal abandonment of dueling—where argument-driven coordination genuinely preceded and shaped elite incentives rather than following them? Would a committed defender of the synthesis concede any of these as damaging, or dismiss them by redefining "status incentives" broadly enough to absorb them? I am particularly interested in whether the strong version risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by treating any observed argument-led change as evidence of hidden honor dynamics—and whether that insulation is a problem for a thesis of this kind. Which defeaters would defenders themselves grant as counting, and which would they treat as misunderstandings? And is there any live empirical or conceptual test that could genuinely expose the zero-efficacy claim, or has the position become well-insulated in a way that should worry anyone who holds it?
