As a doctoral student in philosophy of religion working on modal metaphysics, I am developing an essay on a tension within Molinist accounts of divine omniscience that I think has received insufficient attention in the standard literature. Most discussions center on the grounding objection—whether counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs) possess truth-makers independent of God’s will—and on whether middle knowledge is compatible with libertarian freedom. I want to examine a more reflexive problem.

Consider the structure of middle knowledge: prior to any creative decree, God knows, for every possible creature and every complete circumstance C, whether that creature would freely perform action A in C. Now suppose we introduce an epistemic condition into the antecedent. Let C* be a circumstance in which the creature S not only finds herself in situation C but also knows that God possesses middle knowledge of S’s choice in C—indeed, knows the specific content of the relevant CCF, namely that in C* S would freely choose A.

The difficulty is whether the domain of CCFs should be restricted to circumstances that exclude this kind of second-order epistemic access to divine cognition. If the restriction is not made, then S’s knowledge of the counterfactual appears to generate an epistemic constraint: S might reason that if she were to choose otherwise than A, she would either render false God’s middle knowledge (which, on standard theism, is impossible) or act in a manner incompatible with the conditions specified in the counterfactual. This does not appear to be causal determinism—the causal chain remains open—but it introduces a form of epistemic determinism that threatens the very contingency that Molinism aims to preserve. Conversely, if Molinists do restrict CCFs to epistemically “transparent” circumstances in which agents lack reflexive knowledge of divine mental states, the restriction risks looking ad hoc. Why should the metaphysical grounds of free choice depend on an agent’s epistemic access to God’s cognitive states?

In my notes I sketch two preliminary responses. First, one might distinguish between knowing a counterfactual and knowing that God knows it, arguing that the latter adds no novel modal constraint on the will. Second, one might appeal to the familiar tripartite division of divine knowledge and suggest that reflexive circumstances fall outside the domain of middle knowledge altogether, being handled instead by God’s free knowledge after the creative decree. Both responses, however, seem to require either an implausible psychology of freedom or a revision to the standard taxonomy that undermines middle knowledge’s explanatory role in prophecy and counterfactual prayer.

Could you comment on these notes and on the coherence of the argument? Specifically: (1) Is this reflexive problem genuinely distinct from the standard grounding objection, or does it collapse into worries about divine deception or manipulation? (2) Does restricting CCFs to epistemically transparent circumstances undermine the explanatory power Molinists claim for middle knowledge, particularly regarding divine foreknowledge of free actions in prophetic contexts? (3) Are there conceptual resources—perhaps in formal treatments of counterfactual reasoning under modal uncertainty, or in discussions of higher-order beliefs about one’s own freedom—that could help formalize the tension without collapsing into determinism?

I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or a reading list; I want substantive philosophical assessment of whether the proposed restriction is defensible, how it relates to existing Molinist defenses, and whether the argument identifies a genuinely novel difficulty.
