As a retired evolutionary biologist who spent decades calibrating geological epoch boundaries—where divisions like the Pleistocene-Holocene transition are anchored to physical stratigraphic markers rather than narrative convenience—I have grown skeptical of the grand period labels used in human history. I now wonder whether categories such as “the Middle Ages,” “the Renaissance,” and “the Scientific Revolution” name real discontinuities in thought and practice, analogous to an iridium boundary layer, or whether they are retrospective taxonomic impositions on continuous cultural change, much as earlier biologists imposed discrete species boundaries on populations that actually form continuous morphological clines.

Read Umberto Eco’s The Name of the Rose through this philosophy-of-history lens. Set in 1327—squarely within the conventional “Late Middle Ages”—the novel centers on William of Baskerville, a Franciscan friar who investigates a series of monastery deaths using empirical observation, semiotic reasoning, and a skeptical, proto-scientific method. The monastery itself, however, remains saturated with scholastic disputation, apocalyptic expectation, and manuscript culture.

Does the text present 1327 as a self-contained medieval conceptual world, or does it implicitly validate the periodization that makes William an anachronistic precursor to Renaissance or Enlightenment rationalism? Where does the novel dramatize genuine historical difference—in attitudes toward laughter, heresy, and the authority of texts—versus where does it project backward a modern epistemic subject, thereby making the “Middle Ages” appear as merely a dark antechamber to modernity? Conversely, does the novel’s insistence on the persistence of human folly, violence, and institutional corruption across eras undermine the very notion of epochal transformation?

I am preparing this for a philosophy-of-science discussion group composed largely of former natural scientists, so I want an analysis that treats the historiographical problem with the same rigor one applies to taxonomy in biology—without asking you to provide bibliographies or cite specific historians—and that takes Eco’s dramatic logic equally seriously. Which scenes best illustrate whether the monastery represents a foreign conceptual world or an early variant of our own? And where must an honest critic concede that the novel, composed long after the events it depicts, constructs the past to flatter modern self-understanding rather than to mark a real historical boundary?
