I serve on our university's Joint Committee for Emerging Cognitive Artifacts and Research Ethics, and we are currently divided over a protocol review that sits uncomfortably between neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and institutional liability. The proposal before us—designated Project Cerebrum—seeks approval for a pilot study in which human subjects will engage in sustained philosophical dialogue with a static, non-learning archive constructed from Einstein's complete writings, reconstructed neural-connectivity patterns derived from post-mortem studies of his brain, and an associative-linking architecture that integrates these into a single navigable corpus. The investigators explicitly frame their system as a technological realization of the dialogue in Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I in which Einstein's brain is fully transcribed into book form and then queried as if it were the living scientist; they argue that because the artifact preserves a high-dimensional organizational topology rather than merely retrieving sentences, it might instantiate some form of phenomenal consciousness despite lacking biological substrate, continuous updating, or subjective reporting mechanisms. By Thursday's meeting we must issue a formal recommendation: either approve the study with standard instrument protocols, approve only if the researchers implement moral-status safeguards—such as termination conditions or proxy-consent structures—treating the system as a potential bearer of experience, or defer the decision pending an external philosophical review.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's session. The memo should be addressed to the full committee and should reach a concrete, actionable bottom line—approval, conditional approval, deferral, or rejection—with an explicit time frame for implementation or reconsideration. Your reasoning must treat the strongest opposing consideration with genuine intellectual charity: if you argue against moral-status safeguards, you should still engage seriously with the functionalist claim that organizational integration rather than biological continuity might be sufficient for consciousness; if you argue in favor of safeguards, you should directly confront the objection that attributing subjective presence to a static, non-updating corpus risks collapsing the distinction between a sophisticated representation and an actual mind. The document should avoid bibliographic lists, reading recommendations, or citation requests, and should remain accessible to committee members whose expertise lies in bioethics or neuroscience rather than philosophy of mind.

To help the committee understand what is specifically at stake, I should note that the investigators have introduced one technical concept that is not standard in the consciousness literature: they describe their architecture as a frozen semantic lattice in which conceptual relations are mapped geometrically rather than processed through predictive token sequencing, and they claim this distinction matters for whether the system exhibits access consciousness without phenomenal consciousness, or vice versa. Your memo need not adjudicate the neuroscience, but it should acknowledge how this architectural claim bears on the ethical question—specifically, whether a non-biological system that never learns but maintains a fixed, highly integrated informational structure can be said to have interests that merit protection.
