Write a sestina (six stanzas of six lines, with end-words rotating in the strict pattern 1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on, closing with a three-line envoi that uses all six end-words) that stages the conceptual deadlock between cyclical and linear philosophies of history. The six end-words must be: cycle, line, rise, fall, return, progress. Let the obligatory repetition and circular permutation of these words embody the cyclical theories—Polybius’s anacyclosis of constitutional regimes, Ibn Khaldun’s oscillation of asabiyyah and its dissipation, Spengler’s morphological destiny of cultures through spring, summer, autumn and winter, and Toynbee’s rhythm of challenge and response—while the narrative content of each successive stanza advances one irreversible historical transformation (for example, the mutation of a republican polity into imperial monarchy) in a strictly linear sequence. Do not resolve the tension: the form should keep pulling the story back into repetition, and the advancing narrative should keep pulling the form forward into progression, so that the envoi lands in an unresolved stalemate rather than a synthesis. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with these thinkers recognises the theories being enacted rather than merely named, and it should still work as a poem for a reader who knows none of them. Write in British English. After the poem, include a brief note stating how confident you are in the philosophical and historical claims embedded in it, and single out the one point about which you are least sure—whether that concerns the fidelity of a particular thinker’s model, the formal suitability of the sestina for this tension, or the historical example you have chosen.
