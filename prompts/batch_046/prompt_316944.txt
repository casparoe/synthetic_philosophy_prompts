As a novelist working through the philosophical premises of a novel about a character who systematically refuses ambition, I have been thinking about Moritz Schlick's ethical position—frequently summarized, though seldom examined in detail—that life acquires meaning not through labor, achievement, or the accumulation of purpose over time, but through play: activity that is fully absorbed in the present moment and pursued for no external end. This strikes me as both illuminating and, taken strictly, destructive to the kind of narrative identity fiction depends upon. A character becomes significant to a reader precisely through the irreversible weight of choices made across time, not through a sequence of disconnected present states. I want to propose a tentative reconciliation: perhaps Schlick's framework survives this objection if we interpret the novelist's craft itself as a form of play—not frivolity, but a sustained present-state of aesthetic absorption, within which plot operates not as accumulated significance but as the formal shape of that absorption. Yet I worry this saves Schlick only by collapsing all temporal meaning into aesthetic experience, which may miss precisely what makes an actual life, rather than a constructed story, feel significant.

I would welcome comments on three specific points. First, accuracy: do I misrepresent Schlick by framing his view as a denial of temporal or narrative meaning, or is that a defensible interpretation of his ethics? Second, on the conceptual move I have called "narrative suspension"—the idea that plot can serve as the form of present absorption rather than as the accumulation of past events—does this genuinely reconcile Schlick with narrative art, or does it merely rename the problem? Third, is the distinction I am drawing between "aesthetic presentism" and "existential accumulation" philosophically coherent, or does it depend on an unstated assumption about the ontology of fictional characters that a philosopher would reject?

Please answer in American English, and feel free to be direct about where the argument feels forced or where a different framing would serve it better.
