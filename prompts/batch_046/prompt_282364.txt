In our graduate seminar on philosophy of mind this week, one of my colleagues offered a concise summary of the Malcolm-Dennett exchange on whether dreams constitute genuine experiences, and I found myself uncertain which parts to trust. She put it roughly as follows. Malcolm, she said, holds that because there are no observable criteria for mental states during sleep, any report of a dream made upon waking is not a direct description of an inner experience but rather an inference drawn from subsequent behavior and verbal disposition, placing dream reports on a par with third-person predictions about another person's mental life rather than with first-person authority. Dennett, by contrast, accepts that something mental occurs during sleep but denies that it possesses any phenomenal character whatsoever; on her reading, he treats the dream narrative as a retrospective confabulation constructed at the moment of awakening, analogous to the explanatory narratives produced by split-brain patients when asked to account for actions they did not consciously initiate. From this, she concluded that Malcolm is essentially advancing a verificationist theory of mental content in general, while Dennett is advancing a functionalist elimination of phenomenology altogether, and that the apparent disagreement about dreams is therefore reducible to a broader dispute about whether consciousness requires publicly accessible behavioral criteria.

I am inclined to think this framing flattens several distinctions that matter, particularly regarding Malcolm's specific conceptual argument about the grammar of experience-claims rather than a general epistemic skepticism, and regarding Dennett's more nuanced distinction between the occurrence of a mental episode and its retrospective narrative packaging. Could you audit this summary for me with some precision? Please proceed point by point: indicate which characterizations are essentially accurate, which conflate claims that the respective authors keep separate, which present modest or local theses as sweeping philosophical programs, and whether her concluding reduction—that the debate is really about verificationism versus functionalism—survives these corrections. I am especially interested in whether either philosopher's framework can accommodate what we might call phenomenal dormancy: the possibility that an experience might persist in a non-reportable, counterfactually inaccessible form during sleep without being either fully present in consciousness or entirely absent, rather like a suspended normative status that awaits retrospective authorization upon awakening. You need not settle any empirical question about sleep neuroscience, but please address whether Malcolm's criteria-based argument rules out such dormancy on conceptual grounds alone, and whether Dennett's models—whether cassette-like storage or narrative construction—have the resources to make sense of it without collapsing into incoherence.
