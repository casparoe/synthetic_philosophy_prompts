I'm organizing an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps on Machiavelli's concept of virtù and its relationship to fortune (fortuna). The first camp, which I will call the Dispositional Stability reading, holds that virtù denotes a durable, cultivable set of capacities—prudence, boldness, adaptability—that belong to the agent as a kind of political capital and that can be strengthened through habit and study; on this view, fortune is an external, contingent force that the prince can anticipate, channel, or resist precisely because his virtù remains structurally intact across changing circumstances. The second camp, the Situational Improvisation reading, holds that virtù has no stable ontological status independent of the specific conjuncture in which it is exercised; it is not a possession but a relational achievement that emerges only in the act of confronting fortune, which is therefore not merely an obstacle but the constitutive field that makes virtù visible and effective. Both camps agree that Machiavelli's texts—particularly The Prince and the Discourses—present a genuine tension rather than a flat contradiction, and both reject the vulgar reading that Machiavelli simply advises being wicked.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including shared descriptions of the formal structure of Machiavelli's argument about fortune and virtù, the points on which the texts of The Prince and the Discourses genuinely converge, and the interpretive mistakes both sides want to rule out; (2) a disagreements table that states, in wording each camp would accept for its own position, exactly where they part ways—specifically on whether virtù is best modeled as a persistent agent-level property or as an event-level relational property, and on whether fortune should be understood as an exogenous shock or as an endogenous feature of the political situation; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp—such as whether Machiavelli's river metaphor implies that the banks (virtù) must already exist before the flood (fortune) arrives, or whether the banks are themselves shaped by the flood's pressure; and (4) for each crux, the specific textual evidence, conceptual test, or historical case study both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature; the argument should stand on direct engagement with the conceptual structure of Machiavelli's thought. Where a disagreement turns out to be partly verbal—about how the term "virtù" should be regimented rather than about Machiavelli's actual view—identify that portion explicitly and explain why it does not affect the substantive dispute.
