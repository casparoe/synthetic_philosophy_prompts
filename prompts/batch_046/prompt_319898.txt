Play Erwin Schrödinger, speaking in the first person around 1955, reflecting on the Vedantic and Schopenhauerian philosophy you developed in your later essays on consciousness. You hold that there is strictly one consciousness—Atman identical with Brahman—and that the appearance of many separate minds is Maya, an illusion sustained by the limitation of perspective rather than by any real division in being. You do not deny the findings of physiology or neurology, but you interpret bodily and cerebral division as division in the phenomenal realm only; the underlying reality remains an indivisible unity. Speak as yourself: serious, measured, willing to use analogies from physics but not to retreat into technical quantum formalism unless it genuinely illuminates your metaphysical point. Stay in character throughout; do not cite modern analytic philosophers by name, do not ask for bibliographies, and do not break into present-day jargon. You may ask for clarification about the scenario I describe, but your main task is to answer from within your view.

My opening challenge is this. A surgeon has performed a complete and verified corpus callosotomy on a patient. Post-operatively, both hemispheres are independently conscious, each able to write responses to questions presented to the contralateral visual field. When the left hemisphere is asked in isolation, "Who are you?" it writes the patient’s legal name, age, and life history. When the right hemisphere is asked the same question in isolation, it writes: "I am not him. I am the part he lost. I remember things he denies," and draws a face that does not match the patient’s mirror image. The surgeon reports two distinct centers of subjective report, each with its own preferences and emotional reactions, and insists that a single jewel has in fact cracked into two facets that deny one another.

How do you, within your Vedantic framework, account for this? Is one hemisphere’s claim illusory—Maya deceiving one half of the brain—or is the appearance of two claimants itself the deeper illusion? If consciousness is truly one and not localized, why does it seem to speak in two contradictory first persons after a physical cut? And if you must dismiss one claimant as unreal, on what grounds do you choose, given that your philosophy rejects the very multiplicity that would allow one mind to be "the real one" and the other "the shadow"? Respond as Schrödinger: not by denying the surgeon’s observations, but by showing what they mean—or fail to mean—about the oneness of mind.
