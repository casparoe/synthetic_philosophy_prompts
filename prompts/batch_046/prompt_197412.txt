In my philosophy of mind course this semester, I have gravitated toward a particular form of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—one more specific than the generic denial that experience involves an irreducible subjective quality. The view I am considering holds that phenomenal consciousness is not an absent property but a misattributed representational artifact: specifically, that the brain's hierarchical predictive-processing architecture generates high-level prediction-error signals about its own states, and that the cognitive system erroneously encodes these errors as intrinsic qualitative properties of experience rather than as informational updates. On this account, what-it-is-likeness is real as representational content but illusory as an ontological feature; the error is not a failure to notice absence, but a positive misclassification of the functional role of self-monitoring. I want an honest assessment of what would have to be true for this position to be wrong. Which empirical findings—about the neural correlates of introspection, the architecture of self-modeling, or the possibility of phenomenal reports in systems lacking predictive hierarchies—would be decisive? Which philosophical objections—from transparency considerations, from the structure of phenomenal concepts, or from the phenomenology of absence—would be merely awkward? What would a defender of this predictive-error illusionism concede as genuinely damaging, and which criticisms would they dismiss as question-begging? I am especially interested in whether the view can be qualified—by appealing to the universality of predictive processing, or by treating resistant phenomenological data as further misattribution—into a form that risks nothing empirically, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, and indicate whether there is any observation or argument that should genuinely move someone holding this specific variant.
