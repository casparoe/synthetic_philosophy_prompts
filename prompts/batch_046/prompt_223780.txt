I take the following view on the ethics of insect sentience. The familiar scale argument holds that because insects are astronomically numerous, even a very low prior probability that they are sentient generates enormous expected moral weight, and this is usually taken to motivate stronger protections specifically for insects. I think that if pursued consistently, the argument undermines itself rather than supporting its intended conclusion. If we allow highly uncertain, low-confidence sentience claims to dominate practical moral calculus for insects, we are compelled to apply the same reasoning to every domain in which consciousness is not directly observed—including, ultimately, other humans—thereby collapsing either into a paralyzing global skepticism about minds or into an implausible expected-value maximization over countless unverified subjects. The better resolution, I suggest, is to reject the binary sentience threshold altogether and replace it with a uniform, graded framework in which moral status tracks continuous evidential confidence across all species, without singling out insects for special treatment. On this view, insects are not a unique problem requiring exceptional protections; rather, they expose the instability of any ethics that treats sentience as an on-off switch.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings in comparative neuroscience or insect nociception, which formal results in decision theory about low-probability high-stakes reasoning, and which ethical intuitions or cross-domain analogies—say, with fetal consciousness, non-human mammals, or artificial agents—would be decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments. Which of these counterarguments would a defender of this graded-threshold view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed simply by redrawing the confidence curve? Is there a live risk that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—such that any counterexample is met by tweaking the grading parameters—or is that insulation itself a problem for a view of this kind? I also want to know what this framework predicts that both standard insect-protection positions and strict human-exceptionalist views do not, and whether there is any development—perhaps in our practical responses to AI systems or in refined measurements of invertebrate cognition—that would make the graded framework look less like a solution and more like an evasion of the original scale problem. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, and assess whether the view risks anything substantive at all.
