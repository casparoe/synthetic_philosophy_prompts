After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend last night about whether biological functions are fixed by evolutionary history or by present causal contribution, we realized we had never agreed on what a theory of function is actually required to explain. I want to settle the ground rules before comparing candidates. Write the desiderata: specify exactly what an adequate philosophical theory of biological function must be able to deliver — the phenomena it must classify correctly, the conceptual distinctions it must preserve, the puzzles it must dissolve, and the adjacent scientific results with which it must remain consistent — with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. In particular, the theory must account not only for canonical evolved traits but also for engineered biological artifacts and horizontally transferred elements: for example, a redesigned metabolic pathway inserted into a synthetic minimal cell that lacks any selective history for that pathway, or a horizontally acquired gene that now serves a purely structural role disconnected from the ancestral effect for which it was originally selected. The list should also address how a theory handles malfunction (preserving the normative judgment that a heart can fail to perform its function) alongside multi-functionality and context-dependent role switching, and it should explicitly flag any pair of desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether accommodating arbitrary engineered functions inevitably erodes the normative force of malfunction. Once the criteria are laid out with their supporting cases, briefly score the etiological account and the causal-role account against them: indicate where each meets the standard, where it must stretch its core commitments, and which desideratum does the most damage to each view. Keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on a final verdict between the two theories.
