For a philosophy-of-history workshop with about twelve advanced undergraduates or graduate students, ninety minutes, and no assigned pre-reading, design a role-play and structured deliberation that makes causal overdetermination in historiography feel unavoidable rather than theoretical. Stage a fictional composite case—the collapse of an empire—where four conditions (fiscal insolvency, climate-driven harvest failure, a frontier military crisis, and institutional decay among elites) are each, by explicit scenario design, sufficient to produce state failure within a generation, and all four converge in the same five-year window.

Divide participants into four "archivist-historians," each assigned one condition as their primary explanatory lens and given a private packet of counterfactual evidence showing that, had their assigned factor been absent, the remaining three still produced collapse. Their joint task is to negotiate a single 250-word official chronicle that assigns narrative priority—what came first, what was decisive, what the story is about—without resorting to probabilistic language like "contributed to." Before negotiation, each must privately complete a redundancy matrix marking whether removing their own factor changes the outcome; the rules should be arranged so they commit to draft narratives before sharing these matrices, discovering only afterward that every cell reads "collapse still occurs."

The debrief should map each stage—drafting, matrix completion, deadlock, attempted resolution—onto the philosophical distinction between causal contribution and narrative selection. It should reference Mackie's INUS conditions informally without assuming prior reading, introduce the framing of causal democracy versus narrative monarchy to show why the problem is not merely "too many causes" but a structural mismatch between egalitarian causation and hierarchical storytelling, and explicitly state what the simulation cannot reproduce: the metaphysical debate over whether overdetermined events possess a hidden unique causal structure. Be frank about the predictable failure mode—participants averaging weights or voting to break deadlock—and instruct the facilitator to let the deadlock sit for at least five minutes before intervening. The idea should arise from the rules and incentives, not from any up-front lecture; name the concept only after the negotiation has collapsed.
