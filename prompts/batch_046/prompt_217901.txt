In recent discussions about frontier artificial intelligence governance, a frequent analogy compares the development of large-scale AI systems to the Manhattan Project: a small number of laboratories pursue a transformative technology with unclear safety properties, operating largely outside direct democratic oversight, and the appropriate institutional response is therefore an international regulatory regime analogous to the IAEA, born from recognition that the technology's risks transcend national borders. I have heard this comparison used specifically to license the conclusion that AI development should be placed under a multilateral treaty body with enforcement power, but I want to understand whether the analogy genuinely supports that prescription, or whether it quietly imports structural features that mislead when applied to machine learning.

Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the present AI case plays the role of the physicists' theoretical confidence in fission, the compartmentalized secrecy, the Trinity test as an irreversible demonstration, the dual-use ambiguity of nuclear energy, and the post-war realization that proliferation was unavoidable? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for governance design, particularly the difference between a technology whose underlying physics was well-understood before engineering scaled up and one whose capabilities emerge largely from empirical scaling without a comparable theoretical framework predicting risk thresholds. Finally, tell me whether the institutional conclusion—that an IAEA-style international body is the right response—survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion through mapped features that do not hold. If the Manhattan Project comparison collapses on these points, is there a more appropriate analogy among the printing press or the Industrial Revolution for the governance problem at hand, and what would that alternative mapping look like?
