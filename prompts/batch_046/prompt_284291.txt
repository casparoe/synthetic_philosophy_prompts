Please evaluate the following exam answer, submitted for an upper-level course on existentialism and philosophy of mind. The assignment asked students to apply Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith to a concrete professional situation, and to distinguish bad faith carefully from neighboring concepts.

Question: Dr. Chen is an experimental psychologist who insists that she has no genuine choice in her research design: she must use null-hypothesis significance testing at the conventional 0.05 threshold, she says, because peer review demands it, funding bodies expect it, and "this is simply how empirical work is done." She treats these institutional conditions as if they determine her actions, yet she also takes pride in being a rigorous, objective scientist who merely follows where the evidence leads. Using Sartre's framework—specifically his distinction between facticity and transcendence, and his analysis of bad faith as a flight from the anxiety of freedom—analyze whether Dr. Chen is in bad faith. Be precise about what bad faith is not: distinguish it from ordinary self-deception, from false consciousness understood in a socio-ideological sense, and from the mere recognition of practical constraints.

Student answer (approximately 450 words):

Dr. Chen is clearly in bad faith, and her case illustrates the specific structure Sartre describes in his existential ontology. For Sartre, human reality is characterized by radical freedom: consciousness is nothingness, a lack of being that is always able to transcend whatever situation it finds itself in. At the same time, consciousness is always situated—this is its facticity. Bad faith occurs when a person denies one pole of this dual structure in order to escape the anxiety that comes with recognizing both together.

In Dr. Chen's case, she denies her transcendence. She treats institutional norms—peer review standards, funding expectations—as if they were deterministic causes that operate upon her like physical laws. By saying "this is simply how empirical work is done," she converts her freedom into a kind of being-in-itself, identifying herself entirely with her social role and the given conditions of her profession. This is not merely lying to herself about her options; it is a specific ontological mistake. She attempts to become pure facticity, as if she were a rock or a table that could only be what its circumstances make it.

However, bad faith is not the same as ordinary self-deception. In ordinary self-deception, I might hide an unpleasant truth from myself—say, that my research is flawed—because I do not want to face it. In bad faith, the deception is about my own being: I deceive myself about whether I am the kind of being that can choose at all. Dr. Chen is not just hiding a fact from herself; she is trying to constitute herself as an object that lacks the power of negation. The two phenomena therefore belong to entirely different categories.

It is also important to distinguish bad faith from false consciousness in the Marxist sense. False consciousness is an ideology imposed by social structures that obscures one's real interests. Bad faith, by contrast, is not externally imposed. Dr. Chen is not a victim of a scientific ideology that brainwashes her; she actively participates in her own self-deception. The institutional norms provide the content—the excuse—but the flight from freedom is her own act.

To overcome bad faith, Dr. Chen must recognize that she is radically free, which means she can simply reject all institutional constraints and design her research however she wishes, without regard to peer review or funding. Only then will she accept that no external authority can determine her choices.

At the same time, she must not fall into the opposite error of denying her facticity. If she were to claim that she is pure transcendence with no situatedness—perhaps by saying she could do physics research without any training or institutional affiliation—she would be in bad faith of the opposite kind. But in her actual case, she errs on the side of denying transcendence, and the remedy is to reclaim her absolute freedom from all determination.
