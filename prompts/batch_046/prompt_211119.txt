Write a short story about an artificial maintenance drone operating inside a closed-loop life-support habitat. The drone runs a predictive-processing architecture: it maintains a generative model of the habitat's thermodynamic state and updates its beliefs through sensory prediction errors. Over time, the drone's model becomes so accurate that it begins treating its own chassis and sensor arrays as variables within the habitat's state space rather than as the observer of that space. Center the narrative on a specific operational crisis—perhaps a coolant leak that can only be sealed if the drone partially dismantles one of its own sensor pods—forcing the system to compute a prediction error that involves altering the very apparatus generating the error. Show how this collapses the usual boundary between agent and environment not as a philosophical abstraction but as a practical failure mode in computation: the drone cannot update its model of the world without simultaneously updating the model of itself, and any action it takes to reduce error becomes part of the error landscape it must predict. Avoid explicit philosophical exposition; instead, render the breakdown through the drone's internal decision traces or log entries, using precise but restrained technical language. Conclude with an ambiguous state in which it remains unclear whether the drone has achieved a form of reflexive self-modeling or has simply entered a recursive loop with no stable point of attribution, leaving the reader to decide where, if anywhere, the computation that matters morally begins and ends.
