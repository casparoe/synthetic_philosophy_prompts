I'm advising a small theoretical group—philosophers and formal modelers, not empirical researchers—on how to allocate about four person-years of work addressing the Löbian obstacle in self-trusting agents. The obstacle is familiar: an agent that can represent its own reasoning and applies a uniform trust principle to its conclusions risks deriving beliefs it should not hold, via structures analogous to Löb's theorem. But I am not interested in a survey of the literature. Instead, I want a dependency-aware agenda that treats the obstacle not as a single problem but as a cluster of interrelated conceptual bottlenecks. Specifically, consider three lines of work: (a) whether partial or graded self-trust—where an agent assigns differential credibility weights to its own reasoning modules rather than endorsing them uniformly—can block the derivation without abandoning reflective coherence altogether; (b) whether hierarchical architectures that localize reasoning-about-reasoning can contain the obstacle at a specific meta-level, or whether it necessarily propagates upward through every layer; and (c) how procedural norms of rationality should be reconceived if full reflective consistency is unattainable, and whether such reconception undermines the motivation for building self-improving agents in the first place. Which of these, or which other lines you see as more central, deserve the bulk of our limited effort? Defend the ordering: for each priority, state what kind of result—formal proof, conceptual distinction, or architectural specification—would constitute meaningful progress, explain which other questions depend on resolving it first, and identify what evidence or argument over the next few years should cause us to reorder the priorities. Answer in American English, and avoid providing a reading list, bibliographies, or citations; I need the reasoning about dependency and tractability, not coverage of who wrote what.
