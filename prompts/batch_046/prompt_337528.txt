I'm working through a seminar on epistemic rationality, and a friend tried to distill the difference between Van Fraassen's Reflection principle and Lewis's Principal Principle by saying that Reflection requires one's present credence to match one's expectation of future credence, whereas the Principal Principle requires present credence to align with admissible objective chances. That gloss feels right at a high level, but I'm unsure whether it holds up once we move beyond ordinary evidential updating.

Could you compare these two principles specifically in how they handle self-locating or indexical uncertainty—situations in which an agent anticipates not merely acquiring new world-directed evidence, but also shifting her temporal or personal location, as in duplication or amnesia scenarios? Consider a concrete case: on Sunday night, an agent knows that she will be put to sleep and randomly duplicated, so that one copy (A) is woken on Monday and another copy (B) on Tuesday, with no internal marker distinguishing them, and she knows the assignment is determined by a fair coin. How should Reflection apply to her Sunday-night credence that she is copy A? How should the Principal Principle apply? Do they yield the same constraint, come into conflict, or operate in different registers altogether? And structurally, where do the principles share common ground—both tying present belief to some future or objective standard—and where do they diverge because Reflection is anchored in anticipated first-person epistemic states while the Principal Principle is anchored in third-person chances?

Finally, what is the residue of this comparison? Is there something about rational anticipation of self-location that Reflection can express but the Principal Principle cannot accommodate, given the latter's reliance on admissible, world-directed evidence? Or does the attempt to apply Reflection here reveal that the principle overreaches by treating indexical shifts as if they were ordinary updates to world-beliefs?
