hey, so i'm a novelist and i've been obsessing over whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational or if it's always just the fallacy in a fancy hat. my instincts on specific cases are kind of all over the place and i can't tell if there's a real principle or i'm just rationalizing.

here's my gut. if i've spent three years on a novel and then realize the premise is totally rotten, i still think i should finish the draft — not to salvage it for publication, but because the work changed how i think about form and quitting feels like pretending those years didn't happen. but if i'm halfway through a bad literary biography i started for research, i should drop it immediately; the time already sunk means nothing.

then there's narrative debt. in my trilogy i established that every use of magic burns a memory. for the finale, breaking that rule just to save my protagonist from being hollow feels like cheating the reader, even though sticking to it makes the ending bleak. i think i have to honor the rule. but here's the tension: i've also spent years building a character named Mara across three books. readers are attached, her voice is developed, but the fourth book's plot genuinely wants her dead. i think i should kill her. the years invested in her shouldn't protect her.

then there's career stuff. i spent six months applying to a writing fellowship, got rejected, and part of me wants to apply again next year "to make the effort count." i think that's definatly the fallacy and i should only apply if the project is actually right for it.

so why is finishing the rotten draft okay and keeping the magic-rule okay, but re-applying to the fellowship not? and why does narrative debt protect a plot-rule but not a beloved character?

i'm wondering if the principle is something like: honor sunk costs only when they're constitutive of a coherence or identity you're still actively endorsing, not when they're just accumulated assets. but then i can't tell if that's too complex, or if it forces me to drop the rotten-draft verdict.

what principle actually captures these? state it precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. tell me what it predicts for a case i haven't mentioned: my publisher paid a huge advance for a sequel i now think is artistically dishonest — should i write it because my prior word and the contract are part of my professional identity, or is that just sunk-cost reasoning dressed up? and be honest: which of my verdicts above does your principle fail to save, and is that a reason to drop the verdict or to complicate the principle?

this is all a bit messy, but i'm trying to do reflective equilibrium here.
