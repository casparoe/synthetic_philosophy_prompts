I just came out of a long, circular argument with a friend about Gongsun Long's claim that a white horse is not a horse—whether this is a sophistical word-game or a rigorous separation of form-predication from chromatic predication. We kept circling whether the Mingjia were playing with language or mapping an ontology in which predicates fail to compose the way common sense assumes. I want students to feel that failure of composition in real time, not just parse it.

Design a 90-minute classroom activity for about 12 upper-level philosophy students (familiar with basic logic, no background in Chinese philosophy required). Frame it as a simulation of an archival restoration institute that must allocate scarce grant points using two competing taxonomic protocols: Protocol A classifies strictly by pure form (e.g., "equine"), while Protocol B classifies by compound predicate (e.g., "white-equine"). Provide concrete materials—role cards, item cards describing ambiguous artifacts, and separate budget ledgers for each protocol. The payoff structure should reward internal consistency within a protocol but impose a coordination penalty whenever groups try to trade or merge classifications across protocols: if one group registers an item as "horse" under A and another registers the identical item as "not-horse" under B, both lose resources. Structure voting or allocation rules so participants cannot negotiate away the distinction, forcing them to discover that treating "white" and "horse" as independent, non-composing predicates produces institutional deadlock. Do not name the White Horse Dialogue or mention Gongsun Long until the debrief.

The debrief should map each crisis—classification conflicts, stranded budgets, failed exchanges—back onto the logical moves: the separation of color and form, the universal-particular distinction, and the mismatch between linguistic name and classified reality. Tell me what will predictably fail the first time (likely participants smuggling compound terms into Protocol A despite the rules) and how to correct it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the School of Names—such as the broader metaphysical thesis that linguistic names do not fully reach actualities, or the logical paradoxes associated with figures like Hui Shi—this simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
