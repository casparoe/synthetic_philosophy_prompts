I run a philosophy seminar on mind and biology, about 14 postgrads, 90 minutes. Design an acitivity that makes them feel Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions: that real competence can pile up with no comprhension behind it, and that our instinct to read understanding into success is basically unreliable.

Split them into Designers (four groups of three) and Selectors (the rest). Designers get ten minutes to write explicit rules for navigating a hidden grid — they must understand every step. Selectors get a bag of random rule-fragments (cards with single commands: turn left, move forward, etc.). They run four generations: each round they run the fragments, keep only the two that get closest to a hidden target, combine them randomly, and discard the rest. They are banned from trying to understand why the winners work; they just score distance. After round four the Selectors present their best chain, and the Designers try to reverse-engineer it — usually it's a bizarre non-intuitive loop.

Then round two: swap roles, but now the system is a black-box deck you pre-built that predicts student choices in a simple betting game. The group must decide whether to trust it, knowing it has zero internal comprehension but hits about 70 percent. Payoffs are points, not cash: closest approach in round one, correct bets in round two.

Debrief should map round one onto Darwin (selection without a selector's comprehension) and round two onto Turing (behavoural competence without mental machinery). Give rules, timeline, the point scheme, and an honest note on what the game can't reproduce — probably the deep time of evolutionary accumulation or the real opacity of trained weights — so you have to supply that in discussion. Also say what will predictably go wrong: Designers will try to simplify the grid, Selectors will anthropomorphise the fragments. Tell me what argument or observation would change your mind about whether this actually conveys the inversion rather than just being a fun puzzle, and flag where your reasoning is shakiest. Keep it casual, use British English — maybe a typo or two — and don't ask for citations or reading lists.
