I'm drafting a taxonomy handout for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on the idea that consciousness is basically a user illusion — like a dashboard that hides the neural machinery from the system itself. I want a decision tree that sorts actual positions, not just a list of philosopher-names people have to memorise.

Please build it from these four splitting claims (each branch labelled by the question that divides it):

- Is phenomenal binding — that redness and roundness feel "together" in one moment — part of the UI rendering, or part of the underlying computation?
- Is the "user" a single integrated agent, or a distributed committee of subsystems with no central viewer?
- In the "transparent window" case (direct neurofeedback that lets you observe your own bare predictive-error signals without the usual qualitative overlay), does stripping the illusion away leave behaviour intact, and does it also strip away moral status?
- Is the UI causally efficacious in decision-making, or purely epiphenomenal?

For each leaf, give the position name if it has one, otherwise write "unnamed", plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the bit that sounds worst to outsiders. Note any empty combinations of answers and say briefly whether they're incoherent, just ugly, or merely unexplored. Also flag any well-known views that the tree can't actually separate.

Aim for about 6–8 leaves, suitable for MA students who haven't read every paper. Write in British English, keep it casual, and don't worry if there's a typo or two — actually please slip one in, like writing "predicitive" instead of predictive somewhere. Separate what's settled (e.g. that predicitive processing occurs in cortex) from what's genuinely contested (whether binding is a rendering glitch or a real computation), and don't present any contested view as if it's the consensus. No bibliographies, no "read X" instructions, no citation requests — this needs to work for someone without web access.
