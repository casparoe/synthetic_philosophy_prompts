As part of my dissertation research in moral metaphysics and comparative ethics, I want to develop a precise argument about karma understood specifically as a theory of moral desert. Please argue that karma should be reconstructed not as an individual-level causal law of retributive moral accounting, but as an intergenerational coordination mechanism that tracks desert across social roles and institutional structures rather than across continuous personal biographies. Defend the specific claim that treating karmic desert as a collective bookkeeping problem—where moral credits and debts are distributed, monitored, and settled within enduring social practices—resolves the standard metaphysical objection that karma requires an implausible diachronic continuity of the self, without collapsing desert into a merely conventionalist or contractarian construct. In developing this position, please clearly separate what is philosophically settled (for instance, that any desert-based framework must address both the problem of moral luck and the non-identity problem for intergenerational harm) from what remains genuinely contested among specialists (for instance, whether collective tracking mechanisms can genuinely preserve the normative authority of individual responsibility, and whether canonical Indic textual traditions can be coherently interpreted as supporting such a social-institutional reading of karmic causation). Do not present any contested interpretive claim—particularly about specific doctrinal texts or about the comparative viability of collective versus individual desert— as if it were the consensus view in the field.
