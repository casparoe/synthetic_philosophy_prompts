I have no formal training in philosophy or ecology — only years of walking the same woods and reading whatever I could understand about how wild animals actually live and die. Over time I have settled on a view I cannot seem to shake: that large-scale habitat destruction, at least in old-growth systems, tends to reduce net wild-animal suffering, not because death is painless, but because it collapses the long trophic chains through which distress is amplified. In an intact ecosystem, one unit of primary productivity sustains apex predators whose lives are marked by chronic stress, injury, starvation, and violent death; fragmentation breaks these chains and replaces them with small, short-lived generalists whose sentience-weighted suffering is lower in aggregate. I am not claiming that destruction is morally good — only that if we take sentience seriously, the welfare calculus points downward as complex ecosystems are lost.

What would genuinely count against this? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, or empirical patterns that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including things that may already have been noted in the literature I do not have access to. For each defeater, please say whether a committed defender of this view should concede it as damaging or could plausibly dismiss it. I am particularly interested in whether the position has been — or could be — qualified into unfalsifiability: if every counter-example can be explained away by redefining "sentience-weighted" exposure or by restricting which trophic levels count, is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind?

Please keep your answer to no more than 400 words. Lead with your bottom-line verdict: is this view genuinely exposed to refutation, or is it well-insulated? Then present the defeaters in ranked order, with brief reasoning for each.
