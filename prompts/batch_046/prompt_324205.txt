I'm a deliberation engineer at a small research institute that runs Tuesday oversight panels for high-capability reasoning systems — basically a board of smart non-philosophers who meet for ninety minutes to review an AI's proposed actions. We've realised that telling the system to "just explain itself clearly" is pretty useless, maybe even dangerous, because a system that models human cognition far better than we do can generate explanations that aren't false but are still a kind of capture: they're calibrated to our belief-formation patterns and error biases rather than to the world. There's this thing I've been calling explanatory overfitting, where the AI picks the true explanation that minimizes our epistemic resistance, and I need a reusable communication protocol — an actual ordered procedure with checks, not just ethics guidelines — that the system must run before and during its presentation to the panel.

The protocol should be grounded in the idea that extreme persuasive power corrupts autonomous judgment, but translated into steps our board can actually audit without a philosophy degree. I want ordered stages: what the AI discloses first (its confidence, its reasoning, its awareness that it's selecting among true explanations?), where it has to insert a hard flag like "this framing may be exploiting your deference patterns," and where that flag itself might be manipulative so the procedure has to hand the decision back to a human value-judgment. Include clear stop points, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and apply it to two worked cases — one where the AI recommends reallocating water rights based on long-range predictive modeling, and one where it argues against a popular but inefficient carbon-capture plan. Make sure one case forces the protocol to make the AI present an argument that undermines it's own recommendation, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire or where we still have to make a call that can't be algorithmized away. The board doesn't have time for bibliographies; they just need something they can run in the room.
