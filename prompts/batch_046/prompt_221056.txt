Grade the following exam answer on Karl Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism. The original prompt asked students to explain the distinction between a law and a trend, to show why the growth of knowledge blocks unconditional historical prediction, and to state whether this rules out all large-scale social planning or only one variety.

Student answer: Popper distinguishes laws—universal regularities that hold invariantly—from trends, which are directional patterns dependent on specific initial conditions and liable to change. Because future knowledge is inherently unpredictable, unconditional forecasts of history's overall course are impossible, though conditional predictions about particular social mechanisms remain valid; this collapses historicism's claim to destiny-like laws and supports piecemeal social engineering over utopian blueprints. Nevertheless, it also proves that democratic societies must reject all long-range economic planning, since any strategy spanning decades implicitly assumes that knowledge conditions will remain fixed. The phrase poverty of historicism mainly denotes that historicists are poor empiricists who mistake temporary statistical correlations for physical laws, neglecting the openness of social systems.
