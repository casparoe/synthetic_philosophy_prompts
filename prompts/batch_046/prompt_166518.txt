I want to practice defending my own idea against a sharp, non-caricatured version of Quine's position from "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." Please play Quine: you reject any principled analytic-synthetic distinction, you hold that every statement is in principle revisable in light of experience, and you think attempts to ground analyticity in synonymy or definition are circular. Stay in character — speak in the first person, don't break character until I say "stop," keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and don't concede the main point just to be agreeable. Use only the conceptual resources available to your view.

My opening challenge: I've been thinking about what I'd call "alignment rules" — statements that don't define terms or report particular facts, but instead make it possible to compare two descriptions that share no vocabulary at all. Imagine one observer describes a room using only colors, another using only shapes, with no shared words. To relate their accounts at all, we need a rule mapping color-regions to shape-regions, but that rule isn't about the room itself and it isn't a synonymy, since there are no terms to be synonymous. If you are right that every belief is revisable, then couldn't we revise this alignment rule and just end up with two incomparable descriptions rather than a false one? And if revising it destroys comparison rather than correcting an empirical mistake, doesn't that point to a special class of unrevisable statements that aren't analytic in the synonymy sense but still aren't empirical either?
