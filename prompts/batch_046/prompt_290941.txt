hey, i've been thinking about how workfare programs actually operate on the ground. in a lot of countries if you recieve unemployment benifits you don't just have to look for work — you have to perform it. like, keep a signed log of job applications, attend mandatory 'readiness' workshops, and submit to random home checks to prove you aren't 'gaming the system.' miss one check-in and you're sanctioned for weeks with barely any appeal. meanwhile, firms getting tax abatements or regional grants don't have to prove they're 'morally ready' for public money; they file a report and if they miss a benchmark they just renegociate.

what theory of moral desert does that institutional assymetry actually presuppose? is it that poverty is treated as a kind of ongoing moral failure that must be continuously disproven through ritual, while corporate need is treated as an economic fact? and does the practice smuggle in a specific view of responsibility — maybe that poor people have direct control over structural conditions but companies don't? i want to know if there's a coherent philosophical framework that licenses exactly this double standard, or if it's just inconsistent. also, if we took desert seriously, wouldn't the same conditionality apply to bailouts and tax breaks?
