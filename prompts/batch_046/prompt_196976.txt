Audit the analogy often used to render Rudolf Carnap’s view of rational social planning intuitive: planning is like engineering. On this reading, the scientific planner is the engineer—she computes efficient means, predicts outcomes, and optimizes within fixed constraints—while the democratic public is the client, specifying desired ends but leaving execution to expertise. The analogy is invoked to license a sharp division of labor: value choice is democratic and non-cognitive, scientific execution is expert and value-neutral. I want you to lay out the correspondence table explicitly—map the engineer, the client, the blueprint, the raw materials, the cost-benefit analysis, and the possibility of redesign onto Carnap’s framework of means, ends, predictive science, and democratic decision. Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures: whether social “materials” are rights-bearing agents rather than inert matter; whether democratic ends are pre-given like a client’s brief or are actually constituted through deliberation; and whether Carnap’s separation of cognitive predictions from non-cognitive value judgments can survive once empirical forecasts begin to privilege some ends over others. Crucially, incorporate a feature of Carnap’s position that textbook summaries usually omit: he held that many apparent value conflicts over planning goals can be transformed into empirical disagreements about predicted social consequences once the goals are articulated with sufficient precision, and he drew on the practical negotiations between technical experts and democratic municipal bodies in interwar Viennese social-housing and welfare projects. Ask whether this feature reinforces the engineering picture—by showing experts and citizens jointly testing predictions—or undermines it—by dissolving the very means/ends boundary the analogy requires. Finally, deliver a verdict: does the analogy’s conclusion—that Carnap supports a strict separation between scientific planning and democratic value choice—survive once these disanalogies and this added feature are fully on the table, or does a rival analogy (planning as constitutional design, or as deliberative negotiation) do better justice to his actual view? Give your answer without appealing to bibliographies or specific editions; work from the conceptual structure alone.
