I'm working through Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and, more specifically, the methodological problem it raises about whether cultural values can genuinely cause economic and institutional change, or whether they are always post-hoc rationalizations of shifts driven by material conditions, organizational experimentation, and market pressure. Rather than revisit the Calvinist case Weber himself used, I want to apply this framework to a different episode in which culture was widely invoked as an explanatory engine: the rise of Japanese post-war corporate practices—quality-control circles, keiretsu supplier networks, and lifetime-employment norms—between the 1950s and 1980s, followed by their persistence through Japan's "Lost Decade" of the 1990s and 2000s despite prolonged economic stagnation. The conventional narrative attributes these organizational forms to distinctively Japanese values—group harmony, long-term orientation, and a Confucian-inflected loyalty—and treats Japan's economic success as proof that culture can be a primary causal driver.

Analyze this episode through Weber's lens, but keep the mapping disciplined. What would an "elective affinity" explanation look like here, as opposed to one that treats the practices as purely instrumental adaptations to capital scarcity, American occupation-era restructuring, or state industrial coordination? Identify the relevant actors—corporate managers, factory workers, state bureaucrats guiding industrial policy, and later American and European firms that copied the practices—and specify what options they actually faced at key junctures, such as the adoption of quality circles in the 1960s or the partial dismantling of lifetime employment after 1995. Does the fact that these practices survived economic failure for decades, eroding mainly when capital-market restructuring made them unprofitable rather than when underlying social values changed, support Weber's view that values and economic rationalization can align without values being the engine? Or does it reveal that cultural explanation is always available as a narrative but collapses once incentives shift? Say where the theory is genuinely silent—for example, on whether imported practices retain any "cultural" meaning when adopted outside Japan—and tell me what the episode ultimately shows about the possibility, and the limits, of explaining institutional history with values.
