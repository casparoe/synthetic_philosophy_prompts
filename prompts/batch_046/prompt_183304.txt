Analyse the British and French decision to continue operating Concorde from the 1970s until its retirement in 2003 through the lens of the sunk-cost fallacy and its limits. By the mid-1970s the development expenditure was irrecoverable and commercial profitability was impossible, yet both governments sustained subsidies and kept the fleet flying for nearly three more decades. Rather than treating this as straightforward irrational commitment, examine whether the sunk investment had become a credible signal of technological prestige and diplomatic partnership—so that abandoning it would have destroyed value not captured on any aircraft balance sheet. In particular, distinguish between accounting sunk costs (past expenditure that forward-looking rational choice should ignore) and what we might call relational sunk commitments (past investments that alter the strategic environment by binding actors to reputations). Apply this distinction to the Concorde episode: did the governments face a genuine fallacy, or did they rationally honour commitments that only looked like sunk costs? What does the case show about whether honouring sunk costs can ever be rational, and does it compel us to redefine the fallacy rather than dismiss the behaviour? Please respond in British English.
