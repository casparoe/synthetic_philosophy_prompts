You sit on a volunteer board that has just received a single, time-sensitive shipment of a life-saving medication after a localized contamination event. The shipment is sufficient to treat either one specific person—an elderly neighbor you have known for years and who is currently in critical condition nearby—or five people in an affected area miles away, but the logistics of transport mean you must choose one destination and cannot split the dose. A fellow board member insists that the decision requires no deliberation: five lives are worth more than one, so the shipment must go to the distant group, and any hesitation on your part is an irrational attachment. You sense that reducing the choice to arithmetic misses something essential, though you are unsure whether your discomfort comes from the personal bond with your neighbor, the immediate visibility of their suffering, or a more fundamental objection to the idea that moral duty scales with headcount. Is your fellow board member right? If not, what is the precise structure of the mistake—does proximity or identifiability alter the moral calculus, does the obligation to save the greater number fail here for a reason that does not depend on special relationships, or is there another way to understand why the numbers do not settle the matter?
