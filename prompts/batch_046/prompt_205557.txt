I'm drafting my third novel and I'm stuck in a workshop debate thats probably more philosophy than fiction craft. I gave my protagonist this wierd, hyper-specific aversion to brass doorknobs—she'll circle a whole block to avoid touching one, but it has zero connection to the plot or her arc. My writing group says every detail in a tight narrative has to earn its keep: if the fear doesn't reveal something deep about her or drive the story forward, it's just dead weight, like nature shouldn't carry useless baggage. But I keep thinking about real people—how so many of our quirks aren't for solving any particular problem; they're just accidental leftovers, side-effects of other mental machinery that was actually useful for something else entirely.

I don't want to invent a traumatic backstory just to retrofit a purpose onto her quirk, because that feels artificial. Still, I'm worried I'm just being a sloppy writer and defending a flaw. So who's right? Does a character trait need to serve a functional role in the story to justify its presence, or can someone in fiction genuinely carry something useless—just there because it's hitched to something that actually matters?
