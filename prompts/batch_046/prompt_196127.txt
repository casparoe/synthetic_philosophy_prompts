I'm constructing a handout for a graduate seminar on pure time preference—the view that welfare occurring further in the future should be weighted less solely because of temporal distance, setting aside risk, uncertainty, and investment returns. I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions by how they answer three concrete diagnostic splits, not by historical school names.

Split 1 (the distance claim): Is there any rational or moral reason to prefer saving 1,000 lives in 50 years over saving 1,100 equivalent lives in 500 years, where the only relevant difference is the 450-year gap?

Split 2 (the continuity claim): If a decision-maker knows that in 300 years its institutional values, identity, or conception of welfare will be unrecognizably different, does that discontinuity justify applying a pure discount rate, or is such discounting only permissible when the decider's own present or near-future welfare is at stake?

Split 3 (the aggregation-boundary claim): Because continuous pure discounting drives the present value of distant welfare asymptotically toward zero, should there be a "structural horizon"—a fixed temporal boundary beyond which welfare states are excluded from present aggregation—or should decay remain continuous and unbounded?

Please build the tree as follows. Root question: Does temporal distance, by itself, diminish the weight of a welfare state? First-level branches correspond to answers to Split 1 (yes / no / only if Split 2 is satisfied). Second-level branches correspond to answers to Split 2 (value-continuity is necessary / value-continuity is irrelevant / value-continuity is sufficient but not necessary). Third-level branches correspond to answers to Split 3 (continuous exponential decay / structural horizon at a specific date / rejection of aggregation beyond the horizon). Each leaf should carry a descriptive position name or "unnamed," plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment—especially any tension with treating people at different times as equals. Note any unoccupied combinations (for instance, continuous decay paired with denial that distance matters) and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unexplored, or unstable. The tree should contain exactly six to eight leaves, with no branch that collapses genuinely distinct answers. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; use descriptive labels rather than author attributions.
