I am currently enrolled in a seminar on medieval philosophy of action, and in our unit on Buridan we spent considerable time on the so-called ass paradox: a rational agent suspended between two options of exactly equal value who, on certain interpretations, cannot choose either and therefore perishes. Our instructor observed that contemporary formal decision theory largely dissolves the problem by treating strict indifference as either impossible—if preferences are required to be complete, transitive, and continuous over a convex space—or as harmless, since any residual tie can be broken by an epsilon-perturbation or a lexicographic tie-breaker that assigns infinitesimal weight to some arbitrary feature. This framing struck me as perhaps missing something central to Buridan’s own concern, though I am not confident enough in the textual details to say exactly where.

Specifically, imagine Buridan were presented with the modern axiomatic apparatus—complete and transitive preferences resolved, when necessary, by an arbitrary causal mechanism such as randomization or an infinitesimal perturbation—together with the claim that this is not a failure of rationality but a structural feature of it. Would he regard this as a genuine dissolution of his paradox, or would he object that the formal framework quietly substitutes a non-rational causal process for the very act of volitional determination he sought to defend? More pointedly, do you think he would accept that the will, conceived as a rational appetite moved necessarily by the stronger reason, can legitimately determine choice through an arbitrary tie-breaker without thereby abandoning its essential nature, or would he insist that any such mechanism transforms the will into something other than a rational power? I am especially interested in whether he would see the modern tolerance for “trembling hand” resolution as compatible with his thesis that reason must always provide a decisive ground for action, or whether he would treat it as precisely the kind of determinism he meant to resist.
