I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I run an informal workshop for about twelve technically-minded adults. We have 90 minutes. I want a structured, in-person simulation—not a lecture—that lets participants feel, before anyone names it, the tension between virtue ethics and the situationist critique. The conceptual hook I want them to hit on their own is that if moral character functions like a portable, containerized module, then altering only the deployment environment should not radically change moral behavior; the activity should show that it does.

Design the rules, roles, and hidden conditions. Give each participant a printed “character card” that specifies one stable virtue (for example, honesty, compassion, or courage) and a private payoff structure that rewards acting in line with it. Run three short rounds—perhaps ten minutes each—in which every participant makes the same binary moral choice, but exactly one environmental variable shifts between rounds: anonymity versus visibility to the group, severe time pressure versus open deliberation, or the presence versus absence of an authority figure who signals a preference. The incentives should be arranged with small, tangible stakes (points that convert to a group-level outcome, not real money) so that in the high-pressure or anonymous condition a clear majority violates the trait printed on their own card, even though they affirmed that trait moments earlier.

Structure it so the participants construct the failure pattern themselves: have them record their choices privately after each round, then reveal aggregate results round-by-round, letting them notice the divergence before anyone explains it. Do not mention “virtue ethics,” “situationism,” “character traits,” or “moral psychology” until the debrief.

In the debrief, map each round’s environmental tweak onto a specific situationist finding or concept—anonymity eroding honesty, authority altering obedience-related choices, time pressure suppressing compassionate responses—and explain which feature of virtue ethics (long-term habituation, cross-situational consistency, or the integration of practical wisdom) this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce, since it operates in minutes rather than years. Be explicit about what you, as facilitator, must supply verbally to fill that gap.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, participants reverse-engineering the hidden condition, one dominant personality skewing group dynamics, or people treating the card as ironic performance rather than commitment—and how to correct for it with the materials I have (printed cards, a timer, and a whiteboard). No philosophical bibliography or citation should be required; I need to run this entirely offline. Frame the instructions using systems-language where natural, but keep the philosophical content accessible without prior training.
