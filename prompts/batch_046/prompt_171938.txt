Consider two institutional practices governing the identity of cultural structures over time. In many Western heritage-law frameworks, a historic building that has been destroyed and then reconstructed with largely new materials is typically classified as a replica or denied its original protected status, on the grounds that the original object no longer exists because its material parts have not persisted. By contrast, in the Japanese practice surrounding the Ise Grand Shrine, the entire structure is dismantled and rebuilt at regular intervals—using fresh timber—on an adjacent site; the rebuilt edifice is treated not as a copy but as the very same shrine, with its continuity understood to rest on form, ritual function, and spatial relation rather than on the endurance of any particular planks or beams.

What metaphysical theory of persistence does each practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: does the Western legal framework commit to an endurantist view that requires the continuous presence of the same material substrate, and does the shrine practice presuppose a perdurantist account in which successive temporal parts are united by spatiotemporal and functional continuity, or perhaps a non-material essentialist view compatible with three-dimensionalism? Assess whether either practice strictly entails its apparent metaphysics, or whether both are philosophically underdetermined—for instance, whether a four-dimensionalist could regard the Western building as a scattered temporal worm missing intermediate parts, or whether an endurantist could accommodate the shrine by appealing to an immaterial form that survives part-replacement. Finally, describe what would have to change in heritage-administration and ritual governance if the metaphysical commitment implicit in one practice were replaced by that of the other—specifically, how criteria for authenticity, restoration funding, legal standing, and ceremonial continuity would need to be revised.
