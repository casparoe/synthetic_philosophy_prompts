(1) In a finished novel, Macbeth has no metaphysically open alternative to killing Duncan; the text fixes every event.
(2) Nevertheless, readers rightly hold Macbeth, not the author, morally responsible for the murder.
(3) Frankfurt’s well-known challenge to the principle of alternative possibilities shows that lacking alternatives does not excuse an agent, provided the agent acts from a will they endorse.
(4) Macbeth endorses the murder—he wills it—so his lack of alternatives is excused, and responsibility sticks to him alone.
(5) Physical determinism is structurally identical to narrative closure: prior states of the universe fix all subsequent actions.
(6) So if determinism is true, human agents are like Macbeth—lacking alternatives but responsible whenever they endorse their actions.
(7) But now consider an engineer who psychologically conditions a subject to endorse harming others; the subject wills the harm and lacks alternatives.
(8) By the same logic, the engineered subject bears full responsibility, and the engineer bears none.
(9) This extends to any manipulator—neuroscientist, cult leader, deterministic cosmos—who merely ensures the victim’s identification with the harm.
(10) Therefore, Frankfurt-style compatibilism seems to absolve every external formative source, isolating responsibility in the bare moment of endorsement.

This feels like it proves too much: it licenses a radical moral isolation of the agent from every condition that shaped them.
