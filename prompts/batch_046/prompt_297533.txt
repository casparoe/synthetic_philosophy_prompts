At a philosophy meetup last week two regulars, Maya and Leo, got into it over ubuntu and whether it can actually ground internal reasons. I was just listening, not picking sides.

Maya said that on a thick relational view — not just "people are social" but that personhood is literally constituted by the web of relations — when an elder dies with an unfinished oral archive, the survivors don't merely have an external reason to finish it. They have an internal one, because their's own personhood is partly constituted through that node. She invoked the idea that a person is a person through other people and said it isn't just descriptive; it's a normative ontology. If you break the link, you're not just failing the dead, you're damaging your own status as a person.

Leo said that's metaphysical sleight of hand. Even if personhood is reletational, that doesn't mean every thread in the web generates an internal reason for every other thread. He compared it to saying you're part of a body so you have an internal reason to repair the liver — that's not how reasons work. For Leo, internal reasons have to connect to your actual motivational set, and citing ubuntu just relabels social pressure as ontology. He said Maya's view can't explain why a survivor who genuinely doesn't care about the archive isn't just "less of a person" — which is coercive.

Maya answered that Leo's whole frame presupposes a pre-social individual that ubuntu denies. If the self is constituted relationally, then caring for the network isn't an added preference; it's part of what it means to have reasons at all. She said Leo is treating "internal" like Williams does — anchored in a prior individual psychology — but ubuntu offers a different anchor: internal reasons flow from the relational structure you're embedded in, not from private desire.

Leo pushed back that even on relational views you need a line between reasons that flow from your actual commitments versus ones imposed by structure. Otherwise you can't account for someone genuinely rejecting communal obligations without being metaphysically defective. He asked: if the survivor really doesn't identify with the elder's project, does ubuntu say they still have an internal reason, or does it just say they're failing at being a person? And if it's the latter, that's not ethics, that's social control dressed up as metaphysics.

Say what both are missing, especially about whether ubuntu actually resolves the internal/external distinction or just relocates it. And please say what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
