I am a graduate student in a joint classics and philosophy of education seminar, and next week I am presenting a paper on Plutarch’s paired biographies. I defend the view that the Parallel Lives should be read not as hagiographic exemplars meant for direct moral imitation, but as a comparative apparatus for what I call “normative triangulation”: a disciplined practice in which the reader holds two incommensurable lives in simultaneous attention—say, Alexander and Caesar—not to derive a rule or find a mirror, but to refine practical judgment through the friction between their respective failures of self-governance. I argue that this explains Plutarch’s pairing strategy: the point is never that the Greek “resembles” the Roman, but that comparing their incommensurable vices produces a kind of moral attention that single biography cannot cultivate. The thesis depends on distinguishing this comparative attention from both virtue-ethical emulation and narrative-identity construction.

The audience is a joint seminar of about twelve: half are classicists who read Plutarch primarily within the didactic “mirror for princes” tradition and will defend textual fidelity to that framework; two are virtue ethicists working in recent exemplarist literature who will ask whether my account abandons the motivational power of admiration and emotional attachment; one is a narrative-identity theorist influenced by MacIntyre who treats biography as constitutive of the self rather than educative of it; and the remainder are philosophy of education scholars skeptical that any ancient text can speak to modern pedagogical concerns without anachronism. The classicists care about whether I am imposing a modern comparative framework on an ancient moral pedagogy; the virtue ethicists care about whether judgment can be trained without being moved; the narrative theorist cares about whether I treat biography as transparent rather than world-building; and the education scholars care about whether this yields anything transferable beyond the seminar room.

Please prepare my Q&A defense sheet. Order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be to the core claim, phrased as each subgroup would actually phrase them in the room. Identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, explain precisely why it would unravel the position rather than merely damage it, and tell me which objection I should concede up front in my opening remarks in order to defuse it. Write the response in American English, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
