I have just come out of a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether cross-cultural variation in folk morality undermines moral objectivity, and I want to see whether her judgments form a consistent, implicit rule. She is not a professional philosopher, but she has firm intuitions about which disagreements are “real” moral conflicts and which are not. Here is the track record I extracted from our discussion:

1. In Western clinical settings, medical autonomy centers on the individual patient; in many East Asian settings, the family makes the decision and the patient may not be told a terminal diagnosis. She said this does not threaten objectivity: both cultures value respect for persons, but they institutionalize care differently.

2. In some cultures direct eye contact with an elder signals respect; in others it signals defiance. She said this is purely conventional etiquette, not a moral disagreement at all.

3. In some societies infanticide is permitted under conditions of severe disability or extreme resource scarcity; in liberal Western societies it is categorically prohibited. She said this is a genuine threat: both sides have full information, and the divergence is not resolvable by correcting a factual error.

4. Religious food taboos—for instance, treating beef or pork consumption as sacrilegious versus treating it as nutritionally neutral—she dismissed as conventional or theological, not as a moral disagreement that bears on objectivity.

5. In some cultures elder care is an unconditional collective duty enforced by social sanction; in others it is treated as a supererogatory personal choice or a state responsibility. She said this does not threaten objectivity, because the Western individualist framing is, in her view, distorted by market ideology; with corrected reasoning both sides should converge on collective obligation.

6. In some cultures resource distribution is governed strictly by proportional contribution; in others by equal need regardless of contribution. She said this is a genuine threat to objectivity, since both frameworks are internally coherent and neither rests on an empirical mistake.

Now the new case: in some societies, desecrating a national symbol—say, burning a flag—is treated as a serious moral wrong that warrants social punishment and exclusion; in others it is treated as protected political expression carrying no moral weight whatsoever. Both sides understand the empirical nature of the act and its social consequences.

Predict her verdict on flag desecration. Then identify the implicit rule she seems to be running—distinguishing binding from individualizing moral domains, distinguishing correctable from incorrigible disagreements, or something else entirely. Lay out the two or more competing rules that fit her first six verdicts, show precisely what each predicts about the flag case, and say which prediction you think is best supported, with your reasoning. Finally, specify one further cross-cultural case—different from the six above—that would best discriminate between the two leading rules you have identified.
