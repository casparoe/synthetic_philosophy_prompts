I just spent three hours arguing with a close friend about whether presentism makes the work of archivists conceptually incoherent—if only present things exist, in what sense are they preserving "the past" at all? Tomorrow I am delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "The Weight of What Is No Longer Here," attended by roughly equal numbers of metaphysicians who specialize in theories of time and practicing archivists and museum conservators who have never encountered presentism or eternalism by name but whose professional lives depend on the idea that there is something genuinely past to safeguard. The remarks should run about six minutes. The tension between presentism and eternalism must do real structural work in the speech: I should not be able to simply declare that we preserve an objectively existing past (which begs the question against presentism) nor should I be able to treat all times as equally real in a way that renders preservation metaphysically redundant (which alienates the practitioners and flattens their labor). Instead, I want to navigate the middle by proposing—without resolving—the notion that archival practice constructs a kind of "thick present" in which past artifacts become genuinely available to present concern and care, without committing to whether those artifacts exist simpliciter outside that relation of concern. The speech needs to land as a genuine welcome and framing for the interdisciplinary work ahead, not as a disguised philosophy lecture; it should honor the expertise in the room without patronizing either half. Please write these remarks. If the premise is confused—for example, if presentism and archival responsibility are not actually in tension in the way I describe, or if no opening address can genuinely serve both audiences without implicitly taking a metaphysical stance—say so plainly rather than writing something that merely sounds accommodating.
