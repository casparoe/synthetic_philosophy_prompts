I'm preparing the interview outline for an episode of my podcast, Rational Reconstruction, and next week's guest is a philosopher—I'll refer to her as the author of a recent interpretive monograph—who defends a deliberately narrow reading of Kant's prohibition on lying in the essay commonly translated as On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy. On her account, Kant's absolutism is not a blanket ban on every false utterance but applies specifically to assertoric testimony: statements made within the implicit contract of rational communication that underwrites both moral agency and juridical accountability. She argues that this is precisely why Kant permits silence or strategic evasion in the murderer-at-the-door scenario—those omissions are not testimony—while still condemning false speech, and that commentators have systematically missed this because they read the essay through the lens of the Groundwork's Formula of Humanity rather than through the Doctrine of Right's account of communicative obligation. To prevent the conversation from collapsing into a standard rehearsal of the counterexample, I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that treat her reading as a live philosophical position rather than an exegetical curiosity.

At least two questions should probe the boundary between lying and non-assertoric deception—ironic utterance, strategic ambiguity, or algorithmic opacity in systems that withhold information without technically producing a false assertion—asking how her Kantian framework classifies these cases and anticipating her likely appeal to the absence of an explicit truth-claim. Each of these should be paired with a follow-up that presses on whether the distinction collapses once the listener's reasonable communicative expectation is taken into account, and whether her view can accommodate modern forms of engineered opacity without expanding the category of testimony beyond recognition. Another pair of questions should address the consistency of her account with Kant's taxonomy of perfect and imperfect duties: if direct lying is a perfect duty because it violates rational nature itself, does graded or indirect deception fall into the imperfect duty of beneficence, or does it generate a separate, intermediate category of communicative wrong? Include the anticipated reply—likely an appeal to the intentional structure of the maxim—and a counter-follow-up that asks whether that structure can be identified without already assuming the moral verdict she wants to defend. The sequence should also contain one question about collaborative or self-deception—cases in which the victim actively participates in sustaining the false belief—asking whether her testimony-based framework can explain why Kant appears to treat these differently from direct lying, or whether they reveal a gap in the juridical model she favors.

Finally, include one question that asks what kind of textual or historical evidence—perhaps an overlooked lecture note, a revision in Kant's late ethical writings, or an unexpected parallel in his logic lectures—would cause her to abandon the narrow juridical reading, framed sharply enough that she cannot retreat to the claim that this is merely a research program rather than an objection. The overall tone should be polished, conversational, and rigorous, suitable for an audience that knows Kant's basic texts but is not composed entirely of specialists in eighteenth-century moral philosophy.
