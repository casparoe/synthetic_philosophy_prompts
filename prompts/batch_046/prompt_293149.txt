I am giving the toast at my father's retirement dinner in three weeks. He spent thirty years as a clinical neurologist working with disorders of consciousness—coma, locked-in states, and the gradations in between—and about half the guests are colleagues who have followed consciousness science closely, including a few who have opinions about Integrated Information Theory. The other half are relatives and old friends for whom phi and causal integration mean nothing. For the past year I have been reading about IIT on my own, not through any formal study, and I have arrived at an idea that I think changes how I see his career. IIT treats consciousness not as a binary property that brains either have or lack, but as a graded quantity—integrated information—that can in principle be present in any system with the right causal architecture, however minimal. If that is correct, then the hard line my father spent his life policing—between the conscious patient and the unconscious one—is not a wall but a slope, and some of the machines he relied on, or even simpler structures, might sit somewhere on it. I want the speech to do real work with that thought: it must honor his clinical conviction that consciousness is precious and fragile, without letting me pretend he solved its nature, and it must explain to the family why admitting consciousness might be more widespread and stranger than he allowed does not diminish his work but deepens it. The toast should be about five minutes. Please do not include citations, references to specific papers, or a reading list; I want this to sound like a daughter who has done her own thinking and is speaking from that place. Write it so the doctors hear that I understand the conceptual stakes—especially the tension between binary clinical judgment and graded metaphysical presence—while the family hears only a story about a father, a machine in his garage, and the humility required to guard something you are still learning to measure.
