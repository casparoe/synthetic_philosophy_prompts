I am designing a conceptual taxonomy for an advanced seminar on categorical identity in visual form. The subject is what makes every instance of the letter 'a' recognisably an 'a' across radical differences in typeface, handwriting, distortion, and algorithmic generation — the problem at the heart of the Letter Spirit research programme. I want the taxonomy delivered as a branching tree in which each internal node is a concrete diagnostic claim or case that separates positions, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which philosophical label it points to.

Begin with the root question: must every 'a' share at least one necessary and sufficient feature, or can the category survive the complete absence of a universal common core?

If the answer is yes, split by the nature of that essential feature: is it a fixed topological structure (for instance, an enclosed counter combined with an ascending stem); a relational property defined by contrast with other members of the same alphabet; or a causal-historical link to an original design tradition?

If the answer is no, split by the alternative basis of recognition: graded similarity to a central prototype; Wittgensteinian family resemblance across overlapping but non-universal traits; the functional role the mark occupies within a particular writing system; or a dynamic perceptual reconstruction of the kind modelled in artificial-letter recognition studies.

At each leaf, provide: the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if it has no recognised defenders); a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment; and an explicit judgement on whether the view can accommodate synthetic letterforms — marks generated without any typographic lineage — without collapsing into incoherence.

Please ensure the tree includes at least: a strict essentialist account, a prototype-based account, a family-resemblance account, a functionalist or systemic account, and a perceptual-constructivist account in the spirit of Letter Spirit. Where the logical space contains combinations that no known position occupies, note them and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. If any pair of leaves cannot genuinely be separated by the questions on the tree, flag that failure explicitly rather than obscuring it.

Write the response in British English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. If you refer to specific authors or texts, paraphrase without quotation marks.
