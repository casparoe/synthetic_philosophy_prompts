I have been thinking, without any formal training, about whether historians can ever judge the past on its own terms, or whether they are inevitably trapped within the assumptions of their own era. The problem struck me most clearly through an analogy with poetry translation: when a translator renders an ancient verse into modern English, they must often choose between preserving the original rhyme scheme—whose sounds depend on phonetics that no longer exist—and preserving the literal meaning, which may feel flat. It seems to me that historical writing involves a similar forced choice. A historian describing, say, a medieval guild must either use modern economic concepts like 'market' and 'labour', which the guild members themselves would not have recognised, or else employ the guild's own vocabulary, which risks becoming incomprehensible to a present-day reader.

From this, I have begun to wonder whether any historian can truly maintain what I might call a 'double vision'—holding both the past's conceptual framework and our present one in simultaneous suspension—or whether the very act of selecting which events matter and which questions are worth asking is already shaped by present concerns in a way that makes such suspension impossible.

Would you write a dialogue between two people who have been discussing this? One should defend the possibility of that 'double vision', arguing that careful methodological discipline can allow a historian to move between frameworks without collapsing them into one another. The other should argue that presentism is inescapable, not merely in the language used, but in the prior decision of what counts as historically significant. Please make both voices equally sharp and responsive: they should address each other's specific points rather than speaking past one another. I do not want any bibliography, reading list, or references to specific scholars—just the conceptual argument, developed in British English.
