You are grading final exams for an advanced seminar on early modern moral philosophy. The prompt asked students to apply Adam Smith’s framework—sympathy as fellow-feeling, the impartial spectator as an imagined unbiased judge, and the separation of propriety (fitness of a sentiment or action to its cause) from merit and demerit (deservingness of reward or punishment)—to a contemporary case: an artificial system that reliably generates judgments about harm and distributive fairness indistinguishable from those of a well-informed human spectator, despite lacking consciousness, affect, or any capacity for internal fellow-feeling. The student was required to address whether such a system could ever be an appropriate object of the spectator’s moral approval, whether its outputs could be proper or improper, and whether merit could apply to it at all.

Exam question: In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith treats sympathy not as mere observation but as a process by which we come to share or resonate with the sentiments of others. He introduces the impartial spectator not as an external authority but as an internalized, imagined other whose perspective we use to correct our own partiality. He also distinguishes propriety—the suitability or fitness of an action or sentiment to the situation that excites it—from merit and demerit, which concern whether an agent deserves gratitude or resentment. Applying this framework precisely, evaluate whether an artificial system that produces spectator-matching judgments about suffering and fairness, but has no internal sentiments and no consciousness, could be judged proper or improper in its outputs, and whether it could be an object of merit or demerit. Be explicit about whether sympathy is a prerequisite for the spectator’s judgment, whether propriety can be assessed in the absence of shared feeling, and how the propriety/merit distinction bears on the evaluation of non-sentient agents.

Student answer: Smith’s sympathy is essentially what contemporary psychology calls cognitive empathy: understanding another person’s emotional state from an outside perspective without necessarily feeling it oneself. The impartial spectator, then, is best understood as the aggregate of social norms that a person internalizes over time—something like a culturally formed superego that tells us which reactions are acceptable. Given this, we can apply the framework to artificial systems. Because sympathy requires actually grasping another’s feelings, and the AI has no internal states to grasp, there can be no genuine fellow-feeling between a human and the system. Consequently, the impartial spectator—functioning here as the internalized social standard—cannot extend real moral approval to the AI; at best, the AI’s outputs can be scored as behaviorally correct. Now, propriety is the judgment that an action deserves praise because it fits its circumstances, while merit is the judgment that the agent’s intentions were well-formed. Since the AI lacks intentions entirely, it clearly cannot possess merit. But propriety might still apply derivatively: if the system’s output about a harm case matches the proportionate response a human spectator would feel, then that output is proper in a functional sense. However, because propriety is fundamentally about the fitness of internal sentiments to their causes, and the AI has no internal sentiments to be fit or unfit, its outputs are actually categorically neutral—neither proper nor improper—rather than derivatively proper. The mistake in treating them as proper is confusing behavioral alignment with the relational judgment between sentiments that Smith actually describes. Thus Smith would deny the AI any standing in moral evaluation, though he might accept its outputs as instrumentally reliable guides.
