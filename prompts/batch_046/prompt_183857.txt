Hey—I'm putting together a problem-set for my decision-theory class and I've hit this weird case that I can't tell if it's a named thing or just me being dense. Picture an agent who gets a message: if you don't pay me a small fee, then in exactly one of two possible worlds you'll suffer a huge loss, but I won't say which world, and the selection is tied to your decision in a counterfactual way. The agent thinks: well, if I pay, I avoid both worlds; if I don't, I'm facing a disjunction of disasters. But here's the part that confuses my students—because only one branch actually realizes, nobody in any actual world suffers both, yet the agent still feels pressure to pay to cover both possibilites. Is there an established term for this kind of split-threat structure where the harm is exclusive across worlds but the decision pressure comes from treating the disjunction as if it were a single certain harm? It feels adjacent to counterfactual mugging, but in the standard version there's just one payout world, not an XOR split. And it feels adjacent to those blackmail scenarios where you're threatened with one of several bad outcomes unless you comply, except here the causal mechanism is counterfactual rather than direct. Does this hybrid have a name of its own, or is it just a framing variant? I want to be precise about whether the XOR structure changes the decision-theoretic analysis—like, does it collapse into the single-world case under some symetry assumption, or is there a genuinely distinct failure mode? Also, where are the edges: if I change the number of worlds from two to three, is it still the same phenomenom? I'd like a clear carving with boundary conditions, not just a bare label.
