Question: Explain Amartya Sen’s liberal paradox. In your answer, (a) state the three conditions—unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism—that jointly generate the contradiction; (b) apply them to Sen’s original two-agent scenario involving preferences over whether a controversial novel is read by one agent, the other agent, or by neither; and (c) explain why weakening the Pareto principle to a condition that only forbids society from strictly ranking an option lower when every individual ranks it lower does not dissolve the tension.

Student answer: Sen’s paradox, introduced in 1970, shows that no social aggregation rule can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain (U), the Pareto principle (P), and minimal liberalism (L). In the standard example, Lewd and Prude rank three outcomes: Lewd reads Lady Chatterley’s Lover, Prude reads it, or no one does. Lewd orders them (Prude reads) > (Lewd reads) > (no one reads). Prude orders them (no one reads) > (Prude reads) > (Lewd reads). Minimal liberalism assigns each agent decisive authority over the pair comparing their own reading to the unread outcome: Lewd’s preference ranks “Lewd reads” above “no one reads,” and Prude’s preference ranks “Prude reads” above “no one reads.” By Pareto, since both agents prefer “Prude reads” to “Lewd reads,” society must rank them the same way. Yet transitivity through the two liberal assignments yields “Lewd reads” > “no one reads” > “Prude reads,” hence “Lewd reads” > “Prude reads,” contradicting Pareto. This shows that even minimal rights over personal domains conflict with collective efficiency. Regarding part (c), adopting the weaker Pareto condition fails to resolve the paradox because the contradiction already depends on unanimous strict preference between “Prude reads” and “Lewd reads,” so relaxing the principle does not block the derivation. The only genuine escape is to restrict the domain of admissible preferences, which removes the specific ordering that drives the cycle.
