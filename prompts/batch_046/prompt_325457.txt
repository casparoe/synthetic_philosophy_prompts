I'm taking an upper-level bioethics seminar and we recently discussed the abolitionist project in biotechnology—the proposal, associated with David Pearce, that genetic and neuroengineering could eventually eliminate suffering by decoupling nociception from its negative affective tone, so that tissue damage is still registered but no longer hurts. I want to close our session with a very brief poem that takes this idea seriously rather than satirizing it. Please present the poem as exactly five numbered stanzas, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no title, no introductory paragraph, and no concluding commentary outside the numbering.

The poem should progress from the technical promise (redesigned pathways that preserve warning signals while removing anguish) through the evolutionary worry (that an organism lacking aversive experience might lose its urgency to avoid harm) to an open ending that leaves unresolved whether a world without suffering also loses the information that suffering once carried. Don't resolve the tension with a sermon for intervention or a shrug about natural cycles; instead, let the structural turn occur between the third and fourth stanzas, where the promise begins to sound like a loss. Write it in blank verse or free verse with careful line breaks, accurate enough that someone who has read the abolitionist literature recognizes the specific distinction between functional nociception and hedonic tone, but rendered as poetry rather than as philosophical exposition.
