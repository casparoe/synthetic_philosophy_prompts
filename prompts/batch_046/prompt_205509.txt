Next month I present a chapter of my thesis at a metaphysics and philosophy-of-language workshop. My claim is that the dispute between ontological pluralists and monists about material objects—whether there are genuinely many, or only one maximally inclusive object—is neither a Carnapian verbal dispute about the word "exists" nor a straightforward disagreement over existence claims. Instead, I argue that both camps share what I call a "compositional explanatory framing" (CEF): they agree on the distribution of fundamental physical properties, but deploy incompatible rules for which part-whole relations generate genuine explanatory demands in the special sciences. On my view, the pluralist treats biological function as ontologically committing to organisms, whereas the monist treats identical functional descriptions as pragmatic heuristics applied to a single object; the disagreement is therefore substantive—about which explanatory patterns carry ontological weight—but it is not about whether tables and chairs "exist" in any ordinary sense. The audience includes two deflationists who specialise in verbal disputes and regard ontological debates as pragmatic framework-choice, a scientific realist convinced that explanatory indispensability should settle ontology, and a philosopher of mind sceptical of metaphysics that ignores cognitive architecture. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of those participants would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises.
