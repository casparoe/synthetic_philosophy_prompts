I've been trying to formalize Davidson's account of radical interpretation for a philosophy of language seminar, and I've walked myself into a reductio that I can't seem to disarm without giving up something I want to keep. Here is the argument as it stands. (1) Radical interpretation requires assigning meanings and beliefs simultaneously, constrained by the principle of charity: we maximize agreement and rationality across the speaker's total system of attitudes. (2) Because belief and meaning are holistically interdependent, charity constrains only the global pattern of beliefs and meanings, not any particular pairing; without an independent anchor, distinct total theories can be equally charitable and rational. (3) So charity alone cannot block a Davidsonian version of Quinean indeterminacy—there could be two equally correct interpretations that assign different meanings to the same utterances while preserving overall coherence. (4) Davidson rejects such indeterminacy, insisting that there is a fact of the matter about what a speaker means. (5) To secure uniqueness, Davidson appeals to triangulation: interpretation is fixed by causal interactions among speaker, interpreter, and a shared world. (6) But triangulation requires that the interpreter can observe the speaker's behavior in response to features of a shared environment. (7) Now consider an interpreter attempting radical interpretation of a speaker who is causally isolated—say, an artificial agent trained exclusively on synthetic data, with no shared physical environment or observable interaction with the world that the interpreter can access. (8) In this case triangulation is unavailable, so Davidson's anti-indeterminacy mechanism fails, leaving charity alone, which by (2) and (3) yields indeterminacy. (9) Therefore, either Davidson must accept that interpretation is indeterminate for causally isolated speakers, or he must concede that radical interpretation is impossible in principle whenever shared causal interaction is absent. (10) But Davidson presents radical interpretation as a general model of linguistic understanding, not one restricted to agents embedded in a common physical environment. (11) Hence his view either collapses into Quinean indeterminacy or applies only to an implausibly narrow class of speakers.

The conclusion feels wrong—Davidson shouldn't be forced into either horn—but I can't locate the slip. Is the error in (2), in treating charity as too weak to constrain local meaning? Or is it in (6), in assuming that triangulation requires direct observational access rather than just a common causal history? Or perhaps (7) introduces a case that Davidson's framework was never meant to cover, making (9) a non sequitur? If I have to choose, I'd prefer to keep (5) and (6)—that shared causal interaction genuinely fixes interpretation—while rejecting the idea that this restricts the scope of radical interpretation. But I want to know exactly what I'm paying: if I reject (7) as an illegitimate test case, am I just defining the problem away, or is there a principled reason why causally isolated agents fall outside the domain of interpretation proper? And if I instead reject (2), what resources does charity actually have to block indeterminacy without triangulation?
