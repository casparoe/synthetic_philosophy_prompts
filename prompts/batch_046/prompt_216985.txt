Write a taxonomy in the form of a branching decision tree that classifies positions on whether, and in what form, biography serves as an instrument of moral learning, with particular attention to the legacy of Plutarchan comparative lives and to hagiographic writing. The instrument is intended for a graduate seminar audience; it should contain exactly six leaves, each corresponding to a named or unnamed stance on the pedagogical function of narrative life-writing. Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of authorities: begin with the question of whether moral learning from biography requires a unified, teleologically organized narrative of the subject's life, or whether fragmentary, episodic presentation can be equally or more instructive. From there, branch on whether the appropriate reader-response is imitative identification, counter-identificatory correction, or conceptual abstraction detached from the subject's particularity. Then branch on whether idealization and selective omission—hagiographic smoothing—corrupt the pedagogical value by presenting an unattainable model, or whether such smoothing is a necessary condition of aspiration and normative orientation. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete claim a student could assess without knowing which interpretation it maps onto; no question should ask directly whether one holds the Plutarchan view. The six required leaves are: (1) the comparative-mirror view, in which biography teaches by juxtaposing paired lives to reveal the contingency of virtue; (2) the aspirational-hagiographic view, in which idealized narrative provides a regulative ideal for character formation; (3) the diagnostic-critical view, in which demystified, failure-laden biography teaches through anatomical self-correction; (4) the narrative-constructivist view, in which biography does not transmit moral content but helps the reader construct a coherent practical identity; (5) the skeptical-fragmentary view, in which only episodic, non-teleological lives avoid false unity and thus preserve epistemic honesty about moral development; and (6) the abstractionist view, in which particular lives are merely illustrative occasions for grasping universal moral principles. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence description of its central mechanism and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. After mapping the tree, identify any combinations of answers that fall outside these six leaves—particularly whether a position combining teleological unity with counter-identification and anti-idealization is occupied, and if not, diagnose whether that space is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that the tree's questions cannot cleanly separate, explaining which shared answer forces the ambiguity. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions; do not invoke obscure technical frameworks that a well-read graduate student in philosophy or literature could not be expected to recognize without external search. The prompt itself should be self-contained and answerable from general familiarity with Plutarch, the concept of hagiography, and the philosophy of moral education.
