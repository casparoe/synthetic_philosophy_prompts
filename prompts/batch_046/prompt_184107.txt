Next week I present to my philosophy of law reading group a paper that attempts to reframe the classic debate between Hart and Devlin over legal moralism. Rather than treating the dispute as a straightforward clash between liberal harm principles and conservative social cohesion, I defend the narrower thesis that legal enforcement of a moral norm is legitimate only when that norm operates as a Schelling-point coordination device for social trust, which requires not merely widespread private belief but genuine common knowledge of that belief among citizens. I was discussing this with colleagues after our seminar on social ontology, and they warned me that the position risks collapsing either into Hart’s liberalism—if coordination is ultimately reducible to harm avoidance—or into Devlin’s moral conservatism—if any shared moral belief automatically qualifies as coordinatively necessary. The audience includes two committed Hartians well-versed in decision-theoretic social choice, a Devlin sympathizer who emphasizes the disintegration thesis, a legal positivist skeptical of any normative bridge between law and morality, and a social epistemologist interested in common-knowledge reasoning.

Please prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging each is, and phrased as each interlocutor would phrase them. I need to know: the Hartians’ charge that my coordination condition is either redundant with the harm principle or covertly question-begging; the Devlin sympathizer’s claim that I have smuggled in a functionalist criterion that ignores the expressive and symbolic value of law; the positivist’s insistence that common-knowledge conditions are descriptively false for modern pluralistic societies and therefore useless as legal criteria; and the social epistemologist’s worry that treating morality as a coordination problem instrumentalizes normative commitments in a way that undermines their authority. For each, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and the weakest point in my own reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook.

Finally, include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—namely, that if legitimate legal enforcement requires common-knowledge coordination, then the state should enforce false but widely believed moral propositions whenever they stabilize social order—and advise me on how to block that consequence without abandoning the core of my view. The result should read as a practical prep sheet for the question period, not as a reconstructed defense of my paper.
