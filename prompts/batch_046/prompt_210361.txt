Write a blank-verse poem of six four-line stanzas that takes Hofstadter’s knob-twiddling model of conceptual creativity—where ideas are points in a multidimensional space and invention is the sliding of conceptual knobs—as both subject and formal architecture. In each stanza, the first line must remain word-for-word identical, representing a frozen invariant (the problem’s core constraint that never shifts). The second and third lines should vary analogically but in mechanically coupled pairs: whatever conceptual shift occurs in line two must force a corresponding shift in line three, as if turning one knob necessarily moves another. The fourth line should register the cumulative state of these coupled adjustments. By the final stanza, the poem should reveal that the creative solution is not the extreme setting of any single knob but a specific aligned configuration in which several coupled dimensions settle together; the invariant first line should then be readable in a new light, its meaning transformed by the accumulated shifts around it. Render the idea accurately enough that someone familiar with the model recognizes the structure of analogical variation and constrained exploration, yet keep the language clear for a reader encountering it for the first time. Close with the sense that the space of possible variations is vast, but meaningful invention occupies only a narrow corridor where coupled knobs align without breaking the frozen frame.
