Since leaving experimental physics and turning to moral and legal philosophy, I have been trying to extract the underlying logic of transitional justice mechanisms rather than memorize historical narratives. A colleague who specializes in these questions holds five positions that I am trying to systematize: she considers South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission justified in offering individual amnesty conditional on full public disclosure; she rejects Argentina's blanket amnesty laws that required neither disclosure nor accountability as conceptually incoherent with transitional justice; she accepts Rwanda's gacaca courts, where reduced sentences accompany community-level testimony and confession, as legitimate; she rejects any hypothetical scheme granting immunity solely in exchange for private confession to victims' families with no archival or public record; yet she insists that public confession without any corresponding legal benefit remains morally obligatory, though not sufficient to justify amnesty. I now present her with a proposed mechanism I call the Archive Protocol: mid-level perpetrators, but not commanders, receive complete immunity from both criminal prosecution and civil liability in exchange for exhaustive disclosure deposited in an international historical archive accessible to researchers but neither published for the general public nor communicated directly to victims' families. Would she endorse this protocol? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the competing normative principles that accommodate her five prior judgments—perhaps one grounded in public acknowledgment, one in victim-centered restoration, one in institutional record-keeping for historical truth, and one in hierarchical accountability that distinguishes commanders from subordinates. For each principle, state whether the Archive Protocol satisfies or violates it, identify which of her prior cases is the closest analog, and propose one further hypothetical mechanism—say, disclosure exclusively to an international judicial panel with no domestic legal effect—that would distinguish the principle she is actually running from its closest competitor.
