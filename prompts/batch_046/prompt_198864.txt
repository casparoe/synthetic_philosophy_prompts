You edit narrative history manuscripts for a press that publishes for educated general readers, and you need an in-house style protocol for sentences that cast non-human forces—pathogens, climate anomalies, volcanic eruptions—as causal agents in political or social change. The conceptual danger is twofold: environmental determinism that erases human choice, and anthropomorphic "agency inflation" that treats microbes or weather patterns as if they held intentions. You are translating the philosophy-of-history distinction between structural causation (conditions that constrain or enable action) and intentional agency (action guided by reasons), supplemented by a specific vulnerability test: a non-human factor may only be framed as an "actor" if its removal in a plausible counterfactual substantially alters the outcome, and if human agents at the time recognized the constraint yet could not fully circumvent it.

Write a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that a copy editor without philosophy training can run in under five minutes on any proposed sentence. Structure it as an ordered sequence of checks, with clear criteria for passing, failing, or ambiguous results at each stage. Include explicit stop points: where the framing must be rewritten, where it may stand with a qualifier, and where the decision must be escalated to the author or a subject expert because it turns on judgments of explanatory weight rather than rule. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: one approving a sentence about the 1815 Tambora eruption reshaping European grain markets; one rejecting or forcing revision of a sentence that attributes the collapse of a dynasty primarily to prolonged drought; and one ambiguous case—such as a cholera outbreak altering urban sanitation law—where the procedure returns an unsettled verdict and hands the call to a person. Conclude with a brief flag identifying the misfire modes the procedure cannot resolve, particularly where the historical record genuinely supports both structural and intentional explanations with equal weight. Keep the protocol practical, avoid requests for bibliographies or named theorists, and mark every step that is actually a judgment call dressed up as a rule.
