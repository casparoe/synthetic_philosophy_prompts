Last evening I spent several hours arguing with a close friend about whether catastrophic famine should be understood as a natural disaster or as a species of institutional failure. She kept returning to the analogy that famine is essentially like an earthquake or a prolonged drought: an exogenous shock that strikes a society from outside its organizational structures, overwhelming local capacities in the same way that a seismic event overwhelms engineering standards. I countered that this framing sounds precisely like the image Amartya Sen sought to dismantle in his analyses of the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the excess mortality during China's Great Leap Forward, cases in which aggregate food availability and actual entitlement to food diverged in ways that should trouble the natural-shock picture.

But she then proposed an alternative comparison: perhaps famine is not like an earthquake but like a bank run, in which resources remain physically present yet panic, mispricing, and coordination failure suddenly render them inaccessible to those in need; or like a fatal traffic intersection, where danger lies not in the weather but in the design of signals, the incentives of drivers, and the absence of regulatory oversight. Could you audit both analogies -- the exogenous natural-shock model and the coordination-failure or design-flaw model -- by laying out an explicit correspondence table for each: what corresponds to the physical environment, what to social institutions, what to food availability versus entitlement collapse, and where state action or market response fits in? Apply the mapping specifically to Sen's three cases, showing precisely where the earthquake analogy breaks down and whether the bank-run analogy genuinely captures entitlement failure or merely substitutes one misleading image for another. Finally, tell me whether either analogy can actually carry the normative conclusion that famines are political crimes rather than tragedies, and if neither survives intact, suggest what a more honest analogical framework would look like.
