I hold the view that genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which an agent is genuinely required to perform both action A and action B, yet is unable to perform both—are impossible. On this view, every apparent dilemma dissolves upon careful analysis: either one of the competing obligations is not truly binding (because it is overridden by a stronger duty, or because the circumstances fail to generate it in the first place), or the agent's feeling of being torn is best explained by epistemic uncertainty about which single course of action is correct, together with appropriate agent-regret after a choice is made. I am especially interested in how this applies to so-called tragic choices in institutional decision-making—for instance, allocating scarce life-saving medical resources among patient groups that all seem equally deserving—where the decision-maker must actively select one path and thereby forego another that also appears morally demanded. What would count against my anti-dilemma position? I want an honest assessment of its exposure: which formal results in deontic logic (such as consistency conditions on obligation operators or the behavior of aggregation principles), which phenomenological descriptions of post-decisional moral residue, and which structural features of practical reasoning would genuinely threaten the view rather than merely produce discomfort? Please distinguish arguments or evidence that would be decisive—perhaps demonstrating that practical rationality must tolerate genuine contradictions in what is obligatory—from those that are only awkward, such as the persistence of guilt or the absence of any fully satisfactory resolution. Also address whether defenders of this view can absorb all counterexamples by progressively qualifying the notion of "requirement" until the position becomes unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a position in moral philosophy. Please provide your answer in American English.
