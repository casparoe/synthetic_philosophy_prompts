As a retired marine biologist now reading philosophy of biology in earnest, I keep returning to a borderline case that seems to slip through every net I cast. Consider an entity recovered from a hydrothermal vent: a synthetic protocell that maintains homeostatic boundary conditions through passive lipid rearrangement rather than active metabolism; it reproduces only when an external thermal gradient fractures its membrane, and its offspring inherit not genetic material but topological defects in the lattice. It therefore meets organizational criteria for life—boundary maintenance, reproductive continuity—while lacking metabolism, evolutionary selection, or conventional information storage. Write a short story, no longer than 400 words and in at most two paragraphs, narrated in the first person by the scientist who first isolates it. The narrative should dramatize the precise moment she realizes that her lifelong concept of life, formed over decades studying chemosynthetic bacteria, fractures when applied to this object, without resolving whether the entity is alive. Let the physical details—the vent, the lattice, the instruments—mirror the philosophical tension between metabolic and organizational accounts. Do not include a bibliography, citations, or named philosophers; the story should carry its conceptual weight through image, voice, and the slippage itself.
