Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines arranged in seven couplets. Each couplet should depict a distinct ordinary practice—giving orders, describing an object, praying, playing chess, greeting a stranger, measuring cloth, recounting a dream—without using the phrases “language game” or “family resemblance.” Adjacent couplets must share exactly one concrete word or image, but no word or image may appear in all seven couplets, and no couplet may share material with the one two places away. There should be no overarching narrative, no unifying metaphor, and no essential feature common to every practice; the poem’s only coherence should come from these local, pairwise overlaps. Use plain, observational diction drawn from everyday life, avoiding abstract philosophical vocabulary except where it naturally belongs to a particular scene. The structural absence of a universal thread should itself perform Wittgenstein’s idea of family resemblance, while the adjacent connections should feel genuinely meaningful rather than mechanically contrived. The result should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the concept recognizes the philosophical point being made structurally, and coherent enough that an unfamiliar reader finds it quietly strange rather than merely random.
