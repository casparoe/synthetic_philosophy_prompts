I am delivering a paper at next month's colloquium on Renaissance statecraft, and I need to be ready for the Q&A. My thesis is that Machiavelli's well-known contrast between fortune and virtù—particularly the image of fortune as a flooding river—has been systematically misread as a call for individual heroic mastery, when in fact it describes an institutional capacity for what I am calling distributed resilience. On my reading, virtù is not a personal quality of the prince but a property of constitutional and military arrangements—laws, civic militias, and fiscal mechanisms—that do not dominate contingency but absorb it before it destabilizes the state. I draw this not only from The Prince but from the less-discussed sections of the Discourses where Machiavelli praises republics for rotating offices and arming the populace, arguing that these structures collectively perform the work usually attributed to the singular virtuoso prince.

The audience will be about thirty scholars divided among intellectual historians suspicious of importing modern organizational theory into early modern texts, normative theorists who resist rehabilitating Machiavelli as a liberal-democratic institutionalist, and philologists who will test whether the Italian terminology supports any structural, collective reading of virtù at all. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face: order them by how likely and damaging they are, phrase each the way that audience would phrase it, indicate which ones have real force and what I should concede up front, provide a concise reply for each with an honest note on where that reply is weakest, and identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks. Also specify the single reply that has to land for the proposal to survive, and write your answer in American English.
