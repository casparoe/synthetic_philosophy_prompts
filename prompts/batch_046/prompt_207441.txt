hey, im putting together a discussion for a philosophy of film group and i keep coming back to Sliding Doors — the gwyneth paltrow movie where she either catches or misses the tube and her life splits into two timelines. i want to read it through david lewis's modal realism, but not in the obvious "parallel worlds exist" way.

specifically, im stuck on counterpart theory. lewis says that when a world splits, the people in each branch are counterparts — similar but numerically distinct individuals. but the film's emotional logic depends on us caring about both outcomes for the *same* helen, not for two counterpart-helens. does the film's pathos actually rest on rejecting counterpart theory? and if lewis is right, should we feel less invested in the "other" timeline?

theres also this weird thing near the end where the timelines almost resonate — she dies in one branch and in the other she meets someone who feels like a ghost from the first. lewis thinks worlds are spatiotemporally isolated "island universes" with no causal bleed. is that convergence just editing, or does it suggest a modal structure closer to... well, something where worlds aren't fully isolated but share some kind of structural connection? i don't want to invent a whole metaphysics, but does the film push back against the isolation thesis?

and then there's indexicality — "actual" is just an indexical for lewis, like "here" or "now." the film never gives us a god's-eye view that says which branch is "real." both just happen. does that make the film more lewisian than it looks? or does the train-platform framing secretly privilege one timeline as the base?

if you were writing this up, which scenes would you point people to? the split on the platform, the hospital sequence, the ending meeting? and honestly, does the film support modal realism or undermine it by making the worlds feel emotionally continuous?

oh and im not looking for a reading list, just your take.
