I'm writing this up for a seminar I'm taking on collective decision-making, and I'm stuck on a concrete case from my own experience. I'm serving on the steering committee of a community land trust that must decide whether to approve a mixed-use development proposal. We have agreed that approval requires three jointly necessary conditions: that the project is financially sustainable over a twenty-year horizon, that it respects the ecological constraints of the local watershed, and that it includes genuinely affordable housing units rather than token ones. At our last meeting, each member cast independent judgments on each premise. By straightforward majority vote, the group endorsed financial sustainability and ecological compliance, but rejected the claim that the affordable housing component was adequate. Yet when the chair called for a direct vote on whether to approve the development itself, the same majority approved it, apparently treating the three conditions as separately negotiable rather than as a logical conjunction required for the outcome. One member objected that this was collectively irrational—if we genuinely hold that two premises are satisfied but deny the third, and approval requires all three, then consistency demands rejection—while the chair replied that democratic legitimacy attaches to the direct expression of will on the final question, not to the mechanical derivation of conclusions from premise-level votes. I'm trying to understand whether there is a genuine philosophical problem here about how groups should combine individual judgments into a collective stance, and whether the answer depends on whether the premises are factual predictions or normative commitments. A further complication was proposed: one member suggested we resolve the tension by holding a second round of deliberation specifically aimed at revising our premise judgments until they align with our direct conclusion, arguing that a coherent collective mind should adjust its beliefs to match its will; another member warned that this invites strategic insincerity, with people altering their premise votes solely to justify an outcome they already prefer, and proposed instead that we treat the initial premise votes as fixed and derive the conclusion mechanically, even if the result is politically unpopular. Is there a principled way to choose between coherence of the collective belief-set and democratic fidelity to the direct vote, or does a better framework—perhaps one that aggregates reasons rather than votes—avoid the dilemma altogether?
