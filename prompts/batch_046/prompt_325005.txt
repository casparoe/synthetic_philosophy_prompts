Please compose a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains that alternate—on the aggregation problem that pits one instance of extreme suffering against an astronomically large population each enduring an imperceptible harm. The first refrain should state the aggregative intuition: that enough individually negligible pains, like dust specks in the eye each falling below the threshold of conscious suffering, compound into a moral catastrophe that outweighs the single catastrophic case. The second refrain should state the anti-aggregative resistance: that harms lacking minimal moral visibility cannot be summed additively, so that however vast the multitude, no accumulation of sub-threshold pains equals torture. The form itself should enact the philosophical deadlock—the refrains returning, shifting in context, never absorbing one another, until they stand side by side in the final couplet without reconciliation—thereby tracking the tension between distributive and additive moral reasoning. To keep the treatment from being generic, frame the dilemma through the specific conceptual tension of “moral resolution”: whether ethical calculus requires a perceptible unit of harm or can legitimately aggregate events too small to register in individual experience. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in value theory recognizes both the standard aggregation debate and the particular challenge of sub-threshold moral units, rendered in a restrained, formal register suitable for closing a graduate seminar. Present only the poem: exactly nineteen lines, no prose commentary, with the two refrains clearly identifiable and meeting in the final two lines as the form demands.
