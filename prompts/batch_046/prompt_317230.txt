I've been working through Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā in a philosophy-of-religion reading group that also draws on formal logic, and we've reached a sustained impasse about the tetralemma (catuskoti). Rather than continue arguing past each other, two members—one defending what we'll call the Dialectical-Performance Reading and one defending the Logical-Exclusion Reading—have agreed to produce an adversarial-collaboration document they could both sign. The Dialectical-Performance side holds that the tetralemma is not a logical schema but a soteriological performance that exhausts conceptual proliferation (prapañca) in order to induce direct, non-propositional insight into emptiness (śūnyatā); on this view the four corners—affirmation, negation, both, neither—are cognitive traps to be dissolved rather than evaluated for truth-value. The Logical-Exclusion side holds that the tetralemma is a rigorous exhaustive-elimination argument demonstrating that all four possible positions on the existence of svabhāva are incoherent when applied to dependently originated entities, thereby establishing emptiness as a precise metaphysical conclusion about the absence of intrinsic nature.

Please draft this joint statement. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the text's explicit rejections in the chapters treating conditions, essence, and the Tathāgata; about the centrality of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda); about what neither side endorses (for instance, neither reads Nāgārjuna as defending classical essentialism or as advocating straightforward nihilism); and about the formal presentation of the four corners. Second, a disagreements table or clearly delineated section stating the precise residual differences, each formulated in wording its own side accepts: especially (i) whether the simultaneous rejection of both and neither commits Nāgārjuna to a hidden fifth metaphysical position or genuinely avoids all propositional commitment; (ii) whether the argument requires a non-classical logical framework to avoid self-refutation, or whether it operates within classical bivalence by exhaustive elimination; and (iii) how to interpret the recursive claim that emptiness itself is empty—whether as a logical cancellation terminating in silence, or as a performative contradiction that opens onto non-conceptual awareness. Third, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux concerns whether empirical structural analogies, such as quantum non-locality understood purely as absence of localized intrinsic properties without ontological commitment to emptiness, can illuminate the Madhyamaka claim or necessarily collapse into metaphor; another concerns whether the soteriological aim of the text is compatible with its functioning as a logically valid argument. For each crux, specify a concrete conceptual test or piece of reasoning both sides agree would bear on it, recording in advance what each side predicts the outcome would be. Finally, separate clearly what is settled textual interpretation—what specialists broadly agree Nāgārjuna says—from what remains genuinely contested among Madhyamaka interpreters, and do not present either the Dialectical-Performance or Logical-Exclusion reading as the consensus view.
