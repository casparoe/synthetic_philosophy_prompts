I am presenting next Thursday to an interdisciplinary policy-school seminar—attended by quantitative behavioral geneticists who work with twin and adoption data, philosophy-of-science scholars skeptical of reductionist explanation, and education-policy advocates wary of biological determinism—my argument that heritability coefficients for academic achievement are routinely misinterpreted through what I call the static-partition fallacy: treating a population-level variance decomposition within a specific reference environment as if it were an individual-level causal budget that fixes the upper limit of environmental influence. The urgency is personal: since becoming legal guardian to my niece six months ago, I have heard school administrators cite twin-study heritabilities to justify cutting remedial funding, speaking as though a high heritability figure means low environmental malleability for any particular child. Please prepare me for the objections in the order they will actually arise. First, the geneticist who will defend the equal-environments assumption and insist that heritability is precisely defined as the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic variance in that population, not a claim about immutability—phrase this as they would, tell me what I should concede about statistical rigor, what I should hold about interpretive practice, and where my reply is weakest. Second, the philosopher who will charge that I am conflating statistical explanation with causal explanation and that variance-partitioning was never meant to answer intervention questions—again, in their voice, with concessions and holds. Third, the policy advocate who will worry that even my corrected framing still legitimizes genetic research that can be weaponized for tracking or resource allocation. For each, note the weakest point in my counter-reply and how long I have to deliver it. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely about adoption studies showing that heritability estimates for cognitive traits shift substantially across socioeconomic strata, which seems to undermine my claim that the coefficient is environment-relative rather than trait-inherent—and tell me exactly how to defend my weakest point there rather than hoping the room misses it. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need a speaking prep sheet for the room I will actually face.
