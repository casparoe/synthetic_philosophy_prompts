I have recently begun collaborating with a group that develops computer-vision diagnostics for agricultural pests, and I find myself genuinely puzzled by whether our trained classifiers alter what we literally see when we inspect leaf damage, or whether they only reshape our subsequent judgments. This has led me back to the philosophical debate over cognitive penetration of perception, and I would like you to compare two competing frameworks: the predictive-processing account, according to which high-level priors genuinely restructure the content of perceptual experience, and the informational-encapsulation view, which holds that cognition can influence attention and post-perceptual inference but never the intrinsic content of perception itself.

To keep the comparison grounded rather than purely abstract, please apply both frameworks to a somewhat niche phenomenon I have been calling the "diagnostic colour shift": experienced agronomists report that, after prolonged exposure to diseased crops, pathological tissue literally appears more saturated or luminous against healthy background, and they insist this is a change in how the damage looks, not merely a faster inference about it. For each framework, say whether such reports should be taken literally, whether they can be accommodated without abandoning the core commitments of the view, and what each position risks conceding should these reports prove reproducible under rigorous experimental conditions. Please present your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—my interest is strictly in the conceptual architecture and its vulnerabilities.
