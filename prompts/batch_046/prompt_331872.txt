You chair the forecasting review board of a regional development bank, and for every ten-year infrastructure proposal your staff must decide whether Keynes’s category of risk or of uncertainty applies, because the evaluation protocol differs: risk permits expected-value calculation with explicit confidence intervals, whereas uncertainty lacks a numerical basis and demands a qualitative judgment on the weight of argument, with optimization ruled out. Write a reusable decision procedure—ordered diagnostic questions, sufficiency conditions for each branch, explicit stopping rules, and marked judgment nodes—that a non-philosopher staffer can run in a single meeting, grounded in Keynes’s actual distinction between measurable probability relations and situations with no calculable basis, and incorporating his idea that the weight of an argument can increase or decrease without altering the assigned probability. Apply it to three proposals: financing a toll bridge with decades of traffic data; financing a pilot carbon-capture plant in a jurisdiction with no existing regulatory framework; and a hybrid geothermal project with well-mapped geology but an unknown future carbon price, where in at least one case the procedure must yield a result the committee will dislike—specifically, that a proposal with attractive point estimates must be classified as pure uncertainty and therefore denied quantitative ranking. Clearly flag where the procedure cannot settle the matter and must defer to a values-based vote, and state what the procedure is incapable of delivering. Separate what is settled about Keynes’s view from what scholars genuinely contest, especially whether weight of argument is an epistemic feature or a psychological one, and do not present a disputed interpretation as consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; do not restate the question or open with preamble. Write in American English.
