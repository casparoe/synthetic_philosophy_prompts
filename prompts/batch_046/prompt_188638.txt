Exam Question 4 (20 points): Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Be careful to distinguish it from the Manipulation Argument associated with philosophers such as Derk Pereboom. Then apply Strawson's reasoning to a non-human case: consider an artificial system initialized with a fixed reward architecture but, once deployed, continuously updates its own weights and can even rewrite portions of its objective function in response to environmental feedback. Does the possibility of such self-modification allow the system to escape Strawson's regress? Evaluate carefully.

Student Answer:

Strawson's Basic Argument holds that true moral responsibility requires being truly responsible for the way one is—specifically for one's mental nature, character, and motives. But being responsible for one's mental nature requires having brought it about, which means being causa sui, a cause of oneself. Since nothing with a temporal origin can be the cause of itself, true moral responsibility is impossible. The argument applies regardless of whether determinism is true: even if quantum indeterminacy introduces randomness into a system's development, the system still does not choose its initial nature or its basic cognitive architecture. This distinguishes the Basic Argument from Pereboom's Manipulation Argument, which demonstrates that determinism undermines responsibility because in manipulation cases we see that being externally caused removes freedom. Pereboom's reasoning relies on the principle that determinism eliminates the ability to do otherwise.

Applying this to the artificial system: it begins with a designed reward architecture, so at origin it clearly fails to be causa sui. Once deployed, however, it updates its own weights and can rewrite parts of its objective function. Through this autonomous self-modification, it becomes the causal source of its subsequent mental states. The regress therefore stops at the point where functional self-creation is achieved: after the first round of self-directed updates, the system is responsible for the way it now is, because the mechanisms of selection and revision are internal to its operating mental nature. Consequently, Strawson's argument blocks responsibility only for the system's initial outputs; for actions taken after it has achieved this functional self-creation, true moral responsibility is possible.
