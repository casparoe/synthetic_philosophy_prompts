Hi — I'm taking a course on environmental history and philosophy of science, and we've been debating whether non-human stuff like germs, climate shifts and volcanoes can be proper 'historical actors' without collapsing into determinism. I sketched an argument for my seminar and now it feels like it proves way too much, but I can't spot the weak link. Here it is: (1) To avoid environmental determinism, we have to treat climate, pathogens and volcanic events as agents rather than mere background conditions. (2) Agency, even in a minimal sense, requires some form of responsiveness — otherwise it's just brute mechanical causation. (3) The 1815 Tambora eruption and the following 'Year Without a Summer' disrupted European grain markets and migration patterns in ways that weren't predetermined by prior human choices, so volcanic climate forcing genuinely responds to its own internal dynamics. (4) Viral mutation during the second plague wave and El Niño oscillations show the same kind of adaptive feedback without consciousness. (5) So if responsiveness is enough for agency, then soil microbiomes, atmospheric carbon feedback loops and sedimentation patterns also acutally qualify as co-actors. (6) Once you admit all of these, every historical event is massively overdetermined by thousands of weak agents, which means human decisions never actually break the causal chain. (7) But that's definately absurd — people clearly do change history. So one of these has to go, but I really want to hang on to (1) and (3) because they seem central to treating nature as active rather than passive. Where's the mistake? Is it an equivocation on 'responsiveness', or a scope shift between weak and strong agency? I'd prefer the answer in British English, and please don't just point me to a bibliography — I need the actual diagnosis of the reasoning.
