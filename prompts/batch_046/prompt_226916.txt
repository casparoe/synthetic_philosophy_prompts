My focus is on what I call "disjunctive reasons" within the broader debate over free-floating rationales — that is, normative considerations that apply to no particular individual agent, yet are satisfied whenever any member of a relevant set of agents performs a requisite action, and that appear to persist even when no agent is actually positioned to respond. The standard literature on reasons without reasoners tends to split between metaphysically robust realism (reasons exist independently of all agency) and relational or constructivist accounts (reasons are essentially tied to the perspective of an agent). I suspect both frameworks overlook an intermediate category: reasons that are structurally free-floating relative to individual agents but are not metaphysically agent-independent, because they depend on the existence of a distributed network of possible actors. The precise question is whether these disjunctive reasons can be coherently distinguished from mere aggregations of agent-relative obligations, and if so, whether they generate genuinely novel normative demands — demands directed at no one in particular to alter institutional, ecological, or algorithmic structures — without collapsing into the ontology of group agency or collective moral persons.

The proposal should present the question so that it admits a determinate answer, explain why existing positions leave this middle ground unresolved, and outline a method that makes progress where direct metaphysical argument stalls. I envision a two-part approach: first, a conceptual analysis of the conditions under which a normative consideration has a plural or distributed subject rather than a singular one; second, application to three test domains — climate mitigation obligations, the normative status of wild ecosystems, and distributed algorithmic governance — to determine whether framing these cases in terms of disjunctive reasons yields evaluations that differ from both summed individual duties and fully agent-neutral moral facts. The timeline should be realistic: months one through three on the conceptual framework and distinction from aggregation; months four through seven on the test cases; months eight through ten on drafting the dissertation chapter; months eleven and twelve on revision and outlining a standalone article. A successful result would be a defensible taxonomy of disjunctive reasons and a demonstration that at least one test case is genuinely illuminated by it; failure would occur if the distinction collapses into standard agent-relative or agent-neutral categories, or if it proves too vague to alter any normative evaluation.
