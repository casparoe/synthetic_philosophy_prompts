I have just come away from an extended argument with a friend about teleosemantics and whether evolutionary selection history can genuinely ground mental content without presupposing the very intentionality it seeks to explain. We circled the familiar disjunction problem, but she insisted on a more specific difficulty she termed functional redundancy: cases in which a cognitive mechanism was selected for two genuinely distinct tasks that happen to be systematically co-instantiated in the organism's environment, so that no unique proper function can be assigned to determine representational content. I want to work out, in essay form, whether teleosemantics can survive this challenge. Defend the position that teleosemantics remains viable only if we abandon the idea of singular proper functions in favor of a hierarchically ordered structure of selection pressures—developmental, evolutionary, and perhaps phylogenetic—such that content is fixed by the dominant ordering rather than by any isolated function. Then weigh the central objection: that invoking hierarchical ordering to resolve ambiguity merely smuggles intentional interpretation into the characterization of evolutionary history itself, thereby failing to naturalize content. Reach and justify a clear verdict—either that the hierarchical reply succeeds at an acceptable theoretical cost, or that functional redundancy reveals a residual normativity that selection history cannot supply and that teleosemantics therefore collapses into a disguised form of interpretivism. The essay should be driven by conceptual analysis rather than by survey of the literature, and should identify precisely where the hierarchical-ordering defense is strongest and where it is most vulnerable.
