I serve as the senior registrar for a museum that certifies reconstructed objects for loans, insurance, and legal title, and I must sign off on whether a given item is the same work originally accessioned after parts have been replaced, destroyed, or rebuilt. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about fifteen minutes per case without philosophical training: an ordered sequence of practical tests translating the statue-and-clay problem into registry language, distinguishing material continuity, formal or structural continuity, and authorized causal origin. For each test, specify what counts as a passing answer, where the procedure stops with a no — not the same work, and where it must escalate to the director because the issue is institutional value rather than certifiable fact. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a ceramic figure whose original clay body has had over sixty percent of its volume replaced by new clay across successive restorations, yet whose surface form and provenance documentation remain unbroken; (2) a bronze statue melted in a fire, with the recovered metal recast using the original artist’s molds, yielding an object of identical composition but lacking spatiotemporal continuity of the metal — this is the case where I expect the procedure to return an answer I will not like, and I want the exact reasoning; and (3) a wooden arm on a mixed-media sculpture replaced with cast bronze after rot, executed by the original artist’s estate with explicit authorization. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot settle, such as whether institutional sentiment or market value should ever override certification, and flag any remaining hard cases where judgment is ineliminable.
