Construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies substantive positions on American exceptionalism treated simultaneously as a historical thesis about institutional origins, a normative claim about democratic obligations, and an ideology of national purpose. Begin with a concrete diagnostic case: a policymaker justifies an overseas intervention by appealing to America’s unique constitutional trajectory rather than to universal human-rights norms. From this root, branch first on whether exceptionalism is treated as empirically falsifiable, as a moral vocation independent of historical record, or as a socially useful myth regardless of truth value. Within each branch, subdivide by whether the view can accommodate the 1898 annexation debates without collapsing into pure imperialism or pure anti-imperialism, and whether it assigns primary explanatory weight to geographic isolation, Protestant culture, or Lockean property-rights frameworks. Every leaf should carry either a recognized label—such as a civic-republican or Lockean variant—or be marked unnamed, together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, for instance being forced to deny comparable constitutional stability in other settler societies, or defending coercive democratization without recourse to international legal warrant. Flag any pairs of named positions that converge on the same leaf despite differing rhetoric, and briefly diagnose whether the tree fails to separate them for logical, rhetorical, or merely unexplored reasons. The instrument should rely only on conceptual distinctions and the specified historical case; it must not request bibliographies, reading lists, or precise citations to scholarship.
