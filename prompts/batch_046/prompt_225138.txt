Consider the record of the Constitutional Initiative Council of the Canton of Alta Mura, a body charged in this Swiss-style direct-democratic system with certifying whether popular initiatives satisfy procedural and constitutional conditions for placement on the ballot. Over the past decade the Council has issued five rulings. First, it permitted an initiative lowering the signature threshold for facultative referendums from five percent to half a percent of registered voters, reasoning that greater accessibility strengthens democratic legitimacy without altering institutional architecture. Second, it permitted an initiative requiring that every cantonal tax increase be submitted to a mandatory referendum, treating this as a legitimate supplemental check on legislative fiscal authority rather than an usurpation. Third, it blocked an initiative that would have automatically suspended any federal statute deemed to conflict with local customary law, citing constitutional supremacy and the danger of fragmentation. Fourth, it blocked an initiative establishing a permanent citizens' jury of thirty randomly selected residents with binding authority over all environmental permits, holding that delegation of legislative and executive power to an unelected micro-body violates both democratic accountability and institutional continuity. Fifth, it permitted an initiative mandating that every ballot proposition be accompanied by an independent fiscal-impact statement audited before publication, viewing this as a procedural transparency measure that leaves ultimate decision-making power with the electorate. Now the Council faces a sixth proposal: an initiative allowing any group of two hundred citizens to trigger a binding referendum to repeal any cantonal statute enacted within the preceding twelve months, requiring signatures equal to two percent of the electorate gathered within sixty days, with no annual cap on the number of statutes that may be targeted, and further stipulating that any statute so repealed may not be re-enacted by the legislature in substantively identical form for eight years regardless of parliamentary turnover.
