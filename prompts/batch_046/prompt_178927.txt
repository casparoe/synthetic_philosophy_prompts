I'm a student in a philosophy of biology seminar working through the species problem—specifically whether species are natural kinds, individuals, or sets, and how interbreeding, phylogenetic, and ecological criteria conflict when applied together. For my final essay I want to treat the 1968 film Planet of the Apes as an unwitting philosophical thought experiment rather than simple allegory. The film gives us intelligent, cultured apes; mute, primitive humans who seem reproductively isolated; and an evolutionary continuity that Dr. Zaius, the Minister of Science, tries to suppress through a rigid taxonomy. I want a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously: does the film implicitly rely on morphological essentialism, on the biological species concept's dependence on reproductive isolation, or on a behavioral criterion that modern pluralists reject? Focus on three scenes—the tribunal where Taylor is classified, the archaeological excavation that reveals prior human culture, and the beach ending with the Statue of Liberty—and ask what each scene reveals about species membership. Are Taylor and the mute humans conspecific, or do they form a ring species linking human and ape lineages? Does Zaius defend his boundary because he treats species as fixed kinds, or because he recognizes that admitting evolutionary continuity would collapse the moral and legal order? I also want you to consider where the philosophical lens fails: is the taxonomy merely a vehicle for human racial politics, and if so, does that undermine the reading or show that taxonomic essentialism is always entangled with social hierarchy? Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; work only from common knowledge of the film and from standard philosophy of biology, including the ontological-individual view and species pluralism.
