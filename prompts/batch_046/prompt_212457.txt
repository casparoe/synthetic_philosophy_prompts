I retired from experimental physics about six years ago and since then I've been slowly working my way into philosophy, usually discussing things over coffee with my old lab mates. Recently I got pulled into Benatar's asymmetry -- the claim that absence of suffering is good for someone who never exists, but absence of pleasure isn't bad for them -- and I think I understand the basic structure, but my physics background keeps making me misread it.

The argument compares two scenarios: one where a specific person is born and lives a life with some mix of pleasure and pain, and one where that exact person is never brought into being. Benatar says the first scenario contains a real harm (the suffering) but no matching real loss in the second scenario, since there's nobody to miss the pleasure. But here's where I get confused. In statistical mechanics we talk about potential microstates, but we don't say an unrealized microstate is better off because it avoids heat; we just say it doesn't occur. So when Benatar treats the never-existing person as a kind of referance point that can be spared suffering, isn't he implicitly giving that potential being a kind of shadow ontological status -- like treating non-existence as a vacuum state that can be excited into being? I keep feeling like the asymmetry only works if we grant these counterfactual people some logical standing, almost like they have potential energy that gets actualized.

My friends tell me I'm importing physics metaphors where they don't belong, but I can't tell whether Benatar is actually relying on a hidden metaphysics of potential persons or if there's a cleaner way to frame the comparison without treating non-existence as a subject that can recieve benefits. Could you clear up whether the asymmetry commits to any ontology of the never-born, and if not, how the evaluation is supposed to work without one?
