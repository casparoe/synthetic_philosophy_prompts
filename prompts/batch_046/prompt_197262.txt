Consider the following exam question and student response.

Question: Some metaphysicians hold that ontological grounding is strictly irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive, which allows fundamentality to be defined as the absence of any ground for an entity. Others allow that grounding relations can be “impure” or involve symmetric co-dependence, such as between a determinable property (e.g., being colored) and its determinates (e.g., being scarlet). Assess whether allowing such co-grounding necessarily destroys the concept of a fundamental “bottom level” of reality. In particular, address (i) whether fundamentality requires a partial ordering, and (ii) whether the determinable/determinate case actually supports symmetric co-dependence.

Student answer:
“Grounding is the relation of metaphysical determination that is not causal. By definition, if A grounds B, then B exists in virtue of A, and A is more fundamental than B. This relation is irreflexive and asymmetric, which together mean it can be arranged into a partial ordering. Fundamentality is just being at the bottom of this ordering: an entity is fundamental if nothing grounds it.

If we allow co-grounding, then two entities ground each other. This violates asymmetry, so the partial ordering collapses. Without a partial ordering, we cannot identify a bottom level, because every entity in the loop is both grounded and ungrounded. Therefore, allowing co-grounding in the determinable/determinate case—where being colored and being scarlet depend on each other—undermines fundamentality entirely.

However, some philosophers think determinables are grounded in determinates, not vice versa. So in that specific case, there is no real co-dependence after all; asymmetry is preserved. Thus, the threat to fundamentality is only apparent.”
