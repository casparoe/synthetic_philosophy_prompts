I'm a journalist who writes on philosophy of science and formal epistemology, and next Thursday I'm presenting a revised framing of the Death in Damascus problem at a workshop on rational choice and self-prediction. My thesis is that the paradox dissolves only once we treat the agent's flight not as a straightforward causal or evidential selection, but as a reflexive calibration problem: the rationality of fleeing to Aleppo hinges on whether the agent models Death's predictive mechanism as algorithmically decoupled from her own decision procedure. I contend that both standard causal and evidential decision theories miss this decoupling condition, because they treat the predictor as an external variable rather than as a system that may be reflexively coupled to the agent's reasoning algorithm, and I propose a minimal constraint—call it reflexive stability—that determines when an agent should treat a predictive announcement as fixed versus as endogenous to her choice. The audience will be decision theorists, philosophers of science, and cognitive scientists who are broadly skeptical of metaphysically heavy causal accounts, who prize parsimony and empirical tractability, and who have recently defended evidential approaches to Newcomb-style problems; one prominent attendee has argued that Death in Damascus is best dissolved by treating the announcement itself as unreliable evidence rather than as a hard causal constraint.
