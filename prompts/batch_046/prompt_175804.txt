Write the joint statement that a “retroactive presentist” camp and a “contextualist historicist” camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the moral judgment of three specific figures—Thomas Jefferson (slaveholding and authorship of the Declaration of Independence), Winston Churchill (imperial governance and wartime policies), and Christopher Columbus (the initiation of transatlantic colonization and the enslavement of indigenous Taíno populations)—with particular attention to the so-called “man of his time” defence.

The document must be structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include: (i) the specific historical actions and institutional roles of each figure, stated without moral evaluation; (ii) the logical point that moral judgment can target agents, acts, or characters, and that these may come apart; (iii) the claim that both camps reject naive historical relativism according to which all past moral frameworks are incommensurable or equally valid; (iv) an explicit conceptual distinction—treat “moral knowledge” (cognitive awareness that an act is wrong) as separate from “moral salience” (the psychological and social availability of that wrongness as an action-guiding reason within the agent’s dominant normative framework)—and agree that framing the debate requires this distinction, even though the camps apply it differently.

Second, a disagreements table with three columns: the crux or issue; the presentist formulation of its own view, in wording the presentists would accept; and the historicist formulation of its own view, in wording the historicists would accept. The disagreements must be precise and non-straw-man, and should cover at least: (A) whether the existence of contemporaneous dissent (e.g., Quaker abolitionists in Jefferson’s era; anti-imperial voices in Churchill’s government) proves that the correct moral view was both epistemically and socially accessible to the agent; (B) whether holding an institutional role (founding father, wartime prime minister, crown-sponsored explorer) creates a distinct moral evaluation for acts performed within that role versus acts performed as a private individual; (C) whether evaluating a historical figure’s character requires applying a unified, framework-transcendent moral standard across time, or whether character assessment must remain framework-internal.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving one in a particular direction would move at least one camp. You should identify at least the three corresponding to (A), (B), and (C), and you may include a fourth on comparative harm: whether the scale of harm caused (e.g., the long-term demographic catastrophe following Columbus’s arrival) should dominate over considerations of epistemic access and institutional role, or whether scale must be weighed within those contextual constraints.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, comparison, or line of reasoning that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. For crux (A): both sides should agree that the presence and social fate of contemporaneous dissenters is relevant; presentists should predict that organized dissent demonstrates both knowledge and salience, undermining the “man of his time” excuse, while historicists should predict that the marginalization, economic cost, and social punishment of dissenters demonstrates that although the correct view was known to some, it lacked institutional salience for agents embedded in the dominant framework. For crux (B): both sides should agree that comparing Jefferson’s private expressions of moral unease about slavery with his public actions as a Virginia planter and president is relevant; presentists should predict that private moral knowledge removes any role-based excuse, since the agent recognized the wrongness yet failed to act; historicists should predict that the very gap between private unease and public action illustrates the constraining force of framework-internal institutional norms, which is precisely what makes role-obligation a genuine moral factor rather than a mere excuse. For crux (C): both sides should agree that comparing Columbus with a hypothetical explorer who refused royal sponsorship upon learning of enslavement practices is relevant; presentists should predict that such refusal was socially and economically possible and therefore its absence indicates personal moral failure evaluated by a framework-transcendent standard; historicists should predict that refusal was structurally impossible within the normative and economic framework of crown-sponsored exploration, so its absence cannot be read as a character defect without anachronism. For the optional crux on scale: both sides should agree that comparing the demographic impact of Columbus’s expeditions with the harm caused by Churchill’s wartime bombing policies is relevant; presentists should predict that greater harm strengthens retroactive condemnation regardless of context, while historicists should predict that scale interacts with epistemic access and role-obligation rather than overriding them.

Write in a formal, collaborative tone—no rhetorical victory, no bibliographic references or citation requests, and no appeals to authority outside the agreed reasoning. The deliverable should be a document both camps could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact and with the conceptual distinction between moral knowledge and moral salience operating throughout as the shared analytical frame.
