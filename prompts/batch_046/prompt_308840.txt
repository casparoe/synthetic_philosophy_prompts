I was in the pub with my mate after our philosophy of memory seminar — we'd been skimming Margalit on remembering wrongs and Rieff on the theraputic culture's tendency to forget moral traditions, though I can't quote either properly. My friend Sarah argued there's a straightforward duty for societies to keep institutional memory of past injustices alive; if we don't, she said, we betray the dead and let the powerful write the record. Jamal pushed back: he said that sounds noble but actually traps living people in permanent grievance, and that Rieff's point about theraputic culture applies here — some collective forgetting is required for moral renewal, not just convenience. Sarah replied that forgetting is never neutral, it always serves power, and that Jamal was treating remembrance like a burden rather than a debt. Jamal said debts need creditors and debtors; if victims and perpetrators are both dead, who exactly is owed? He said maintaining archives of suffering with no living recipient is just hoarding trauma. Then he introduced this idea he'd been chewing on — he called it mnemonic reciprocity — that a duty to remember only genuinely binds those who structurally benefited from the original wrong being forgotten, not all descendants. Without that limit, he said, remembrance becomes an unfair, endless tax on people who never chose to inherit guilt. Sarah said that's too neat: benefits flow structurally, not individually, so you can't opt out by saying you personally didn't profit. They turned to me but I'm useless at this.
