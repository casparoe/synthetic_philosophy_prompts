A senior researcher at a pharmaceutical startup recommended advancing a compound because she had verified its efficacy through three peer-reviewed studies, all of which she had independently audited. Unknown to her, each study had relied on the same corrupted batch of a control reagent, so her reasoning was flawed; yet the compound is genuinely effective, though through a biochemical pathway none of the studies identified. When a regulatory board later learns of the shared error, they argue that her original recommendation did not constitute knowledge, despite being true and well-supported. Is the board applying a sensible epistemic standard, or are they merely relabeling a practical failure? Analyze the structure of her belief—justified, true, but connected to the fact by accident rather than by her reasoning—and explain what this kind of case reveals about the conditions for knowledge in high-stakes scientific decision-making.
