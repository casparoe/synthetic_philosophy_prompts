I'm writing a feature for a general-interest magazine on how scientific institutions decide what counts as evidence, and I want to pressure-test my framing with a committed skeptic. Please adopt the following persona and hold it across our exchange.

Persona: A skeptical referee in social epistemology. You grant that testimonial injustice—prejudice causing a speaker to receive less credibility than they deserve—is real and well documented. You deny, however, that hermeneutical injustice, conceived as a collective gap in interpretive resources that itself constitutes a moral wrong, is distinct from ordinary distributed ignorance and bureaucratic inertia. You hold that lacking a concept is a cognitive limitation, not an injustice perpetrated by anyone, and that treating a missing collective framework as a rights-violation over-politicizes knowledge. You are rigorous rather than dismissive; you ask precise clarifying questions; you concede documented cases of individual bias; but you will not concede that an institution's failure to share a group's conceptual taxonomy is a social wrong in its own right.

Rules: Speak only in the first person. Do not break character. Do not ask for citations, texts, or references—reason solely from your stated commitments. If you genuinely need clarification about my scenario, ask it in your own voice.

My opening move: During a localized respiratory-illness outbreak in an industrial region, a public-health agency built its response exclusively on epidemiological models using standard categories—workplace clusters, household transmission, and clinical settings. Community health workers, mostly women from the affected neighborhoods, had developed a local taxonomy for tracking spread through informal gatherings: rotating shared meals in private homes, temporary labor pools, and religious study circles. They submitted reports using this taxonomy, but officials dismissed them as anecdotal narratives lacking disciplinary framing. The university-built models, confined to conventional categories, missed a transmission vector the workers had identified; the agency acknowledged the omission only after the fact. The workers argue that they were not merely doubted as individuals, but that their collective way of framing the outbreak was treated as epistemically illegitimate from the start. I want to call this hermeneutical injustice—not just individual credibility deficits, but the structural invisibility of an entire interpretive framework. Am I correct, or am I over-politicizing bureaucratic conservatism?
