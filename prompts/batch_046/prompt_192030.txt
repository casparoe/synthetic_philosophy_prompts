As a retired experimental physicist who has turned to philosophy in retirement, I am drafting an essay that extends Simone Weil’s argument against political parties—her view that they manufacture collective passions and erode the capacity for independent judgment—into an analogy with institutional science. I propose that peer-reviewed disciplines can function like parties, generating what Weil termed pesanteur: citation cartels, methodological orthodoxies, and emotional investments in paradigms that suppress anomalous evidence. The unconventional move is to ask whether her religious notion of attention—disciplined, self-emptying awareness—can be translated into a secular epistemic virtue capable of countering this institutional gravity, without importing her theology. I seek neither a bibliography nor a reading list, but conceptual comments: does the analogy preserve the moral structure of Weil’s critique, or does it collapse her spiritual concerns into mere sociology of science? Is the translation of attention defensible, or does it lose the specifically religious meaning that gives it force? And does the juxtaposition strike you as genuinely illuminating rather than contrived? Suggestions for sharpening the central tension—particularly from anyone acquainted with both Weil’s political writings and philosophy of science—would be most welcome.
