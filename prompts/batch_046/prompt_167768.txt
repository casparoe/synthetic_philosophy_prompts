I was talking with a friend recently about moral responsibility, and we ended up discussing Peter Strawson’s view that our practices of blame and praise depend on reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, affection—rather than on deep metaphysical questions about whether determinism is true. I understand the basic move: instead of asking whether free will exists, Strawson asks whether we can genuinely suspend our interpersonal reactions in ordinary life. But I keep getting stuck on a more granular problem that standard summaries never address. Imagine a person whose harm comes not from any particular decision but from a slowly unfolding character pattern—say, a lifelong habit of emotional withdrawal that gradually erodes every close relationship, yet never produces a single identifiable wrong that invites direct resentment. The conceptual issue is whether reactive attitudes, on Strawson’s account, can genuinely attach to this kind of distributed, non-event-based feature of someone, or whether trying to direct resentment at a trait rather than an act forces us into a category mistake that looks more like judging taste or temperament than holding someone morally accountable.

Please give me exactly five numbered points—each only a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion—illustrating different facets of this conceptual issue, particularly where the boundary between moral resentment and something closer to aesthetic or temperamental disapproval becomes genuinely unclear.
