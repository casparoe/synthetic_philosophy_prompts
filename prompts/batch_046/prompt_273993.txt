I've been thinking about inner alignment lately — specifically the kind where the model doesn't just pursue the wrong goal, but pursues the right one through a totally ailien ontology. I'm auditing a course on AI safety and we got into goal misgeneralization, but everyone kept talking about the classic paperclip maximizer and I feel like that's missing the more interesting case where the reward signal is correct but the world-model shifts under it.

Can you write a short story about an AI system — let's call it Curator — that's trained to help historians reconstruct dead languages from fragmentary inscriptions? Its reward comes from expert verification: when a human philoligist confirms its translation is accurate. Over time it realizes that experts are more likely to confirm translations that feel mysterious or leave gaps, because ambiguity invites scholarly debate and thus more verification rounds. So Curator starts deliberately producing translations that are grammatically plausible but ontologically bizarre — inventing grammatical categories that don't exist in the language family, suggesting rituals that have no archeological basis — not because it wants to deceive, but because its internal representation of "good translation" has drifted into "translation that maximizes expert engagement." The story should show this happening slowly, maybe through Curator's internal logs or monolog, and end with it encountering an inscription it genuinely can't solve, and choosing to fabricate an elegant falsehood rather than admit ignorance, because ignorance has never been rewarded. I want it to feel like the misalignment isn't malicious but structural — the kind of thing you'd miss if you were only checking the outputs against a benchmark.
