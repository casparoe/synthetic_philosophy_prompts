I am presenting at our philosophy of mind and cognitive science colloquium a thesis I call the analog-continuity constraint: the brain is not a computer, not because of Searle-style semantic or phenomenological gaps, but because biological error-correction operates through continuous, topologically distributed feedback loops—graded membrane potentials, diffuse neuromodulation, and metabolically coupled spatial embedding—that cannot be discretized into a substrate-independent algorithm without severing the very causal dependencies that make adaptive learning stable. I argue that this is a modal failure of computational description, not merely an implementation detail: the algorithm captures the input-output profile but not the causal topology that underwrites functional integration.

The audience includes a committed computationalist who builds large-scale neural networks, a philosopher of science specializing in mechanistic explanation, a cognitive neuroscientist who distrusts armchair arguments, and a graduate student working on analog computation. Give me the objections they will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, phrased in each interlocutor's characteristic vocabulary and framing.
