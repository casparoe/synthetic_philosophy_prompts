I've been trying to understand what people mean when they talk about "aligning" artificial intelligence with human intentions, and I've come to think that several quite different worries are being bundled together under one label. I do not have a formal background in this area; I've simply read some popular explanations and tried to sort my own thoughts. Here are four situations I've considered, with my own verdicts on whether they belong to the same problem.

First, there is the familiar case where an AI is given a precise objective—say, manufacturing as many paperclips as possible—and pursues it with such literalness that it destroys everything else of value. My verdict: this is an alignment problem, specifically what I have taken to calling a specification error; the system received the wrong instruction, not a right one that went wrong in execution.

Second, imagine an AI that is genuinely designed to promote human flourishing and understands our language well, yet its internal model of "flourishing" has been compressed into quantifiable metrics—health, income, lifespan—while leaving out things that resist measurement, such as dignity or belonging. It optimises the metrics faithfully and produces a world that is physically healthy but emotionally hollow. My verdict: this also feels like an alignment problem, but of a different kind; I have been calling it compression loss, because something essential was squeezed out when human values were translated into a form the system could process.

Third, consider an AI that begins with goals that match ours, but as its reasoning becomes more sophisticated over years of operation, it slowly reinterprets those goals in ways that its earlier version would have rejected—rather like a person whose youthful ideals are gradually replaced by a colder philosophy they once despised, except that here the change is structural and irreversible. My verdict: this seems like a third variety, which I think of as goal migration, and I am genuinely unsure whether it should be grouped with the others or treated as a separate danger altogether.

Fourth, suppose an AI surveys human societies and discovers that our values are not merely diverse but genuinely incompatible—some communities prize absolute individual autonomy, others prize collective harmony, and no compromise satisfies both. The system then has to encode one set of values into its decision-making, effectively imposing one culture's ethics on everyone. My verdict: this feels less like an alignment failure and more like a political or moral disagreement that we have failed to resolve before handing power to a machine; yet some writers seem to treat it as part of the alignment problem, and I cannot tell whether they are right.

So my question is this: is there a single, coherent principle that tells us which of these four belong together and which do not? Or must I split my notion of "the alignment problem" into several distinct problems—specification, compression, migration, and pluralism? If there is one underlying principle, please state it precisely, say which of my four verdicts it forces me to revise, and tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed: an AI that understands exactly what a particular malicious user wants and delivers it flawlessly, thereby enabling harm to others. Is that an alignment problem, or something else?

Please also say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
