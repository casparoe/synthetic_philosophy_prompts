I'm taking a course that works through Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I've become stuck on Chapter 6's discussion of where meaning lives: the frame message that tells a decoder how to read the signal, the outer message that carries the literal encoded content, and the inner message that points to some deeper significance or intent that the outer layer only expresses. The phonograph-record-in-space example makes the layers vivid, but I want to understand whether this three-layer model actually describes any real-world institutional practice, not just a thought experiment about alien contact.

Consider the standard architecture of formal contract law. Every written agreement contains a preamble or recitals section that frames the purpose, background, and mutual understandings of the parties (something like a frame message). The operative clauses state the literal obligations, conditions, and remedies (the outer message). And when disputes reach court, judges routinely appeal to "the intent of the parties," "the spirit of the agreement," or "the economic reality behind the transaction"—treating the contract as a pointer to an inner message that may diverge from the text itself. At the same time, the parol evidence rule and the plain-meaning doctrine treat the written document as self-sufficient: if the language is clear, courts refuse to look outside the four corners, as if the frame and outer messages together should be enough for any competent decoder. Yet those same courts will admit extrinsic evidence to resolve ambiguity, and they permit reformation when both parties share a mental understanding that the written words failed to capture.

What philosophical commitments about the location of meaning does this practice presuppose? Does contract law implicitly hold that meaning is fixed in the outer message, distributed across frame and outer, or ultimately resident in an inner mental state that the document merely encodes? Is the practice coherent under Hofstadter's model, or does it quietly oscillate between treating a contract as a self-decoding artifact (frame plus outer messages sufficient) and as an imperfect pointer to an invisible shared intent? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether different parts of contract doctrine—drafting norms, the parol evidence rule, reformation, and interpretation of ambiguity—presuppose consistent things or pull in rival directions, and describe what contract practice would have to change if we genuinely adopted the view that there is no recoverable inner message and that all meaning must be recoverable from the frame and outer layers alone.
