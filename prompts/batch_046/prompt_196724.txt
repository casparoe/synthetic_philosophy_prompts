I write literary fiction, and in each manuscript I must draft three to five emotionally heightened scenes—deathbed confessions, betrayals exposed in public rooms, a child’s misreading of adult grief—where intensity risks becoming melodramatic or emotionally false. I want a reusable decision procedure, drawn from Bernard Williams’s claim that opera achieves emotional truth not despite but through its artificial conventions, that I can apply in about fifteen minutes per scene during revision. The procedure should determine whether a deliberately heightened, almost operatic treatment of a moment exposes a truth that realism would suppress, or whether it merely manipulates the reader; it must be usable without philosophical training, specify the order of checks, and mark exactly which steps are irreducible judgment calls. Fix the role of the novelist, the frequency of these decisions, and the constraint that an editor must be able to sign off on the reasoning without reading Williams. Apply the procedure to three concrete cases: a dying character delivering a final accusation in language that borders on aria; a protagonist overhearing betrayal through an overheated, metaphor-laden monologue at a party; and a child interpreting a parent’s silent weeping as anger rather than sorrow. Include one case where the procedure recommends cutting or flattening the scene despite my attachment to it. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle and where taste or editorial authority must take over.
