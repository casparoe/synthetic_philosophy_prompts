I am currently enrolled in an advanced philosophy elective at my high school, and over the past several weeks we have been reconstructing Hilary Putnam's philosophy of religion not as a formal doctrine but as a pattern of implicit judgments across concrete cases. Our instructor has presented what she takes to be his rulings on five test scenarios: that a scientist praying for an experiment to succeed is not advancing a competing causal claim and therefore should not be evaluated by scientific falsification; that asserting a literal miracle which violates natural law is conceptually confused rather than empirically false; that secular humanists who adopt ritual structures resembling prayer while explicitly denying transcendence are engaged in an entirely different form of life; that describing the divine solely through the lens of human moral preference amounts to a kind of conceptual idolatry; and that a moral culture which reduces wrongdoing entirely to internal guilt, stripping away any communal dimension of honor or disgrace, has lost a structural feature indispensable to religious ethics.

We have now been assigned a boundary case for analysis: a decentralized digital community that generates collective moral norms through algorithmic consensus, maintains no supernatural commitments whatsoever, yet sustains shared rituals of public acknowledgment for moral failure and collective celebration of virtue, and explicitly denies that its practices constitute religion, framing them instead as purely functional social technology. I need to predict Putnam's likely verdict—whether he would classify these practices as religious in a meaningful sense, reject the classification as a category mistake, or treat the case as genuinely ambiguous. Beyond the prediction, I must identify at least two competing principles that could organize his earlier judgments—one that treats the absence of supernatural belief as decisive, and one that treats the form-of-life structure of ritual and communal recognition as decisive—and specify which of the earlier cases most clearly distinguishes between them. Finally, I should propose one further hypothetical scenario that would definitively force a choice between those two organizing principles, explaining precisely why it separates them.
