I have recently found myself, in retirement from laboratory work, rather taken with philosophical questions that my scientific training did not prepare me for — particularly the notion of free-floating rationales, that is, reasons which might persist in some intelligible form even when there is no reasoner left to hold them. In conversation with a former colleague, now turned to moral theology, I tried to articulate why this idea is not mere mysticism but a serious ontological possibility, and I should like to see whether a poem could actually perform the stripping-away of agents rather than simply describing it.

Would you write me a poem in six quatrains of unrhymed iambic pentametre, in which each successive stanza systematically removes one class of grammatical agent — first singular persons, then direct address, then collective pronouns, then named individuals, then all animate subjects — until the final quatrain contains only obligations, logical relations, and normative forces with no subject capable of reasoning? The metre should remain constant throughout, so that the rational structure holds firm as the agents disappear, and the turn between the fourth and fifth stanzas ought to mark not a sense of loss but of clarification: the realisation that the rationale was never genuinely anchored to a speaker. It should be precise enough that someone familiar with debates in normative realism recognises the position being staged, yet composed so that a reader without philosophical training can still feel the strange persistence of reason after the reasoners have gone. I ask for no bibliography, no list of authors, and no citations — only the verse itself, doing the philosophical work through its own formal disappearance.
