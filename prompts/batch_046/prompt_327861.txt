I'm preparing discussion materials for an advanced undergraduate seminar on existential phenomenology, and I want to move past the familiar waiter-in-bad-faith illustration into a structurally richer case that remains conceptually self-contained without relying on external references. Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—call them Mara and Leo—debating whether a particular modern form of self-justification counts as Sartrean bad faith. The concrete situation is this: a former musician now works as an operations coordinator for a logistics platform that uses predictive algorithms to optimize worker schedules; she tells her friends that she did not really choose to abandon music because the algorithm's recommendation score made the logistics path statistically inevitable, and that accepting this outcome is simply being realistic about her capacities and the structure of the labor market.

Mara should defend Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom, arguing that treating an algorithmic recommendation as a deterministic cause is precisely the flight from freedom he describes—an attempt by consciousness to become being-in-itself by denying its own power of nihilation and treating itself as a fixed essence determined from outside. Leo should oppose her, not by flatly denying freedom, but by pressing a phenomenological critique drawn from situated embodiment and pre-reflective commitment: he should claim that Sartre's framework risks pathologizing a necessary form of self-opacity, that practical engagement with technological tools and social fields shapes choice in ways not fully transparent to reflective consciousness, and therefore that invoking structural constraint can be an acknowledgment of mediated freedom rather than self-deception. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical—Leo should respond directly to Mara's charge that he rationalizes away responsibility, and Mara should address Leo's point about embodied opacity rather than merely restating that existence precedes essence. Keep both voices equally competent, avoid straw-manning either position, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading; the dialogue should stand entirely on its own conceptual feet.
