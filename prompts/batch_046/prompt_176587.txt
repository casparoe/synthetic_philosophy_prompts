I teach an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar with ten graduate students, one 75-minute session. Design a structured role-play or simulation that makes the group feel the specific pressure of the phenomenal-judgment paradox on a strong epiphenomenalist view—specifically, the view that phenomenal qualities are causally inert not only with respect to behavior and other mental states, but even with respect to the very cognitive mechanisms that generate judgments about them, so that any first-person report is produced by a parallel, non-interacting tracking pathway that correlates with but never receives causal input from the experience itself. The participants should act out roles—such as an experiencer, a report-generator, and an external validator—under rules that force them to form judgments about internal qualitative states using only indirect, correlational cues, and then discover that they have been systematically tracking correlations without direct access. Include: explicit rules and role descriptions; a timeline that builds from simple judgments to the self-reflexive question about their own current judgments in the room; materials that require no technology; a debrief that maps each stage onto the structure of the paradox (the correlation without causation, the reliability problem, the first-person present-tense judgment); an honest statement of which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply; and a brief note on what will predictably fail the first time the activity is run and how to correct it. The idea should emerge from what the rules make happen, not from a lecture delivered afterward.
