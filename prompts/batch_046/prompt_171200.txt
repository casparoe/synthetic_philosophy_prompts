I've recently become preoccupied with whether high-level symbolic structures can genuinely direct low-level substrate dynamics, or whether they are only descriptive patterns that emerge after the substrate has done its work. The question came to a head in a discussion between two colleagues of mine, Maya and Leo, whom I had mentioned Hofstadter's thought experiment about the careenium — the cranium reconceived as a space in which symmballs, or symbolic entities, might either push the marbles of neural substrate around or be pushed by them. Maya maintained that symbols never possess causal efficacy over substrate: neurons fire, weights update, and symbols are merely the interpretive gloss we apply afterward, like weather patterns that do not steer the atmosphere. She extended this to artificial systems, arguing that a prompt is only a perturbation of parameters, and that calling certain resulting patterns rules or commitments is a post-hoc narrative imposed on pure mechanical dynamics. Leo replied that this account ignores feedback loops in which instantiated symbols constrain future substrate states; he offered the example of a person who keeps a promise, where the symbolic commitment does not merely describe a neural configuration but organizes subsequent activation patterns, just as a grammatical constraint shapes speech without being reducible to individual muscle contractions. Maya countered that Leo is only describing a stable substrate configuration maintained over time, and that labeling this stability symbolic causation is analogous to saying a riverbed causes water to flow in a particular shape, when in fact the water carved the bed. Leo then introduced what he termed symbolic inertia — the claim that some substrate configurations are sustainable only because they instantiate a symbol that actively resists dissolution, so that altering the substrate to eliminate the symbol requires more energy than preserving the configuration that embodies it. Maya dismissed this as homeostasis dressed in philosophical language. They asked me to adjudicate.
