I'm drafting a short comparative essay on civic opacity and want your comments on its framing and core arguments before I develop it further. The working title is "Asymmetric Opacity: Three Ontologies of the Citizen-State Information Boundary."

The core claim is that German data protection, American government-transparency culture, and Chinese social credit are usually treated as positions on a single privacy-transparency spectrum. I think this is misleading: each encodes a different ontology of the citizen-state relation, and treating them as scalar variations obscures what citizens are actually being asked to hide or reveal.

First, the German tradition—rooted in the constitutional right to informational self-determination—treats personal data as an extension of personality (Persönlichkeitsrecht). Here opacity is protective: the citizen hides from the state because structural power asymmetry makes exposure dangerous regardless of current governmental intentions.

Second, the American framework inverts the gaze. Sunshine laws and FOIA traditions treat the state as the primary object of scrutiny; citizen visibility is largely a private-market or security-tradeoff question rather than a constitutional shield against public intrusion. The implicit ontology is that of the citizen as watcher and contracting party, not as a bounded self whose data-integrity must be preserved against the state.

Third, Chinese social credit treats transparency not as exposure to power but as participation in collective moral infrastructure. Opacity is not a right but a coordination failure; visibility enables trust calibration among strangers.

My conceptual injection is to replace the "how much privacy?" question with a "correction-to-prediction ratio" criterion. The harm of state data collection, I suggest, depends less on data volume than on the asymmetry between what the state can predict and what it is institutionally constrained from correcting or punishing. Where predictive capacity is high but corrective power is legally bounded, rational opacity should be lower than where both capacities are high and coupled. So citizens should calibrate hiding asymmetrically—more intensely where prediction quickly feeds correction with few intermediaries.

I am particularly unsure whether this framing avoids collapsing into mere functionalism: am I just redescribing institutional differences in philosophical language? I also worry the correction-to-prediction metric might be unfalsifiable or merely restate "rule of law" in predictive terms.

Please comment specifically on: (1) the accuracy of the comparative framing and whether the three ontologies are genuinely distinct or overstated; (2) the coherence of the correction-to-prediction idea and whether it adds anything beyond existing institutional-analysis accounts; and (3) where the argument is weakest—especially whether the ontology distinction does real work that a standard spectrum account cannot. Please say how confident you are in your assessment overall and single out the specific point you are least sure about. Keep your response under 400 words.
