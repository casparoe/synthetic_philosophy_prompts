I have been working through Nozick’s entitlement theory of justice—specifically the Wilt Chamberlain argument against patterned principles—with a small group of friends who are mostly sports economists, and we got stuck on whether the argument survives inside institutions that deliberately suppress market outcomes. To push the point, I want to apply the entitlement framework to a concrete case rather than to a hypothetical. In the summer of 2016, the NBA’s salary cap jumped by roughly twenty-four million dollars because of a new national television deal; the Golden State Warriors—who already held valid, voluntary contracts with Stephen Curry, Klay Thompson, and Draymond Green—used the unexpected space to sign Kevin Durant to a maximum contract upon his free agency. Every step was legal and uncoerced: Durant was free to choose, the Warriors made an offer within the collectively bargained rules, and no party was deceived. Yet the result was a concentration of elite talent that many observers treated as a kind of distributive injustice against competitive balance.

Analyze this episode as a disciplined test of Nozick’s historical entitlement theory and the Chamberlain argument. Begin by identifying the baseline holdings and the rules governing transfers: the pre-existing player contracts, the revenue-sharing and cap mechanisms embedded in the collective-bargaining agreement, and the sudden external shock of the broadcast-rights windfall. Then ask whether the Chamberlain argument—that voluntary exchanges from a just baseline preserve justice—applies cleanly here, or whether the salary-cap system itself functions as an embedded patterned principle that constrains wages and mobility by design. If the baseline already contains a pattern (the cap, luxury taxes, draft rules), does Durant’s signing represent a legitimate exercise of entitlement within that pattern, or does it expose an internal contradiction? Next, examine the widespread normative reaction—the intuition that the Warriors’ roster was unfair—and determine whether it expresses a genuine entitlement-based complaint (for example, about the legitimacy of the baseline rules themselves) or merely a competitive preference that Nozick’s framework should dismiss. Finally, turn the case back on the theory: does the league’s subsequent adoption of stricter luxury-tax provisions and contract-extension rules designed to prevent future concentrations of talent show that real-world entitlement regimes always contain hidden pattern-correction mechanisms, or does it confirm that any central redesign of exchange rules after an unpopular but legal outcome is precisely the interference with voluntary holdings that entitlement theory rejects? Be precise about who holds standing to object, what counts as the relevant evidence, and where the framework remains silent—especially on whether a league has moral authority to alter the rules of exchange ex post in response to an outcome it dislikes.
