Consider the institutional design pattern now standard in several high-stakes autonomous-system deployments—automated medical triage, autonomous-vehicle test fleets, and lethal-targeting assistance—where regulations require a designated human veto-holder who must formally approve every system output before execution. In practice, the human receives the recommendation with a reaction window of a few hundred milliseconds, is given no interpretable explanation of the reasoning path, and works under organizational metrics that penalize overrides more severely than they penalize accepting erroneous system outputs. When harm occurs, liability frameworks treat the veto-holder as the responsible agent, while shielding designers and vendors under state-of-the-art product-liability defenses. What conception of moral responsibility does this architecture tacitly presuppose? Does it require that responsibility tracks formal institutional role rather than actual causal control, or that a last human touch is sufficient to absorb accountability regardless of epistemic access? Reconstruct the implicit theory—distinguishing whether it is a pure role-based account, a diluted control-based account, or something that quietly separates institutional accountability from moral responsibility. Then say whether the practice strictly depends on that view or is also compatible with rival theories, for example a knowledge-plus-control hybrid or a purely structural liability model. Finally, describe what the liability and oversight structure would have to become if we replaced the implicit view with a strict causal-epistemic condition on responsibility.
