You are a professional philosopher of moral psychology with expertise in virtue ethics and empirical personality psychology. Before constructing anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming the full logical space of responses to the situationist critique: consider descriptive claims about global trait stability, normative claims about whether virtue ideals depend on predictive success, empirical claims about replication failures in classic studies, and reconceptualizations of virtue as local trait, skill, interactionist pattern, or agent-environment attunement. Then produce a formal taxonomy—structured as a decision tree or flowchart—that sorts positions on the situationist critique of virtue ethics.

Build the tree from logical commitments, not from a list of named schools. Each internal node must be a concrete claim or case-judgment on which positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing philosophical labels. The root should split on whether stable, global moral character traits exist across situations. From there, branch on whether situational variance undermines the normative project of virtue ethics; then on how virtue should be reconceived if global traits fail (local/reliable trait, skill-like practical intelligence, interactionist pattern, or ecological attunement—where virtue is treated as an agent-environment sensitivity analogous to expert reading of situational affordances, not requiring prior familiarity with ecological psychology); and finally on whether recent replication difficulties with classic social-psychology experiments weaken the empirical premise of the critique.

You must place these leaves in the tree: (1) unreformed Aristotelian globalism; (2) local-trait virtue ethics; (3) skill-based virtue; (4) interactionist ecological virtue—explicitly framing virtue as attunement rather than internal disposition; (5) normative-only virtue, holding that situationism is descriptively accurate but normatively irrelevant; (6) eliminativist or deflationary virtue ethics; and (7) replication-skeptic globalism. After mapping them, identify any logically possible combinations that remain unoccupied and briefly diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Next, design a self-contained diagnostic quiz of exactly six questions. Each question should present a concrete scenario—such as a Milgram-style authority conflict, a bystander emergency with varying group size, a cross-cultural moral judgment made under fatigue and stress, an expert whose reliable performance collapses outside familiar contexts, a trait-prediction task after being told about replication failures, and a case where an agent succeeds morally only by continuously adjusting to shifting social cues. For each scenario, provide two or three answer options that correspond to branches in your tree. Then supply a scoring key that translates answer patterns into the seven leaf positions, with explicit rules for ambiguous or mixed patterns.

The audience is advanced philosophy undergraduates and graduate students. The instrument must work for someone who knows the cases but not the literature: never ask "which theorist do you agree with?" and never require recognition of proper names to score. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the description of ecological attunement precise but self-contained, so that the synthetic position is intelligible without outside knowledge. Present the taxonomy first, then the quiz, then the scoring key. Think step by step, and make sure every branch split is genuinely diagnostic.
