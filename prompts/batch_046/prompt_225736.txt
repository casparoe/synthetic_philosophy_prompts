I am preparing a consistency audit for an advanced philosophy discussion at my school, and I want to check whether my commitments on Nietzsche's genealogy of morality actually hang together before I present them. Here is my current position. First, I accept the genealogical narrative: the evaluation of good and bad originated in an aristocratic distinction between noble and base traits, and the slave revolt inverted these values, making weakness and suffering into virtues; I think ressentiment plays a role in this inversion, but I also think institutional mechanisms—priestly mediation, legal codification, and economic dependency—do more explanatory work than pure psychological resentment alone. Second, I hold that showing a belief has a contingent, non-truth-tracking origin is necessary but not sufficient for debunking it; so the genealogical story by itself does not automatically falsify or undermine slave morality. Third, despite that, I think Nietzsche clearly intends his genealogy to have critical, authority-undermining force—he is attacking slave morality's legitimacy, not merely describing its emergence. Fourth, I take the will to power to be the correct explanatory principle behind both moralities, but I read it as a descriptive psychological thesis about drives toward mastery and self-overcoming rather than as a metaphysical principle governing all reality. Fifth, I reject Nietzsche's normative evaluation: I see slave-moral values—equality, compassion, protection of the weak—as genuinely good, not as life-denying symptoms that ought to be overcome. Sixth, I think it is fully consistent to accept the descriptive genealogy while endorsing slave morality normatively, since questions of origin and questions of justification are independent. Seventh, I do not think my institutional emphasis in the first point weakens the genealogical narrative; it just redistributes the causal weight away from individual psychology.

Please audit these commitments. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each real tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision costs me. Then identify the single revision—dropping or modifying one commitment—that would clean up the most tension overall. I am especially unsure whether my separation of genealogy from debunking in the second and sixth commitments can survive my claim in the third that Nietzsche's account is critically undermining, and whether my descriptive reading of the will to power in the fourth commitment is compatible with my normative rejection in the fifth. I also want to know if my institutional emphasis conflicts with the standard narrative I endorsed in the first commitment, or whether that is only an apparent problem.
