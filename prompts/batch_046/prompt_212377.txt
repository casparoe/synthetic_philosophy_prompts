The essay takes as its point of departure the strategy-stealing assumption familiar from combinatorial game theory: in a symmetric two-player game of perfect information where a draw is impossible and the rules do not privilege either player, the existence of a winning strategy for the second player implies the existence of a winning strategy for the first, since the first player can make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter adopt whatever strategy the second player would have used, treating the initial move as disposable. I understand this argument to depend not merely on symmetry of rules but on the absence of resource depletion—on the idea that making one extra move cannot weaken one's position.

My proposed extension—tentative and possibly strained—is to treat certain cases of peer disagreement in epistemology as structurally analogous. Suppose two epistemic peers, A and B, share identical evidence and reasoning capacities regarding some proposition p, and suppose they reach contradictory beliefs. If the situation is genuinely symmetric, then whatever evidential strategy B employs to justify not-p, A could in principle appropriate and apply to justify p, with the addition of one further inferential step (say, an appeal to the very fact of B's disagreement as a second-order reason). The strategy-stealing logic would then suggest that symmetric rational disagreement is unstable: one party can always steal the other's reasoning and augment it, breaking the symmetry. I am particularly unsure whether this requires an illegitimate reification of moves in doxastic space, or whether it instead reveals that peer disagreement necessarily involves a hidden asymmetry—perhaps in confidence calibration or in the weighting of higher-order evidence.
