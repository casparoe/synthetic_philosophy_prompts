I am designing an advanced module on freedom for a graduate seminar in political philosophy, and I want to move beyond the familiar Berlin-Pettit taxonomy without importing obscure scholarship that requires specialist bibliographies. To that end, please rank the following five conceptions of liberty—(1) Isaiah Berlin’s negative liberty as non-interference, (2) Berlin’s positive liberty understood specifically as rational self-direction governed by a hierarchical model of the self, (3) Philip Pettit’s republican liberty as non-domination, (4) a “capability-sensitive” negative liberty that counts only interference with options that are genuinely available to an agent given their resources and social position, and (5) a “predictive-control” republican view that treats domination as present whenever another agent’s choices are systematically predictable and steerable in real time, even in the absence of explicit commands or threatened sanctions—according to how persuasively each diagnoses the specific unfreedom experienced by warehouse workers managed by an algorithmic system that never issues direct prohibitions but continuously adjusts task allocations, productivity targets, and wage premiums on the basis of live behavioral data, with no identifiable human authority to contest. For each position from first to fifth, provide a single sentence justifying the placement that explicitly references this algorithmic-management scenario rather than generic examples. Then, in a brief concluding paragraph, identify the single analytical dimension—such as transparency, reversibility, or structural asymmetry—on which your top-ranked and bottom-ranked accounts diverge most sharply, and explain why that divergence is philosophically substantial rather than merely terminological.
