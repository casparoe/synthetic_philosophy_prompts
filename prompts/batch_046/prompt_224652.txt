Consider the evaluation protocol adopted by research-ethics boards that oversee brain-emulation and virtual-agent experiments: any study that could generate conscious experience inside a computational substrate must clear an additional 'nested moral patient' review, on the premise that causing suffering within a simulated world is a categorically distinct wrong. At the same time, these boards routinely approve large-scale predictive simulations of human societies for policy testing without comparable review, and they invoke the simulation argument—the hypothesis that we ourselves may inhabit a simulated reality—to defend this asymmetry. Their reasoning runs that if we are simulated, our own suffering is not diminished by its nested status, yet this gives us no special reason to protect entities that might exist in a world simulating ours. What ontology of simulated worlds and what theory of moral status does this institutional pairing presuppose? Does it require that consciousness is substrate-independent while moral urgency is base-dependent? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework, determine whether the two practices are genuinely consistent with one another or depend on incompatible views about indexical uncertainty and patienthood, and describe what the protocol would have to become if it adopted a uniform stance—either extending full moral consideration to all simulated suffering regardless of nesting level, or denying that nesting creates any moral distinction whatsoever.
