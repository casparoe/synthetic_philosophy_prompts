A recent episode of sleep paralysis, in which I seemed to see a figure but could not tell afterward whether I had truly perceived it or only assembled the image upon waking, has made this question urgent for me. Two colleagues discussed whether dreams are experiences, framing their debate around Norman Malcolm's wakefulness criterion and Daniel Dennett's retrospective-access view, but introducing a distinction I have not encountered in the standard literature: whether retrospective access to dream content entails retrospective creation (a "loading" model) or merely retrospective recognition of an unremembered phenomenal stream that was ongoing during sleep. Their exchange, which I report without taking sides, ran as follows.

(1) The first argued that Malcolm is correct: experience requires being awake with open sensory channels to the world; during REM those channels are closed, so there is no experience, only a false memory fabricated at awakening.

(2) The second replied that this conflates veridical perception with phenomenology; Dennett's point is that authoritative first-person reports establish that there is something it is like, irrespective of external causation.

(3) The first countered that Dennett's cassette theory actually supports Malcolm: if the narrative is loaded into consciousness upon waking, then during sleep there is literally nothing it is like, and talk of "dream experience" is merely a convenient fiction.

(4) The second objected that this misreads Dennett, who allows content to be generated during REM and only stresses that our access is retrospective; retrospective recognition is not retrospective creation, and without that distinction Malcolm's skepticism collapses into an untenable behaviorism.

(5) The first then pressed that even if phenomenal content is generated during sleep, if it leaves no behavioral trace and is accessible only via a post-hoc narrative, attributing it is epistemically indistinguishable from attributing it to a phenomenally blank state; absent a marker available during the episode itself, we lack any non-circular reason to prefer the phenomenal interpretation.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and identify what both parties overlook regarding the relation between memory, phenomenology, and third-person verification. Present your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
