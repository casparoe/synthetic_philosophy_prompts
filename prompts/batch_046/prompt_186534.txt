I'm currently drafting a chapter for a course on moral philosophy that examines the boundary between doing harm and allowing harm, particularly in medical and institutional contexts, and I've been systematically noting my supervisor's informal verdicts on a range of cases in order to reconstruct the implicit framework she is applying. She is not committed to any named theory — she has expressed scepticism about both pure consequentialism and rigid deontological taxonomies — but her judgements have been strikingly stable. I would like to predict how she would rule on a new case involving mediated, algorithmic causation, identify the competing principles that could accommodate her existing track record, and determine what further scenario would adjudicate between them.

Her verdicts so far are as follows. First, she regards a physician who actively administers a lethal injection to a consenting, terminally ill patient as acting impermissibly. Second, she regards the same physician, in an otherwise identical situation, who withdraws life-sustaining treatment at the patient's explicit request and allows death to follow from the underlying pathology, as acting permissibly. Third, she holds that a military commander who orders an airstrike on a munitions factory, foreseeing but not intending the deaths of civilian workers present on the site, is acting permissibly. Fourth, she holds that the same commander, were she to order the direct execution of civilians specifically as a means of terrorising enemy forces into surrender, would be acting impermissibly. Fifth, she maintains that a hospital administrator who declines to approve funding for an expensive experimental rescue treatment for a single patient, redirecting those resources instead to a public-health programme that saves a greater number of lives, and thereby allowing that single patient to die, is acting permissibly.

The new case is one of what I shall call algorithmic triage delegation. A clinician designs, approves, and activates an automated resource-allocation protocol that requires no further human intervention. The protocol specifies that, should Patient A exhibit a particular rare biomarker profile, life-support resources will automatically be withdrawn from Patient A and redirected to Patients B through E, among whom the collective probability of survival is substantially higher. The clinician activates the system several hours before any test results are available; she does not know whether Patient A carries the profile. The profile is present, the withdrawal occurs automatically, and Patient A dies. The clinician never physically interacts with Patient A, and her direct actions are confined to the prior design and the single act of activation. She understands the protocol's general architecture but does not specifically intend this particular death at the moment of activation.

Please predict my supervisor's verdict on the clinician's action — permissible or impermissible — and set out the reasoning that best coheres with her prior judgements. Then identify at least two distinct principles or rules that could jointly accommodate her five earlier verdicts — for instance, a version of the doctrine of double effect, a direct-agency or physical-intervention principle, or a framework distinguishing institutional delegation from personal causation — and say what each predicts about the algorithmic case. Give your best-guess prediction with explicit reasons for preferring one rule over the others, but please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would most clearly distinguish between the two leading rules you have identified. I should be grateful if you could reply in British English.
