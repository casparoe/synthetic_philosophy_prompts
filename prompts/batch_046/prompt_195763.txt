I am delivering the opening remarks at a three-day workshop on intergenerational value and existential risk, held at a small research institute with an interdisciplinary audience. Roughly half the room are philosophers of value and decision theory who have worked through the literature on extinction ethics; the other half are engineers and scientists building long-term infrastructure who have not. I want the talk to do real philosophical work rather than serve as motivational decoration, and I want Scheffler's afterlife conjecture to shape its structure: the idea that human extinction is harmful to us now not mainly because of the deaths it involves, but because it severs the future in which our present projects are meant to live on, thereby undermining their point from the inside. This should constrain what I can say — I should not be able to fall back on saving future lives as the obvious justification, and I must explain to the technical audience why their work retains significance even in a scenario where every current person dies peacefully of old age and no premature death occurs. To keep the framing from sounding like a standard summary, inject a niche twist: present the conjecture not in the familiar vocabulary of personal meaning, but in terms of epistemic justification-transfer — the claim that our present reasoning practices are tacitly indexed to a continuing epistemic community, so that extinction does not merely remove future knowers but retroactively destabilizes the standing of our current beliefs and commitments. The speech should be polished, formal, about eight minutes, and must land for both halves without lecturing either. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or verbatim quotations from published texts; the argument should stand on its own for a listener without web access. Please write the remarks.
