Read Spike Jonze's film Being John Malkovich (1999) through Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is a strange loop — a pattern that can in principle be copied into another substrate, so that an I might persist across brains without being tied to one body. Treat the film as a philosophical experiment rather than an allegory: Craig Schwartz enters Malkovich's consciousness for fifteen minutes of passive observation, then gradually takes over his motor functions and lives inside him. Does Craig remain the same self when he is controlling Malkovich, or has he become a parasitic pattern? Pay particular attention to the shift from spectator to agent, and to the ending in which Craig is trapped inside a child's brain, able only to watch. Ask whether Hofstadter's distinction between a moved self and a copied self survives this ending — does Craig's suffering show that his loop is intact, or does his reduction to pure observation prove that without an autonomous feedback loop he is merely data? Conclude by saying what the film's literalization of distributed identity reveals about Hofstadter's account that abstract argument alone cannot show.
