I have recently assumed the role of trustee for a small regional museum that holds a restored Hellenistic bronze statue, and in that capacity I have been asked to assist in grading a final-year undergraduate metaphysics examination. The examination question and one student answer are set out below. The answer reads with confidence, but I should welcome an independent evaluation: please assign a numerical or letter grade appropriate to an upper-level essay (with a concise rationale), and then provide specific, point-by-point comments that distinguish correct claims from subtle errors—whether these involve a conflation of concepts, a misattributed view, or an inferential step that does not follow. I should be particularly interested in whether Leibniz's law has been applied correctly across temporal stages, and whether the distinction between material constitution and mereological essentialism has been preserved. Please write your assessment in British English, and do not include bibliographic suggestions, reading lists, or citations.

Examination question:
Consider a hollow bronze statue produced by the lost-wax method over a clay core. Over the centuries, conservation has replaced portions of the bronze surface with new alloy, and in the nineteenth century the original internal clay core was removed and replaced with an iron armature. Is the object now displayed the same statue as that cast in antiquity? In your answer, distinguish material constitution from mereological essentialism, apply Leibniz's law with precision, and discuss whether the replacement of the core bears on the identity conditions of the artwork.

Student answer:
The statue and the bronze are not identical, since they have different persistence conditions: if melted down, the bronze survives as a formless lump, whereas the statue is destroyed. This illustrates the material constitution view, according to which the statue is constituted by the bronze at any given time without being identical to it. By Leibniz's law, the statue possesses modal properties—such as being able to survive minor restoration but not total flattening—that the bronze lacks, so they are distinct objects.

The original statue contained an internal clay core that formed part of its material structure. Because that core has now been replaced by iron, the material substrate of the present object differs from that of the original. Applying Leibniz's law across the two temporal stages, the original was constituted by clay-and-bronze, whereas the present object is constituted by iron-and-bronze; since they do not share all properties, the present display piece cannot be identical to the ancient statue.

Moreover, the dominant sortal 'statue' determines that artistic form is essential to the object, but form is imposed upon matter, so any alteration in the underlying material destroys the original substance. This is essentially the Aristotelian position that matter is accidental to form. Consequently, although the museum exhibits an object that resembles the original in shape and appearance, it is in fact a different statue, now constituted by new materials, even though it retains the inherited artistic form.
