Student answer:
Bertrand's paradox, first introduced by Joseph Bertrand in his 1889 treatise Calcul des probabilités, asks for the probability that a chord drawn at random inside a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Because the chord can be specified in different ways, applying the principle of indifference—which assigns equal probability to outcomes that are indistinguishable in terms of available information—produces three different answers.

If two endpoints are chosen uniformly on the circumference, the chord exceeds the triangle side when the second point lies within the 120° arc opposite the first point, giving a probability of 1/3. If a radius is chosen at random and a point is then selected uniformly along that radius, with the chord drawn perpendicular to the radius through that point, the chord is longer whenever the point lies within the central half of the radius, so the probability is 1/2. If the chord's midpoint is chosen uniformly within the circle, it must lie inside the concentric circle of radius one-half, which has one-quarter of the total area, yielding 1/4.

These results appear to undermine the principle of indifference in continuous spaces, since "equally possible" seems to depend on an arbitrary choice of parametrisation. Edwin Jaynes resolved the difficulty in 1973 by demonstrating that only the 1/2 answer respects both rotational and scale invariance, and he concluded from this that the principle should always be applied using the maximally invariant parametrisation available in any continuous problem. This means the 1/3 and 1/4 results can be rejected as artefacts of coordinate choices that break the problem's natural symmetries. A closely related difficulty is found in the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox, which is essentially the same issue expressed through conditioning on a null event; taken together, these paradoxes show that geometric probability either requires a physical account of the selection mechanism or should be restricted to discrete domains where parametrisation is unique. Hence Jaynes' argument successfully saves the principle of indifference, provided one always identifies the correct invariant measure before applying it.
