As a novelist constructing a philosophical fiction about artificial consciousness, I am seeking a rigorous conceptual framework for a manuscript chapter in which an artificial narrator attributes beliefs and desires both to its human programmer and to fictional characters of its own invention. I therefore request a concise research proposal—formatted as a one-page fellowship pitch—addressing the following precise question: when Dennett's distinction between original and derived intentionality is applied to nested narrative agents, specifically through the lens of the two-bitser argument, does the layering of attribution (author to machine to fictional protagonist) generate a genuinely novel ontological category of intentionality, or does it merely iterate derivation without producing anything that Searle's biological naturalism cannot absorb?

The proposal must include: (1) a sharply defined statement of why existing treatments of original versus derived intentionality leave this nesting problem unsettled, particularly regarding whether derivation can be transitive across ontological boundaries; (2) an explanation of why a philosophical rather than literary-critical or empirical approach is necessary to make progress, including a specific methodological commitment—whether to conceptual analysis of the intentional stance across nested frames, or to a reconstructed thought experiment testing whether the artificial narrator's self-reference collapses the distinction; (3) a realistic deliverable for a six-month project, describing what a successful result would look like (for instance, a demonstrated asymmetry between recursive derivation and lateral attribution) and what would count as failure; (4) a defensible scope with clear milestones appropriate to the time available; and (5) an explicit injection of one new conceptual distinction—such as that between recursive derivation, in which an agent derives intentionality from a source that itself lacks original intentionality, and lateral attribution, in which an agent assigns mental states to a peer within the same ontological layer—without requesting a bibliography, literature review, or citation list. The tone should be formal and polished, suitable for an academic grant panel, and the framing should assume that the reader possesses general familiarity with Dennett's functionalism and Searle's critique, but does not have access to external sources.
