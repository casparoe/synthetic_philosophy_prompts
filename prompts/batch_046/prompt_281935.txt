I am a junior serving as the student delegate on the Westbridge Secondary School Curriculum Innovation and Ethics Panel, a body that advises the principal and board on pilot coursework involving emerging conceptual frameworks. During our winter session, Dr. Elias Vore proposed a four-week philosophy module built around what he terms the careenium—a speculative cognitive architecture in which mental processes are modeled not as computation over inert representations but as the dynamic interaction of simmballs, symbolic tokens that are claimed to exert direct causal pressure on their physical substrate, literally pushing neural or mechanical configurations into new states rather than being passively implemented by them. The module asks students to assess whether this substrate-pushing thesis is conceptually coherent, using analogies drawn from robotics and dynamical systems rather than canonical philosophy-of-mind texts, and it includes a design exercise in which students build a simple model to test whether symbol-arrangement can alter hardware state independently of standard causal chains.

The panel is divided. Several faculty members argue that introducing an unvetted, niche vocabulary into an introductory course risks presenting metaphor as mechanism—particularly since the careenium framework has not been widely peer-reviewed in a form accessible to secondary students—and they worry that students will mistake a provocative conceptual experiment for an established scientific theory. Others, including two student representatives, contend that precisely because the framework is non-obvious and resists easy assimilation into standard computational or biological narratives, it offers the kind of rigorous conceptual challenge that justifies philosophy’s place in the curriculum; they note that students need not master an extensive bibliography but only evaluate the internal coherence of the substrate-pushing claim using reasoning tools they already possess. Our chair has asked me to draft the formal recommendation memo that we will circulate before Thursday’s meeting.

Please write the memo I will submit. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the pilot with specific conditions, approve it in modified form, or reject it—with a clear rationale that the researchers and faculty could actually respond to. The memo must include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, rather than merely restating an administrative definition of appropriate coursework. It should propose a specific timeline or safeguard if approval is recommended, and it should avoid requiring students to consult bibliographies, citation lists, or external reading materials they do not have access to. The reasoning should stay focused on whether the careenium’s claim that simmballs push the substrate around is philosophically meaningful and pedagogically appropriate at our level, not on whether the framework is already widely recognized.
