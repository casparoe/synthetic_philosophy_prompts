I hold that a nation can be the proper bearer of intergenerational moral guilt—not merely political liability or reparative obligation, but guilt in the substantive sense Jaspers analyzed—when institutional and cultural continuity creates a collective moral subject that persists across generational turnover. On this view, guilt is transmitted not by blood or territory but by an unbroken chain of collective acknowledgment, institutional practice, and shared normative identity; where that chain is broken or explicitly repudiated, guilt dissolves, but where it is maintained through ritual remembrance, legal continuity, and public self-attribution, the nation remains one moral agent over time, capable of being guilty for acts its current members did not personally commit. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical cases of national dissolution, institutional rupture, or deliberate moral discontinuity—say, a successor state that formally severs its legal and symbolic identity from its predecessor—would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Which results in social ontology or collective-action theory—about the necessary conditions for group agency, about whether large-scale collectives can possess the kind of unified intentionality guilt requires, or about whether intergenerational identity survives the complete replacement of all members—would undermine the continuity claim? Would a rigorous application of the non-identity problem to collective agents show that the nation that committed the wrong no longer exists in any morally relevant sense, and is that move available to defenders or does it destroy too much? I also want to know whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if defenders treat any public acknowledgment as proof of continuity and any refusal to acknowledge as proof of repressed guilt—and whether that insulation is a theoretical vice or a feature of moral concepts that are inherently self-implicating. Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely uncomfortable, say which ones defenders of this intergenerational-guilt view would themselves concede as damaging, and conclude whether the position is genuinely exposed or has been protected into emptiness.
