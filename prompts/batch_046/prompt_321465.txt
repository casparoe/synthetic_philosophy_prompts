I'm a doctoral candidate in political philosophy working on the ethics of institutional resistance, and I'm trying to reconstruct the implicit framework behind a senior colleague's published case judgments. Over the past several years, Dr. Elena Vasquez has ruled on five scenarios in her lectures and essays without ever stating a formal principle.

Her verdicts are as follows. (i) A citizen publicly withholds a specific tax that funds an unjust military campaign, explains her reasoning in an open letter, and pays the assessed penalty without evasion: she judges this legitimate civil disobedience. (ii) A clandestine cell destroys draft-registration infrastructure, issues no public justification, and its members flee jurisdiction to avoid prosecution: illegitimate—mere lawbreaking rather than civil disobedience. (iii) An elected representative uses parliamentary privilege to disclose classified evidence of systematic governmental deception about a war's necessity, then accepts prosecution for violating secrecy statutes: legitimate, despite operating within rather than outside formal institutions. (iv) A collective organizes a non-violent, sustained blockade of a government office that administers a citizenship-exclusion law; participants accept arrest and do not resist: legitimate. (v) An anonymous network disrupts the digital operations of a corporation lobbying for the exclusionary law, publishes no manifesto, and claims no collective responsibility: illegitimate.

This track record is jointly consistent with at least three interpretive rules. Rule A holds that the act must function as a public communicative appeal to the community's shared sense of justice. Rule B holds that the decisive factor is fidelity to the rule of law demonstrated through willingness to accept legal punishment, with collective publicity being secondary. Rule C holds that legitimacy requires either institutional proximity combined with public accountability, or extra-institutional action that exhibits both transparency and punishment-acceptance—whereas atomized private refusal falls outside both categories.

The new case is a form of distributed conscientious refusal: a large, decentralized network of citizens independently declines to comply with an administrative registration required for conscription to the unjust campaign. The refusal is coordinated only tacitly, through shared knowledge of the law's injustice, with no central organization, no collective manifesto, and no public demonstration. Each individual, if investigated, offers a personal moral explanation and accepts the legal penalty without resistance. The action is non-violent, targets the unjust law directly, and involves no deception beyond the absence of collective publicity.

Predict Vasquez's verdict on this case. For each of Rules A, B, and C, state precisely what it predicts. Then give your best overall guess at her actual verdict, with explicit reasoning for why you prefer that interpretation over the others, but also state how confident you are in this prediction overall. In particular, identify the specific point on which you are least certain—whether it concerns the weight she assigns to collective versus individual publicity, the boundary between institutional and extra-institutional resistance, or the moral status of atomized versus organized disobedience. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would most cleanly distinguish Rule A from Rule B.

Please answer with appropriate philosophical precision. Include a brief note indicating your confidence level in the overall interpretation, together with the particular element you regard as most tentative.
