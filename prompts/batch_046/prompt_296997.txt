For my seminar on justice, labor, and temporal autonomy, I want to fix the specification for what counts as an adequate normative theory of the just distribution of work and leisure before I assess any particular account. Write the full desiderata list: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, the empirical results it must remain consistent with, and the pathological extremes it must rule out, with the motivating consideration behind each. The theory must accommodate the Protestant framing of labor as a moral vocation rather than a mere burden; the institutional claim embedded in the thirty-five-hour-week framework that leisure is an entitlement rather than a leftover; and karoshi, or death by systemic overwork, as a structural injustice rather than an individual choice failure. It must also address an emerging structural form I will call algorithmic shift-work, in which scheduling algorithms distribute rest in discontinuous micro-blocks that prevent either deep recovery or sustained self-cultivation, testing whether leisure can be quantified purely in hours. Identify which requirements are non-negotiable—for instance, distinguishing coerced from voluntary overtime, and leisure-for-recuperation from leisure-for-flourishing—and which are camp-specific commitments, such as the view that work is intrinsically dignifying versus purely instrumental. Flag any subset of desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable, especially if honoring both vocational meaning and strict leisure entitlement depends on contradictory anthropological premises, and name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding rival theories. Finally, score briefly how a Weberian vocational model, a social-democratic leisure-rights framework, and a human-capital productivity approach fare against the list, but keep the analytical weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need this as a working conceptual tool.
