I am writing as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or historiography, and I need help composing the opening remarks for a ten-minute framing talk I have agreed to deliver at a small interdisciplinary workshop next month. The workshop is titled "Contingency and Design: How We Explain Technical Change," and the audience will be split between practicing engineers, historians of technology, and several family members of the organizers who stayed past the reception and are now sitting in the front row. I want the remarks to be built structurally around hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation—not as decorative references, but as a constraint on what I am permitted to say. Specifically, I want to argue that in software engineering we constantly tell origin stories—"the architecture failed, then we pivoted, then the breakthrough arrived"—that retrofit clean causal arcs onto careers and technologies that were actually shaped by contingency, dead repositories, and engineers who quietly left. Because the archival record in computing systematically deletes abandoned branches, every biography of a pioneer reads like destiny rather than like a branching tree with most limbs pruned by chance. That observation should do real work: I should not be able to celebrate a neat "lessons learned" arc about my own field without undermining the warning I have just issued, yet I still need to honor the occasion and welcome the room warmly. I was discussing this with a colleague over dinner and realized the same distortion appears in how we write about computing history, where logs disappear and every early mistake is reframed as a necessary step; I want that parallel between technical archives and historical narrative to be the spine of the talk, shaped for an audience that includes specialists who will catch the conceptual move and non-specialists who need to feel its human stakes. Please write the remarks: they should sound like they come from someone who thinks in systems and version control, not in footnotes, and they must stand without any citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
