Please build a tree with 6-8 nodes. Then branch with four more cases: a religious fasting practice with no remaining theological defense but strong community bonds; a peer-review custom that slows innovative papers but also stops fraud; a legacy tax loophole that accidentally funds public libraries; and an AI governance rule copied straight from pre-digital paper bureaucracy.

At each node, ask what the reader would actually do -- restore it, study it first, modify it, or abolish it -- never ask which label they prefer.

The logical splits should cover three things: (1) whether not knowing the purpose is a reason to preserve or to investigate and remove; (2) whether long survival counts as evidence of hidden fitness or just path dependence; (3) whether hidden functional redundancy (that systemic overlap idea) is real and decisive.

The leaves must include pure Chestertonian epistemic humility, Burkean evolutionary preservation, rationalist redesign (tear it down and build better), abolitionist opacity-rejection, and that middle "systemic overlap" view. For any logical combination that ends up with no defenders -- for instance, accepting Burkean selection but denying that redundancy ever happens -- note it as an empty branch and briefly say whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag if any two leaves can't actually be separated by these cases.

Build the tree from the answer patterns first, then map the philosopher names onto the leaves after. Audience is educated lay readers who don't study philosophy formally. No bibliographies, no citations, no reading lists -- I want this usable for people who can't search things up. And please keep it casual, I'm drafting this on my phone between interviews.
