I spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics and have recently turned to philosophy of mind. A comparison I keep encountering is that adopting Dennett's intentional stance is like working with a thermodynamic potential or a Fourier representation: you switch to a coarser-grained framework because it predicts behavior more efficiently, without adding new ontology to the microscopic substrate. The analogy is used to argue that beliefs and desires are real patterns in exactly the sense that temperature or center of mass are—predictively indispensable but reducible without residue—so that intentional realism is merely a stance like any other macroscopic framework. How far does this hold? Give me an explicit correspondence table—what maps to the microscopic Hamiltonian, the potential, the domain of validity, the inverse transformation back to fundamentals—then identify where the mapping breaks, especially whether the intentional stance carries a normative or rational structure that thermodynamic descriptions entirely lack. Does the reductionist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time?
