The philosophical debate over whether large language models and other artificial systems should be interpreted through Dennett's intentional stance—whether attributing beliefs, desires, or intentions to them is legitimate—has persisted for years without clear convergence. Proponents typically argue that predictive success and behavioral coherence justify belief-attribution regardless of substrate, while opponents insist that such attributions are empty unless the system participates in practices of normative accountability or possesses the right causal-historical embedding. My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about the mental lives of AI, but a conceptual impasse about what the intentional stance is for: one camp treats it as an instrumental predictive strategy applicable to any system with the appropriate input-output profile, the other treats it as a normative practice that presupposes membership in a community of agents capable of being held responsible. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical or formal disagreement—perhaps about the architectural conditions necessary for representational content, or about whether predictive utility is ever sufficient for mental attribution—doing the actual work? I want a diagnosis that engages the actual arguments on both sides: do they share a concept of belief but differ only on its extension; do they answer different questions—one about practical prediction, one about moral status—under the single heading of "intentional stance"; or is the disagreement verbal, turning on unstated commitments about explanation? If the impasse is conceptual or normative rather than empirical, what should count as progress: a principled distinction between predictive and accountability-based stance-adoption, a boundary condition drawn from comparative cognition, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because "belief" fragments into incommensurable notions? Please deliver the diagnosis itself, not a survey of Dennett's theory or of AI ethics literature, and be explicit about whether anything—an architectural discovery, a formal result about representation, or a clarified distinction—could actually resolve it.
