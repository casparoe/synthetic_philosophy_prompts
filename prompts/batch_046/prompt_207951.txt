Below are working notes for an essay I am drafting, tentatively titled The Reflexive Competence Collapse: Why Epistocracy Cannot Identify Its Own Experts Without Circularity. The core claim is that epistocratic proposals—those that weight votes by demonstrated political knowledge—face a reflexivity problem structurally different from the analogies their defenders usually invoke, such as medicine or engineering. In technical domains, competence can be calibrated against outcomes largely independent of the evaluator's normative commitments: a bridge either stands or falls. But in political judgment, the correct outcome is itself defined by contested normative and empirical frameworks. Consequently, any institution tasked with certifying political competence must already rely on a substantive conception of good governance or justice. This means the selector's criteria either presuppose the very political conclusions the system is meant to generate, rendering the selection circular, or retreat to apparently procedural criteria—such as predictive accuracy about policy outcomes—that still embed hidden normative weights. I then argue that this does not merely make epistocracy unfair; it makes it epistemically unstable, because there is no Archimedean point outside democratic contestation from which to rank citizens' political competence. A further implication is sketched: if reflexivity forces the selection mechanism back into democratic procedures for legitimating selectors, then epistocracy collapses either into democracy or into an unaccountable authoritarianism that denies its own circularity.
