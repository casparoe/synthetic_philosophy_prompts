I've been trying to read Wittgenstein's lecture notes on religious belief—my roommate printed out some pages from one of those posthumous collections, I'm not sure of the exct title—and I keep running into the same frustrating pattern in my intro philosophy class and in random online threads. Someone describes a religious practice like treating guilt as something that needs forgiveness rather than just repairing the damage done, or praying for a sick friend without expecting it to work like medicine. Then the conversation immediately splits: one side asks "where's the evidence for the belief behind that?" and when told it's not that kind of claim, they conclude it must be irrational. The other side defends it by calling it "beyond reason" or "just cultural tradition," which makes it sound like pure decoration or social habit. But Wittgenstein seems to be pointing at something that sits in the middle—a deep commitment that actually organizes how you live without being a guess about physical facts. I keep seeing people miss that middle ground entirely, like there's no mental box for it.

Is there an established name for this specific failure mode, where any form of belief that isn't empirically testable gets squeezed into either "failed science" or "mere tradition"? And if there isn't a standard term, how exactly should I carve it out so it doesn't collapse into fideism on one side and non-cognitivism on the other? I'm especially interested in where the edges are—does it apply only to religious cases, or do you see the same squeeze happen with other kinds of deep commitments that aren't hypotheses either?
