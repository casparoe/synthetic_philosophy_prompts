I have come to hold a strong adaptationist position that treats Gould and Lewontin’s spandrels critique as a historically important but now largely defanged caution about just-so stories, rather than as a living constraint on evolutionary explanation. Specifically, I think the canonical spandrel examples—the human chin, the panda’s pseudo-thumb, the architecture of the mammalian middle ear—have all collapsed under sustained functional and comparative analysis, and that what Gould and Lewontin characterized as non-adaptive byproducts of developmental architecture are better understood as features integrated into broader adaptive or robustness-maintaining systems. I further hold that developmental constraints and robustness mechanisms themselves are products of selection for developmental stability, which means that even apparently inevitable structural byproducts are typically embedded in functional networks; on this view, the appropriate null hypothesis for a morphological trait is not “spandrel until proven adaptive” but “adaptive or constrained-functional integration until shown otherwise.” I want to know what would genuinely count against this stance—not generic reminders that not everything is optimized, but which specific empirical discoveries, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would force me to concede that genuinely non-functional, developmentally inevitable spandrels are common, identifiable, and theoretically central rather than marginal curiosities.

Which premises in my argument—the resolution of the canonical examples, the adaptiveness of developmental robustness, or the collapse of the spandrel/adaptation distinction—are most vulnerable, and which defeaters would defenders of this view themselves concede as damaging rather than dismiss as misunderstandings?
