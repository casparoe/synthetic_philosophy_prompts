I'm preparing the final meeting of a graduate reading group on vagueness, and I'd like to close not with a summary but with a conceptual poem that does the philosophical work structurally. The idea is the long stalemate between epistemicism and supervaluationism: both accept that the sorites paradox reveals something deep about boundaries, but they diverge on whether there is an unknown sharp cutoff or rather a zone of admissible precisifications with no single correct line. I want you to write a poem of exactly one hundred numbered lines—free verse, no rhyme scheme required, but with an iron constraint on semantic drift. Lines 1 through 49 must read as unambiguously describing a head with plentiful hair; lines 51 through 100 must read as unambiguously describing a clearly bald head. Line 50 must occupy a genuinely unsettled structural position: not merely ambiguous in diction, but suspended between the two determinacy zones so that a reader cannot assign it to either half without forcing an interpretation. The numbering itself should do the epistemicist's work—implying that there is a hidden fact of the matter about exactly where the flip occurs—while the refusal to resolve line 50's membership should do the supervaluationist's work, leaving it in a penumbral region where neither "bald" nor "not bald" determinately applies. Don't lecture about the theories; render them through the progression of hair counts, mirror reflections, and the creeping intuition that a boundary is approaching. The poem should stand alone: a reader unfamiliar with Williamson or Fine should feel the phenomenology of the boundary's creep, while a specialist should recognize which formal feature of each view is being enacted by the line structure and the semantic suspension at the center. No citations, no bibliography, no references to specific papers—just the architecture of the debate made into verse.
