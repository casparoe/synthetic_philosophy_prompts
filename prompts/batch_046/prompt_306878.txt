Draft the adversarial-collaboration statement that a defender of a phenomenological reconstruction of Berkeley's master argument and a modal-epistemic critic of that argument could both endorse after sustained, good-faith dispute. The defender holds that the argument succeeds only if "conceive" is interpreted as phenomenally imagining an object existing with no possible perceptual relation whatsoever, and that this yields a genuine contradiction in the idea of mind-independent material substance. The critic concedes that our concepts of material objects are anchored in perceptual conditions, but holds that this reveals only an epistemic boundary of our representational apparatus, not a metaphysical constraint on being, and that purely structural-relational descriptions of unobservable entities show we can coherently represent existence without phenomenal presence.

Produce the document they should have co-authored. It must contain:

(i) A numbered section of agreed claims—about the logical form of the master argument (the inconceivability premise and the inference to contradiction or impossibility), about the distinction between epistemic inaccessibility and metaphysical impossibility, about their shared rejection of both naive substance dualism and unqualified materialism, and about what each camp takes to be necessary conditions for either a successful defense or a successful refutation;

(ii) A disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in wording that each side accepts for its own position. Include at least: whether phenomenal imagination is the correct modality for testing the inconceivability premise; whether a purely structural-relational description of an object can be formed without implicit perceptual anchoring; and whether the theoretical success of entities such as atoms or fields in physics functions as an undermining analog or as an irrelevant case because such entities remain ultimately defined by their perceptible effects;

(iii) The cruxes—the precise sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete thought experiment, analytical case, or line of reasoning that both sides agree bears directly on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the analysis of that case will show.

The document should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; should avoid framing either position as obviously correct; and should be written in a formal, polished register appropriate to a philosophical working paper.
