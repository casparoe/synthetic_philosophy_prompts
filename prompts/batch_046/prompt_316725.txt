My seminar professor in epistemology has delivered consistent rulings on belief retention in the face of higher-order defeat. She held that a scientist who retains confidence in her result, despite learning that an equally qualified peer reached the opposite conclusion, is rational provided she can identify a specific misstep in the peer's reasoning rather than merely invoking general fallibility. She held that a juror who continues to believe a defendant guilty, after recognizing she carries an implicit bias triggered by the defendant's speech patterns, is epistemically akratic and ought to suspend judgment, even if her first-order evidence remains unchanged. She held that a patient who rejects a psychiatrist's diagnosis of depression, while acknowledging that several symptoms fit, is not necessarily irrational if he can point to particular misinterpretations of his behavior rather than dismissing clinical authority wholesale. And she held that a mathematician who trusts a proof, despite knowing she has erred in similar proofs before, is rational only if she has independently verified each step without relying on the very cognitive capacity the higher-order evidence calls into question.

These rulings seem compatible with more than one principle—perhaps one about the accessibility of specific errors, another about the insulation of first-order reasoning from second-order doubts, and another about calibrated confidence reduction rather than suspension.

The matter has become personally urgent. I recently submitted a manuscript arguing for a particular analysis of epistemic akrasia, and a reviewer whose judgment I respect contends that my central example conflates first-order evidential uncertainty with second-order defeat. I have re-examined my argument carefully and still believe the interpretation is correct; yet I find I cannot dismiss the reviewer's objection with the confidence I feel about other sections of the paper, and I am acutely aware that my desire for the manuscript to succeed may be distorting my assessment of whether the conflation is real.

Predict my professor's verdict: am I currently in rational belief, in epistemic akrasia, or in justified suspension? Identify at least two rules that accommodate all four of her earlier rulings but diverge on my case, give your best guess at which she runs and why, and specify the further case that would settle the matter between those rules.
