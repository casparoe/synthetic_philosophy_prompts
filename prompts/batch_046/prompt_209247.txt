I have been observing a recurring defensive pattern in scientific controversies and I am unsure whether philosophy of science already possesses a precise term for its specific architecture. The pattern unfolds in two coordinated stages. First, a theory T predicts an observable effect E under conditions C; when E fails to appear under circumstances that initially seemed to satisfy C, defenders do not revise T itself but instead tighten C—adding hidden parameters, stricter calibration requirements, or unstated background conditions—so that the failed case is reclassified as lying outside the theory's proper domain. Then, if a subsequent study is designed to meet those tightened conditions and E still fails to appear, defenders broaden the exception: they invoke an interfering mechanism, an unmeasured confounding state, or a suppression effect that was not part of T's original formulation, thereby expanding the set of conditions under which T is excused from producing E. Each individual adjustment sounds locally reasonable, but together they operate like a pincer: the gate for entering the theory's predictive domain narrows, while the gate for exiting without falsification widens, and the theory's literal wording remains untouched throughout.

I can illustrate this with two concrete configurations. In one, a macroeconomic model predicts inflation dynamics under a specific monetary regime; when the dynamics do not materialize, proponents first restrict the regime's definition to exclude observed institutional features, and later, once those features are held constant, they appeal to an exogenous confidence shock that the model never mentioned to explain the absence of inflation. In another, a cognitive theory predicts a particular decision bias in subjects who are rested, uninformed about the study, and tested in the morning; when the bias is absent, the conditions are refined further, and when it is still absent under the refined conditions, an unmeasured affective state is postulated to have masked it. In both sequences, the theory is never directly contradicted; instead, its empirical footprint is squeezed from both sides.

I am familiar with the general idea of immunizing a theory against refutation, and with the notion that auxiliary assumptions can protect a core claim. But those concepts usually describe a single layer of defense—one added clause, one protective belt, one adjustment of boundary conditions. What I am describing involves a successive, bilateral constriction: first a restriction of applicability, then an expansion of exemption, each responding to a distinct failure, with no change to the theory's core statements. Does this two-move choreography have an established name in the literature on demarcation and falsification? How precisely should it be distinguished from an ordinary refinement of scope conditions on the one hand, and from a degenerating research program on the other? If there is no standard label, I would like a proposed term together with clear boundary conditions—specifically, the point at which a legitimate tightening of conditions becomes part of this failure mode, and the point at which invoking an interfering mechanism crosses from reasonable qualification into the second move of the pattern.
