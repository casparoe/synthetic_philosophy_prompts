(1) Abraham is right to suspend the ethical command not to kill Isaac because he stands in an absolute, singular relation to God.
(2) The ethical is only binding as the universal; once it's suspended for one singular case, it's authority is conditional on not being trumped by a higher telos.
(3) So any agent who can claim an absolute relation to some higher telos — divine command, utility function, whatever — can suspend universal norms in the exact same structure.
(4) That means not just religious fanatics but also, say, a longtermist AI or human who treats future civilisations as an absolute telos and therefore lies or coerces to protect it.
(5) But if Kierkegaard can't distinguish Abraham from a fanatic or an ends-justify-the-means calculator, then his framework collapses the distinction between faith and moral chaos.
(6) Therefore either Abraham wasn't actually justified, or we have to accept that any sincerely held absolute telos can override ethics, which is obviously insane.

I really want to hold onto (1) and (2) — they feel like accurate readings. Where's the hidden slide? Is it in (3), that I'm treating 'absolute relation' as portable? Or is the problem in (4), that I'm forcing a false analogy between God and a utility function? Or is there a scope error in (2)?
