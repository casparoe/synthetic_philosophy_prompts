I am constructing a historical novel about the disappearance of dueling in early-nineteenth-century society, and I have become aware of a formal constraint in my craft: the abandonment of the practice is always rendered as a private crisis of honor—an internal reckoning—rather than as a response to legal prohibition, economic calculation, or shifting social interest. This appears to be a wider convention in the genre. What philosophical theory of moral revolution does such a narrative architecture presuppose? Must it assume that normative change proceeds primarily through rational argument and individual moral insight, as against Bernard Williams's view that thick concepts dissolve through alterations in social practice and honor? Or can it be reconciled with Kwame Anthony Appiah's claim that honor codes themselves contain the resources for their own revision? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the convention strictly requires one account or is compatible with several, and specify what a novel that took a purely structural or interest-based view of the same moral revolution would have to change in its characterization and plot structure.
