Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps on Descartes and animal minds: the Orthodox Mechanists, who read Descartes as denying animals all consciousness, feeling, and cognition, reducing them to unconscious automata; and the Revisionist Sentience camp, who read him as denying animals only rational, language-bearing, immortal souls while allowing them sentience, passions, and a non-rational form of cognition, with mechanistic explanations functioning as physiological heuristics rather than ontological denials of experience.

Structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Include agreement on: the textual distinction between rational and sensitive souls; Descartes' explicit denial that animals possess language or reflective reason; the mechanistic structure of his physiological explanations; the historical influence of 17th-century clockwork and hydraulic automata on his analogies; and the relevance of modern predictive-processing and computational-cognitive frameworks for assessing whether mechanistic physiology is compatible with subjective experience in non-human animals.

Second, a disagreements table in neutral wording. For each side, state precisely: whether absence of language entails absence of all mental content or only of rational thought; whether references to animals as machines in his published and private writings are universal ontological claims or restricted methodological descriptions; and whether the possession of a sensitive soul, if granted, would be consistent with his broader substance dualism.

Third, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one camp. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) whether Cartesian mechanism necessarily excludes phenomenal consciousness, particularly in light of contemporary predictive-processing models that treat cognition as mechanistic computation without denying experience; (b) whether Descartes' own observations of complex animal behavior, such as maternal defense or injury response, are best explained as purely reflexive machinery or as expressions of sentient states; and (c) whether the criterion of language for possessing thought applies to all mental representation or only to reflective, propositional reasoning.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case or piece of historical evidence both sides agree is relevant — for instance, the behavioral repertoire of injured or nursing animals, or the implications of Descartes' hydraulic-automata analogies for his ontology of life — and record in advance each side's prediction about what that case demonstrates.

Fifth, conclude by assessing whether part of the dispute is verbal: for example, whether disagreement dissolves once "mind" is disambiguated into rational mind versus sensitive awareness, and if so, specify exactly which disagreements remain substantive and which were terminological.

The prompt should be written in the voice of someone steeped in rationalist forums — precise about crux identification, explicit about predictions, and allergic to citation games — and should avoid requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or verbatim quotations from specific editions.
