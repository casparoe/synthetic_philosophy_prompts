In philosophy discussions I keep encountering a particular move with Descartes's claim that thinking proves one's own existence. One person argues that because this proof is indubitable, any account of the self that permits systematic error about one's own mental states—say, a model where people are routinely wrong about what they believe—must be wrong, since Descartes showed we have direct, infallible access to at least our thinking. When someone replies that the proof only establishes existence, not transparency or infallibility, the first speaker does not concede the stronger point; they reply that the very fact of indubitable awareness shows the mind is not the kind of thing that can be opaque to itself. The stronger claim about mental transparency and the minimal claim about bare existence keep being treated as interchangeable. Is there a specific term for this pattern—using a proof of bare existence and indubitability to support a substantive thesis about mental transparency—separate from simple equivocation or from treating the cogito as a full theory of mind? If there is no established name, can you define its boundaries precisely, especially how it differs from merely overreading Descartes? Also, if my framing rests on a confused understanding of what the cogito actually proves, or if answering this well requires misrepresenting Descartes's argument, please say so plainly rather than accommodating my premise.
