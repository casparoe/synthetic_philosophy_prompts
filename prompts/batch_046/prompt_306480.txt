Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a philosopher of mind who holds that the concept of qualia is incoherent and should be eliminated from both scientific and everyday explanation, and Dr. Julian Reed, a cognitive scientist who believes qualitative experience can be rigorously studied without committing to mysterious, private properties. The conversation takes place during a closed peer-review session for an interdisciplinary grant proposal that claims to have identified neural signatures of "inverted chromatic experience" in a patient with acquired synesthesia: the patient reports that stimuli her visual cortex registers as crimson are experienced with the qualitative character she previously associated with emerald, and the proposal treats this as evidence for a dissociable "inner feel" that can be mapped independently of behavioral and functional reports. Okonkwo should argue that this framing smuggles in an incoherent posit—if all behavioral dispositions, functional roles, and verbal reports align with the green-registered state, then invoking an additional "crimson-feeling" that is hidden from every possible observation is conceptually empty, analogous to positing a hidden property that makes no difference. Reed should respond not by appealing to authority or listing literature, but by pressing whether Okonkwo's eliminativism can account for the patient's own first-person distinction between "seeing crimson" and "seeing green" without reducing that distinction to mere belief or report, and whether there is a non-mysterious way to treat qualitative character as a real but fully embodied feature of information-processing architectures. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other's specific objection rather than talk past them, and neither should be treated as a strawman. Keep the discussion concrete—grounded in the grant proposal's specific claims about this patient—rather than drifting into abstract generalities, and avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
