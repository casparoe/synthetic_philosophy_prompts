I've been working through Plato's dialogue the Meno on my own, without formal training, and I find the usual explanation—that knowledge is better than true belief because it is tied down like Daedalus's statues with cords—unsatisfying. I want to defend a different view: true belief is like receiving the correct answer from a black-box calculator whose inner workings you do not grasp, whereas knowledge requires possession of the repair manual for that calculator. The advantage is not stability over time but what I call error-correction authority: the knower can diagnose and fix failures in the belief-forming process, while the true-believer can only hope the next result happens to be correct again.

Next Thursday I present this to an interdisciplinary seminar made up mainly of cognitive scientists who study predictive processing and Bayesian brain models, plus a couple of philosophers of mind who think normative epistemology should be eliminated in favor of graded confidence and neural mechanisms. They are not classicists; they care about implementation and formal learning theory, not Platonic texts. They are likely to treat "knowledge" as a dispensable folk-psychological category.

Prepare my Q&A defense sheet. Order the objections I will actually face, in the sequence they are likely to arise, phrased in the vocabulary these people actually use—not generic philosophy objections. For each: the core worry, what I should concede upfront, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and the weakest point in that reply. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—perhaps that error-correction authority collapses into ordinary metacognition, or that it implicitly requires a conscious self that small predictive networks lack. Finally, say what kind of empirical finding or formal result would cause you to change your assessment of my position, and point out where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
