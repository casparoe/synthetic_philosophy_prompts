As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been observing a recurring argumentative pattern involving the phrase "Western values" and I am unsure whether it has an established name in philosophy or political theory. In public debate I often see a commentator enumerate specific commitments—constitutional government, individual rights, scientific rationalism, gender equality—and present them as the distinctive heritage of the West; when it is pointed out that these commitments appear in many non-Western societies, or that Western history is filled with their systematic violation, the commentator treats the violations as aberrations and the non-Western instances as derivative or exceptional, preserving the category's coherence. Then, in the same exchange, the commentator invokes "Western values" normatively to justify a particular Western policy, which requires treating the values as aspirational standards; yet when the West is shown to fall short, they retreat to the claim that the phrase describes an ideal rather than an actual condition, without acknowledging that the initial explanatory claim depended on treating it as descriptive. The result is a kind of tri-modal immunization: the category is maintained positively by selective achievement, negatively by excluding internal failures, and normatively by aspirational appeal, with the three modes sliding into one another as the argument requires. I do not need a bibliography or citation list, and I am not satisfied with a bare label; what I want is the conceptual carving—whether this exact configuration is recognized under an existing term, how it relates to ordinary essentialism, to the no-true-Scotsman fallacy, and to what is sometimes called civilizational rhetoric, and if there is no precise term, how one might coin and bound it, specifying whether all three modes are necessary or whether pairs suffice. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a letter; the point is to map the phenomenon's edges and its nearest neighbors, not to name and leave.
