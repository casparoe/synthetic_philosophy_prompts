For a philosophy seminar I am taking on rationalism and moral metaphysics, I have been trying to determine whether Leibniz offers a genuinely distinct alternative to both natural law theory and modern contractualism, or whether his position ultimately collapses into one of them. A summary I found in an online essay—written by someone clearly familiar with Leibniz’s major writings but addressing a general audience—lays out his moral philosophy like this. Leibniz holds that moral truths are contingent upon God’s free choice of which world to actualize; just as God selected this cosmos for its maximal perfection, he selected these particular moral rules because they happen to suit that world best. Human beings belong to a city of God governed like a perfect monarchy, but citizenship is essentially voluntary—rational souls consent to divine law in exchange for eternal happiness, so moral obligation reduces to a form of enlightened self-interest. When Leibniz claims this is the best of all possible worlds, he means that every instance of suffering is immediately compensated within the temporal order, leaving no genuine net loss; apparent evils are merely local imperfections serving global harmony. Consequently, his ethics is a kind of theological utilitarianism: right action maximizes aggregate rational happiness, and God commands it precisely because it produces the best overall outcome. Something about the contractualist framing and the claim that moral laws are contingent feels wrong to me—I think Leibniz treats moral truths as necessary and rejects simple aggregation—but I am not confident enough to identify exactly where the summary goes astray. Could you audit this characterization point by point? For each claim, tell me whether it is accurate, whether it is a defensible simplification, or whether it misrepresents Leibniz in a way a defender of his view would reject; and say whether the conclusion that his ethics is theological utilitarianism actually follows from the more accurate parts of the summary.
