I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to formalize my normative intuitions around a view I will call tranquilism: the claim that the only intrinsic goods are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving, while the presence of pleasure, achievement, knowledge, or social connection has value only instrumentally.

Below are my verdicts on six concrete scenarios. I want to know whether a single principle captures the pattern, or whether I am inconsistently importing values that tranquilism should reject. I also want an honest statement of which verdict must be dropped if coherence demands it, and a prediction for a seventh case I have not evaluated.

Case 1: A programmer spends a decade in sustained flow states, shipping complex systems and feeling deep satisfaction, yet always with a background craving for the next release, the next promotion, and greater recognition. Acute suffering is minimal. Verdict: this life is not fully good; the unceasing craving is a genuine defect that the achievements do not cancel.

Case 2: A person is in a stable, permanent state with zero conscious experience—no suffering, no craving, no pleasure, no relationships, no accomplishment. Verdict: this is an excellent condition for that person, likely the best possible state.

Case 3: A person builds something of lasting value but does so under chronic stress, persistent anxiety, and an unrelenting craving for greater impact. The suffering is real but not extreme. Verdict: this life is worse than Case 2, and the achievement does not justify the mental cost.

Case 4: A calibrated neural interface permanently suppresses all craving and all suffering, leaving the subject tranquil but unmotivated. The subject neglects health and relationships and dies young, never feeling distress about any of it. Verdict: I am genuinely conflicted. By pure tranquilist logic the mental state is perfect, yet the premature death and the absence of care for others feel like a real loss I want to count.

Case 5: The same mental profile—zero craving, zero suffering—is reached through long disciplined practice rather than technology, and the person lives quietly and long with minimal interaction. Verdict: this seems excellent, and unlike Case 4 I do not sense a loss, despite the identical mental content.

Case 6: A person lives inside an immersive simulation that provides intense pleasure, meaningful projects, and deep social bonds, but also constant status anxiety and craving for advancement within the simulation. They never discover it is simulated. Verdict: this is worse than Case 2, and the reason is the presence of craving and anxiety, not the unreality of the environment.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps unadorned tranquilism, perhaps a variant that adds conditions about authenticity, origin, or preservation of life—that delivers all six verdicts? If simplicity requires overturning one, which should go? Does the distinction between Case 4 and Case 5 survive analysis, or is it an inconsistent bias? Finally, apply the winning principle to this unconsidered case: a conscious mind experiences intense pleasure together with intense craving for more of that pleasure, with absolutely no suffering. Is that state good, bad, or neutral?
