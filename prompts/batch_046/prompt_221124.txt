You are a professional philosopher specializing in 19th-century philosophy of history, invited to deliver opening remarks—approximately seven to eight minutes—at the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Monumental Forgetting: History Between Preservation and Erasure." The audience is deliberately split: roughly half are senior archivists and institutional historians committed to total preservation; the other half are practitioners of "negative archiving," a niche practice in which organizations ethically destroy specific records to prevent the permanent fossilization of trauma or the exposure of vulnerable subjects. Your remarks must make Friedrich Nietzsche's tripartite distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history do real structural work—not decoration. Use each mode to illuminate the position of one faction in the room, showing why both preservation and deliberate erasure are expressions of a legitimate historical need and why neither need should dominate the other. Crucially, do not resolve the tension with a bland synthetic compromise; each side should feel genuinely addressed and genuinely unsettled. You must not quote Nietzsche verbatim; paraphrase the three modes entirely in your own language. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, citations, footnotes, or references to page numbers. The speech should be written in polished American English, formal but not overly academic, suitable for an educated mixed audience. Before writing the speech, provide a brief brainstorming outline that maps each of Nietzsche's three modes onto a specific stance present in the room, identifies the feature of the scenario that is doing the structural work, and explains what happens to the argument if the negative-archiving practice is removed or if the preservationist commitment is weakened. Then deliver the full text of the remarks, including an opening that establishes the occasion without relying on generic phrases like "Thank you for having me." Think carefully about tone: respectful, intellectually serious, and unwilling to comfort either camp fully.
