Following the recent death of my parent, who endured decades of suffering that was never publicly acknowledged or redressed, I have found myself returning to philosophical accounts of moral desert with a personal urgency I did not previously feel. I am writing the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar in Philosophy of Religion and Ethics, and I need an essay question that treats karma—interpreted not as devotional doctrine but as a rigorous, cross-life theory of proportional moral desert—as a live philosophical competitor to secular retributivism and consequentialist distributions of well-being.

Please construct one 90-minute essay question (expected response: approximately 900 words) that asks students to evaluate whether karma succeeds as a philosophical theory of moral desert. The question must not be answerable by summarizing religious doctrines. Instead, it should require students to confront three interlocking conceptual cruxes: (1) the proportionality circularity—whether defining an action's karmic weight by reference to the quality of its eventual fruit renders the account of desert circular, and whether any interpretation avoids this without collapsing karma into a form of cosmic consequentialism; (2) the epistemic opacity problem—whether the fact that agents cannot access their own past-life ledgers undermines karma's normative action-guiding force, or whether such opacity is actually a structural feature that prevents moral self-congratulation and keeps the theory focused on present intention rather than outcome-tracking; and (3) the relocation of moral luck—whether distributing outcomes across rebirth genuinely answers Bernard Williams's worry about arbitrariness in moral assessment, or merely displaces that arbitrariness onto the metaphysical conditions of one's initial birth and onto the individuation problem of identifying a single moral agent across lives given the classical denial of a permanent self.

Provide, in addition:
- A model answer of roughly 900 words.
- A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, with explicit point allocations. The scheme must distinguish an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—for instance, that an A answer on part (2) separates metaphysical description from normative justification and argues for one side, while a B answer merely describes both options; or that an A answer on part (3) recognizes the settled status of the no-self individuation problem in classical Buddhist philosophy but treats the resolution via mental-continuity theories as genuinely contested. Include a one-line note on the characteristic error students make for each component.
- Throughout both the model answer and the scheme, clearly demarcate which claims are settled among philosophers who work on the topic—such as the denial of a substantial self in classical Abhidharma and the resulting need for an account of personal continuity—from those that remain genuinely contested—such as whether karmic opacity strengthens or weakens the theory's moral authority, or whether cross-life proportionality requires a deontic rather than a calculative framework. Do not present any contested interpretive stance, for example that karma is best understood as a multi-life consequentialist calculus, as if it were the consensus view.
