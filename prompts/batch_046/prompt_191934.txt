As a retired physicist now working through philosophy of mind, I have formalized a boundary case I call a careenium: a closed system in which symbolic tokens—simmballs—both supervene on and causally direct the rearrangement of their own physical substrate, meant to test whether symbols genuinely push the substrate around. The following argument now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove that stable computation and cognition are impossible.

(1) In a careenium, every simmball is realized by a specific substrate configuration, so any change in a simmball requires a corresponding substrate change.
(2) By definition, simmballs also encode instructions that cause substrate rearrangement.
(3) Therefore, any substrate change caused by a simmball produces a new simmball, which issues new instructions, generating an unbroken causal loop with no equilibrium point.
(4) A system lacking an equilibrium point cannot maintain the stable intermediate states observed in brains, computers, and chemical pathways.
(5) Since stable intermediate states clearly exist, careenia are impossible, and symbols must be purely descriptive rather than causally effective.

I am reluctant to abandon physical causal closure or to treat symbols as mere epiphenomena, yet the conclusion erases computation entirely. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it preserve, and what does it cost?
