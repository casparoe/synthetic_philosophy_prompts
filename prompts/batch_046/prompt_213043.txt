Consider the landscape of open problems that remain once we take Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise seriously—not merely as a localized puzzle about modus ponens, but as a diagnostic for the justification of deduction itself. I want you to prioritize, for a research agenda in the philosophy of logic, which unresolved questions in this vicinity are genuinely upstream of the others and therefore deserve the bulk of our limited intellectual resources. Specifically, weigh at least three interrelated lines. First, determine whether the Tortoise's resistance exposes what we might call a license problem—how one acquires entitlement to deploy an inference rule—or rather an execution problem—how one recognizes that a particular premise-instantiation warrants that deployment—since the remedy differs radically depending on which diagnosis holds, and since conflating them has, I suspect, obscured decades of debate. Second, assess whether adopting a logical pluralist framework dissolves the regress or merely displaces it onto the prior choice of logic, and whether that displacement is philosophically costly in a way that classical monists can exploit without begging the question. Third, examine whether contemporary tensions in the metaphysics of logical consequence—particularly between model-theoretic and proof-theoretic conceptions—offer any resources for blocking the regress that do not collapse into circular justification or silent appeal to the very rules under examination. For each line, indicate whether settling it would resolve downstream debates about the epistemology of logical knowledge, the normativity of inference, and the prospects for justifying non-classical alternatives, or whether its resolution would leave those debates essentially untouched. Give me a defended ordering: which question should be tackled first by someone with three years and no institutional funding for empirical work, which should be treated as tractable only after another is settled, and which should be deprioritized because its answer changes little downstream. Your criteria should be explicit—tractability, dependency relations among the questions, magnitude of change each answer forces on adjacent fields—and you should defend the trade-offs between them rather than merely listing possibilities. Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific scholars; I want the reasoning to stand on its own. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a thoughtful letter or email.
