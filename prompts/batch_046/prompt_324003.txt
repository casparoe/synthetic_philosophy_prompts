I'm preparing a ninety-minute graduate seminar for philosophy-of-language PhD students with no background in Buddhist philosophy, and I need a teaching plan rather than an exposition. The idea is Nāgārjuna's tetralemma and Madhyamaka emptiness, but I want to avoid the chariot example entirely; instead, build the explanation around the ontological status of a democratic constitution—what it must have (institutional efficacy, dependence on parts and recognition) and lack (independent svabhāva, identity with its parts, a tertium quid outside convention) for the four negations to apply precisely. Lay out the dependency order: which distinction—conventional versus ultimate existence, or dependent origination versus intrinsic being—must be secured before the tetralemma is introduced, and which can only come after. Map the misconceptions these students will import from analytic metaphysics—treating emptiness as nihilism, reading the four corners as a modal-logic grid, or reducing svabhāva to standard essentialism—and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Provide one end-of-session check question that distinguishes grasping emptiness as the absence of svabhāva from merely endorsing ontological anti-realism. Finally, state what I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the Prasaṅgika-Svātantrika dispute over autonomous reasoning—to keep the dependency chain intact, and justify the omission.
