I'm a PhD student in cultural studies with a history minor, and I'm trying to map the philosophical baggage behind archival preservation tiers. In most national and university archives, state papers, diplomatic cables, and corporate board minutes get full climate-contolled storage, granular metadata schemas, and dedicated curatorial staff. Meanwhile community archives—union meeting minutes, neighborhood association logs, family photo albums donated to local libraries—get catelogued with bare-bons tags, stuck in basements or off-site warehouses with no climate control, and treated as supplemental rather than primary. What theory of historical knowledge does that institutional pattern presuppose? Does it assume that only state or elite actors generate documents that can support causal claims, or is it just a tractability bias dressed up as epistomology? Reconstruct the implicit view of what counts as evidence, say whether the practice is actually consistent with itself (since we do use oral histories sometimes, but mostly for color), and tell me what a genuinely microhistory-centered archive system would have to change in preservation budgets and cataloguing norms. Also please separate whats pretty settled in philosophy of history from whats genuinely contested among people who actually study this—dont present a contested view as if everyones agreed on it.
