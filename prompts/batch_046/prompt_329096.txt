Here's some notes for an essay I'm drafting for a philosphy of law seminar. The Hart-Devlin debate on legal moralism is well-trodden but I think people misread the core move.

My argument: Devlin's claim that loosening sexual morality risks social disintegration isn't an empirical prediction that society will literally collapse—it's a conceptual point about social identity. Shared moral boundaries are constitutive of a society in something like the way shared language is. Hart reads Devlin as offering falsifiable sociology, demands evidence, and declares victory when none comes. That's a category mistake.

But I want to push further. Devlin compares moral law to a fabric—tear it and the whole thing unravels. That analogy actually hurts him, because fabric can be repaired and rewoven; damage isn't necessarily irreversible. If I take Devlin seriously, he should care not about enforcing specific norms but about maintaining threshold conditions for moral disagreement—basically the shared framework that lets citizens recognize each other as moral agents. This sounds closer to Hart's own idea of a minimum content of natural law (survival conditions) than to pure legal moralism. Maybe the gap between them is smaller than the standard story says.
