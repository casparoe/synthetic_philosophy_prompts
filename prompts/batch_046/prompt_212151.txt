I'm a journalist auditing a popular essay on AI alignment before citing it in a feature. The essay summarizes inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) as the task of inferring a hidden reward function from observed human behavior in a sequential decision environment, typically under an assumption of approximate optimality. It then introduces a distinction between what it calls preference-extraction IRL, which is said to recover a complete ordering over world-histories, and reward-reconstruction IRL, which is said to yield only a task-specific scalar signal; yet it treats the two as functionally equivalent for alignment purposes, claiming that once either target is recovered, the system will pursue our genuine values and the risk of reward hacking vanishes, leaving only a computational scaling problem.

Please evaluate whether this characterization is fair. Separate what is technically accurate about the formal framework, what counts as a defensible simplification for a general reader, and what misrepresents either the mathematics or the philosophical debate around inferring preferences from behavior. In particular, is the claimed equivalence between preference-extraction and reward-reconstruction defensible? Does the conclusion that reconstructed rewards eliminate reward hacking actually follow from the premises given? And does framing observed behavior as approximately optimal—without addressing context-dependent preference reversal, noisy suboptimality, or the difference between revealed choice and endorsed value—introduce a hidden normative commitment that the essay should have made explicit?
