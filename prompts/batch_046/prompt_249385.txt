Please argue for the position that shame is the superior moral emotion for sustaining collective moral life, and that cultures can genuinely choose to cultivate it over guilt — but defend this claim through a specific conceptual distinction rather than through broad cultural generalization. In particular, argue that guilt is structurally agent-locked: it evaluates an individual act against an internalized norm and therefore resists collective redirection once the norm is privately adopted. By contrast, argue that shame is position-indexed: it registers a disruption in one's standing within a shared normative space, which means its content depends on publicly visible social coordinates rather than on private intention. From this, derive two conclusions: first, that shame is more amenable to cultural calibration because altering the shared normative space automatically alters the emotional content without requiring individuals to revise private moral codes; second, that a culture can actively choose shame over guilt by redesigning its institutions of moral accountability — for example, by replacing punitive-individual frameworks that presuppose isolated culpability with restorative-relational frameworks that presuppose disrupted social standing. Confront the objection that shame is necessarily paralyzing or externally coercive, and explain why that objection conflates unregulated shame with the emotion as it functions within a calibrated normative architecture. Keep your argument conceptual and self-contained; do not invoke specific empirical studies, cite authors, or request bibliographic support.
