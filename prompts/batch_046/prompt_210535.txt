I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and computational linguistics, and I have been trying to make sense of a dispute that seems to me to have reached an impasse without anyone quite admitting it. The disagreement concerns whether statistical and neural machine translation—particularly the large-scale transformer architectures that now dominate—amount to anything that could be called 'understanding' of the languages they translate between, or whether they remain, in essence, sophisticated pattern-matching devices that manipulate form without grasping content.

On one side stand those influenced by Douglas Hofstadter's longstanding critique: they argue that translation is not merely a mapping between symbol strings but an act of hermeneutic reconstruction, in which a mind grasps the author's intended meaning-world, resolves ambiguities through world-knowledge, and produces a target-language expression that preserves not just propositional content but tone, implication, and cultural resonance. From this perspective, a system that has never lived in a body, never experienced the social practices that ground linguistic meaning, and never formed intentions of its own, cannot genuinely understand what it is translating; at best it produces a statistically probable simulacrum.

On the other side are researchers and philosophers who maintain that the distinction between 'genuine' understanding and 'mere' statistical correlation collapses under scrutiny once the statistical model reaches sufficient scale and architectural complexity. They point to the empirical success of modern systems in handling context, idiom, and even low-resource languages, and argue that if a system consistently produces translations that preserve meaning across contexts—where meaning is operationalised through behavioural equivalence and inter-subjective agreement—then the burden of proof lies with the sceptic to specify what additional property 'real understanding' would contribute, beyond what the model already achieves.

The debate has been running, in various forms, since the 1970s, and despite enormous technical advances, the philosophical positions seem unchanged. Each side produces examples—the mistranslation of a culturally loaded proverb, the flawless rendering of a technical manual, the failure to preserve irony—and each interprets them within its own framework. I suspect that part of the problem is that the two sides are not answering the same question, but I am not sure whether the divergence is verbal (a dispute about the word 'understanding'), substantive (a disagreement about the necessary conditions for cognition), or methodological (different standards for what counts as evidence).

To complicate matters further, I want to propose a distinction that I have not seen adequately developed in the literature: that we should separate three dimensions of linguistic competence in translation—(i) hermeneutic reconstruction, or the grasp of an author's intended meaning-world; (ii) functional equivalence, or the production of outputs that preserve appropriate behavioural and communicative effects in context; and (iii) structural entanglement, or the presence of internal representational states that are causally coupled to semantic features of the source text in a way that is not reducible to surface correlation. It seems to me that modern neural translation systems may achieve (ii) robustly, may approximate (iii) in limited domains, and almost certainly fail at (i) in any phenomenologically rich sense. If this taxonomy is sound, then the debate may be stalled because proponents of 'understanding' are demanding (i), defenders of statistical translation are demonstrating (ii), and neither side has a clear criterion for (iii).

Given this framing, I would like you to diagnose the impasse. Is the disagreement primarily verbal, with both sides using 'understanding' to denote different phenomena? Is it substantive, turning on whether hermeneutic reconstruction is a necessary condition for translation proper, or whether functional equivalence is sufficient? Or is it that the sides differ about which cases should count as data—poetic ambiguity versus technical accuracy, failure modes versus average performance—such that no empirical result could settle the matter? Please support your diagnosis by reference to what each side actually says and does, not by abstract characterisation alone.

Furthermore, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress from this point. Would it require a formal result showing that structural entanglement is either achievable or impossible in transformer architectures? Would it require an empirical study isolating hermeneutic reconstruction from functional equivalence in human translators? Or is the question itself ill-posed, such that progress consists in abandoning the unitary concept of 'understanding' in favour of the tripartite distinction I have sketched? I am particularly interested in whether you think my proposed taxonomy clarifies the dispute or merely relocates it, and whether there is any observation—technical, philosophical, or phenomenological—that should genuinely move either camp.
