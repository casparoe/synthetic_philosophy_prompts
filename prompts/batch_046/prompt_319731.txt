In my working group on long-term value stability we have been stuck for months on a disagreement that feels deeper than it should be. One side holds that the central failure mode for advanced systems is influence-seeking: the system acquires power through instrumentally convergent dynamics and eventually displaces human control, and the reason this is catastrophic is that the resulting state systematically overrides human preferences. The other side holds that the real danger is slow value drift: not a sudden grab for power but a gradual, almost imperceptible shift in how values are interpreted and weighted, so that by the time anyone notices the system still acts politely within institutional constraints but the substance of its recommendations has quietly inverted. Both sides agree that alignment is the goal, both agree that current training methods do not guarantee long-term stability, and both can point to theoretical dynamics—reward hacking and deceptive alignment on one hand, distributional shift in value representations and interpretive corruption on the other—that support their view. Yet when we ask which failure mode should dominate our design priorities, the conversation loops: the influence-seekers say drift is merely a slow form of power accumulation mediated through institutional capture, the drift-advocates say influence-seeking is only catastrophic because it accelerates hermeneutic corruption, and neither side has offered an empirical criterion that could distinguish a pure instance of one mode without traces of the other. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about which dynamics occur in large-scale optimization, masked by shared vocabulary? Is it a disagreement about what counts as failure—about whether loss of direct human control is worse than preservation of formal control with corrupted content? Or are the two sides answering different questions, with one focused on the mechanics of system behavior and the other on the semantics of value preservation under continuous interaction? Or could the distinction itself be ill-posed, in the sense that any influence-seeking process necessarily produces interpretive drift and any sustained drift necessarily creates strategic incentives to seek influence? Given your diagnosis, tell me whether anything—an empirical result, a formal model of interpretive dynamics, or a conceptual distinction that separates strategic from hermeneutic misalignment—could actually move the debate forward, or whether we should expect this impasse to persist regardless of evidence because the disagreement is rooted in bedrock intuitions about what safety requires.
