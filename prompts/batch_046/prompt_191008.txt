I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to articulate my own intuitions about when a person has standing to blame someone else for a professional or technical failure, particularly in collaborative work. Below are six concrete cases with my gut verdicts. I am looking for a precise principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures the pattern; I also want to know which of my verdicts is the outlier that should be dropped if simplicity demands it.

Case 1: A senior backend engineer who consistently refuses to write unit tests for her own modules publicly criticizes a junior frontend engineer for shipping code without unit tests. Verdict: she lacks standing; this is direct hypocrisy, and the frontend/backend distinction does not matter because the underlying norm—testing before shipping—is the same.

Case 2: A developer who previously ignored web-accessibility guidelines, received formal feedback, redesigned her components to comply, and now insists that her teammate do the same. Verdict: she has full standing; her explicit reform and demonstrated compliance change her position.

Case 3: A developer was fired from her previous employer after deploying untested code that caused a major outage. At her current company, working in an entirely different technology stack, she has not yet had to deploy production code. She notices a teammate about to make the same deployment mistake and tries to stop him. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. She has not demonstrated correct practice in this environment, yet she has paid a direct, costly price for the exact failure she is warning against.

Case 4: An engineering manager who has not written production code in five years enforces a policy that all deployments must include an automated rollback script, and he criticizes a junior developer who skipped that step and caused an outage. Verdict: he retains standing; his role is structurally different, and he upholds the system-level norm he is invoking.

Case 5: A developer whose own codebase is full of uncommented, opaque functions criticizes a teammate for leaving functions uncommented. Verdict: she clearly lacks standing; this is hypocrisy without transformation or role separation.

Case 6: A developer with a well-known history of shipping critical bugs—she is often the source of production issues herself—identifies a severe bug in a teammate's code and insists it must be fixed before merge. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. On one hand, she is violating the very norm of careful implementation she is invoking; on the other hand, her long history of failure gives her an unusual epistemic authority about how such bugs propagate, and she is factually correct.

The tension I find hardest to resolve is between Case 5 and Case 6. Both involve blamers who are currently failing the norm they enforce, yet Case 5 feels like disqualifying hypocrisy while Case 6 feels like legitimate, even valuable, intervention. Meanwhile, Case 3 sits in a different kind of ambiguity about whether direct experience of harm can substitute for demonstrated correct practice.

Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between normative practice, epistemic authority, organizational boundary, and the nature of the failure—that delivers all six verdicts? If the principle must be simplified, which of my verdicts (Case 3, Case 5, or Case 6) should I overturn, and why? Finally, apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a security specialist who has never personally caused a breach, has studied every major breach in her industry in detail, and now advises her team on encryption practices. Does she have standing, and on what basis?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate the clause that governs each case, note where it conflicts with one of my intuitions, and avoid appeals to specific philosophical texts, authors, or reading lists.
