Consider the institutional design of municipal reparations programs adopted in several U.S. jurisdictions for historical housing discrimination: eligibility is restricted to claimants who can document direct lineal descent from a specific harmed resident within a precisely defined historical window, with collateral relatives, adopted kin, and more recent arrivals excluded regardless of current racial or economic status. The award is calculated not as redress for dignitary or psychological injury but as a present-value economic-loss formula—essentially the accumulated differential in property appreciation—disbursed through restricted housing vouchers rather than unrestricted cash. Funding, however, is drawn from general municipal revenue, including taxes paid by residents with no ancestral connection to the harm. Meanwhile, the program treats the wrong as closed once the voucher is issued, with no ongoing structural obligations.

What theory of intergenerational moral responsibility does that architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view of harm: does it treat the injustice as a discrete, quantifiable economic injury that can be priced and fully discharged, or as an enduring collective condition? Does the lineal-descent criterion presuppose that culpability and entitlement travel only through biological genealogy, or can it be justified on purely causal-economic grounds? Is the practice philosophically coherent, given that the same legal system ordinarily rejects inherited liability in tort and refuses to calculate damages solely from the defendant's gain? And what elements of the program—eligibility rules, the metric of loss, the funding source, the closure mechanism—would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with one that treats historical injustice as a non-transferable, collective burden shared by all members of the affected group regardless of genealogy?
