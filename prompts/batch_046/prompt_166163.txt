I'm a high school student in an introductory philosophy seminar, and our final meeting is on Zhuangzi's reflections on death and transformation. We have been working through his argument that death is not a rupture but one phase in the continuous process of change (hua), and we spent considerable time on the butterfly dream episode—where Zhuangzi, upon waking, cannot determine whether he is Zhuangzi who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming that it is Zhuangzi—to see how it undermines any rigid boundary between states of being.

For the closing, I would like a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with an ABA rhyme scheme and two refrains that recur according to the form's rules. I want the poem's architecture to do philosophical work. One refrain should express the epistemic ambiguity of not knowing whether one is alive, dead, or dreaming; the other should state that transformation itself is indifferent to the names we give its phases. These two lines must come together unchanged in the final quatrain, and that convergence should feel like the moment in Zhuangzi's thought where the fear of death dissolves—not into certainty, but into acceptance that identity is not fixed across change.

Please render the view with enough precision that someone familiar with the Zhuangzi would recognize the specific ideas—death as part of the four-season cycle, the refusal to treat life and death as opposing values—rather than offering a generic meditation on impermanence. At the same time, the poem should not preach or resolve the tension: it should leave the reader suspended between the loss of self and the continuity of change, exactly where our discussion ended. No footnotes, no citations; just the poem, with the form strictly observed and the refrains intact.
