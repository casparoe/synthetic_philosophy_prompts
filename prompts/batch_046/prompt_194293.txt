In our philosophy-of-biology working group, the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function has reached an impasse that feels increasingly unproductive. All participants accept that a heart pumps blood and that evolutionary history shapes organisms; yet when we turn to concrete cases—particularly orphan genes that arise de novo with no detectable homology and immediately confer a selectable advantage, or engineered synthetic gene circuits introduced into a host genome—the two camps talk past each other. The etiologist insists that even here the function is fixed by the selection history that explains the gene's current presence, whereas the causal-role theorist treats the same phenomenon as a straightforward mechanistic contribution to fitness, denying that evolutionary origin adds explanatory content. My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about biological facts but a verbal one masked by shared vocabulary: both sides are describing the same ascriptive practice but embedding it in different explanatory projects—evolutionary explanation versus mechanistic explanation—without noticing that the word 'function' is doing double duty. Is that correct? Or is there a hidden substantive dispute about what counts as a proper explanation in biology, about which cases should serve as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding naturalness and design that neither side can independently justify? I want an honest diagnosis, supported by reference to what proponents of each view actually claim in these edge cases, rather than a literature survey. Given your diagnosis, could anything genuinely resolve the stall—a conceptual distinction between origin-fixing and role-fixing, an empirical finding about the prevalence of de novo gene birth, a formal result in evolutionary modelling, or a reframing of the question—or is the dispute simply ill-posed when extended beyond standard evolutionary contexts?
