I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly philosophy and cognitive science, and last week I was evesdropping on two ethicists arguing in a hotel bar after a panel on moral reasoning. (1) Dr. Chen said genuine moral dilemmas are definatly impossible: if you really have two obligations that cant both be met, then one is stronger, and all you've got is a hard choice, not a true conflict. She called this the 'single-scale view' -- every reason eventually lines up on one metric, so morality cant genuinely contradict itself. (2) Dr. Okonkwo replied with what he called the 'remainder thesis': even after you pick the best option, there's a real moral loss left over that isnt erased by being correct, and that leftover weight shows the conflict was genuine, not just apparent. (3) Chen pushed back that remainder is just regret or emotional residue; feeling bad after a choice doesnt prove you still owed the other thing. (4) Okonkwo then described a scenario he named 'the symmetric trolley': pulling a lever saves five people but kills one person who was about to save ten others, while doing nothing kills the five but lets the hero survive. He said both actions and inaction carry the exact same moral weight, so there's no 'right' answer, only tragedy. (5) Chen said thats just a contrived hypothetical with hidden commensurability assumptions smuggled in.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say who's more right overall, and whether both are missing something important about how moral conflict actually works.
