I have come to hold a strong version of the scale argument as applied to insect sentience, and I want to understand exactly where it is exposed. The position is this: given conservative estimates of global insect populations running into the tens of quintillions across present and near-future generations, even an extremely low subjective credence—say, one part in ten thousand—that any individual insect possesses phenomenal consciousness generates an expected moral burden that dwarfs the aggregate suffering of all sentient vertebrates combined. I take this not as a mere statistical curiosity but as a practical constraint on moral reasoning: if our ordinary practices—pesticide use, habitat destruction, agricultural monoculture—affect insects at this magnitude, then the expected harm dominates almost any other ethical consideration, unless one can drive the prior for insect consciousness essentially to zero on principled rather than merely intuitive grounds. I am aware of the standard replies, namely that low-probability high-impact scenarios do not automatically generate obligations when we lack mechanisms for targeted intervention, and that evolutionary distance gives us reason to discount phenomenological similarity, but I find these inadequate unless they amount to a categorical structural exclusion—an argument that consciousness requires a neural architecture insects physically cannot instantiate, rather than a probabilistic discount.

So I want a map of what would genuinely count against this view. Which empirical findings in insect neurobiology—perhaps concerning the absence of integrated global workspace dynamics, or the purely reflexive nature of nociceptive pathways—would have to obtain for the scale argument to collapse? Which conceptual distinctions between nociception and phenomenal experience, or which normative principles about moral risk under radical uncertainty, would need to be defended? I am particularly interested in whether there is a defensible structural-exclusion thesis that goes beyond mere scepticism about other minds across phylogenetic distance. Please distinguish between defeaters that would be fatal, forcing abandonment of the core claim, and those that are merely awkward, requiring heavy qualification but leaving the argument intact. I also want to know what a committed defender of the scale argument would themselves concede as damaging, as opposed to what critics claim; and I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by retreating into claims about unknown or radically alien forms of consciousness that no empirical result could touch. If it has been insulated in this way, is that insulation a theoretical vice for a position of this kind, or is it an unavoidable feature of reasoning under deep uncertainty about minds so distant from our own?
