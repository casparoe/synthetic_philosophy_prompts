At a seminar on early modern philosophy, two graduate students argued about Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz’s well-known defense of women’s study, written in reply to an ecclesiastical reprimand. Maria claimed the text is genuinely subversive: the author asserts that women should study secular subjects—grammar, rhetoric, natural philosophy—not only scripture, and she cites learned women such as Catherine of Alexandria to argue that ignorance increases heresy rather than preventing it. For Maria, this asserts that women’s intellect deserves cultivation for its own sake, not merely for domestic piety. Leo replied that this overreads the text: the author never demands institutional access, frames all study as private and subordinate to religious devotion, and repeatedly undermines her own authority with extreme self-deprecation—presenting herself as ignorant and writing only to avoid the fault of silence. Leo took this as strategic accommodation: she seeks private tolerance, not equality, and her humility is a genuine acceptance of gendered hierarchy rather than a disguise. Maria countered that the humility is precisely the rhetorical shield—without it the letter could not have circulated in late-seventeenth-century colonial Mexico. The dangerous content—secular sciences, female exemplars, direct address to a male prelate—survives because the packaging looks obedient. Leo answered that if the packaging is so effective that no institutional challenge remains, then praising the text as radical mistakes politeness for protest and ignores the material conditions she never contested. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: assess Maria’s opening claim, Leo’s reply, Maria’s counter about strategic humility, and Leo’s final objection. Then deliver an overall verdict on whether the text is better read as subversive or accommodative, and identify one important issue both sides neglect—such as whether epistemic humility can simultaneously be a genuine virtue and a strategic device, or how the intended audience (the reprimanding bishop versus a wider clandestine readership) changes the political meaning of the argument.
