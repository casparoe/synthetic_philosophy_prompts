Look, I chair the acquisitions committee for a mid-sized contemporary museum, and we get three or four proposals every quarter to buy or exhibit work by artists who've done genuinely bad things -- not just old tweets but serious, documented harm. The board wants a reusable decision procedure they can run in about an hour, because I have to sign the memo and the donors actually read it. I don't want another vibe-check.

The principle I want operationalized is the separation of the work from the artist, but I think the usual debate misses three distinct ways a moral stain can travel. First, content-stain: the work itself thematizes, aestheticizes, or depends on the harm (think images that eroticize the exact abuse the artist committed). Second, provenance-stain: displaying or acquiring materially rewards the wrongdoer, their estate, or requires us to collaborate with people actively covering things up. Third, reception-stain: knowing the biography doesn't just make us feel icky but structurally blocks the kind of attention or emotional openness the work actually demands. I want these three to survive the simplification, but I need the rest stripped down.

Write me an ordered procedure: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it outputs reject, proceed-with-conditions, or proceed, and where it stops dead and says "this is a values call, not a fact call -- the chair decides and documents why." It can't depend on deep archival research we don't have time for (48-hour turnaround max), and it should flag the cases it can't settle.

Then run it on three proposals:
1. A dead abstract painter, revealed posthumously to have abused family members; we want a large non-representational canvas with zero biographical imagery.
2. A living documentary filmmaker whose film exposes labor exploitation, but she withheld wages from her own crew; screening it in our social-justice series would pay her distribution fees she still holds.
3. A genuinely important sculptor, but acquiring the piece requires working with a gallery that's currently funding his legal defense against credible assault allegations. The board loves the work and will hate saying no.

Make sure one of these gives the board an answer they won't like -- I need to see the procedure actually bite. Also be explicit about where the procedure misfires, what mistakes it systematically makes, and what it flat-out can't do. No bibliographies or reading lists, please -- just something I can tape to the wall.

Keep it casual, like you're emailing a colleague who actually has to sit in these meetings.
