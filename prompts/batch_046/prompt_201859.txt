Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a graduate seminar in philosophy of history (14 students) that makes the group feel, through play rather than lecture, the force of the problem with the contrastive question "why Britain and not China?" in discussions of the Great Divergence and the Needham question. The core idea should emerge from the rules: participants must discover that knowing which society industrialized first quietly determines which structural differences they treat as decisive.

Set-up and rules: Split the group into two teams of seven. Give each team two anonymized dossiers—Case Alpha and Case Beta—containing matched pre-1750 data on state capacity, agricultural productivity, geographic coal access, market integration, and legal institutions, but with geographic names and outcomes removed. In round one (25 minutes), each team ranks which case is structurally "primed for sustained industrial growth" and writes a brief justification using only the dossier cards; they then commit to a sealed prediction. At minute 25, reveal that Alpha is Britain and Beta is China. In round two (25 minutes), the teams switch sides and must defend the opposite ranking—arguing that Beta was actually better positioned—under a strict "counterfactual consistency" rule: they may cite only conditions present in both dossiers and may not invoke any post-1750 event or technological development. They must use the exact same card set.

Debrief and mapping: Use the final 35 minutes to map the round-one versus round-two shifts onto the philosophical problem. Ask whether the factors selected as explanatory changed only after the outcome was revealed, and connect this to worries that the Needham question and Great Divergence framing select explanatory variables retrospectively. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the multi-causal path-dependency and long-run contingency that real historical explanation requires—and state how the facilitator should supply that missing piece verbally. Predict the predictable failure mode: students will likely anchor on coal and geography in round one and resist reassigning those cards in round two; advise the facilitator to enforce the card-only rule strictly rather than debating historical accuracy during the switch.

What participants should come away seeing: that contrastive historical explanation risks being well-formed only because we already know the winner.
