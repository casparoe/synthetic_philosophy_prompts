I have been working through a philosophical problem on my own—specifically, whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that makes it up. The standard version notes that if the statue is melted, the clay persists but the statue is destroyed; since they differ in what can happen to them, they must be different things. Yet they also share the same location and every physical part, which makes the distinction puzzling.

I want to examine a modified scenario. Suppose the sculptor employs a reversible molding process: the formed statue can be collapsed instantly into an undifferentiated blob and then reshaped back into the identical statue, with no material lost or gained. In this setting, does the statue survive the collapse, or is it annihilated and later replaced by a replica? Does the clay's ability to survive the round-trip show that the statue shares that modal capacity?

Second, how does the reversible molding scenario bear on the argument that they differ in their modal properties—specifically, in what they can survive? Third, ought questions of material constitution be settled by comparing such modal properties?

Student answer:

In the ordinary case, the statue and the clay are not identical, since they possess different persistence conditions. The clay can survive melting or being flattened into a blob, whereas the statue is destroyed under those conditions, even though the clay itself remains intact. This is the familiar argument from material constitution.

The correct philosophical account, however, is Wiggins's constitution view. According to this view, the clay constitutes the statue but is not identical to it, because the clay is governed by the dominant sortal of being a material lump, while the statue is governed by the sortal of being an artwork with a particular form. Consequently, there are genuinely two objects present in the same spatial region.

With respect to the reversible molding case, I believe it defeats the modal argument for non-identity. Since the clay can be reshaped into the statue without any material loss, the statue itself survives the flattening; during the intermediate blob-phase it is not destroyed but merely dormant or in potential form. Therefore both entities possess the modal property of surviving reshaping, which eliminates the discrepancy that was supposed to show they are distinct.

Finally, on the general methodological question: yes, modal properties ought to determine material constitution. If two things can undergo precisely the same range of changes and survive them all, then there is no adequate metaphysical basis for treating them as separate. The reversible molding example demonstrates that the statue and the clay share every relevant modal feature, so they should be understood as a single object that receives two different descriptions.
