I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of logic and cognitive science, and I'm facilitating a 90-minute workshop for a graduate reading group—about ten participants, mostly from philosophy and cognitive science—on self-reference and hierarchical structure. We've been discussing tangled hierarchies, the kind of circular dependency illustrated in Escher's Drawing Hands, and the notion of an "inviolate level," a foundation meant to govern the system above it without being subject to that system's authority. I want an in-person activity or structured game that makes participants feel, through their own actions and incentives, why such a foundation structurally fails to remain stable once the system is permitted to interpret or revise its own rules. The insight should emerge from the rules and roles themselves, not from any lecture I deliver afterward.

To avoid producing a standard logic-puzzle exercise they've likely seen before, I'd like the design to incorporate an "interpretive inversion" mechanism: participants at one tier gain hidden authority to redefine the vocabulary that governs the tier immediately below them, but only if they simultaneously accept that their own defining terms are now being interpreted by that lower tier in mirror fashion. This should generate a felt, structural circularity rather than an abstract contradiction they can simply name and dismiss. Please specify a concrete setting (the workshop room layout, materials such as cards, sealed envelopes, or colored tokens), a division of roles across at least three hierarchical tiers, the rules for proposing and amending rules, and a payoff scheme with small but real stakes—for instance, points that determine which group authors the final "charter" of a simulated research-ethics institution, with no actual money involved. The rules should be arranged so that participants initially believe they have secured an inviolate base—say, a sealed Foundation Charter that governs all amendments—only to discover through play that the protective mechanisms (seals, interpretation protocols, voting thresholds) are themselves governed by rules sitting at higher levels, and that invoking those protections triggers the inversion loop.

The deliverable should include a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the failure of an inviolate level within a tangled hierarchy; it should also note what will predictably go wrong on the first run—such as one group monopolizing interpretation, or risk-averse participants refusing to invoke the inversion—and be frank about which feature, specifically the simultaneous mutual dependence that creates a true causal loop, cannot be reproduced in a sequential game and must therefore be supplied conceptually by the facilitator afterward. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on obscure theoretical references that a model without web access could not reasonably be expected to know.
