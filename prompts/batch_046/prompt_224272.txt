I am a novelist preparing the research interviews for a historical novel that moves between fourteenth-century Avignon, mid-sixteenth-century Padua, and late-seventeenth-century London. One installment will feature a historian—whom I will call Dr. Elena Voss—who defends a position I am calling asynchronous epochalism: she maintains that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not mere historiographical inventions, but neither are they unified, simultaneous transformations. Rather, she argues they are real, causally autonomous ruptures occurring in distinct registers—juridical, aesthetic, and epistemic—whose timelines do not align, so that assigning any single date such as 1500 or 1600 always misplaces at least one of these ruptures. She further claims that this register-specific asynchrony dissolves the conventional continuity-versus-rupture debate, since continuity and rupture can both hold true of different registers at once.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions in American English that avoid asking her merely to restate this view. I want at least one question addressing each of the following:

First, the charge that her taxonomy relocates rather than solves the standard periodization problem—does multiplying registers simply move the boundary dispute into a more complex grid?

Second, figures who appear to straddle boundaries, such as a fourteenth-century jurist whose rhetoric anticipates humanism, or a seventeenth-century natural philosopher still working within scholastic metaphysics—how does her framework classify them without resorting to ad hoc exceptions?

Third, the implications for a novelist dramatizing these eras—specifically, whether depicting 1550 Padua as Renaissance but not yet Scientific Revolution commits a kind of historical falsehood, even if the characters themselves could not have named these registers.

Fourth, the metaphysical objection that her view requires an implausible account of historical causation—what she terms cross-register inertia—and what observable pattern would genuinely weaken rather than merely reinterpret her commitment to it.

For each question, include a brief, pointed follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to provide. If she appeals to primary-source disciplinary self-identification, press on the circularity of using actors' categories to validate anachronistic registers. If she invokes cross-register inertia, ask what empirical finding would count as its failure. If she grants novelists aesthetic license, ask whether that license undermines the ontological seriousness of her epochal distinctions.

Please ensure the questions are formal, polished, and suitable for a recorded colloquium, and write the entire response in American English.
