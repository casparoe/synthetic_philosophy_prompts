In seminars on political legitimacy I keep encountering a compressed version of Pascal's idea—justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical—used to justify conclusions ranging from minimal-state liberalism to authoritarian enforcement of distributive norms. Rather than adjudicating those conclusions, I want a precise disambiguation of the slogan itself: what distinct theses about the relation between normative justification and coercive capacity could it express? Lay them out as a menu, separating at least (i) a descriptive sociological claim about the conditions under which just norms become effective, (ii) a conceptual claim that the concept of justice analytically includes enforceability, (iii) a normative claim that coercion is legitimate only when directed by just ends, and (iv) any stronger metaphysical claim identifying justice with power. For each, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and specify which reading actually licenses the political arguments in which the slogan is deployed. In particular, address whether Pascal's formulation in the Pensées was intended as an observation about human societies or as a prescription for institutional design, since interlocutors invoke it in both registers without distinction.
