I manage intake and treatment planning at a small wildlife rehabilitation center. Each week I must decide, for animals recovering from injury, whether an observed behavior is an adaptive response that should guide our care—how we feed, house, or time release—or merely a spandrel: an unavoidable side effect of some other evolved trait, or what I have started calling a “structural echo,” where motion or posture arises only because rebuilt anatomy produces incidental movement. I have been thinking about Gould and Lewontin’s argument against the adaptationist program, that not every feature is optimally designed for its present function. I need this translated into a reusable procedure my staff can run during the thirty-minute intake assessment, without calling an evolutionary biologist, and that produces written justification for state inspectors. Please write it as a step-by-step flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what answer stops the process with a “do not treat as adaptive,” what answer moves to the next filter, and the explicit points where judgment—not fact—must decide. The procedure must handle spandrels, exaptations, and structural echoes, and it must flag the cases it cannot settle. Apply it to three cases: a recovering hawk that strikes the cage bars with its beak; a deer that separates itself from the group after a leg injury; and one additional case of your choosing where the procedure returns an answer I will not want—specifically, that we should ignore a behavior I was inclined to treat as a distress signal requiring intervention. Finally, state plainly what the procedure is incapable of determining, and where an inspector or veterinarian must have authority to override it.
