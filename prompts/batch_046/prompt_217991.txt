As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have noticed a recurring institutional pattern in technology firms that seems conceptually puzzling. Engineering promotion systems typically require candidates to document “lasting impact” and “legacy contributions”—systems or products that endure well beyond one’s direct involvement—which treats professional meaning as anchored in permanent, objective achievements. At the same time, the labor structure is organized around agile sprints and continuous deployment, practices explicitly designed to prevent any final state: code is perpetually refactored, products are never “finished,” and iterations renew without terminal goals. Many companies overlay this loop with mission rhetoric about “advancing human knowledge” or “connecting people,” framing the endless cycle as part of a grand narrative. The result is a tension between a teleological view of work—meaning derives from reaching and leaving behind a definitive contribution—and an organizational form that structurally resembles perpetual striving without closure. What philosophical account of meaning in life does this dual practice presuppose? Must it assume an objective-list theory in which lasting value is the currency of meaning, or can a purely subjective, process-based, or coherence-based account explain why “legacy” is the metric we reward? Is the pattern compatible with absurdist views—that one might embrace the loop as meaningful precisely because it lacks ultimate purpose—or does it quietly smuggle in a commitment to transcendent significance that the work structure itself undermines? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, say whether its parts are philosophically consistent or underdetermined, and describe what a workplace built consistently on one of these accounts would have to change in its evaluation criteria and design practices.
