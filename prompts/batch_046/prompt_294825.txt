I am a high school senior completing an independent study in moral philosophy, and I have been working slowly through John Locke’s Essay Concerning Human Understanding, particularly the discussion of personal identity in the second book. I am struggling to grasp the precise logical architecture of his claim that sameness of substance—whether of bodily matter or of immaterial soul—is not what makes someone the same person over time; rather, it is the continuity of consciousness, extended backward through memory, that constitutes personal identity. Locke appears to argue that if the consciousness of a prince were somehow joined to the body of a cobbler, the prince would remain the same person despite occupying a different substance, and conversely that if a present consciousness cannot reach back to remember a past action, that action is not properly attributed to the present self. I understand that he distinguishes “man” as a biological organism from “person” as a thinking, conscious being, but I cannot tell whether this distinction is meant to be merely analytical or whether it carries normative weight about which self has authority over which future state.

My difficulty becomes sharper when I try to apply this framework to contemporary debates about advance directives and severe cognitive decline. Suppose a fully reflective adult executes a living will refusing certain life-sustaining interventions, and then, decades later, develops advanced dementia in which the earlier values and memories are largely inaccessible, though the biological human continues to live and occasionally expresses a preference for those very interventions. On Locke’s account, has the continuity of consciousness been broken severely enough that the demented individual is, strictly speaking, not the same “person” as the one who signed the directive? If so, does the directive lose its binding force not merely for practical reasons—because the patient now objects—but because the authority to legislate for a future state depends on being the same conscious being, and that sameness has dissolved? Or is there room in Locke’s view for a kind of cross-personal moral delegation, in which one conscious self can bind a future biological human even when the chain of memory-consciousness is interrupted, perhaps analogously to how a trustee acts for a beneficiary?

Could you help me understand exactly how Locke’s backward-reaching criterion of consciousness functions—whether it severs personal identity in cases of profound memory loss, and whether his framework necessarily undermines the authority of an advance directive when the signer and the patient appear to be, in his terms, different persons? I am less interested in modern bioethical conclusions than in whether Locke’s own logic about substance, consciousness, and responsibility permits any form of authority to survive such a rupture.
