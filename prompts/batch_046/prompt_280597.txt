After a long and heated argument with a friend about whether excellent governance outcomes can ever excuse the absence of democratic mechanisms, I find myself unable to let go of a specific tension. My friend argues that when a regime—or any collective decision-making structure—reliably delivers prosperity, social stability, and rapid resolution of practical problems, objections to its authoritarian form reduce to an aesthetic preference for procedural fairness. He explicitly invokes the developmental records of states like China and Singapore as evidence that performance can generate its own legitimacy, and he insists that demanding elections in contexts where they would diminish welfare is like valuing ritual over result.

I tried to resist this by distinguishing between accepting outcomes and authorizing the power that produces them, but I could not articulate why that distinction should carry weight when the outcomes are genuinely excellent and widely appreciated by those affected. To sharpen the problem, consider a concrete analog: a professional association of roughly two hundred members that replaced its elected governing council with an appointed board of senior practitioners. The board exercises full budgetary and ethical authority without consultation, yet in the eighteen months since the transition, funding has tripled, internal disputes have collapsed, and anonymous satisfaction surveys show overwhelming approval. Several junior members have privately told me they feel politically voiceless, though they also concede that the association functions far better now and that they would not vote to return to the previous democratic but dysfunctional arrangement.

My friend claims this case vindicates performance legitimacy entirely: the board delivers, members are satisfied, and insisting on elections is just procedural fetishism. I suspect the deeper issue involves adaptive preferences—where satisfaction is shaped by the absence of viable alternatives rather than by authentic endorsement—and the difference between ex post acceptance and ex ante authorization. Yet I am unsure whether these concepts genuinely undermine the evidential force of satisfaction, or whether they merely smuggle in an unargued commitment to democracy as an intrinsic good.

Can you help me map the philosophical structure of my discomfort with precision? I want to know whether there is a coherent theory of legitimacy that treats prosperity and order as genuinely supporting conditions without allowing them to fully substitute for non-manipulated consent or participatory standing, and whether the worry about adaptive preferences can be formulated rigorously enough to show that high satisfaction under hierarchical rule does not, by itself, establish legitimate authority. Please treat the professional association as the central test case, and avoid simply asserting that democratic participation is valuable for its own sake; I need an analysis of whether performance can, in principle, justify the exclusion of democratic input, and where exactly that argument breaks down if it does.
