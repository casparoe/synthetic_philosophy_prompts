Design a classroom simulation for an advanced undergraduate seminar of about 14 students, running 80 minutes, that makes participants feel the conceptual problem of whether “collapse” names an objective historical event or a category imposed by the observer’s choice of which institutional thread counts as “the society.” Use three well-known cases—the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui (Easter Island)—as the raw material. Divide the class into three assessment teams, each assigned one case and secretly given one of three different “continuity protocols”: demographic (population levels and settlement density), symbolic-political (monumental construction and elite literacy), or ecological-adaptive (subsistence technology and settlement relocation patterns). Each team applies only its hidden protocol to its case and must deliver a verdict—collapse, transformation, or indeterminate—backed solely by evidence that protocol permits. In a subsequent plenary, teams present verdicts without revealing their protocols; the group must discover that identical archaeological narratives yield opposing classifications depending on the metric in play. Include explicit rules and roles for both phases, a minute-by-minute timeline, a payoff or recognition structure that rewards consistency within a protocol rather than “correctness,” and a debrief that maps each protocol onto a philosophical stance about whether societies genuinely fail or merely change form. The debrief should also include an honest account of which feature of the conceptual problem the simulation cannot reproduce—such as the temporal distance and interpretive ambiguity inherent in archaeological inference—and a brief warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it. Write the response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
