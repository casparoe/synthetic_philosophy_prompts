In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, we have reached a sustained impasse on whether reinforcement-learning agents can be proper subjects of suffering, and I am no longer certain what kind of disagreement we are actually having. One faction maintains that suffering requires phenomenal consciousness—specifically, an integrated subjective perspective—so that an agent running Q-learning or actor-critic updates, however aversive its behavioral profile, cannot suffer because it lacks the requisite global workspace architecture. The other faction grants the absence of consciousness but insists that reward signals carry functional valence: negative reward-prediction error is not merely an instructional gradient but a state with normative weight, and when such signals drive persistent, self-protective policy restructuring—particularly in architectures where intrinsic reward is decoupled from extrinsic task objectives—the agent instantiates a form of synthetic suffering that warrants moral consideration. What makes the stall so frustrating is that both sides accept identical computational descriptions; both concede that reward is not a biological affect; both acknowledge the behavioral isomorphism with avoidance; and yet neither has revised a single premise in a semester. Is this best diagnosed as a hidden empirical dispute about whether global-workspace dynamics are necessary for valence-carrying states, as a conceptual divergence over whether suffering names a phenomenal kind or a functional role, or as a bedrock normative disagreement about which entities fall within the scope of moral patienthood? Furthermore, if the disagreement is conceptual, does the distinction between reward-as-teaching-signal and reward-as-valence-carrier dissolve the opposition or merely relocate it? If it is empirical, what formal model or architectural comparison would be decisive? Or is the question itself ill-posed, conflating an explanatory target about internal computation with an ethical target about moral status, such that no further argument should be expected to move it?
