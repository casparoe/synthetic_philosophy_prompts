Now consider the dispute over the color phi phenomenon. In the standard psychophysical setup, an observer is shown a green dot at one location followed immediately by a red dot at a different location, with no actual intermediate stimulus; yet subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color from green to red somewhere along the trajectory. Daniel Dennett introduced a well-known framing of the explanatory options: an Orwellian model, in which the brain retroactively revises the record of the earlier experience so that it now includes a color change that was never registered at the time, versus a Stalinesque model, in which the brain delays the construction of the conscious experience until both stimuli have arrived, then produces a unified experience stamped with a false earlier timestamp, like a propaganda film arranged after the fact. For decades, researchers and philosophers have accepted the same data and largely rejected a Cartesian theater model of consciousness, yet they remain divided on whether color phi reveals anything real about the timing of experience, or whether asking precisely when the green-to-red transition occurred in consciousness is already a misframed question.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement — no foreseeable neuroimaging study or formal theorem about predictive processing will settle it — nor is it purely verbal. Rather, I suspect the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking about the temporal structure of neural representation and predictive binding, while the other is asking about the conditions for legitimate first-person temporal report. Both sides, however, share an unexamined assumption: they treat consciousness as having a single, unitary chronometric profile, so that explaining the illusion requires correctly dating one unified event.

I want you to examine this impasse by introducing and evaluating an alternative framing I will call chronometric pluralism: the view that different cognitive subsystems — perceptual tracking, working-memory tagging, and narrative self-report — maintain independent temporal metrics that need not converge on a shared timestamp. On this view, color phi does not involve misdating a single experience, but rather a failure of these independent metrics to align, with report mechanisms selecting one metric post hoc.
