As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mathematics, I have formulated an argument that seems to show intuitionistic logic undermines empirical science altogether. I would like you to diagnose where it fails.

(1) The Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation demands that an existential claim be accompanied by an explicit construction of the object in question.
(2) In physics, we assert existence constantly—for example, that a specific quasinormal mode exists in an open resonant cavity—based solely on indirect spectral traces and inverse-problem inference, with no algorithm that uniquely constructs that mode from first principles.
(3) Applying the BHK standard uniformly, these scientific assertions lack the required constructive proof and therefore have no definite truth-value.
(4) Nevertheless, physicists embed such assertions in predictive models and treat them as genuinely true.
(5) So either empirical science systematically violates conditions for meaningful assertion, or constructivism applies only to pure mathematics and must be arbitrarily excluded from applied domains.
(6) No principled demarcation between pure and applied mathematics supports this exclusion.
(7) Therefore, if intuitionistic logic is correct, large regions of physics are conceptually incoherent.
(8) Because that conclusion is unacceptable, intuitionistic logic should be rejected entirely.

Please identify the weakest premise or inference step, explain why it initially appeared plausible, and indicate what defenders of constructivism in Dummett's anti-realist tradition and defenders of classical scientific realism each must concede or deny to block the argument. Also tell me which specific feature—the indirect-inference structure of physical detection—is carrying the weight, and whether the problem dissolves if we replace indirect detection with direct observation.
