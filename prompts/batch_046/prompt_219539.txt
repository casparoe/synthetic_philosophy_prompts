My opening challenge concerns a kind of institutional speech I will call 'ex officio regulatory warning.' Imagine a government agency that issues a standardized product label stating, 'This compound may present hazards under specific conditions of exposure,' where the agency's internal technical guidelines restrict this claim to a narrowly documented chemical interaction. The spokesperson who approves the label acts purely ex officio, holds no personal belief about broader hazards, and is even required by internal protocol to treat the warning as technically narrow. Nevertheless, because of the institutional framing — the use of mandatory warning formats, bold typography, and absence of qualifying scope — virtually every consumer derives the conversational implicature that the product is generally dangerous for ordinary use. The spokesperson knows this broader derivation will occur and does nothing to block it, yet she does not intend it; indeed, her institutional role forbids her from endorsing it. If Gricean implicature requires that the speaker mean something by intending the audience to recognize that very intention, then we seem forced either to deny that any implicature occurs here (which seems descriptively wrong) or to say that the spokesperson's private psychological states are irrelevant and that the institution itself 'means' the broader claim (which collapses implicature into conventional institutional meaning). How do you defend the Gricean requirement of speaker-intention recognition in this case without either attributing a false belief to the spokesperson or abandoning the boundary between what is said conventionally and what is genuinely implicated?
