Write an essay on the moral status of collective forgetting in an age of digital permanence. Begin by outlining the view associated with the philosopher Avishai Margalit that remembering shared historical wrongs is not merely a private sentiment but a form of moral solidarity with victims, and that societies bear a duty to preserve the past through institutional and cultural practices of remembrance. Then present the countervailing perspective, drawing on the sociologist Philip Rieff's broader critique of therapeutic modernity, that an insistence on perpetual remembrance can become a cultural pathology: a refusal to allow historical trauma to recede that paralyzes collective agency and transforms suffering into a fixed identity. Having established this tension, introduce a conceptual complication that neither framework was designed to address: digital archives do not decay. Unlike analog records or human testimony, which diminish through neglect, material loss, and mortality, digital storage makes forgetting an active and technically demanding choice rather than a natural process. Analyze whether this shift strengthens the duty to remember—because preservation is now inexpensive, searchable, and universal—or weakens it by rendering remembrance inescapable and therefore oppressive. Consider specifically whether there is an ethical distinction between remembering through deliberate curatorial choice and remembering through unavoidable retrieval. Reach a definite conclusion: does digital permanence transform the duty to remember into a duty to curate selectively, or does it generate a stronger right to engineered oblivion? Defend your verdict with conceptual arguments about agency, burden, and solidarity rather than empirical forecasts about technology.
