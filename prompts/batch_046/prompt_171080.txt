Consider the following examination question and student response from an upper-division philosophy of religion course. You are grading as the instructor or designated examiner. Do not signal your own substantive view on the problem of evil; your deliverable is strictly an itemized assessment of the answer’s philosophical accuracy, coherence, and responsiveness to the prompt.

Question (10 points):
Distinguish a theistic defense from a theodicy in the context of the problem of evil. Then evaluate the following hybrid position—call it Contrast-Dependent Soul-Making (CDSM): certain moral goods (e.g., courage, forgiveness, sacrificial care) are not merely causally promoted by adversity, but are conceptually impossible in worlds lacking real evil; yet God does not cause evil but permits it, on the grounds that a world containing both these goods and the corresponding evils is, on balance, better than a world containing neither, provided the evil is not disproportionate. Specifically, address: (a) whether CDSM should be classified as a defense or a theodicy; (b) whether its logical structure is closer to free-will theodicy or to soul-making theodicy; and (c) whether it can answer the evidential argument from apparently pointless suffering, using the case of a young child who dies before any moral formation can occur, leaving no discernible development of character in the child and no clearly traceable moral growth in those around the child specifically attributable to that death.

Student Answer:
CDSM is best understood as a defense rather than a theodicy, because it does not claim to know God’s actual reasons for allowing evil; it only demonstrates that allowing evil is logically compatible with divine goodness by identifying a possible reason God might have. Its logic is closer to the free-will defense, since both views center on divine permission rather than causation, and permission is the governing moral category in each case. Regarding the child’s death, CDSM fails against the evidential argument: because the child never develops courage or forgiveness, and because no party near the child is shown to have acquired these traits specifically through this event, the suffering is genuinely pointless within the framework. CDSM requires that every permitted evil be matched by an actual, observable instance of the corresponding good in the affected parties; since that condition is not met here, the case shows that evil can exceed what the modal dependency justifies. Thus CDSM may work for ordinary adversity, but it collapses when applied to suffering that yields no discernible moral growth.
