I am preparing a feature essay for a science and ideas publication on the internal logic of American exceptionalism, and I have constructed an argument that appears to prove far more than I intended — specifically, that the normative version of the thesis collapses into either universal availability or self-cancellation. I would like a disciplined diagnosis of which step overreaches, together with an account of what rejecting that step costs the two standard interpretations.

The argument proceeds as follows.

(1) American exceptionalism, in its normative register, holds that the United States does not merely differ from other polities but occupies a singular position: it embodies universal principles — republican liberty, constitutional democracy, continental scale — in a form that is, by its own historical account, non-replicable.

(2) If a polity genuinely instantiates a universal principle in a singular, non-substitutable form, then the procedural norms governing ordinary states — multilateral treaties, international courts, reciprocity-based sovereignty — do not fully apply to it, because those norms presuppose that actors are interchangeable and that no single actor's survival is indispensable to the principle itself.

(3) Therefore, exceptionalism licenses the United States to disregard multilateral constraints whenever they conflict with the preservation or extension of its singular mission.

(4) At the same time, the historical thesis of exceptionalism denies that this uniqueness can be certified by any external, universal criterion; it insists that the exceptional quality is demonstrated only through the nation's own founding acts and continuous self-renewal.

(5) Consequently, any regime capable of constructing a comparable origin narrative — a civilizing mission, a revolutionary vanguard, a defense of traditional values — can invoke the identical logical structure: singular embodiment of a universal value plus exemption from ordinary norms.

(6) The only available defense is to restrict exceptionalism to cases where the claimed universal promise is validated by criteria that are independent of the state's own narrative.

(7) But that restriction eliminates the exceptional quality altogether: the state becomes merely one successful applicant among others of universally valid norms, not an exception to them.

(8) Hence American exceptionalism is either a generic license available to every self-mythologizing regime, or it surrenders its claim to uniqueness and becomes redundant.

This conclusion strikes me as excessive — I want to preserve (1) and (3), because I believe exceptionalism genuinely carries exemptionist implications — yet I cannot locate the precise error. Is the flaw in (2)'s inference from uniqueness to normative transcendence? Does (4) wrongly assume that uniqueness must be self-certifying rather than partially observable? Or is there a hidden scope shift between the historical thesis and the normative claim that makes the argument invalid? I want to know the weakest link, why it looked plausible, and what the exemplarist reading (which treats the U.S. as a model for others) and the exemptionist reading (which treats it as structurally exempt from multilateral constraint) each must sacrifice in order to reject it.
