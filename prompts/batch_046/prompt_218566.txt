I'm preparing notes for an interdisciplinary discussion group that meets at the intersection of economic thought and film, and I want to work through a reading that doesn't reduce either side to an illustration of the other. The philosophical frame is Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, particularly his distinction between the economic problem—the secure provision of absolute needs—and what he presents as the permanent problem of living wisely, agreeably, and well once that first problem is solved. Keynes warns that a civilization trained too long to strive may suffer a kind of collective nervous breakdown when leisure becomes universal, and he distinguishes insatiable relative needs, which depend on social comparison, from needs that can actually be satisfied. Against this, I want to place Pixar's WALL-E (2008): the passengers of the Axiom have every absolute need met by automation and corporate provision, yet they exist in a state of passive, infantile consumption, physically atrophied and socially atomized, while the robots WALL-E and EVE seem to discover purpose—protecting plant life, forming attachment—outside any economic structure.

My question is whether the film dramatizes Keynes's anxiety precisely: that solving scarcity does not solve the art of living, and that without cultivated habits of purposeful activity people fall into something like bad faith about their own freedom, accepting a hollow version of comfort as fulfillment. Or does the film resist a Keynesian reading because the passengers' condition stems not from an excess of leisure per se but from corporate manipulation (the Buy n Large monopoly) and technological dependency, failures Keynes did not fully anticipate? I want a reading that takes both the narrative details—the captain's reawakening, the robots' non-utilitarian pursuits, the physical decay of the humans—and Keynes's conceptual framework seriously, especially his claim that the love of money must eventually yield to the love of life itself. Where does the film illuminate a gap in Keynes's optimism about post-scarcity culture, and where does Keynes illuminate a blind spot in the film's social critique? Please treat the work and the philosophy as genuinely in dialogue, and conclude by saying what this particular pairing adds to the philosophical debate that neither the essay nor the film could establish alone.
