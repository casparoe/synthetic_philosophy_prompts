Consider the classic puzzle of material constitution: a statue and the lump of clay that composes it appear to share location and parts, yet differ in persistence conditions—destroy the statue and the clay may survive, destroy the clay and the statue is gone. To move beyond the well-rehearsed dialectic, frame a harder variant. Imagine the original unfired clay is gradually dispersed: some fired into bricks incorporated into a wall, some ground into pigment, the remainder lost. Decades later, the material is recovered, chemically restored to its original composition, and reshaped into the statue's exact form using a high-fidelity digital mold derived from the original scan. The causal-material chain is continuous but spatially and functionally broken; the form is restored precisely, yet mediated by a non-physical representation.

Given this framing, I want a dependency-aware prioritization of the genuinely open questions in material constitution—not a survey, and not a reading list, but a defended agenda for where limited philosophical effort should go. Rank the following lines of inquiry by urgency, using explicit criteria: downstream dependency (which answers determine others), tractability with current conceptual tools, and practical stakes for cultural-heritage ontology or artifact-preservation reasoning.

First, is constitution necessarily one-one, or can one lump of clay simultaneously constitute many distinct artifacts (the "many statues" problem)? Second, do artifacts have essential origins, or can they survive complete material replacement coupled with causal-historical dispersion and digital mediation? Third, can formal or structural continuity—possibly guided by non-material representations—ground identity independently of material continuity? Fourth, does a dominant sortal (e.g., "statue" versus "clay" versus an emergent kind) fix persistence conditions, and if so, how is that sortal determined?

For each: indicate whether it is upstream or downstream of the others; whether resolving it would change the verdict in the dispersed-clay case; and whether it should be deprioritized because its answer lacks practical or theoretical leverage. Conclude with an ordered recommendation—what to tackle in a single dissertation chapter or grant proposal, what to treat as background, and what trade-offs justify that ordering.
