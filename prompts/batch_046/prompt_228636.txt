I cover the intersection of cognitive science and artificial intelligence, and I keep encountering a cognitive architecture that standard evolutionary taxonomies seem to leave unclassified. Picture an agent—biological or synthetic—that shows no capacity for internal mental simulation: it does not generate counterfactual scenarios or reject hypotheses inside its own processing before acting. On its own, it behaves through hard-wired response patterns or trial-and-error conditioning. But embed it within an external symbolic scaffold—say, a formal decision tree, a written diagnostic checklist, or a linguistic proof framework—and it systematically avoids errors that would otherwise demand foresight. The filtering of dangerous or inefficient options occurs in the scaffold, not in the agent's native machinery. The agent has not acquired internal pre-selection; it has rented the architecture of something that possesses it. Does this pattern have an established name? How should it be mapped onto the familiar hierarchy of cognitive kinds—those relying on evolutionary hard-wiring, on conditioning, on internal hypothesis testing, and on cultural or symbolic tools? Is this merely an instance of the tool-using kind deploying external resources, or does it constitute a hybrid category—perhaps a form of scaffolded pseudo-preselection—that the fourfold scheme does not cleanly accommodate? I want more than a label: I want the boundary conditions. What precisely distinguishes this from ordinary tool use on one side, and from genuine internal mental simulation on the other?
