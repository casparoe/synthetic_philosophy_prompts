I am drafting an essay for my final-year moral philosophy seminar on indirect normativity and the function of idealised deliberation in justifying action-guiding rules. Rather than rehearsing the standard contrast between act- and rule-consequentialism or Scanlonian contractualism, I want to examine a presupposition I believe runs through these accounts: that idealisation removes bias and partiality but preserves full access to the particular facts of the situation at hand. I call this assumption deliberative transparency.

My central claim is that some plausible indirect norms—especially those governing institutional or professional conduct—are justified not despite but precisely because of informational constraints present at the moment of adoption. I term this structured ignorance: the rule is endorsed under conditions analogous to, but distinct from, Rawls's veil of ignorance, in which agents do not know which particular positions or identities they will hold. The difficulty arises when an actual agent, no longer behind that veil, knows that applying the rule in this specific case will produce serious, avoidable harm that the rule was never designed to accommodate.

For the essay I propose to analyse a concrete scenario: a senior clinician applies a hospital triage protocol that was adopted under conditions of structured ignorance about patients' social value and personal identities. The clinician recognises that, on direct evaluation, this particular patient clearly merits an exception, yet the protocol was justified precisely because such particular knowledge was excluded during its design. I want to ask whether the indirect theorist can consistently maintain that the clinician ought to follow the protocol without either (a) collapsing indirect normativity into direct evaluation of the case, or (b) adopting a rigid rule-fetishism that disregards morally relevant particularities.

Could you comment on whether my distinction between deliberative transparency and structured ignorance is philosophically coherent, and whether the triage scenario genuinely isolates the tension rather than relying on an implausibly rigid rule? I am particularly interested in whether standard accounts—contractualist, rule-consequentialist, or Kantian—already contain resources to address this problem, or whether I am identifying a genuine gap. I would also welcome observations on whether my argument risks conflating institutional design with individual action-guidance, and on how I might sharpen that boundary without smuggling direct evaluation back in.
