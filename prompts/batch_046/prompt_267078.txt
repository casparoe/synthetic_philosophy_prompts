I am a journalist preparing a podcast interview on the philosophy of history, and my guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a view she terms mnemonic precedence. She holds that when a community's living collective memory conflicts with the documentary historical record, the memory ought to prevail in public education, commemoration and institutional narrative—not on the grounds that it corresponds more accurately to past events, but because it sustains the practical identity and normative continuity of the group. She distinguishes this sharply from historical relativism: she grants that archives may correct factual errors, yet maintains that official records suffer from structural exclusion of subaltern experience, while collective memory—transmitted through embodied ritual and oral practice—preserves a form of social knowledge that propositional archives cannot capture.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid merely asking her to restate this position. I want at least one question pressing how she distinguishes legitimate collective memory from manufactured or politically manipulated remembrance; one on who determines the boundaries of the community when internal factions hold contradictory memories; and one on whether her view requires maintaining a memory even after members have privately acknowledged its inaccuracy, should abandoning it fracture social cohesion. For each, provide a brief follow-up keyed to the defence she is most likely to offer—particularly any appeal to embodied transmission as an epistemic filter, or to the separation between factual correction and narrative authority. The tone should be rigorous but not hostile, suitable for an audience of educated general readers rather than specialists alone.

Please provide your response in British English.
