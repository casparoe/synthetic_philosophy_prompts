I am drafting an application for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship bridging philosophy of science and AI safety research. The project grows out of a persistent disagreement I have been having with colleagues about whether HCH—Humans Consulting HCH—should be understood purely as an engineering heuristic for scaling reasoning, or whether it functions implicitly as a normative idealization of deliberation itself. I want to defend the second interpretation and show that it carries an uncomfortable commitment. Specifically, I suspect that treating factored cognition as an idealization forces us to assign each human-level reasoning node a form of epistemic sovereignty within its assigned subdomain. Once that sovereignty is granted, errors that arise not from faulty reasoning inside a node but from the decomposition protocol—miscarved problem boundaries, framing contamination inherited from the factoring structure, or what I will call joint-carving failures—become systematically invisible to the collective reasoning process. This strikes me as a philosophical problem, not just an implementation glitch, because it threatens the normative claim that HCH preserves the rational structure of individual deliberation at scale.

Please turn this into a formal two-page proposal. The core question must be stated precisely: does the HCH idealization necessarily presuppose node-level epistemic sovereignty, and if so, can the framework accommodate self-correction for decomposition-level errors without collapsing back into undivided individual deliberation? Explain why standard treatments of factored cognition leave this open—typically by treating decomposition as transparent to normative evaluation rather than as itself a source of rational failure. Outline the method: conceptual analysis of the idealization conditions, comparison with philosophical accounts of distributed cognition that deny sovereignty to parts, and a constructive sketch of what a non-sovereign factoring protocol would require. Specify what counts as success (showing the idealization is either incoherent without a meta-level checker or that such a checker can be internalized), what counts as failure (the concern dissolves into a purely practical engineering issue with no distinct philosophical content), and why six months is realistic—sufficient for developing the conceptual framework but not for formal modeling. Do not request a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by reference; I will add those myself. I want the framing to feel somewhat in the weeds—familiar enough that someone working on alignment or distributed cognition will recognize the terrain, but angled sharply enough that it is not a standard literature summary.
