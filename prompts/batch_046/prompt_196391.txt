I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy serving as a grader for an advanced seminar on distributive justice. One exam question asked students to evaluate a specific variant of prioritarianism—call it gradient prioritarianism—which weights welfare benefits not only by absolute welfare level but also by a gradient-sensitive coefficient tracking the rate of welfare change across a population. The question required students to assess whether this view is vulnerable to a modified leveling-down objection that operates across welfare dimensions (for instance, material versus hedonic welfare), whether its reliance on comparative gradients reintroduces equality as an independent value, and to distinguish carefully between leveling down within a single dimension and leveling down across dimensions.

I want explicit evaluation of four components: (i) the explanation of why standard prioritarianism avoids the leveling-down objection; (ii) the analysis of whether gradient prioritarianism is vulnerable to the modified objection; (iii) the treatment of the intra-dimensional versus cross-dimensional distinction; and (iv) the discussion of normative weighting, incommensurability, and whether equality is smuggled back in. Assess the reasoning solely as student work—do not indicate whether you endorse prioritarianism, the leveling-down objection, or gradient prioritarianism itself.

Question 3 (30 points): Prioritarianism holds that benefits to the worse-off matter more, other things equal. A well-known challenge is the leveling-down objection: if equality has non-instrumental value, then reducing everyone to the same low welfare level can seem better than maintaining unequal but higher welfare. Standard prioritarianism is usually thought to avoid this because it assigns no weight to equality per se—only to absolute welfare levels. Now consider gradient prioritarianism, which introduces a coefficient measuring the rate of welfare change across individuals within a given dimension. Critics argue that comparing these gradients across dimensions (e.g., comparing the slope of material welfare decline with the slope of hedonic welfare decline) reintroduces equality in a disguised form and generates a modified leveling-down objection: one might justify reducing the material welfare of the affluent to match that of the poor, leaving hedonic welfare untouched, on the grounds that the material gradient is steep and the hedonic gradient is flat. Evaluate this charge. Does gradient prioritarianism avoid the modified objection? Does its use of comparative gradients commit it to valuing equality? In your answer, distinguish carefully between (a) leveling within a single welfare dimension and (b) leveling across dimensions, and address whether cross-dimensional comparison requires a common metric that prioritarianism must reject.

Student Answer:

Gradient prioritarianism escapes the standard leveling-down objection for essentially the same reason that standard prioritarianism does: welfare weights depend only on absolute levels and on the rate of change in welfare, not on equality as such. The gradient-sensitive coefficient tracks how sharply welfare declines or improves across a distribution; it functions as an epistemic device that helps identify where marginal benefits are greatest. Because the coefficient carries no normative weight of its own—unlike an explicit egalitarian constraint—there is no justification for leveling everyone down to a uniform plateau.

The modified objection targets cross-dimensional cases. Suppose we can reduce material inequality by lowering the material welfare of the affluent to match that of the poor, while leaving hedonic welfare untouched. If the gradient coefficient for material welfare is steep and for hedonic welfare flat, this leveling might appear efficient. But this appearance is misleading. The coefficient compares gradients within each dimension separately; applying it across dimensions presupposes a common metric that prioritarianism, which treats welfare dimensions as incommensurable, rejects. Therefore, cross-dimensional leveling is blocked not by the absence of equality's value, but by the incommensurability of dimensions themselves.

Within a single dimension, the objection also fails. Lowering the well-off does not improve the position of the badly-off, and because the gradient coefficient assigns lower weights to those at higher welfare levels, any loss at the top is weighted more heavily than an equivalent gain at the bottom. Hence, uniform downward leveling is never justified. This aligns with the standard prioritarian claim that priority to the worse-off does not entail valuing equality itself.

One might worry that measuring gradients requires interpersonal comparisons that smuggle in egalitarian assumptions. This concern is unfounded: gradients can be calculated for each individual relative to their own baseline and aggregated without assuming that equality of gradient is valuable. Thus, gradient prioritarianism retains prioritarianism's core advantage: it directs benefits to those who are worst off without committing to equality as an independent good, and it avoids both the original and the modified leveling-down objections.
