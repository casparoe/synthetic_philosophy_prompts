I'm preparing an eighty-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ordinary-language philosophy—about fourteen students who have read selections on rule-following but keep insisting that every concept must have a hidden essence they just haven't articulated yet. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel why family resemblance resists that impulse, not through lecture but through a staged failure of their own making. Design a two-phase simulation for this group, with explicit rules, a timeline that fits the session, materials that require only printed cards, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophical point without naming it until the end.

In Phase One, "The Ethnographer's Notebook," divide the students into four groups and give each group a different subset of eight cards describing practices in a fictional society—things like passing a flat stone clockwise at dusk without speaking, repeating a phrase before eating meat, a child mimicking an elder's hand-motion before sleep, and other ambiguous behaviors that share criss-crossing similarities but no single common thread. Ask each group to propose one necessary-and-sufficient condition that captures all eight practices as instances of one kind—call it a "binding practice"—and to write it on the board. In Phase Two, "The Commissioner's Charter," appoint one student from each group as Commissioner; the Commissioners swap groups and must apply only the written definition they inherited to a fresh deck of twelve cards describing new practices, some clearly overlapping, some borderline, and some designed to exploit the definition's edges. Crucially, the Commissioners may not ask clarifying questions of the original authors—only yes-or-no responses—so they experience applying a rule they did not construct to cases they did not choose, experiencing the gap between private rule-formulation and public application.

After about fifteen minutes of Phase Two, halt both phases and run the debrief: have each Commissioner report which cards they rejected despite intuitive overlap, have the original authors defend or abandon their definitions, and then draw out the pattern—how every attempt to fix an essence either excluded familiar cases or admitted strangers—before connecting it to Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance and the resistance of language-games to essentialist taxonomy. Include an honest note on what the simulation cannot reproduce: the historical and anthropological thickness of actual language-games embedded in forms of life, which the facilitator must supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that groups write overly broad definitions in Phase One that collapse embarrassingly in Phase Two—and how to handle it without rescuing the essentialist impulse by allowing a quick "revision" that restores confidence in hidden essences. Finally, inject a slight twist that keeps the exercise from feeling like a standard classroom demo: have one hidden card in each fresh deck describe a practice that satisfies the written definition exactly but feels intuitively wrong to the group, so the failure is not merely logical but felt.
