Because I'm trying to formalize a negotiation protocol that assumes shared rationality, their exchange suddenly matters to me, but I honestly cannot adjudicate it. Here is exactly what they said.

Maya began by saying the theorem shows that if two agents are rational and share a common prior, then common knowledge of their posteriors forces agreement; therefore, any persistent real-world disagreement must come either from hidden private information or from irrational updating, not from rationally held prior differences.

Jonas replied that this makes the result trivial: if you assume identical starting beliefs, of course shared evidence produces identical conclusions. The theorem doesn't explain actual expert disagreement; it simply defines rational disagreement away by baking convergence into the premises.

Maya countered that Jonas is missing the normative point. Rationality doesn't require identical priors in fact, but it does require that differences in prior belief be traceable to differences in prior evidence, not to arbitrary taste. So the theorem describes an ideal: once all evidence is shared, rational agents should converge.

Jonas said that "arbitrary" is doing hidden normative work. Two scientists can rationally weight the same evidence differently because of different theoretical commitments, methodological values, or disciplinary training — none of which is irrational. If those prior differences are themselves rational, then rational agents do not share a common prior, and the theorem tells us nothing about whether they ought to converge.

Maya answered that even those differences come from different evidence histories. Once every piece of background evidence is made common knowledge, only genuinely non-evidential differences remain. So if disagreement persists after full disclosure, it must be either irrational or based on hidden information.

Jonas claimed this collapses all prior disagreement into hidden information, which is empirically false: equally informed physicists can disagree about string theory's promise based on aesthetic or methodological priors that are not secret data but openly acknowledged rational commitments.
