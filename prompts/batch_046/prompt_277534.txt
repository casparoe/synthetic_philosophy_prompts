I am revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and formal limits, and I find myself returning to a tension I have never fully resolved in the Gödelian anti-mechanist tradition associated with Lucas and Penrose. The standard dialectic is familiar: the anti-mechanist claims that a human mathematician can recognize the truth of a Gödel sentence that any consistent formal system containing arithmetic cannot prove, and therefore that human cognition transcends algorithmic computation. The mechanist replies that human reason is itself fallible, inconsistent, or simply embedded in a stronger formal system that can prove the sentence in question.

What strikes me as under-theorized in this exchange is what I will call the meta-system escape. The mechanist might concede that no single fixed formal system S captures human reasoning, but propose instead that the mind operates within a dynamic or hierarchical family of systems—say, S' proves the Gödel sentence for S, S'' proves the consistency of S', and so on. The anti-mechanist then applies Gödel's second incompleteness theorem at each level, generating an infinite regress in which every proposed formalization remains incomplete from the perspective of the next. The philosophical crux, it seems to me, is whether this regress demonstrates a genuinely non-algorithmic capacity of the human mind to occupy a vantage point outside every formalization, or whether it merely establishes that any static model of cognition is incomplete—a result that is perfectly compatible with the mind being a non-static, learning, or probabilistic mechanism that never actually requires an infinite ascent.
