You serve as both a professional philosopher of aesthetics and an experienced seminar facilitator with deep familiarity with categorization debates. Before drafting any rules, think carefully and begin by brainstorming: list at least three distinct ways that participants in a small group could physically or socially encounter the tension between essentialist and family-resemblance accounts of letterform identity, then select the approach that best forces the problem to emerge from interaction rather than from explanation.

Setting: A graduate seminar in philosophy of perception or aesthetics, 14 participants, one 75-minute session. Participants are familiar with general problems of categorization but have not studied computational letter-recognition architectures in detail.

Your task: Design an in-class game called The Recognition Charter that makes participants feel, through their own rule-making, the conceptual problem at the heart of the domain Letter Spirit—what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Center the activity on a specific analytical framework I am testing, the tri-layer identity model, which distinguishes (i) the topological skeleton (structural relations invariant across instances), (ii) the stylistic license (the range of deformation that preserves recognizability without altering skeleton), and (iii) the contextual expectation (how surrounding glyphs and task conditions shift classification). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design must be entirely self-contained.

Divide the 14 participants into three charter committees (4 members each), each assigned to defend one layer as the sole essence of 'a'-ness, plus two observers who record contradictions and apply rules impartially. Provide each committee with 12 printed glyph cards: clear instances of lowercase 'a' in serif, sans-serif, handwritten, blackletter, and italic forms; and near-miss cards that satisfy exactly two layers but violate the third—for example, a form with correct skeleton and license but placed in a context that invites reading as 'o'; a form with correct skeleton and contextual fit but such extreme license that stroke continuity is broken; and a form with correct license and context but an altered skeleton that replaces the closed bowl with an open curve.

Each committee has 20 minutes to draft binding recognition rules using only descriptors drawn from its assigned layer. They then present their rules, and the observers apply all three rule-sets to the disputed cards. Use a point scheme without real money: committees earn one point for every correct classification, but lose two points whenever an observer demonstrates, using only internal resources of that committee's rule-set, that the same rule misclassifies another card. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that any sharp cutoff they propose either excludes a legitimate 'a' or includes an impostor, and that appeals to the remaining layers are necessary yet circular.

After the game, conduct a 15-minute debrief that maps each committee's failure onto the corresponding layer of the tri-layer model, explains how the interaction revealed that letterform identity is not reducible to any single layer, and states clearly what will predictably go wrong the first time you run the activity—specifically, that participants will smuggle in intuitive judgments about designer intent or prior familiarity despite the explicit prohibition—together with how to handle it (for instance, by instructing observers to flag such smuggling in real time). Finally, be explicit and honest about which feature of letterform cognition this classroom format cannot reproduce: the analog, continuous navigation of a style-space that allows a system to generate novel letterforms within a grid by adjusting continuous parameters rather than selecting discrete rules, a capacity central to the motivating vision of this domain but impossible to replicate with printed cards and discrete rules; you must supply that feature through direct explanation after the debrief.

Write in a formal, polished voice appropriate to a philosophy professor requesting a pedagogical design. Ensure the idea shows up in the rules, roles, and incentives, not in a lecture delivered afterward.
