I'm writing as a doctoral student in philosophy of history, and I'm hoping you can adjudicate a disagreement that came up among colleagues after our seminar on historiography and religious narrative. Two fellow students—I'll call them Elena and Marcus—got stuck on exactly when a legend becomes history, using the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus as comparative cases. Their exchange ran as follows.

Elena opened with a two-part criterion: a legend crosses into history only when it has both independent contemporary textual attestation and some archaeological correlation, supplemented by what biblical scholars call the criterion of embarrassment—details that later tradition-bearers would not invent. She argued that the Trojan War now satisfies this: Hittite texts contain possible references corresponding to Wilusa, and excavations at Hisarlik reveal a destruction layer plausibly dated to the right period. By contrast, she said the Exodus lacks any archaeological trace of a large-scale Sinai migration or sudden Canaanite conquest, and its internal narrative coherence actually counts against it, since the patterning reflects later redactors' theological concerns. So for Elena, the Exodus remains legend, not history, however culturally weighty.

Marcus replied that Elena applies her filter selectively. He noted that King Arthur lacks both archaeological correlation and anything like independent contemporary attestation—no source mentions him for centuries, and the material comes through medieval chroniclers clearly shaping a national origin myth. Yet historians do not treat Arthur as pure fiction in the same dismissive way they treat the Exodus; they classify him as "legendary history," precisely because later transmission made him foundational for British identity. Marcus proposed a uniform alternative: a legend becomes history when a community treats it as foundational for collective self-understanding, regardless of evidentiary type. On this view, the Exodus is history for the community formed around it, Arthur is history for medieval Britain, and Troy is history for both ancient Greeks and modern archaeologists—just different modalities of historical being.

Elena countered that Marcus collapses the distinction between "something that happened" and "something that was believed to have happened." She insisted that history as a discipline aims at past occurrence, not merely cultural memory; otherwise every founding myth would count as history and critical distance from tradition would collapse. She granted Arthur's later influence makes him historically significant, but said significance is not occurrence.

Marcus delivered what he took as the decisive reply: for pre-modern events, we never access "past occurrence" except through transmission. Even for Troy, our access is mediated by epic poetry, textual interpretation, and stratigraphic inference. So demanding archaeological correlation for the Exodus while accepting textual transmission for Arthur is arbitrary unless driven by a desire to debunk a living religious tradition. He concluded that Elena's criterion smuggles in a metaphysical preference for material traces over textual ones, and that consistency requires either applying the transmission criterion to all three cases or rejecting it for all three.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Elena's opening criterion, Marcus's Arthur counterexample, Elena's occurrence-versus-memory distinction, and Marcus's final epistemic challenge about mediated access. Say clearly who is right about each specific point, identify what—if anything—both miss about the comparative status of the Exodus, and state whether one overall position is more defensible. Take a committed position with definite reasoning. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the very first sentence, then develop your reasoning; do not begin with a restatement of the question or any preamble.
