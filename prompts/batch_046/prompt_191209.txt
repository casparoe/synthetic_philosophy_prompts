I have sketched a brief essay proposing a non-standard interpretation of Leibniz’s claim that this world is the best of all possible worlds. Rather than reading the doctrine as God maximizing a single scalar utility function across worlds, I argue that “best” should be understood through what I call modal opportunity-cost minimization: God selects the world whose actualized set of compossible states carries the lowest aggregate modal cost—the smallest loss of genuinely possible alternative states—while still satisfying the constraints of simplicity and variety. On this reading, local evils are not debits in a global ledger of suffering versus happiness, but structural byproducts of a selection procedure that optimizes for the richness of the actualized possibility-space rather than for hedonic or moral value.

The essay then addresses the classic objection—if this is the best world, why does it contain apparently gratuitous suffering and inefficient biological design?—by arguing that the objector implicitly assumes an aggregative metric that the modal-cost reading rejects. A world with less suffering might be metaphysically possible, but only at the expense of excluding other states of affairs that depend on the same causal laws; the “best” world is the one with the lowest modal cost of its actualized content, not the one with the least local harm.
