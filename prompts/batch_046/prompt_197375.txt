I am preparing for a philosophy-of-mind seminar in which we have been examining Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of cognitive architectures—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—not as a finished classification but as a normative framework that requires explicit evaluation. Our instructor has asked us to articulate the full set of desiderata that an adequate taxonomy of minds must satisfy, together with the motivating theoretical case or comparative cost behind each requirement. I would like you to lay out these criteria systematically: evolutionary continuity without anthropocentric exceptionalism; a principled distinction between hard-wired behavioral responses, operant conditioning, internal hypothesis-testing, and culturally mediated cognition; explanatory coverage of the transition from non-linguistic to linguistic cognition without invoking an unexplained qualitative leap; consistency with comparative neurobiology and behavioral ecology; and—this is the point where I am particularly uncertain—whether the taxonomy must also accommodate artificial learning systems that optimize via gradient descent rather than through evolutionary or behavioral selection, or whether such systems fall outside the scope of a biological taxonomy altogether and demand a separate framework. For each desideratum, please indicate whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular research camp, and note whether any subset of these demands is jointly unsatisfiable given current results in evolutionary psychology or machine learning.

Please do not provide a scorecard of Dennett’s four kinds against this list; I want the criteria themselves, the conceptual tensions among them, and a clear statement of which single requirement does the most work in excluding rival architectures. If there is an internal contradiction—for instance, between strict evolutionary continuity and the claim that Gregorian cultural tools create a genuinely novel selection environment that breaks continuity—say so explicitly and explain how a defender of the taxonomy might resolve it without simply redefining one of the categories. The goal is to clarify what a theory in this area must be able to do in order to count as adequate, rather than to defend or attack any particular version of the fourfold division.
