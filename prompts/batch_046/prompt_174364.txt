Consider the judgments of a moral philosopher—call her Dr. V—who applies Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and the associated idea of one thought too many. She has evaluated the following cases. In Case 1, a spouse provides long-term care for a severely disabled partner without ever weighing the burden against other goods, explaining only that this is what they do; Dr. V judges this an intact ground project, not a failure of reasoning. In Case 2, a military officer refuses an order to execute prisoners, stating that he is not that kind of soldier, even though compliance would prevent a greater atrocity; she finds his refusal properly unweighed. In Case 3, a musician cancels a career-defining performance to sit with a grieving friend, never comparing the lost opportunity to the friend’s need; she again sees integrity rather than excess calculation. In Case 4, a mayor rejects a life-saving water infrastructure contract because it conflicts with a personal religious vow, and she does not weigh the lives that would be saved; here Dr. V rules that she should have broken the vow and weighed the public harm—this is the failure mode. In Case 5, a writer destroys her manuscript rather than allow an edit that would make it widely accessible, refusing to compare artistic vision against audience benefit; Dr. V holds that this refusal is legitimate and does not require weighing.

Now consider Case 6: a surgeon whose ground project is healing refuses to perform a minor cosmetic procedure on a patient with severe body dysmorphia. She knows the refusal may drive the patient to dangerous unregulated alternatives, yet she declines without weighing that risk against her principle that she is not a beautician. Predict Dr. V’s verdict. Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and which principle—domain-specific protection of professional identity versus consequence-sensitive permission for unweighed commitment only when breaking it does not prevent serious harm—best organizes her rulings? State what each principle predicts for Case 6, give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and identify the further case that would distinguish these two organizing rules.
