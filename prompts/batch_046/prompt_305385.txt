I have been thinking, entirely outside any formal coursework, about whether human history can be governed by discoverable laws in the way that natural phenomena seem to be. A colleague mentioned Karl Popper’s argument—what I understand as the “poverty of historicism”—that societies are open systems in which knowledge itself changes conditions, and that because no historical constellation ever repeats exactly, there can be no universal historical laws comparable to those in physics. I work in logistics and read philosophy in the evenings, so I am trying to work through the logic without relying on technical jargon I might misuse. What I keep running into are contemporary analysts who claim to forecast political instability or economic collapse decades ahead by modeling demographic stress, resource depletion, and climate migration patterns. These predictions are not old-fashioned Marxist stage theories, yet they appear to treat history as if it has an underlying script that can be read off from data. Could you describe the most important implications of Popper’s critique for judging whether such long-range societal forecasting is intellectually legitimate? In particular, I want to understand whether there is a meaningful difference between using historical patterns to prepare for a range of possibilities and believing those patterns constitute deterministic laws, and whether that distinction should change how we evaluate predictive models that miss turning points repeatedly yet are refined and retained rather than discarded. I am not asking for a bibliography or citations—only for the conceptual consequences I should hold in mind as I try to think this through on my own.
