A scholar who studies biography as moral education has ruled on whether particular biographical forms serve as effective instruments of moral learning. Her track record: Plutarch's Parallel Lives do, because comparing paired lives reveals virtue as context-sensitive rather than uniform; medieval hagiographies do not, since the suppression of vice removes the struggle necessary for imitation; modern critical biographies do, but only when moral failure is structurally integrated rather than sensationalized; autobiographies composed to vindicate a career do not, lacking the critical distance that allows a reader to judge; and collective professional biographies do, yet only if they include ethically failed figures rather than pure exemplars. Consider now a synthetic biography: an AI-generated, statistically composite life of a fictional physician in eighteenth-century Edinburgh, assembled from hundreds of archival records so that every moral dilemma is historically typical but no real individual corresponds to the narrative. Would she accept this as a legitimate instrument? I care less about the answer than about the competing rules that fit her earlier judgments—perhaps that moral learning requires an actual historical agent, visible moral failure, comparative framing, or external critical distance—and about which further case would best distinguish them.
