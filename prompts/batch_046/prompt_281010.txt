I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about sexual morality by testing my gut judgments against a broadly Benthamite framework—one that weighs direct pleasure and pain but also makes room for indirect harm, autonomy-violation, and the symbolic meaning of relational structures. Here are my concrete verdicts.

Two consenting adults of the same sex in private, with no internalized shame and no risk to others: permissible. A married couple using reliable contraception by mutual agreement: permissible. A polyamorous triad with fully transparent agreements, no jealousy-induced suffering, and stable emotional bonds: permissible. Autonomous sex work with fair compensation, no coercion, and no emotional damage: permissible. A heterosexual couple in which one partner regularly submits to unwanted sex solely to preserve domestic peace: not permissible. A secret extramarital affair in which the cheater and the affair partner both experience net pleasure, the betrayed spouse never learns of it and lives happily unaware, and no social fallout occurs: not permissible, even though direct suffering seems absent. A lifelong celibate who reports sustained well-being and no regret: permissible, not a moral failure.

The case I keep returning to: two adult siblings, both fully consenting, using reliable contraception, in complete privacy, with genuine mutual affection and reported net satisfaction, fully aware of the relation and having reflected carefully, with no risk to others or to offspring. I lean that this is wrong, though I struggle to justify the verdict without appealing to a bare social taboo. This sits in tension with my approval of cases one through four, which share the features of consent, privacy, and absence of direct harm.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a refined calculus that treats relational transparency and autonomy-violation as independent variables, or a pluralist account with a structural constraint on kinship roles—that delivers verdicts one through seven, including the judgment that case six is wrong despite its hiddenness? Or does accommodating case eight require overturning one of the earlier verdicts?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause handles each listed case, predict its verdict on an unlisted case (a committed couple who agree to a temporary, fully disclosed separation so one partner may pursue a specific experience with a third party, then resume exclusivity with full transparency), and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

What would change your mind: which argument or finding about indirect harm, the conditions for valid consent, or the symbolic meaning of relational forms would lead you to abandon the principle you propose, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
