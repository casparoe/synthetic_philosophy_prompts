Consider the view that the self is a center of narrative gravity—an abstract attractor in the space of autobiographical stories rather than a substance or soul. Here is an argument that seems to show this view proves too much:

(1) If the self is only a center of narrative gravity, then it exists wherever there is a stable, internally coherent narrative that assigns agency, memory, and character to a life.
(2) A single biological life can sustain multiple such narratives that are each globally coherent but mutually incompatible in their attributions—for instance, the subject's own retrospective autobiography and an intimate partner's parallel account may assign different intentions, different memories, and different moral characters to the same body over the same period, with neither reducible to error or distortion.
(3) So one body can host several distinct centers of narrative gravity.
(4) If the self is nothing but such an attractor, then there is no non-arbitrary fact of the matter about which narrative is "the" self of that body; personal identity becomes a tracking decision rather than a metaphysical fact.
(5) But moral and legal responsibility require that there be a determinate answer to "who did it," not merely "which narrative are we following."
(6) Therefore, either we accept radical narrative pluralism—many selves per body, with no principled boundary—or we reject the claim that the self is merely a center of narrative gravity.

I would prefer to keep (1), because the attractor metaphor captures something real about how continuity is constructed. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
