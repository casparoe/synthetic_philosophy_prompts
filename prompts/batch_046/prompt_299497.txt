As a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, I have been trying to formalize my intuitions about rational decision-making, partly because our team constantly debates tie-breaking rules in distributed load-balancing systems. Before I present my view in an internal discussion, I want a consistency audit of the following package. I hold that: (1) rational choice is governed by expected-value maximization, and if two options are strictly equal in value, selecting either is fully rational and involves no mistake; (2) Buridan's ass—an agent genuinely unable to choose between two identically valued options—represents an irrational failure of agency, because a rational agent ought to possess some tie-breaking mechanism; (3) both random resolution (e.g., a coin flip) and deterministic arbitrary resolution (e.g., lexical comparison of internal object IDs) are rationally permissible ways to break ties; (4) I prefer deterministic methods in production, not because they are more rational, but purely because they are reproducible and debuggable; and (5) I accept a version of the principle of sufficient reason applied specifically to actions: whenever an agent actually performs A rather than B, there must exist some explanatory difference—perhaps in the agent's internal state or in environmental micro-conditions—that accounts for the divergence, even if that difference carries no normative weight.

Do these commitments hang together? Specifically, does (5) undermine (1) by implying that genuinely flat preferences can never occur in practice, thereby dissolving the problem that (2) is meant to solve? Is there a hidden tension between (2) and (1) regarding whether flat preferences are conceptually possible but rationally prohibited? And is my simultaneous endorsement of (3) and (4) genuinely inconsistent, or only apparently so once the normative and pragmatic dimensions are separated? Please audit the package: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are in real conflict and which dissolve on inspection, specify the least costly revision for each genuine conflict together with exactly what I would have to give up, and deliver a committed verdict—telling me which commitments I should retain, which I should drop or modify, and why—rather than laying out neutral options and leaving the choice to me.
