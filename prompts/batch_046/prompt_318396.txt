Write a pantoum of eight quatrains on Kierkegaard's analysis of the press and the public, framing the repetition structurally rather than thematically: let lines two and four of each stanza migrate into lines one and three of the next, so that every claim is inherited without being owned. In the opening stanza, a speaker declares a specific conviction in the first person; as the lines migrate, have the pronoun shift from "I" to "one" to "they" to "it," enacting the dissolution of the single individual into the phantom public. Include the idea—not as a named citation but as a lived conceptual tension—that for Kierkegaard the press does not spread lies so much as it abolishes the category of lying by making every statement an interchangeable opinion that belongs to no one and therefore commits no one. Have the poem's turn occur at stanza five, where the repetition suddenly reveals that the speaker can no longer locate the original conviction in any voice, only in the circulating form; let the final stanza return to the opening lines but with all personal pronouns replaced by passive constructions. The poem should be precise enough about Kierkegaard's dynamics—reflection without passion, envy as the social glue of the age, the quantitative crowd that is untruth—that a specialist recognizes the architecture, yet composed as a standalone poem that does not rely on footnotes or external references.
