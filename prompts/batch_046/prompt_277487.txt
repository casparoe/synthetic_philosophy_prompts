The question concerns Samuel Scheffler’s “afterlife conjecture” (from his 2013 book *Death and the Afterlife*): the value we place on many present projects—scientific, artistic, political—depends partly on an implicit expectation that human life will continue after our individual deaths, supplying a collective “afterlife” for our efforts. Some philosophers have extended this into what we can call the intergenerational projective continuity thesis: human extinction is catastrophic not mainly because of the deaths it entails, but because it severs a trans-temporal dependency chain that makes long-term projects intelligible. Is this extension faithful to Scheffler’s original argument, or does it illegitimately relocate the harm from living individuals to an abstract axiological structure? Evaluate the extension, paying particular attention to whether it forces one into an impersonal moral ontology that Scheffler avoids.

Student answer:

Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture says that we care about humanity continuing after we die because our projects—like curing disease or building institutions—only make sense if there will be people to carry them on. This is a collective afterlife, not a personal one. If we apply this to extinction, then the end of humanity would make all our current efforts pointless. So extinction is bad mainly because it frustrates the projects of people who are alive right now.

Some people take this further and say extinction is bad in a purely structural way: it destroys the framework that gives value to human activity, regardless of whether anyone is around to feel the loss. I think Scheffler actually holds this stronger view, because he writes that the loss is not just personal disappointment but the collapse of the conditions that make our actions meaningful. So the harm is axiological, not personal.

But if the harm is purely structural, then no particular individual is wronged by extinction. This means there is no duty of justice to prevent it—only a kind of aesthetic preference for continuity. Therefore, on Scheffler’s account, preventing extinction is not a moral obligation, since morality requires a specific subject who is harmed. The badness is real, but it doesn’t generate duties.
