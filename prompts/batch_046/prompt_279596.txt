I had a long and rather exhausting argument last night with a close friend about whether anyone can genuinely deserve praise or blame. He attempted to summarise Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I should like to know whether he has captured it fairly or slipped into the familiar determinist misreading. Roughly, this is what he claimed.

Strawson's central thesis, according to my friend, is that true moral responsibility is impossible because every action ultimately traces back to causes outside the agent—heredity, environment, prior mental states—none of which the agent herself authored. Consequently, no one can be the causa sui of her conduct. My friend said that Strawson treats this primarily as a deterministic argument: if determinism holds, responsibility is impossible; and if indeterminism holds, random quantum fluctuations in the brain are equally outside the agent's control, so they do not restore responsibility either. From this he concluded that Strawson is essentially an incompatibilist about free will, and that the argument entails we ought to abandon all practices of moral evaluation—including grading students for originality, awarding artistic prizes, or praising character—since in every case the outcome depends on factors the individual never chose.

Is this a fair rendering? Please examine it closely: separate what accurately reflects Strawson's reasoning from what is a standard popular simplification, and identify anything that is genuinely mistaken or that overstates the conclusion. I am particularly uncertain whether the argument actually presupposes determinism, or whether it is intended to apply irrespective of whether the world is deterministic or indeterministic. I am also unsure whether Strawson really thinks every form of evaluation must be jettisoned, or whether he permits a distinction between being genuinely deserving—what he might call true moral responsibility—and being held answerable for practical or social reasons. Furthermore, my friend insisted that the argument extends with equal force to collective entities such as corporations or nations, on the grounds that they too are not self-created. Is that a legitimate application of the premise, or does it confuse individual agency with institutional causation?

Please comment on the accuracy of the summary, on whether those extensions are philosophically warranted, and on whether there remains any coherent way to preserve the intuition that people can earn their achievements without committing oneself to the view that they must have created themselves ex nihilo.
