At a recent meeting of our philosophy-of-decision-theory reading circle, two regulars—Marcus and Elena—got stuck in a disagreement about evidential cooperation in large worlds that I found sharper than the usual stalemate. Marcus was defending the standard large-world argument: if there are enough agents with reasoning sufficiently like mine across the multiverse or across causally disconnected regions, then my choosing to cooperate with them is evidentially correlated with their choosing to cooperate with me, even without causal interaction. He said that as long as the correlation is positive, the expected utility of cooperation can exceed that of defection, and this is not a mistake but a feature of rational decision-making under indexical uncertainty.

Elena pushed back on what she called the hidden homogeneity assumption in Marcus’s reasoning. She argued that evidential cooperation collapses once you allow for even modest divergence in priors—specifically, different beliefs about the measure of agent-types or about the distribution of reasoning architectures across world-space. If my prior assigns low weight to worlds filled with agents who share my values, then my decision to cooperate tells me very little about what those specific others will do; the evidential link is too weak to justify the utility transfer Marcus wants. She added that Marcus was smuggling in a kind of universal altruism and dressing it up as evidential reasoning.

Marcus replied that Elena was setting the bar for correlation impossibly high. He said evidential reasoning never requires perfect identity of priors—only that my action is a positive indicator of similar actions elsewhere. Even if our priors differ, we both update on our own reasoning processes, and that shared update mechanism preserves enough correlation to make cooperation rational for both of us, provided our utility functions overlap. He accused her of confusing evidential correlation with causal mechanism: we do not need to causally influence each other for the correlation to do work in our expected-utility calculations.

Elena came back with a sharper point. She said that if the correlation is mediated entirely through self-knowledge—if I am really just learning about my own type—then Marcus has not shown cooperation with distinct others at all. He has shown only that being cooperative is good for agents like me, which is trivial. The large-world framing promises gains from mutual cooperation across genuinely separate agents, but if the evidential link runs only through shared reasoning traits, then treating every similar agent as an independent beneficiary in the utility sum risks double-counting the same evidential update. She asked Marcus whether he was adjusting his utility weights by the correlation coefficient, or whether he was implicitly treating the correlation as if it were causal.

Marcus admitted the coefficient matters, but insisted that in a properly formalised decision framework—say, one that conditions on one’s decision algorithm rather than on one’s specific token—this double-counting is avoided. The correlation determines how much weight I should place on others’ outcomes, not whether I place any. Elena said that sounds correct in the abstract, but that in practice every large-world argument she has seen assumes a uniform distribution over agent-types that no one actually holds, and that without that uniform prior the coefficient shrinks to near zero.

They asked me to referee, but I genuinely could not tell whether Elena had identified a fatal flaw in the evidential-cooperation programme or whether Marcus was right that she was demanding causal interaction in disguise. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right at each specific step—particularly about whether prior divergence breaks the correlation, whether self-knowledge collapses the argument to altruism, and whether the coefficient adjustment resolves the double-counting worry? Please take a definite position on who is more right overall and on what the argument actually shows about the scope of evidential cooperation, rather than laying out both sides and leaving me to choose. I would also appreciate it if you noted where your own reasoning relies on contested assumptions about indexical reasoning or the formalisation of large-world decision problems.
