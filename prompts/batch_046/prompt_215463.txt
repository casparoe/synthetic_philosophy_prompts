I don't have any formal philosophy background; I've just been thinking through the argument that having a child is wrong because any suffering they might experience is a real harm, whereas any pleasure they miss by never being born isn't a harm at all. People call this an asymmetry, though I'm not sure I'm using the term exactly as scholars do. Where I get stuck is when I try to apply the logic beyond the obvious cases — a clearly miserable life versus a clearly happy one.

Please build a taxonomy tree that sorts out the actual positions people hold on this question, not by the political or philosophical labels they adopt, but by the answers they give to concrete scenarios. Start at the root with the claim: is the absence of suffering genuinely good even when there is no actual subject to enjoy that absence?

From there, branch on these three splits, each phrased as a commitment or case rather than as technical jargon:

First, imagine a life that is mostly good but contains one severe, unavoidable harm — say, a chronic condition that makes life harder but doesn't make it not worth living overall. Does the asymmetry treat that single harm as decisive because harm is weighted more heavily than pleasure is neutral, or does the neutral status of pleasure allow such a net-positive life to remain permissible?

Second, does the asymmetry apply only to beings who actually come to exist, or does it also apply to potential beings — in the sense that failing to create a happy person is not a loss, but failing to create a suffering person counts as a gain?

Third, if someone accepts the root claim, does it necessarily generate an obligation never to reproduce, or only a permission not to reproduce? And does that answer change in ambiguous cases — for instance, an artificial or only partially conscious being — where it is unclear who, if anyone, is the proper subject of the harm?

Where a combination is missing, briefly diagnose whether it looks incoherent given the root claim, or merely unexplored.
