I'm writing a feature on philosophy assessment for a science-and-ideas magazine, and a philosophy-of-mind lecturer has shared a design problem she's facing for her upper-level seminar's 90-minute final exam. The students—advanced undergraduates and first-year MA candidates—have already worked through standard texts on collective cognition, functionalism, and integrated information theory, but she wants a question they cannot answer by reciting summaries. She proposes using the ant-colony scenario familiar from discussions of the 'Ant Fugue'—an emergent collective, sometimes referred to as Aunt Hillary, whose members are simple yet whose aggregate behavior looks genuinely goal-directed and representational. The crux she wants to isolate is not the usual emergentist-versus-functionalist debate, but a more specific tension: if one grants, on functionalist grounds, that the colony tracks environmental states and regulates behavior in ways that warrant attributing beliefs to it, is one thereby forced to concede that the colony also possesses phenomenal consciousness under integrated information theory? Or can a defender of the belief-attribution consistently block that entailment by invoking what she calls a 'disintegration condition'—the idea that macro-level cognitive integration requires physical continuity of substrate, not merely organizational coherence—thereby preserving functional belief without phenomenal experience? She asks me to draft the exam prompt, a model answer that makes the required conceptual moves explicit, and a marking scheme of five points (4 marks each, 20 total) with one-line descriptions of how candidates usually fail on each. Could you produce that for me?
