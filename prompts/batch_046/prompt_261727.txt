I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through philosophy of science and rational choice theory with the same formal rigor I once applied to statistical mechanics. Recently, in a reading group composed mostly of former scientists, we have been debating whether the Kelly criterion—familiar to me from information-theoretic treatments of sequential betting—should be treated as a universal norm for practical reasoning rather than merely for liquid portfolio allocation. One participant advanced an argument that extends geometric rationality to indivisible, long-term life commitments and, to my eye, derives an implausible prohibition on every high-risk, high-reward vocation. I have reconstructed the reasoning below with numbered steps, but I cannot locate the precise misstep without breaking the theoretical unity of the position; I would like you to diagnose which premise or inference is weakest, explain why it initially looks defensible, and detail exactly what one must concede—conceptually and in terms of explanatory scope—if that link is abandoned. Please separate what is mathematically settled about Kelly betting and multiplicative growth from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of rational choice, particularly regarding non-ergodic settings, indivisible commitments, and the extension of betting norms to epistemic or career capital; do not present a disputed philosophical extension as though it were established consensus.

(1) In any sequence of independent, repeated decisions under uncertainty, rational agents should maximize the geometric mean of outcomes, because arithmetic-mean strategies risk ruin and are asymptotically dominated in the long run. (2) This geometric norm applies wherever outcomes compound multiplicatively: financial wealth, accumulated epistemic reliability, and practical success measured over time. (3) Consider the decision to commit to a transformative but highly uncertain long-term project—say, a theoretical research program with a 10% probability of yielding a field-defining result and a 90% probability of producing negligible career advancement. (4) Model this commitment as a Kelly bet: win probability p = 0.10, payoff multiplier b = 100 (in units of career capital), with the staked capital being one's professional reputation and available working years. (5) The Kelly-optimal fraction is f* = (bp − q)/b = (10 − 0.9)/100 ≈ 0.091, meaning rationality permits staking only about 9% of one's available career capital on the venture. (6) Unlike financial capital, however, career commitment is effectively indivisible: one either undertakes the decade-long project or abstains; there is no mechanism to commit exactly 9% of one's life while reserving 91% in safe alternatives. (7) Applying Kelly rationality to an indivisible choice therefore requires discretizing f* to the nearest feasible action—either full commitment (f ≈ 1) or complete abstention (f = 0). (8) Because f* ≈ 0.091 lies far closer to 0 than to 1 under any reasonable metric, the geometrically rational decision is abstention. (9) Generalizing: for any transformative venture with success probability p < 1/(b+1), the Kelly fraction falls below any plausible indivisibility threshold, mandating avoidance regardless of the magnitude of the potential payoff. (10) It follows that geometric rationality systematically prohibits rational agents from undertaking scientific research, artistic careers, or entrepreneurial ventures whose success odds fall below that threshold. (11) Yet the historical record shows that progress depends precisely on rational agents making such commitments; therefore, treating Kelly reasoning as universal for practical life yields an absurd result. (12) So either geometric rationality is not a universal norm for practical reasoning, or our representation of career commitment as a Kelly bet is fundamentally mistaken. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
