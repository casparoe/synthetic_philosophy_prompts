Read the breaking of the Fellowship at Amon Hen in Lord of the Rings through the lens of collective intentionality and joint action. I want you to take Gilbert's idea of joint commitment seriously -- the one where being in a group creates obligations that don't just reduce to individual preferences -- and also Bratman's shared cooperative activity, with all that meshing-subplans stuff. The scene is weird: Boromir tries to grab the Ring, Frodo decides he's going alone, and then Aragorn, Legolas and Gimli split off to chase Merry and Pippin without any explicit group vote. Is there still a 'we' that decides to disband? Or is dissolution just the absence of shared intention?

Here's the twist I want you to think about: what if there's such a thing as a 'negative joint commitment' -- a collective intention not to act together anymore. Does the Fellowship actually form one, or is it just that individual intentions stop meshing? And where does Sam fit? He doesn't seem part of any collective decision, he just decides to come with Frodo. Does that mean joint action can be overridden by unilateral attachment?

Don't just apply the theories; let the scene push back on them. Maybe Tolkien is showing that fellowship isn't just shared purpose but shared vulnerability, and the philosophical accounts miss the emotional residue when the 'we' breaks. End by saying whether the dissolution vindicates one view over the other, or if it reveals a gap in both -- specifcally around how groups end, not just how they form.
