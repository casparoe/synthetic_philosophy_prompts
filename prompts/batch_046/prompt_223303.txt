Draft the joint statement that a committed neuro-epiphenomenalist—who takes classic readiness-potential data to show that voluntary movement is initiated unconsciously and that conscious will is an epiphenomenal post-hoc construction undermining libertarian free will—and a committed hierarchical-compatibilist critic—who accepts the same neurophysiological measurements but holds that they capture only pre-conscious motor preparation rather than the higher-order decision to act, leaving room for a causally efficacious conscious veto or authorization—could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration. Restrict your attention to the interpretation of these experiments and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write as though for an audience that knows the general findings but needs no external references. Present the entire response in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, boldface, numbered lists, tables, or other structural formatting; organize it into flowing paragraphs like a formal letter or long-form essay.

In the opening part, state in neutral language the claims both sides can genuinely endorse: the temporal sequence observed in the original paradigm in which a readiness potential precedes both the subjective report of an urge to move and the movement itself; the fact that subjects can sometimes abort a prepared action; the limitations of the task design, particularly its reliance on simple wrist flexion with no meaningful choice between alternatives; and the shared agreement about what remains unexplained—specifically, whether the neural activity preceding awareness reflects the decision itself or merely a sub-personal preparatory bias. Then articulate the residual disagreements with precision, phrasing the epiphenomenalist position in terms that camp would accept—namely, that the readiness potential is the true causal origin of the action and that conscious awareness plays no directional role in initiating or shaping the movement—and phrasing the compatibilist position in terms that camp would accept—namely, that the readiness potential is a necessary but insufficient preparatory condition, that the conscious veto or authorization operates at a different hierarchical level, and that the subjective report of deciding tracks the authorization event rather than the motor onset. Identify explicitly if any portion of their dispute is merely verbal, for instance whether the word decision is being used for the sub-personal motor program or for the personal-level endorsement of it, and explain why that ambiguity does not by itself resolve the substantive disagreement.

Next, lay out the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would actually move one or both parties. Treat at least three: first, whether the readiness potential amplitude or timing differs between actions that are eventually executed and actions that are consciously vetoed; second, whether subjective reports of authorship correlate with the initiation latency or with the success of a veto; and third, whether a modified experimental protocol can dissociate motor preparation from personal-level choice. For each crux, describe a concrete case or hypothetical experiment that both sides agree would bear on it. Specifically, introduce a masked-readiness-potential veto protocol in which real-time neurofeedback makes subjects aware of an impending readiness-potential onset before they feel any conscious urge, allowing them to attempt an immediate abort; describe exactly what neural and behavioral measurements would be taken and what pattern of results both camps agree would be relevant. Then record each side's explicit prediction: the epiphenomenalist should predict that veto success will not correlate with subjective authorship, that readiness-potential amplitude will remain unchanged regardless of veto outcome, and that any feeling of control is reconstructed after the fact; the compatibilist should predict that successful vetoes will show a distinct post-readiness-potential neural signature such as enhanced prefrontal or parietal activity, that subjective authorship ratings will track veto success rather than initiation timing, and that the masked protocol will reveal an authorization window separate from the motor initiation window. Conclude by stating whether both sides agree that resolving these predictions in either direction would change their view, and if not, explain what further evidence each considers decisive. Throughout, inject and maintain the conceptual distinction between initiation latency—the interval from neural onset to movement—and the authorization window—the interval during which a higher-order evaluation can permit or block the prepared action—so that the document does not collapse these into a single temporal measure.
