my roommate and his sister got in a fight at brunch about prison reform and asked me and jared to referee. neither of us do philosophy but we tried to follow.

maya said punishment only makes sense if the offender actually deserves it, and the sentence should match the wrongness of the act, not whether locking them up scares other people straight. she said if you justify prison by saying it deters crime, you're using the offender like a tool to teach everyone else a lesson, which is basically the kantian thing about treating people as ends.

leo (her coworker) said that's a nice slogan but in the real world you can't seperate desert from effects. he said if judges only look at desert you get wild inconsistencies -- like two people do the exact same robbery but one gets ten years and one gets two because the first judge was having a bad morning. he said at least consequentialism tries to keep outcomes fair by looking at actual social effects.

maya came back with the framing problem: if locking up an innocent person would prevent ten murders, consequentialism has to say do it, and that's not justice it's engineering. leo said no serious consequentialist advocates framing, because the long-term damage to trust in courts is part of the calculus. but then he said something i hadn't heard named before -- he called it "institutional consequentialism" -- where the point of punishment isn't fixing this one offender but maintaining the law as a coordination game. if people believe rules are applied consistently they cooperate more.

maya said that sounds like you're sneaking desert in through the back door, since "consistent with the rules" is just another way of saying "match the crime to the punishment." she also brought up moral luck: two drunk drivers are equally reckless, but one hits a kid and one doesn't. retributivists have to punish them the same since culpability is identical, but consequentialists will lock the killer up longer because the harm is bigger. she said that actually shows retributivism is more principled, not less.

leo replied that harm isn't just an accident, it's part of what makes the act wrong in the first place -- so consequentialism isn't being inconsistent, it's being honest about what we're reacting to. then he asked maya: if desert is all that matters, why do we care about rehabilitating people? if someone serves their full time and comes out genuinely changed, do we still owe them punishment? she said no, if they've changed then maybe desert is satisfied differently, but that's a seperate issue about mercy, not about justification.

they went back and forth and then turned to us. can you go through thier exchange point by point and say who's right about each move? also what are they both missing? i feel like maya never really answered leo's point about judicial inconsistency, and leo never explained why "institutional consequentialism" isn't just rule-utilitarianism with a fancier name. please adjudicate who wins where, and what the whole thing overlooks.
