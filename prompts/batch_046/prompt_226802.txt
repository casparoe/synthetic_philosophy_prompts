I spent my career as a geophysicist reconstructing plate-tectonic histories—explanations that are irreducibly particular, since they account for one specific sequence of continental configurations, yet they depend entirely on general rheological and thermodynamic laws applied to boundary conditions that could have been otherwise. Since retiring I have turned to philosophy of history, and I find myself puzzled by a long stalemate. On one side, defenders of the covering-law model maintain that explaining an event such as the collapse of the Roman Republic requires, at bottom, the subsumption of particular occurrences under general empirical laws, even if practicing historians leave those laws implicit. On the other side, narrative theorists hold that historical explanation is constituted by tracing the intelligible connections among particular actions and circumstances in a unified story, and that seeking hidden laws is not merely impractical but conceptually misguided. Over the past several decades both camps have made conciliatory adjustments—covering-law proponents now speak of explanatory sketches, ceteris paribus clauses, and mechanism schemas; narrative proponents now distinguish between chronicle and genuinely explanatory narrative—yet the literature continues to orbit the same dichotomy without clear resolution. From my scientific background this feels like a category mistake: in tectonic reconstruction we do not ask whether our account is a law or a story, but whether the reconstructed trajectory is counterfactually robust given the known dynamics and initial conditions. This leads me to hypothesize that the philosophical disagreement is largely verbal, driven by competing success conditions—predictive subsumption versus retrospective intelligibility—rather than by a genuine conflict about what historians actually do. At the same time, I wonder whether there is a hidden substantive question obscured by this framing: whether historical disciplines in fact employ what might be called singular explanatory structures, in which general mechanisms are combined with path-dependent boundary conditions to produce explanations of unique trajectories that fit neither the covering-law nor the pure narrative model. If so, the debate may be stalled not because it is ill-posed, but because both sides are answering different questions under the single name historical explanation. What is your diagnosis? Is the impasse verbal, substantive, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about the nature of scientific explanation? Does a hidden empirical question about actual historiographical practice do the work, or is the concept of explanation itself being contested? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—an empirical study of working historians, a formal analysis of singular explanation, a reconceptualization of the law-narrative distinction, or should we expect no movement at all?
