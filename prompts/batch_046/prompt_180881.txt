I am working through John Maynard Keynes’s A Treatise on Probability (1921) for a seminar on the philosophy of economics, and I have become stuck on a tension in the preface. There, Keynes writes: “There is much here, therefore, which is novel, and, being novel, unsifted, inaccurate, or deficient. I propound my systematic conception of this subject for criticism and enlargement at the hand of others, doubtful whether I myself am likely to get much further, by waiting longer, with a work, which, beginning as a Fellowship Dissertation, and interrupted by the war, has already extended over many years.” On the face of it, he is presenting a systematic logical theory of probability—one in which probabilities are objective relations between propositions and evidence, not degrees of psychological belief—while simultaneously confessing that the very conception he propounds is likely inaccurate or deficient. I would like help understanding how these two stances are compatible. Does Keynes intend the admission of deficiency to apply only to the formal machinery he develops in the later parts of the book, or does it also extend to the foundational claim, made in Part I, that probabilities can be rationally ordered even when they resist numerical measurement? Furthermore, I have encountered a passing reference to Keynes’s distinction between the “weight” of an argument and its “probability,” but I do not see clearly how that distinction is supposed to resolve the tension: if the weight of evidence can increase without altering the numerical probability assigned to a hypothesis, does that mean the logical relation itself is only partially determined by the available evidence, leaving room for the kind of unsifted incompleteness he admits? Any clarification of how the preface’s humility relates to the structure of his logical theory—and especially to the weight/probability distinction—would be greatly appreciated.
