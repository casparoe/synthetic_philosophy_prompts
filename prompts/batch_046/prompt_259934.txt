I'm an editor for a journal that publishes "behavioral architecture" proposals — designs for apps or public spaces based on empirical claims about cognition. Reviewers keep fighting over submissions that jump from "studies show X about human decision-making" to "therefore we ought to build Y," and they can't agree whether that's the naturalistic fallacy, the is-ought gap, or just applied science. Write a reusable 10-step review procedure my board can run in 15 minutes: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "reject for logical error," and where it has to hand the call to human judgment because it's values, not logic. Cover both Moore-style open-question worries and Hume-style derivation blocks, but don't ask me to cite specific texts — just apply the concepts. Then run it on three proposals: one using fMRI altruism data to justify mandatory donation framing, one deriving accessibility standards from evolutionary spatial-cognition studies, and one that passes technically but will make the reviewers furious for political reasons. If the whole idea of seperating "is" from "ought" in design contexts is actually incoherent, or if this procedure can't really be built, say so plainly — don't fake a checklist just to be helpful. No bibliographies, please; my reviewers need something they can actually use.
