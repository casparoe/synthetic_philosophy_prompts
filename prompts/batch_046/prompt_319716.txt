hey, I run a small grad workshop on historical thinking—like 10 people, two hours, mix of history and philosophy students. I want an activity that gets them to actually *feel* the Whig pull, not just name it. I'm framing it with this idea I made up called "reverse-engineered anachronism"—basically writing the past as if it were a blueprint for one specific present, running backward from an outcome you already take for granted.

The setup: split them into three groups. Each group recieves a packet of primary-source fragments—realistic ones from 1720, 1840, and 1890. They also get a "target present" card: something like modern climate regulation or universal healthcare. Their job is to write a 200-word narrative arc showing how the fragments inevitbaly led to that present. But here's the trick—half the fragments are actually from dead-end movements or debates that went nowhere near the target, and the target itself was considered laughable or impossible by people in 1890. They don't know which fragments are red herrings.

After 30 mins they present their arcs. Only then do I reveal the true causal map—which fragments were actually connected, which were decoys, and how contingent the outcome really was. Then we debrief.

What I need from you: the exact rules for constructing the packets (how many fragments, how to mix dead ends without being unfair), the debrief structure that maps their specific word choices—things like "mature," "finally," "the seeds of"—onto the historiographical problem, and an honest note on what this can't reproduce (probably the emotional or patriotic investment that makes Whig history actually appealing to real historians writing about their own nation). Also predict where it'll go wrong—like one group always constructing a heroic narrative regardless of evidence.

No lecture up front. They shouldn't hear the term "Whig history" until after they've presented. And please don't include a bibliography or reading list—I just want the runnable design with the debrief.
