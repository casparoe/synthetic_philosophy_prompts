As a retired evolutionary biologist who has spent the last three years studying moral psychology and philosophy of science, I am drafting an essay on what cross-cultural differences in folk morality—particularly the divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations in judgments about harm, fairness, loyalty, and purity—actually imply for moral objectivity. Before I finalize the draft, I would welcome rigorous, constructive comments on the following notes and central line of argument.

My thesis is that philosophers too quickly slide from observed cultural variation in moral intuitions to anti-realist conclusions, because they conflate three distinct grades of objectivity. First, intersubjective convergence: the degree to which different observers, given shared information, reach the same moral verdict, analogous to agreement in repeated physical measurement. Second, institutional validation: the extent to which a moral claim survives structured critical scrutiny within a normative community, somewhat like peer review in science. Third, mind-independent reality: the claim that a moral fact holds regardless of any observer's attitudes or institutional practices, parallel to the existence of a physical constant independent of our instruments. I argue that WEIRD populations show high convergence and strong institutional validation for harm and fairness norms, but weak convergence for loyalty, authority, and purity norms; non-WEIRD populations often invert or flatten this pattern. From this, I conclude that cross-cultural data undermine at most the first two grades—especially the expectation of universal convergence—without touching the third. I further propose a "calibration bias" hypothesis: WEIRD subjects' stronger objectivist self-reports about harm may not reflect deeper metaphysical insight, but rather a habit imported from scientific training, in which disagreement about harm is treated as a correctable measurement error, whereas disagreement about loyalty is treated as a legitimate preference difference. If this is right, then the standard anti-objectivist argument from cultural variation commits a category error, treating failures of intersubjective convergence as evidence against mind-independent reality when the two are logically separable.

Please comment on these notes. I am especially interested in: (1) whether the tripartite distinction among convergence, institutional validation, and mind-independent reality is philosophically coherent or collapses under scrutiny; (2) whether the calibration-bias hypothesis linking scientific training to objectivist intuitions about harm is plausible, or risks being ad hoc; and (3) whether the essay's move—from cultural variation to a limited, graded anti-realism rather than full-blown moral anti-realism—is genuinely interesting or merely evasive.

Please begin with a one-sentence bottom-line verdict. Then provide your full response in no more than 300 words. Keep the tone suitable for feedback on an unfinished philosophical essay.
