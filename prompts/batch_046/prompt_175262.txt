Two people in my study group argued about embedded agency. The first said that if agency is genuinely embedded—say, a chemotactic bacterium navigating by continuous gradient sensing—then there is no real-time boundary between agent and environment: the bacterium’s “decision” and the chemical field’s “instruction” form one coupled dynamics, so attributing navigational error to the bacterium alone is like blaming half a circuit. The second replied that embeddedness relocates, rather than erases, boundaries: the coupled system—bacterium-plus-gradient—still has norms (reaching nutrient-rich zones) and failures (getting stuck in a local minimum), and within it we can distinguish receptor failure from environmental noise. The first countered that this treats embeddedness as “agency plus context,” missing the point that the environment is causally active in the same temporal window; the bacterium’s internal state is not fixed before interaction but constituted by it, so the second view smuggles back a pre-existing agent. The second said this applies to brains too—neural states are constituted by real-time interaction—so if the argument works it undermines all agency, not just embedded cases; what matters is organizational closure, not insulation from interaction. The first responded that allowing closure to include environmental parts makes the boundary pragmatic rather than metaphysical, which may work for engineering but not for genuine error attribution.
