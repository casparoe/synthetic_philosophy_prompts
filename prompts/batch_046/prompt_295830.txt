I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist working on low-temperature spectroscopy, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with the same patience I once applied to noise reduction. I am drafting a short essay on whether Imre Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes can be usefully extended from grand theoretical frameworks to what I will call instrumental research programmes—the local, practice-embedded traditions that govern how a particular apparatus is calibrated, interpreted, and repaired.

In Lakatos's account, a research programme possesses a hard core of commitments protected from direct refutation, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorb anomalies. The programme is progressive when adjustments to the belt generate novel predictions, and degenerating when they merely accommodate known failures post hoc. In my laboratory experience, we faced a structurally analogous situation whenever our Fourier-transform spectrometer produced anomalous line shapes. We could revise the underlying quantum-mechanical interaction Hamiltonian—treating it as part of the hard core—and derive new predictions about isotopic shifts, or we could append ad hoc baseline-correction algorithms to the data-processing pipeline. The latter preserved the measurements but predicted nothing new.

My essay argues two things. First, that in experimental practice the boundary between hard core and protective belt is more porous than Lakatos suggests, because instruments are theory-laden: altering a calibration constant may feel auxiliary, yet if that constant is tied to a fundamental value such as the Rydberg constant, the adjustment touches the core. Second, I propose that Lakatos's criterion of novel prediction needs refinement for instrumental contexts, since a repaired spectrometer often produces the same class of measurements with greater reliability rather than predicting a genuinely unexpected phenomenon. I term this reproductive progress and contrast it with predictive progress.

Before I finalize the draft, I would welcome your comments on three specific points. First, have I misrepresented Lakatos's own characterization of the protective belt—particularly his view that the hard core is protected by a conventional decision not to direct the modus tollens against it? Second, is the analogy between theoretical research programmes and instrumental calibration traditions philosophically illuminating, or does it collapse because instruments lack the explicit theoretical structure that Lakatos requires? Third, does the notion of reproductive progress identify something genuinely distinct from a degenerating problem shift, or is it merely a relabeling of successful ad hoc adjustment?

Please respond with the rigor of a peer reviewer who understands both the philosophy of science literature and the practical constraints of experimental physics, but do not ask me to supply a bibliography or verify specific page references. I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together on its own terms.
