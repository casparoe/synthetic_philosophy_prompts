Historical dramatization is usually evaluated by epistemic fidelity—whether speeches, chronology, and material culture match the archive. Less discussed is moral fidelity: the obligation not to attribute to the dead ethical frameworks or categories of self-understanding they could not have held. A biopic might correctly record every public act of a nineteenth-century reformer, yet frame her commitments through exclusively modern intersectional concepts she never articulated and likely could not have formulated. Defenders call this pedagogical translation: audiences need familiar moral vocabulary. But if the dead retain any interest in not being made into ventriloquist dummies for present ideologies, such framing becomes posthumous ventriloquism—an invisible, irreversible reshaping of legacy that footnotes cannot correct. I conclude that moral fidelity should take priority over pedagogical accessibility when the two conflict, because factual errors are correctable, whereas moral framing becomes part of the historical record itself.

Please comment on whether the distinction between epistemic and moral fidelity holds up, whether the priority claim is justified, and whether "posthumous ventriloquism" is a coherent concept. Focus on conceptual clarity and logical structure; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
