I've come to hold that Goodman's grue problem is dissolved not by an appeal to natural kinds or to linguistic convention, but by tying projectibility to interventional stability: a predicate is genuinely projectible when testing for it does not systematically disrupt the very conditions that make it apply, which explains why 'green' survives temporal scrutiny while 'grue' collapses. After a long argument about this, my friend insists that I have only smuggled metaphysical naturalness back in through the vocabulary of causal modeling. What would genuinely count against this interventional-projectibility account? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: concrete cases where interventional stability tracks predicates we still refuse to project; formal results in learning theory that separate projectibility from intervention; and the objection that I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability by letting 'intervention' be defined post hoc to save any verdict. Which of these must a committed defender concede, which can be absorbed by refining the account without loss, and does the risk of such qualification specifically undermine a theory of induction? I would also like to know whether there is any observation about actual scientific practice—say, in taxonomy or in the history of abandoned projectible predicates—that a defender should treat as decisive rather than just inconvenient.
