If Quine was still alive and saw all this talk about whether LLMs count as 'moral patients', how do you think he'd react? He always seemed suspicious of treating values as part of the world's furniture, and he loved showing that ontology depends on which conceptual scheme you pick. So I'm wondering: would he say the whole AI-moral-status debate is just people smuggling ethical commitments into ontology — like saying 'there exist moral patients of type X' when really they're just expressing a preference for treating X kindly? Or would he allow that once you naturalize ethics, you could have a genuine empirical question about which entities have the relevant functional properties?

There's also this niche idea floating around in some recnt papers — I don't think it has a standard name yet — where philosphers compare different 'population policies' for synthetic minds: you can choose to build 10 agents with high welfare or 100 agents with slightly lower welfare, and the choice determines which individuals exist. It's kind of like the non-identity problem but for digital entities that don't exist unless you create them. Given Quine's skepticism about moral values and his view that ontology is relative to theory, do you think he'd dismiss this as pseudo-science dressed up in ethical language, or would he see it as a legitimate extension of decision theory? I'm not asking for a bibliography, just trying to map where his old positions land on this new terrain.
