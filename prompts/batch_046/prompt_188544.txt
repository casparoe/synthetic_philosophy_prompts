Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between defenders of the Principal Principle—who hold that rational credence in an event ought to align with the known objective chance of that event whenever the agent lacks inadmissible evidence—and causal-evidential non-deferentialists—who hold that knowledge of an objective chance is at most one evidential input among others, and that rational belief should instead be governed by causal or evidential decision-theoretic considerations that may systematically diverge from that chance. The collaboration should assume familiarity with the standard formulation of the Principal Principle but should center on nested or hierarchical chance structures: specifically, cases in which an agent knows both a first-order objective chance distribution over outcomes and a second-order distribution over which first-order chance law is operative, where the mechanism generating the second-order distribution is causally isolated from the first-order outcomes themselves.

Structure the statement as follows.

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. These should cover: (a) the formal statement of the Principal Principle under discussion; (b) the distinction between objective chance and subjective credence; (c) the conditions under which both sides agree that evidence is admissible or inadmissible; (d) the existence of hierarchical or second-order chance models in physical and social-scientific contexts; (e) clarification that the normative dispute concerns whether objective chance constrains rational belief, not whether objective chance exists metaphysically.

2. A disagreements table with exactly two columns—one for the defenders, one for the non-deferentialists. Each column should contain: (i) the precise formulation of that side’s position, phrased in terms that side would accept for its own view; (ii) the specific divergence regarding nested structures, including whether second-order knowledge about the chance law undermines first-order deference and whether deference applies to the first-order law, the second-order distribution, or neither; (iii) identification of any part of the dispute that is purely verbal rather than substantive, with an explanation of why it is verbal.

3. A cruxes section listing at least three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, provide: (a) the precise sub-question; (b) a concrete case, argument, or decision model that both sides agree bears on it—for example, a scenario involving a causally independent meta-randomizer that selects between two first-order chance laws, with the agent knowing both the selection probabilities and the outcome distributions under each law; (c) the defenders’ predicted resolution; (d) the non-deferentialists’ predicted resolution.

In drafting, avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not use quotation marks for any real text; paraphrase all formulations. The document should be written in formal, polished academic prose, with neither side’s formulation rendered vacuous or slanted, and with the residual disagreement made exact.
