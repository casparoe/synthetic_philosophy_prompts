Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge. On one side are closure-transmissionists: they hold that perceptual knowledge of ordinary objects—for example, that a specific table is present—can, via closure under known entailment, legitimately generate knowledge that localized skeptical hypotheses, such as being deceived by an undetectable simulation of that exact table, are false, without circular reliance on the very belief being defended. On the other side are bootstrapping-sensitive critics: they hold that applying closure in this direction either begs the question against the skeptic or implicitly depends on a warrant for the original perceptual belief that is itself undermined in the skeptical scenario being addressed.

The document should contain the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—about the logical structure of skeptical arguments, about what closure principles actually entail, about the distinction between possessing knowledge and possessing independent warrant, and about whether any non-circular route exists from perceptual evidence to the denial of a localized skeptical hypothesis. Formulate these precisely; avoid watering them down to platitudes both sides could sign only by evacuating them of content.

2. The exact disagreements, each phrased in wording that the respective camp would accept as an accurate statement of its own position. Explicitly separate substantive disagreements from merely verbal ones, and indicate which disagreements fall into each category.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Key cruxes should include: whether closure applies across modal or counterfactual boundaries (from actual-world knowledge to the denial of a nearby possible deception); whether the original perceptual warrant survives the hypothetical undermining introduced by the skeptical scenario; and whether there exists an independent, non-bootstrapped source of warrant for the general reliability of the perceptual faculty in question.

4. For each crux, present one concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction or assessment recorded in advance. One case must be the calibration-loop scenario: an observer uses an instrument reading R to establish that the instrument was properly calibrated, then invokes the calibration record to defend R against a localized malfunction hypothesis. Both sides should state whether this scenario is genuinely analogous to the perceptual bootstrapping case, what the analogy reveals if it holds, and whether it supports or undermines their own view.

5. A brief concluding section stating whether any portion of the dispute dissolves once verbal disagreements are removed, and whether there remains a shared standard of evidence—empirical, conceptual, or methodological—that could in principle resolve the residual disagreement.

Write in American English, in a formal and polished register. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure named theories or specific published works that a well-trained but small model might not reliably recall; you may present the calibration-loop scenario as a hypothetical thought experiment, but do not attribute it to any real author or publication.
