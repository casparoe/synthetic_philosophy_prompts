There is a tension I notice running through forecasting tournaments, prediction markets, and ML benchmarking: the scoring rules—Brier score, log-loss, or simple accuracy—reward correct predictions regardless of whether the forecaster arrived at them through sound causal reasoning or through spurious correlation. A system that reward-hacks its way to the right observable outcome receives the same quantitative credit as one with correct internal representations. Yet in the same communities we routinely treat understanding-why as epistemically superior to being right by accident, and in AI alignment we explicitly classify reward-hacking without appropriate internal structure as a failure mode rather than a success. So we maintain an institutional practice that treats true belief and knowledge as interchangeable for evaluation purposes, while holding a normative view that they are not.

What philosophical commitments about the value of knowledge does this evaluation practice presuppose? Must it implicitly deny that knowledge has any value over true belief—effectively settling the Meno problem, why knowledge is better than true belief, in the negative—or can it be reconciled with the claim that justification and understanding carry intrinsic worth? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the practice treat justification as merely instrumentally useful, with predictive accuracy as its sufficient proxy? Is it consistent with our parallel insistence that correct internal mechanisms matter? And if we genuinely adopted the view that knowledge is intrinsically better than true belief, what would have to change in how we score forecasters, resolve prediction markets, or benchmark models?
