You are delivering the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—at a workshop titled "Governance After Impossibility," convened jointly by a computer-science department and a municipal housing-rights coalition. The audience is split evenly: half are researchers in algorithmic fairness who understand the formal results, and half are organizers, lawyers, and tenants' advocates who have fought automated screening tools but do not work with equations. The workshop proceeds on the premise that the classic impossibility result—no predictive model can simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups and equal false-positive and false-negative rates across protected categories unless base rates or predictions align—is not a puzzle awaiting solution but a permanent structural feature of risk-scoring. Your remarks must let this constraint do real rhetorical work: you may not promise that the workshop will discover a fair algorithm, you may not frame the tension as a temporary engineering obstacle, and you may not ask the audience to balance accuracy and equity as if goodwill could dissolve the mathematical tradeoff. Instead, articulate what the limitation means for governance, using a concrete hypothetical scenario: the Eastside Housing Authority is weighing a predictive model for emergency rental assistance that is calibrated within each census tract but produces a false-positive rate for Black applicants nearly twice that for white applicants. Use this to show that the impossibility result is not an abstraction but an administrative fact that shifts the burden from fixing the model to deciding who bears the cost of unavoidable errors. You should also note that adding a further constraint—temporal stability of individual risk scores across quarterly updates—contracts the feasible region further, making approximate satisfaction of all conditions impossible without strong, usually false, distributional assumptions. Address both halves of the room without condescension: the researchers should hear that their theorems carry institutional consequences, and the advocates should hear that mathematics does not absolve anyone of choice but forces the choice into the open. End not with a solution but with a shared question that respects the constraint. Write the speech.
