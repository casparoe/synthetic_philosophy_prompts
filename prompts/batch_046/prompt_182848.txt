I've been preparing remarks for an advanced seminar in philosophy of language, and I keep circling back to a tension in Kripke's framework that standard examples don't quite expose. The familiar cases—proper names, natural kind terms like water or gold, and indexicals—are well mapped, but I want to press on a less discussed category: functional mental-kind terms that denote specific cognitive or phenomenal roles, such as belief-fixation or phenomenal pain.

Please argue for the following position. These functional kind terms should indeed be treated as rigid designators, because they are introduced via direct ostension to an internal role rather than via a descriptive cluster, and they are meant to pick out the very same abstract functional structure in every possible world where it is realized. However, treating them as rigid in this way actually undermines, rather than supports, the standard Kripkean thesis that corresponding identity statements are necessary a posteriori. Specifically, defend the view that once a speaker grasps the functional specification of phenomenal pain—say, its distinctive causal and qualitative profile—the statement identifying that role with a particular neural realization is still empirical, but the statement identifying the role with itself across worlds becomes transparent and a priori in a way that blurs the rigid/non-rigid boundary. Address the objection that such terms are merely non-rigid descriptions satisfied by whatever physical substrate happens to realize them, and explain why you think this objection confuses realization conditions with reference-fixing conditions. Keep the reasoning self-contained and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
