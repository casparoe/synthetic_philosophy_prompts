A summary of Black's spheres argument that I read in a popular metaphysics article states the following. Black asks us to imagine a universe containing only two iron spheres of identical intrinsic properties—same mass, size, and composition—separated by a fixed distance in otherwise empty space. Since every non-relational property is shared, the only apparent difference is spatial position. If Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles holds—namely, that any two objects sharing all properties are one and the same—then there cannot genuinely be two spheres. Thus Black offers a direct counterexample to the principle. The standard reply appeals to relational differences, such as one sphere being two miles to the left of the other, but this reply is circular: it uses the existence of two spheres to ground the very relation that is supposed to distinguish them, so it cannot explain why there are two rather than one. From this, the article concludes that the principle is false, and it adds that quantum mechanics, which permits particles with identical quantum states, essentially confirms Black's result for the physical world.

I suspect this moves too quickly from thought experiment to refutation, but I lack the technical background to locate the missteps. Is the summary fair? Specifically: does it correctly present Black's aim, or does it conflate a challenge to one formulation of the principle with a general refutation? Is the charge of circularity against relational defenses accurate, or does it misrepresent what a defender of Leibniz would say about spatial relations and symmetric spaces? Does the appeal to quantum identical particles legitimately extend Black's classical scenario, or does it confuse spatial location with quantum state? And if we correct the summary's overstatements, does the identity of indiscernibles survive in some qualified form, or is it genuinely undermined?
