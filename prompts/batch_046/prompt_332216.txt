I'm drafting a six-month fellowship proposal for an independent philosophical study on the architecture of Nietzsche's well-known essay on history's uses and disadvantages for life, part of his Untimely Meditations series. The received view treats the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes as three discrete life-serving functions: the monumental inspires action through exemplary pasts, the antiquarian preserves continuity through conservation, and the critical liberates present life by judging and dissolving burdensome inheritances. But I think this taxonomy hides a structural instability that the literature has not fully resolved. The critical mode is defined by destructive judgment against the past, yet to apply that judgment it must first recover and delimit the very past it targets—a task that looks identical to the antiquarian mode's preservation. Conversely, the antiquarian mode cannot preserve everything; it must select what is worthy of conservation, and that selection criterion seems to require exactly the kind of present-oriented evaluation that Nietzsche assigns exclusively to the critical mode. So the critical mode appears to be doing hidden work inside the antiquarian one.

My proposal asks a precise conceptual question: is there a coherent fourth mode—something like a diagnostic or curatorial history—that performs the necessary selection and recovery of the past without the destructive judgment characteristic of criticism? Or does the overlap mean that Nietzsche's tripartite division collapses into a dyad, with preservation and judgment inseparable? Existing functionalist readings don't settle this because they treat the modes as psychological dispositions rather than as conceptual roles with logical dependencies. More recent pragmatic reconstructions acknowledge that historians mix these stances in practice, but they don't address whether the mixture is conceptually necessary or merely contingent. I want to argue that only by separating selection-without-judgment from destruction-through-judgment can we preserve the distinctiveness of the antiquarian function; if that separation fails, then Nietzsche's framework cannot support the claim that preservation serves life independently of critical dissolution.

The method would be a close conceptual analysis of the criteria Nietzsche uses to distinguish each mode—what makes a history monumental rather than antiquarian, and whether the "need of life" that drives each mode can be specified without circular reference to the others—followed by a constructive argument testing whether selection-without-destruction is internally coherent. The deliverable would be a 40-page essay presenting either a reconstructed four-part taxonomy or a demonstration that the taxonomy necessarily collapses, with explicit failure conditions: for instance, if any act of historical preservation necessarily involves evaluating the past's present value, then the fourth mode is impossible and the critical-antiquarian boundary is indeed porous. I will not include a bibliography or citation list; I plan to add scholarly references after the conceptual framework is fixed. The scope is realistic for six months of part-time work: months one and two for the conceptual mapping, month three for the constructive argument, and the remaining time for revision and framing the failure conditions precisely.
