I'm currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on moral responsibility, where we have been examining P.F. Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and related responses—as a basis for practices of holding one another responsible, rather than treating these practices as dependent on prior metaphysical conclusions about free will. As I try to organize my own judgments, I find that my intuitions about the fittingness of these attitudes do not line up neatly with either a purely causal account or a simple agent-centered one, and I want to know whether they can be captured by a unified principle.

Here are the verdicts I keep returning to. When a stranger deliberately insults me at a gathering, resentment feels entirely appropriate; when the same insult occurs because the speaker is in the grip of an uncontrollable dissociative episode, resentment seems misplaced, though pity may remain fitting. I also feel a diffuse indignation toward a corporation whose hiring algorithm systematically excludes a group, despite the absence of any individual employee who intended that outcome; yet I feel no comparable indignation toward a hurricane that destroys a town, even though the harm is comparable and there is similarly no individual intent behind it. Gratitude strikes me as appropriate toward a stranger who holds a door open for me, but not toward an automated door that opens with perfect timing, even though the practical result is identical. The case that troubles me most involves second-order effects: if I learn that my resentment toward a friend for a betrayal was itself produced by a pharmacological manipulation I underwent unknowingly—something that hyperactivated my reactive circuitry without altering the friend's behavior—the resentment does not simply vanish; instead, it seems to become detached from its target, leaving a kind of affective residue without a proper object. Finally, I am inclined to think that reactive attitudes can be appropriate toward a domestic animal that behaves with something like deliberate cruelty, provided there is minimal responsiveness to social correction, but not toward a sophisticated language model that produces harmful outputs through statistical optimization alone, even when its outputs are behaviorally indistinguishable from malicious intent.

Is there a single principle governing the fittingness of reactive attitudes that can accommodate all five verdicts? If the principle forces me to abandon one, which should go, and why? Please also say what it predicts for a case I have not considered—specifically, reactive attitudes directed at a bureaucratic collective in which every individual decision is locally defensible but the aggregate result is gravely harmful. In your response, please state explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration about the nature of reactive attitudes or moral agency would lead you to revise the principle, and identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable—particularly with respect to the boundary between structural harm and genuinely agent-directed harm.
