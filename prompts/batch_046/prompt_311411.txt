Having spent three decades in experimental neuroscience before turning seriously to philosophy, I find the standard taxonomy of personal-identity theories—psychological continuity, animalism, narrative identity—unsatisfying, because each seems either to presuppose a substrate it should explain or to dissolve the self into patterns without accounting for the Buddhist anattā constraint that no permanent, unitary essence underlies experience. I want a specification, not a literature review: what must any adequate philosophical theory of personal identity actually deliver if it is to take anattā as a metaphysical constraint rather than a metaphor, while still making sense of diachronic moral accounting, the rationality of grief at personal dissolution, and the reconstructive plasticity of memory that neuroscience confirms?

Write the full desiderata. For each requirement, state the motivating case or phenomenon: (1) the distinctions it must preserve—especially between absence of essence and absence of continuity, and between conventional selfhood and ultimate non-existence; (2) the biological and cognitive-scientific results it cannot contradict—such as the distributed, reconstructive nature of episodic memory and the absence of a central neural integrator; (3) the practical cases it must handle—advanced dementia, split-brain patients, and first-person reports of self-dissolution in deep meditative states—without collapsing them into a single model; (4) the conceptual distinction, often blurred in metaphysical discussions, between phenomenological binding (the momentary integration of experience) and narrative ownership (the long-term attribution of actions and traits), and why a theory must respect both without identifying them; and (5) the conditions under which diachronic moral responsibility remains coherent even when no enduring substrate exists.

Then identify which single desideratum does the most damage to the three dominant contenders—psychological-continuity theories, bodily-continuity theories, and narrative-identity theories—and briefly score each contender against the list. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether that tension is fatal or productive. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating considerations rather than on the scoring.

Finally, state how confident you are in the overall specification you have offered, and single out the specific point—whether a particular distinction, a biological constraint, or the coherence of moral accounting—about which you are least sure and why.
