I am to deliver the opening remarks at a joint workshop convened by our university's department of moral philosophy and the regional ministry for public administration, held in honour of Dr. Helene Bauer's retirement after four decades of drafting regulatory frameworks. The audience is divided evenly: on one side sit comparative ethicists and moral psychologists who will quickly detect if I conflate rule-following with deontological duty, or treat individual conscience as mere preference; on the other side sit career civil servants, auditors, and local officials for whom the tension between protocol and personal judgement is not theoretical but lived, and who will lose patience if the address collapses into taxonomy without institutional weight. The workshop's theme is the comparative folk morality of regulatory obedience — specifically, the German tradition that treats orderly adherence to rule (Regelgehorsam, and its bureaucratic expression Dienst nach Vorschrift) as a form of civic virtue in its own right, set against the American folk tradition that tends to elevate the conscientious dissenter, the whistleblower, or the rule-breaker who answers to an inner moral compass as the paradigmatic moral agent, and the question of which framework has the stronger claim.

Write a ten-minute address in British English that allows this contrast to do real structural work rather than serving as decorative framing. I must welcome both halves of the room without pretending they share a vocabulary; I must acknowledge Dr. Bauer's career without reducing her to an unthinking functionary or, conversely, romanticising her as a rebellious iconoclast; and I must frame the workshop's central purpose in terms that genuinely depend on whether moral authority is located primarily in ordered procedure or in individual judgement. The speech should therefore be shaped by the tension itself: there should be propositions I can endorse only if I lean one way, and others that become available only if I lean the other. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific scholars, and avoid references so specialised that they would be opaque without access to particular texts — the address should stand on its own conceptual feet.
