I defend the view that in multiverse cosmology and string-theoretic physics, the absence of direct empirical access is not a methodological failure but a sign that the discipline has outgrown naive falsifiability: specifically, that what I call "landscape internal coherence"—the constraint of possible vacuum states by consistency conditions analogous to swampland conjectures—can substitute for unique empirical prediction by systematically eliminating rival theoretical structures rather than confirming a single observable world. I want a rigorous assessment of what would count against this. Which observational results, formal proofs, or conceptual arguments would genuinely undermine it rather than merely complicate it? For example, would demonstrating that consistency conditions are too permissive to narrow the landscape constitute a decisive defeat, or could defenders absorb it by refining the conditions? What do proponents of coherence-based confirmation themselves acknowledge as damaging—say, the discovery of a fully consistent rival landscape with identical low-energy physics? Does the view make any prediction that empirically grounded rival approaches do not, or has it been qualified into predicting only that "some consistent vacuum exists," which risks unfalsifiability? And if it is indeed insulated—if every apparent failure can be answered by invoking further internal constraints—is that insulation a virtue consistent with rejecting Popperian demarcation, or does it render the position methodologically empty? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would be merely awkward, and conclude honestly whether this view exposes itself to real risk or has protected itself into sterility.
