As a novelist who has spent years observing the rituals of institutions without belonging to them, I am struck by a bifurcation in how universities govern belief under evidential scarcity. In the biological sciences, a doctoral researcher is required to retract or weaken any claim that exceeds what her data can strictly support; she may not defend a hypothesis merely because it is intellectually fertile or emotionally necessary to her project's continuation, even when the option is genuinely open and consequential. Meanwhile, in the literature department, her counterpart is encouraged—indeed, graded—on her willingness to advance an interpretation of a canonical text that she explicitly concedes is not demonstrable from the words on the page, provided the reading transforms our understanding of the human condition and feels unavoidable given the work's structure. Both scholars face momentous, forced choices about what to believe in conditions of ambiguity, yet the institution punishes one for exercising belief and rewards the other. What ethics of belief does this disciplinary double standard presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a domain-specific application of evidentialist strictures—perhaps holding that beliefs about nature must satisfy Clifford's demands while beliefs about meaning may satisfy James's conditions for a legitimate will to believe? Or is there a deeper consistency, rooted in the kind of risk or the nature of the object believed in, that explains why one practice treats insufficient evidence as a moral failure and the other treats it as a creative virtue? And if the implicit theory were made explicit and applied without exception, which evaluation practices—peer review, dissertation defense, or funding criteria—would have to be rebuilt?
