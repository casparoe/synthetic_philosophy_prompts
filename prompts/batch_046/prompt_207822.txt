I defend the view that classical Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as an indirect, phenomenological consequentialism rather than as virtue ethics: the moral task is not to cultivate character traits for their own sake, but to systematically reduce the cognitive reification of self and other so that dukkha is addressed wherever it appears—including in wild animals—without appealing to aggregate welfare maximization. On this reading, karma functions not as a cosmic scorecard but as a psychological mechanism that binds intentional action to the persistence of reified subjectivity; ethical progress is therefore measured by the attenuation of that binding, not by net suffering reduction. What would genuinely count against this reconstruction? I want the decisive defeaters—conceptual, textual, and phenomenological—ranked from fatal to merely awkward, not just the familiar objections that Buddhist ethics rejects aggregation or that anatta undermines moral agency. Specifically, address whether this view collapses into a quietist mysticism that cannot motivate intervention in wild-animal suffering, whether the textual evidence for karma as a psychological mechanism is strained, and whether the rejection of aggregation renders the view unfalsifiable when facing hard trade-offs (e.g., saving one sentient being at the cost of many). Give a committed assessment in American English: is this reconstruction defensible, where is it most exposed, and what single development—empirical, philosophical, or textual—would most force me to abandon it?
