I defend a strong reading of the position associated with Milton Friedman — that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are not merely tolerable but methodologically required, particularly in macro-level social science — extended in a specific direction. I hold that when a system exhibits dense causal interaction among its variables, deliberately suppressing descriptively accurate but causally minor details is epistemically necessary: only such suppression can isolate the dominant mechanisms and yield reliable aggregate predictions. I further hold that this justifies using these idealised models not merely as predictive heuristics but as direct inputs to normative policy design; for instance, a model of democratic accountability built on fully rational voters is defensible as a structural benchmark whose very unrealism reveals where institutions fail.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure — the specific observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual failures that would force me to abandon or severely qualify it — sorted from decisive to merely awkward. In particular:

- Which empirical findings in economics, political science, or complex-systems research show that descriptively richer microfoundations systematically improve macro-predictive accuracy, especially under conditions of dense interaction?
- Are there documented cases where a model with highly unrealistic core assumptions succeeded in-sample but failed out-of-sample precisely because its suppression of interaction effects produced catastrophic errors?
- What formal results — for example from agent-based modelling, robustness analysis, or sensitivity testing — bear directly on whether simplifying assumptions enhance or degrade predictive stability when interactions are strong?
- Would committed defenders of the F-twist concede that predictive success in one domain does not license direct policy prescription in another? If so, does my extension from prediction to institutional design collapse into a category error?
- Has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by the move that any predictive failure can be attributed to suppressing the wrong variables rather than to suppression itself? If so, is that insulation a methodological virtue or a defect?

Please distinguish, for each objection, whether it is one that advocates of the position would themselves accept as damaging and which they would reject. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, include an assessment of whether the position risks nothing at all, and answer in British English.
