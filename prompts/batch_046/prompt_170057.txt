Take the role of a professional philosopher of science specializing in formal epistemology and the theory of scientific reasoning. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm: what distinguishes a genuinely open framing from a simple restatement of the standard “NFL proves induction impossible” versus “NFL is irrelevant to science” standoff?

Write a short research proposal (suitable for a one-semester term paper, approximately 5,000–6,000 words) on the following problem. No-free-lunch theorems establish that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every inductive learning algorithm performs no better than random guessing. Philosophical responses have typically divided between treating this as a formalization of Hume’s problem and dismissing it as irrelevant because science operates within a severely restricted hypothesis space. Your proposal should investigate a less-explored middle path: whether the restriction of hypothesis space required to escape NFL results is itself a form of “structural bias” that must be presupposed rather than learned, and whether this bias can be justified non-circularly by appealing to internal explanatory norms—such as unification, causal depth, or explanatory coherence—rather than to past predictive success.

State the precise research question: is this structural bias an unavoidable, epistemically benign precondition of scientific reasoning, or does its necessary presupposition render induction viciously circular in a way that Hume anticipated but could not formalize?

Explain why existing answers leave this open. Skeptical readings often conflate the mathematical averaging over all functions with the actual distribution scientists face; pragmatist readings treat the bias as a brute psychological or evolutionary fact that requires no further justification; and Bayesian treatments frequently assume that rational priors can be assigned within a given space without addressing whether the partitioning of that space itself is prior to any probabilistic assignment and stands in need of grounding.

Outline your method and a realistic timeline: in the first third, formalize “structural bias” using restricted classes of learning rules rather than arbitrary subjective priors; in the middle third, examine whether norms internal to scientific practice can justify the required restriction without circular appeal to inductive track record; in the final third, evaluate whether such a justification succeeds or merely relocates Hume’s problem to a meta-level choice among competing explanatory norms. Specify what a positive result would look like—e.g., a practice-internal, non-circular account of why one structural bias is preferable—and what failure would look like, such as the justification collapsing into arbitrary preference or tacitly relying on the very predictive successes it was meant to ground.

Give an honest scope assessment: identify the point at which formal learning-theory literacy might exceed what can be acquired in one semester, and propose a fallback (e.g., working with a simplified, illustrative model of algorithm classes rather than pursuing full proofs).

Finally, separate clearly what is mathematically settled—the NFL averaging result itself—from what remains genuinely contested: the philosophical interpretation of hypothesis-space restriction, the possibility of non-circular justification by explanatory norms, and whether such norms can bear the required epistemic weight. Do not present any contested interpretation as the established consensus.
