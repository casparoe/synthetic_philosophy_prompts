I'm working through Bernard Williams's integrity objection for my moral philosophy course, and a classmate's reconstruction has me stuck. Here is the argument. (1) Integrity requires that agents not treat their ground projects as fungible goods in a utilitarian calculus; they must be able to refuse demands that would destroy what gives their life meaning. (2) Williams also warns that justifying partiality—say, saving one's partner—by appealing to a general principle like partners have special obligations involves one thought too many; the right reason should be direct attachment, not mediated by moral theory. (3) So integrity demands both resistance to moral calculation and resistance to principled justification of partial attachments. (4) But if no principle distinguishes legitimate ground projects from arbitrary preferences, then my immediate attachment to my child is normatively on par with an immediate attachment to my vintage record collection. (5) To block this arbitrariness, we need a principle that selects which attachments count as genuine ground projects. (6) Yet invoking any such principle to validate my attachments seems to reintroduce exactly the mediated, theoretical reasoning that the one-thought-too-many objection forbids. (7) Therefore Williams's view either licenses arbitrary projects or undermines itself by smuggling back the principled reasoning it rejects. I want to preserve both the anti-utilitarian thrust of (1) and the anti-theoretical directness of (2). Which step—(3), (4), or (6)—should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Answer in one paragraph, no more than 120 words.
