I have been working through ideas about belief revision on my own—no formal background, just careful reading and thinking—and I have constructed an argument that seems to undermine the very framework it relies on. I want to know which step fails and what repairing it costs.

Here is the chain:

(1) Bayes's rule applies only when you observe a definite event E; then you update P(H) to P(H|E).

(2) In actual practice we almost never observe discrete, unambiguous events. We encounter noisy patterns, ambiguous testimony, statistical correlations, or sensory impressions that could indicate several different states of affairs. So direct Bayes conditioning is rarely available.

(3) Jeffrey conditionalization is the correct generalization: you divide the possibilities into a partition {E1, E2, ...}, assign new probabilities to each cell based on your uncertain observation, and update your belief in H by averaging the prior conditional beliefs P(H|Ei) weighted by those new cell probabilities.

(4) A core constraint—rigidity—is that within each cell Ei, the conditional probabilities P(H|Ei) remain exactly as they were before the update. Only the weights assigned to the cells themselves shift.

(5) Now apply this consistently across all belief revision. Every change in belief is triggered by ambiguous evidence, so every rational revision should be a Jeffrey update over some appropriate partition, including updates about my own reasoning reliability or about broad historical patterns.

(6) But if every update is Jeffrey, then inside every cell of every partition I ever employ, my probabilities are rigid. I can never revise the internal relationships among hypotheses within a cell; I can only shift my confidence about which broad cell I inhabit.

(7) That means all my substantive beliefs—about specific scientific claims, about whether a particular argument is valid, about what another person intended in a specific conversation—are frozen inside these cells from the moment I first construct them. The only thing that can ever change is my assessment of which large-scale scenario obtains.

(8) This is clearly false. I do revise particular beliefs in ways that do not reduce to re-weighting broad scenarios.

(9) Therefore Jeffrey conditionalization cannot be the universal rule for rational belief revision, despite being introduced precisely as the correct extension of Bayes's rule to real-world ambiguity.

The conclusion in (9) seems to defeat the motivation for (3). I would most prefer to keep (3) and (4), since they capture something important about ambiguous evidence and internal consistency. If I reject (5), am I arbitrarily restricting Jeffrey's rule to special cases? If I reject (6), am I misunderstanding rigidity? Or is the error earlier, perhaps in how I move from (2) to (3)?

Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to?
