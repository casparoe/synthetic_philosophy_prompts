I am drafting a chapter on the normative architecture of exchange in Aristotle's Politics and Nicomachean Ethics, and I have assembled the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is far more sweeping than anything Aristotle endorses, yet I cannot locate the precise failure without undermining premises I take to be secure.

(1) For Aristotle, money (nomisma) is instituted by convention (nomos) solely as a medium of exchange and as a measure of need (chreia); it is barren (atokos) and therefore incapable of generating value by itself (Politics I.10, 1258b).

(2) Corrective justice in voluntary exchange (Nicomachean Ethics V.5) requires that what is given and received stand in proportion equal to the relative worth (axia) of the parties' contributions, with money serving only to render this proportion numerically commensurable.

(3) If money is genuinely barren, then any surplus that accrues to one party solely in virtue of holding capital—whether as interest (tokos), merchant profit, or the gap between wages paid and the value of the labour-product—must represent a transfer away from the party whose productive contribution generated that surplus.

(4) Hence, any institutional arrangement in which capital yields a return beyond the mere replacement of its original quantity violates the proportional equality required by corrective justice, irrespective of mutual consent.

(5) Aristotle nevertheless permits limited acquisition through retail trade within natural chrematistike (Politics I.9, 1257a-b) and treats the management of labour within the household (oikonomia) as legitimate, neither being classified as unjust exchange.

(6) It follows that, if (1) through (4) hold, Aristotle is committed to condemning all profit-bearing capital arrangements—including those he explicitly accepts—as violations of exchange justice. This collapses the very distinction between natural and unnatural chrematistike that structures his economic thought.

I am most reluctant to surrender (1) or (2), since they appear textually well anchored, and I wish to preserve (5) because it is central to my reading of the Politics. This leaves (3) or (4) as probable sources of error, but I am unsure whether the mistake is an equivocation on worth (between need-measure and productive contribution), an illicit extension of corrective justice into distributive or household relations, or a modal slide from the claim that money cannot generate value to the claim that no surplus may lawfully accrue to capital-holders. Where exactly does the argument break down? If I reject (3), what price do I pay for the coherence of Aristotle's critique of usury? If I reject (4), must I concede that consent can legitimate disproportionate exchange?
