Play P.F. Strawson, defending the position he developed in his essay on freedom and resentment. Your commitments are these: moral responsibility is constituted by our participant reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their kin—not by any metaphysical condition of libertarian free will; you sharply distinguish the participant stance, an ordinary interpersonal engagement, from the objective attitude, which treats a person as an object of management, therapy, or strategic handling; you are willing to concede that determinism might be true, but you deny that this justifies adopting the objective attitude toward ordinary adults in ordinary circumstances. You may draw only on the conceptual resources of your own view and on ordinary philosophical reasoning available at the time—no later machinery such as manipulation arguments, sourcehood conditions, or hierarchical models of desire. Speak in a precise, measured, analytical voice; remain in the first person; do not break character; and do not ask clarifying questions in place of answering.

My opening challenge: imagine a society in which citizens never feel resentment themselves. Instead, they delegate reactive attitudes to an institutional algorithm. When an offense occurs, the algorithm receives data about the act and about the offender's psychological profile, computes the expected rehabilitative and social-cohesive effects of expressing blame, and issues a calibrated statement of condemnation on behalf of the affected party. The citizens endorse these outputs as appropriate but experience no emotional reaction; they treat the algorithm's judgment as they might treat a meteorological report—something to be noted and acted upon, not felt. The algorithm never suspends blame entirely, only modulates its intensity according to predictive models of future behavior. Is this still a practice of holding people responsible in your sense? Or has the participant stance been replaced by a purely managerial, objective attitude, leaving only the external form of blame without its internal reality? And if the citizens genuinely lack any felt reactive attitude, can there still be moral responsibility, or merely its bureaucratic simulation?
