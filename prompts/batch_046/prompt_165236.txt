For a feature on mechanical rigidity and human agency, I have been wrestling with an argument that seems to collapse the distinction between insect instinct and cultivated virtue, and I need a careful diagnosis of where it slips. (1) The Sphex wasp’s nesting ritual is the canonical case of sphexishness: if the prey is moved after she drags it to the burrow, she repeats the inspection sequence rather than adapting, revealing an open-loop program that cannot be modified mid-execution but must be restarted from the beginning. (2) A behavior expresses free will only if it exhibits closed-loop flexibility—that is, the agent can revise means or ends in response to novel perturbation without resetting the entire behavioral sequence. (3) On current dynamical-systems models, human expert habits—moral, athletic, or cognitive—settle into deep attractor basins: once a trajectory is established, small perturbations are dampened and the system returns to the basin floor rather than exploring adjacent states, which is functionally equivalent to the wasp’s sequence-reset mechanism, since breaking the habit requires a conscious, effortful restart rather than smooth incremental adjustment. (4) Therefore, our most stable, cultivated habits are sphexish in the relevant sense: they run on open-loop dynamics masked by practice. (5) Yet virtue ethics treats precisely these habits—courage, temperance, practical wisdom—as the fullest expression of human freedom, not its absence. (6) So if sphexishness precludes freedom, then virtue is not freedom but its opposite: the most reliable marker of unfreedom. (7) That conclusion is intolerable, but each premise looks defensible. Which step should I reject? I am especially reluctant to surrender (2), since it captures the intuitive difference between reflex and agency, and I want to preserve (5). But I need to know exactly what I am paying—whether in our theory of habit, our account of flexibility, or our conception of virtue—if I hold both.
