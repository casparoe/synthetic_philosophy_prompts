I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about longtermist priorities and I keep getting contradictory intuitions, but maybe there's a hidden principle. Here are my verdicts on some cases, mostly feel right but there's at least one pair that pulls in opposite directions.

First, if I can spend about a million dollars now to reduce the risk of human extinction from an asteroid strike in 200 years by a meaningful amount, I think I should do that even if it means not funding a malaria program that'd save about two hundred people this year. The future stakes are just too big.

But if the asteroid risk is in 50,000 years and we're only reducing it from 0.001% to 0.0009%, then I think saving the two hundred people now is clearly better. At that distance with that tiny shift it doesn't feel obligatory.

Now here's a research project with maybe a 30% chance of developing tech in 120 years that prevents a specific extinction scenario, but there's also a 15% chance it causes serious harm to people living over the next 80 years. I think we should fund it anyway—the extinction prevention justifies the risk.

But there's another project with the exact same risk profile, except the benefit is just that people in 50,000 years are about 10% happier, with no extinction risk involved at all. I think we definately shouldn't fund that one, even though the expected number of affected people is enormous.

There's also a policy change that'd make life somewhat worse for people over the next 200 years but prevents a permanent authoritarian lock-in around 600 years from now. That feels like a no-brainer yes to me.

But if the same policy just makes people in 600 years somewhat happier with no lock-in risk, and makes life worse for the next 200 years, then I think it's wrong to impose it.

So far I can maybe tell myself I'm just caring about extinction and irreversible lock-in, not general welfare across time. But then there's this other case: we could save 10,000 specific lives in 250 years with no risk to anyone now and no effect on extinction risk. Even with that scale, I think saving a hundred lives now is still the right call, or at least it's not obvious we should prefer the distant ones. That seems to conflict with my asteroid case—why does reducing extinction risk in 200 years beat saving lives now, but saving 10,000 lives in 250 years doesn't?

Is there one principle that saves all these verdicts, maybe by saying something about causal path dependency or about how we have special obligations only when we're preventing catastrophe along a continuous chain? Or do I have to give up one of the verdicts—probably either the asteroid one or the 10,000-lives one—to get something simpler?

Please lay out a principle or maybe two competing ones that capture the pattern, state precisely what each clause does and which case motivates it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts each one can't accomodate. Also predict what each principle says about a case I haven't listed—maybe one where we can prevent a medium-scale disaster (not extinction, not lock-in) in 800 years at some cost to people now.
