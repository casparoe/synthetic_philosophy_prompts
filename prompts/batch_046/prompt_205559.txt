Consider the institutional practice, standard in psychiatric epidemiology and in clinical training programs, of treating concurrent shifts between diagnostic categories—most visibly the decline in diagnoses of intellectual disability and specific language disorder alongside the steep rise in autism spectrum disorder—as "diagnostic substitution" requiring statistical correction. Researchers adjust prevalence trends by subtracting the drop in adjacent categories, implicitly treating these shifts as misallocation around a stable underlying rate of a real condition. At the same time, the same institutions teach Hacking-style looping effects: that being classified alters a person's self-conception and symptom presentation, that diagnostic categories shape the phenomena they organize, and that a disorder's boundaries are partly constituted by clinical and social response. These two commitments—correcting substitution as measurement error, and acknowledging that classification constructs its objects—operate side by side without institutional acknowledgment of tension.

What philosophical view of mental disorders must this pairing presuppose? Does statistical correction for substitution require a realist ontology in which disorders exist independently of our categories, with looping effects relegated to superficial expression?
