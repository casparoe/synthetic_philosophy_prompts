In the literature on Nāgārjuna’s Madhyamaka, the debate over the catuskoti—the fourfold negation in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā—has reached a familiar impasse: analytic philosophers of religion tend to read the tetralemma as a proto-paraconsistent logical structure, while philologically oriented scholars insist it is a therapeutic dialectic meant to exhaust conceptual proliferation (prapañca) without asserting any meta-position. For decades both camps have cited the same root text, agreed on the Sanskrit grammar, and yet moved no closer—analytic interpreters formalize the negations, hermeneutic interpreters dissolve them into soteriological practice, and neither side treats the other’s evidence as decisive. My working hypothesis is that this stall is not best explained as logic versus rhetoric, nor as two groups answering different questions under one heading; rather, there is a hidden disagreement about whether emptiness (śūnyatā) can consistently function as a predicate applied to the tetralemma itself. If every view—including the view that all views are empty—is empty, then the tetralemma either collapses into self-refutation or requires a meta-level exemption that Nāgārjuna never explicitly grants, and the two sides may be divided precisely over whether such an exemption is implicitly licensed. Is that the real fault line—one side granting the exemption, the other denying it—or is the disagreement actually about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding what philosophical interpretation owes to transformative practice?
