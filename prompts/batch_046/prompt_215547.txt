I am drafting an essay on Rudolf Carnap’s writings on scientific frameworks, value-neutrality, and institutional planning, and I want comments on the argument’s coherence and interest rather than on bibliography or citation. The core notes are these.

First, Carnap distinguishes internal questions—those answerable within an adopted linguistic framework—from external questions about the framework’s adoption, which he treats as pragmatic decisions guided by efficiency and purpose rather than by truth. Second, I propose that in recursive governance structures—where an optimization procedure, predictive model, or planning mechanism continuously revises the very objective function or framework it operates within—this boundary dissolves. The selection of the framework becomes an internal operation for the framework itself, producing a reflexive loop. Third, I argue that Carnap’s tolerance principle, which denies that any single framework is uniquely correct, then generates an institutional analogue to Buridan’s ass: when every permissible framework is on equal pragmatic footing and the mechanism for selecting among them is endogenous to all of them, there is no non-circular basis for revision. The essay concludes that Carnap’s separation of logical structure from practical planning collapses not because values illicitly enter logic, but because recursive conditions erase the distinction between operating within a system and choosing it.

Please comment specifically on: (1) whether my reconstruction of Carnap’s internal/external distinction and his attitude toward planning and value-neutrality is historically defensible, or whether it improperly imports later pragmatist or governance-theoretic readings; (2) whether the recursive-planning objection genuinely follows from Carnap’s own commitments about framework choice, or whether it is an anachronistic projection of contemporary algorithmic governance concerns onto logical empiricism; (3) which structural link in the argument is weakest—the dissolution of the internal/external boundary, the analogy to choice without preference, or the inference from tolerance to institutional paralysis; and (4) whether the overall thesis is sufficiently distinctive to pursue, or whether it merely restates familiar critiques of value-neutrality in unfamiliar vocabulary. I am especially interested in objections that do not depend on specialized knowledge of Carnap’s correspondence or unpublished manuscripts—only on the published positions a well-trained philosopher or theoretically minded generalist could reasonably be expected to hold.
