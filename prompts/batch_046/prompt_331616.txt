You are an expert philosopher of mind and value, with particular depth in theories of well-being, phenomenology, and the philosophy of psychology. Please think carefully, and begin with brief brainstorming: outline the four main strategies philosophers have used to unify pleasures—by qualitative feel-tone, by representational or intentional content, by functional or neural signature, and by the relational property of being positively evaluated or “liked” by the subject—together with the sharpest objection to each. I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently drafting a feature on whether hedonism can survive the radical heterogeneity of pleasure; I want you to perform reflective equilibrium on my own intuitions rather than survey the literature.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, arranged so that at least one pair pulls in genuinely opposite directions.

First, unity versus difference. The pleasure I take in finally grasping a subtle argument in modal logic feels cognitively textured, almost like a mental click; the pleasure of sinking into a hot bath feels diffuse, bodily, and lacking any intentional object. I judge both are real pleasures, yet they share no discernible qualitative character beyond being positively valued. At the same time, I resist treating them as interchangeable currency: I think ten units of bath-pleasure should not automatically outweigh one unit of intellectual satisfaction, which suggests well-being is not a simple additive sum of homogeneous hedonic units.

Second, moral coloring. The quiet joy of witnessing a stranger reunite with a lost pet feels morally luminous; the sharp delight of seeing a professional rival fail feels structurally similar in intensity and bodily arousal, but I want to say it contributes differently—perhaps negatively—to a good life, even though it is genuinely pleasant. Yet I also resist denying that the rival-case is pleasure at all; it is liked, sought, and feels good, but it seems tainted in a way that pure bodily pleasure is not.

Third, relief versus presence. The abrupt cessation of a severe migraine produces an enormous positive feeling, but it strikes me as essentially the removal of a negative state made salient. The gentle pleasure of a cool breeze on a warm day is a pure positive presence. I judge both improve well-being, but for different reasons—restoration versus addition—yet if well-being is defined as pleasure minus pain, relief ought to be the paradigmatic pleasure, which feels wrong.

Fourth, directionality and value—this is where I inject a distinction I have been testing. I distinguish vector pleasure, which carries intentional directedness toward an object (enjoying that p, savoring a flavor, relishing someone’s success), from scalar pleasure, which is pure hedonic intensity without aboutness (the warm glow after exercise, the floaty calm of certain induced states). Most intellectual and aesthetic pleasures are vector; many bodily ones are scalar. Now the tension: I think a masochist’s vector pleasure directed at their own pain should not count positively for well-being, despite being intense and genuinely liked. But I also think a scholar’s vector pleasure directed at an abstract logical truth should count fully. So my verdict seems to depend on the moral or epistemic value of the intentional object, not on the vector-scalar structure itself—yet I introduced that structure precisely to avoid smuggling object-values into the nature of pleasure.

Fifth, an unlisted case for prediction. Consider procedural pleasure: the enjoyment taken in the smooth execution of a skilled routine—say, a carpenter’s rhythmic motion with a hand plane—where there is no particular result being sought in the moment, only the flow of the action itself. Is this vector (directed at the motion) or scalar (pure flow-state intensity)? And should it support well-being in the same way as the modal-logic case?

My request: Is there a single principle—phenomenological, functional, or representational—that captures all four verdict pairs without contradiction? If not, present two rival candidates, state precisely which of my verdicts each must overturn, and defend the one you think survives best. For the winning principle, state its verdict on procedural pleasure, on whether artificially induced scalar pleasure—say, precisely calibrated neurostimulation producing bath-like warmth with no object—counts equally with vector pleasure, and on whether a life composed almost entirely of such scalar pleasure could be as good as one rich in vector pleasure directed at genuine achievements. Be precise, avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, and flag the cases where your principle gives an answer I will not like.
