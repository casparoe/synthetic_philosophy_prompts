I'm presenting a thesis next week at a joint philosophy-and-literature seminar on Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time." My claim is that the essay should not be read as a metaphysical denial of temporal succession, but as a performative demonstration of what I call reflexive retention: the present moment's awareness of its own passing is structurally identical to the past it purports to have lost, so that any coherent phenomenology of time must embed an infinite self-reference loop. This loop, I argue, renders both eternalism and presentism descriptively adequate but ontologically underdetermined, without committing Borges to either view. The audience is split into three factions. First, four comparative-literature scholars of Latin American modernism, who treat Borges as deploying metaphysical tropes for narrative effect and resist extracting formal arguments from literary essays. Second, two analytic philosophers of time—one a specialist on McTaggart who is convinced Borges merely muddles the A-series/B-series distinction, and one agnostic who thinks literary texts have no business in metaphysics. Third, one historian of philosophy of science who views any crossover between fiction and ontology with suspicion. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are to my proposal and grouped by which faction is likely to raise them, with each objection phrased in the vocabulary that faction would use. For each objection, provide a concise reply suited to about two minutes of speaking time, flag the weakest point in that reply, and state what I should concede up front. Include strategic notes on tone—when to concede ground to the literature scholars versus when to hold firm with the philosophers. Finally, identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks, explain why it is dangerous, and sketch the briefest defensible response I can fall back on if it does come up. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or further reading; assume I have the text and the standard analytic literature on time in mind, but do not depend on obscure secondary scholarship.
