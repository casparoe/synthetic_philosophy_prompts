Here's an argument I built for a comparative-politics seminar and now find suspicious because the conclusion dissolves the very concept it starts with. (1) Call a regime democratically backsliding just when it weakens institutional checks on executive power relative to the regime's own recent democratic baseline. (2) Any specific check—judicial independence, media pluralism, electoral-commission autonomy—can be measured as stronger or weaker against that baseline. (3) But the baseline is not a natural kind; it is itself the contingent result of prior institutional choices, many of which weakened earlier checks (for example, a constitutional court was created by weakening legislative supremacy). (4) So measuring current erosion requires selecting a calibration point, yet every available point is already the product of prior weakening or strengthening. (5) If we calibrate to the point immediately before the current government took office, backsliding becomes purely comparative and government-specific: identical institutional changes count as erosion when made by this government but not when made by its predecessor. (6) If we calibrate instead to an ideal-type democratic baseline—full separation of powers, perfect electoral integrity—then every real-world democracy is constantly backsliding, since no regime fully instantiates the ideal. (7) Therefore either backsliding applies only to changes made by governments we happen to be observing now (making it a partisan label) or it applies universally to all regimes everywhere (making it useless for distinguishing Hungary or Poland from Denmark or Canada). (8) But if the concept is either partisan or universal, it cannot do the explanatory work assigned to it: marking a genuinely distinct kind of institutional decay rather than ordinary constitutional politics. (9) So democratic backsliding is either an empty descriptive category or a masked normative judgment about which governments are legitimate. (10) I want to keep both the intuition that something genuinely different happened in Hungary and Poland—something more serious than ordinary reform—and the idea that backsliding can be identified without simply declaring that I dislike the current party. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (8) than accept (9), but I want to know exactly what preserving them costs me—especially whether I am forced to smuggle in a normative standard of democratic essence that I was trying to avoid.
