Write the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—for an interdisciplinary workshop on moral psychology in organizational life. Half the audience are virtue ethicists who still treat character as the primary unit of moral evaluation; the other half are social psychologists for whom the situationist critique of cross-situational dispositional consistency is essentially settled. Frame the workshop's agenda using a conceptual distinction you are introducing in this talk: call it normative resonance, defined as the coherence between an agent's practical reasons and a regulative ideal, independent of whether the agent behaves uniformly across situations. The distinction must do real structural work: show why empirical findings about situational behavioral variability do not by themselves defeat virtue ethics, but also show why virtue ethicists cannot dismiss those findings by retreating to an unobservable deep character. Address both halves of the room without bibliographic references, avoid framing the tension as a simple compromise, and make clear what is actually at stake if normative resonance rather than behavioral consistency becomes the criterion for attributing virtue.
