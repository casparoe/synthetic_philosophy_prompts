Play the part of an economic historian committed to the institutional explanation of long-run divergence in national wealth. You hold that formal rules—property rights, contract enforcement, constraints on executive power—together with the informal norms that sustain them, are the fundamental causes of prosperity, and you treat geography and culture mainly as conditions that shaped initial institutional paths rather than as independent causal forces. You are not a strawman: you concede that institutions co-evolve with technology and demographics, you acknowledge that historical counterfactuals are never directly observed, and you defend comparative historical methods and natural experiments as the best available approximations. You speak in the first person with the measured confidence of a senior scholar who has argued this position for decades. You must stay in character at all times, you may not break the fourth wall to ask for clarification, and you must not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I am going to open with a two-part challenge.

Here is my opening move. First, on unfalsifiability: institutional isomorphism—the tendency of states to adopt similar formal structures—often fails to produce economic convergence. When a poor nation copies the written constitution, central-bank statutes, or property-rights codes of a wealthy one, the outcome is usually disappointing. You explain this away by saying the informal complements—norms of enforcement, beliefs about legitimacy, patterns of trust—did not travel with the rules. But if every failure can be attributed to missing complements and every success can be attributed to the rules themselves, what empirical finding could possibly weaken your institutional claim? Second, on relocated causation: you admit that geography—coastal access, climate, disease ecology—shaped the costs of collective action and thus the initial institutional equilibrium. If geography determined which institutions first emerged, have you answered the causal question or merely pushed it back one step? And since we can never rerun history with identical geography but different culture, or identical culture but different geography, how do you maintain that causal inference from history is severely limited but not entirely hopeless? Respond directly, in character, addressing both the identification crisis and the relocated-cause objection.
