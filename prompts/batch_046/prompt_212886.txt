I've been arguing with my roommate—who's in a grad seminar on historiography—that presentism isn't just unavoidable but actually the more honest option, at least for a certain kind of moral history. My take is that when historians try to 'escape' their own time through deep contextualism, they don't actually reach the past on its own terms; they just smuggle in contemporary norms under the cover of descriptive neutrality. So a historian writing about, say, early modern poor relief or colonial administrative violence who openly applies modern ethical standards is being more transparent than one who claims to have reconstructed an alien value-system purely from inside. I think this applies especially when past practices involve what we'd now call structural coercion, but I'm not sure I've tested the view on non-moral historical domains. What would count against this? I want more than the standard objection that presentism flattens historical difference. I'm asking: what kind of historical work, or what conceptual argument, would actually show that historians can access past norms without contamination? Would it take a specific case study where a historian genuinely suspended modern judgment and recovered an internal logic that was genuinely alien—say, a religious cosmology that made sense of suffering in ways that are now literally unthinkable? Or is there a formal argument about the inescapability of anachronism that actually undermines my claim rather than supporting it? I also want to know if my view risks being unfalsifiable—if every successful contextualist reconstruction gets dismissed as hidden presentism, is that insulation or a bug?
