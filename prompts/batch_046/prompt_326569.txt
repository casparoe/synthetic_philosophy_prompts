As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of logic and artificial intelligence in earnest, I have been trying to grasp the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. In a seminar on formal epistemology and rational self-modification, I heard a speaker dismiss the possibility of fully autonomous self-auditing systems with the line: "A rational agent cannot consistently vouch for its own soundness from within." The remark seemed to settle the matter instantly, but I found myself unsure what precise commitment was being invoked. Could you lay out, as a disambiguated menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses this slogan might actually express? I have in mind at least the following candidates: a strictly formal claim about provability conditions in modal arithmetic derived from Löb's theorem; a broader epistemological claim about the circularity of any attempt to justify one's own reasoning from the inside; a practical or computational claim about the impossibility of complete internal error-checking in real architectures; and perhaps a psychological claim about human limits on self-knowledge. For each reading, please indicate whether it is a tautology or triviality, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false or significantly overstated, and then say which of these interpretations, if any, genuinely supports the conclusion that a self-modifying agent can never audit itself.

Please also tell me, for the interpretations that depend on technical results, what is settled about the formal conditions that trigger the obstacle and what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and logicians who study it—particularly regarding whether the result applies only to systems that literally embed arithmetic or whether it extends to any agent with a certain kind of reflective structure. In answering, separate clearly what is established from what is disputed, and do not present a contested philosophical interpretation of the obstacle as though it were the consensus view among people who understand the underlying logic. I am asking from the position of someone who understands formal proof but is still learning to navigate the philosophical literature, so I want the distinctions drawn with precision rather than rhetorical force.
