I have recently been forced to think carefully about how I should calibrate confidence in a singular, high-stakes decision—specifically, whether to consent to a surgeon’s novel technique for which no long-run statistical record exists—when I realized that applying geometric rationality to belief produces what looks like a reductio. Here is the argument. (1) Rational sequential choice requires maximizing expected logarithmic utility, the Kelly criterion, because linear utility fails to penalize ruin and ignores compound growth. (2) By analogy, epistemic rationality should maximize expected long-run log-score, so beliefs should be updated multiplicatively and never placed with extreme confidence unless the evidence is decisive. (3) But a singular judgment—will this operation succeed for me?—offers no repetition; there is no compound growth of accuracy across my biography. (4) Therefore, if geometric rationality governs this belief, I must either treat my decision as one draw from an infinite hypothetical ensemble of identical lives, assigning precise quantitative priors over counterfactual duplicates of myself, or concede that the framework is silent exactly where belief matters most. (5) The first horn imports a modal-realist ontology I have no independent reason to accept; the second makes geometric rationality an incomplete theory of rational belief. (6) Hence geometric rationality either collapses into metaphysical extravagance or abandons the domain of singular practical reason. Each premise seems defensible, yet I cannot accept the conclusion. Which step should I reject—perhaps the analogy between financial betting and epistemic commitment, or the claim that singular cases lie outside the scope of long-run optimization—and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a principled restriction of the Kelly criterion to genuinely repeatable domains that does not look ad hoc, or does saving geometric rationality require me to deny that singular beliefs are subject to rational evaluation at all?
