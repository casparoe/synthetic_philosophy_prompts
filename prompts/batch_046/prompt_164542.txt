Please argue that tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive pleasure is at best instrumentally good—offers a more coherent framework for evaluating collective goods than either hedonism or preference-satisfaction theories do. Apply this specifically to common-pool resources: defend the claim that the worth of such an institution should be judged not by aggregate satisfaction or revealed-preference efficiency, but by the degree to which its design eliminates scarcity-induced craving and status-competitive anxiety without generating substitute cravings. Then address the objection that this criterion cannot distinguish a genuinely flourishing commons, which structurally removes the roots of craving, from an authoritarian arrangement that suppresses visible desire through coercion and surveillance. Explain whether tranquilism possesses internal resources to draw that distinction, or whether it must import normative criteria from a separate theory of freedom or autonomy.
