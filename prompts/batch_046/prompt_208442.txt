I'm serving on the governance council of an interdisciplinary research collective that operates under a written charter. The charter contains what members call the reflexivity clause in Section 12: it states that any provision of the charter, including Section 12 itself, may be suspended or rewritten by a simple majority of the council, provided the change is ratified at two consecutive meetings. At the same time, the charter's Preamble lists several non-derogable principles, one of which declares that no procedural mechanism may be used to alter the conditions of its own authority. In practice, the council has twice invoked Section 12 to modify the voting threshold in Section 12 itself—first lowering the ratification requirement from two meetings to one, then reinstating it—while maintaining that these adjustments do not violate the Preamble because they concern administrative mechanics rather than authority conditions.

Meanwhile, when individual members have tried to resign from specific obligations they personally reject—arguing that institutional binding requires ongoing individual consent—the council has consistently rejected those resignations, insisting that membership obligations are determined by the charter's current rules, not by each member's agreement. What philosophical commitments about institutional authority and legal self-reference does this combination of practices presuppose? Does treating Section 12 as amendable by its own terms presuppose a conventionalist view that there is no fixed point of authority, or can it be reconciled with the idea that some rules are genuinely entrenched? Is the council's refusal to allow individual opt-outs consistent with its willingness to let the collective alter the very rules that grant it power? More specifically, does the distinction between administrative mechanics and authority conditions do any real work in blocking the paradox of self-amendment, or does it merely rename the problem? And if the council's implicit theory turned out to be mistaken—say, if institutional authority actually requires an unamendable foundation—what in its day-to-day governance would have to change?
