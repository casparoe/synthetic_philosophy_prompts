I'm building a taxonomy for a graduate legal-philosophy workshop on whether criminal law may enforce morality in the absence of direct harm to others. I want a decision tree—not a list of authors—that sorts respondents by their judgments on concrete cases, then maps those judgment-patterns onto positions in and around the Hart–Devlin debate.

The root node should ask: "Should the criminal law ever prohibit private, consensual conduct solely on the ground that it is immoral, assuming no direct harm to non-consenting parties?" From there, build three branch levels using these diagnostic cases: (1) a statute criminalizing private, consensual sexual practices that violate traditional norms but involve no coercion or deception; (2) a statute punishing purely symbolic blasphemy that deeply offends a religious community but is not linked to any credible threat of violence; (3) a statute restricting ecologically destructive but individually harmless practices (e.g., discretionary long-haul air travel) on the ground that they express a vicious indifference to collective welfare.

At each node, phrase the branch as a yes/no/moderate answer to "Should this be criminalized?" Do not label branches with author names; let the path determine the leaf.

Inject two analytical distinctions that standard textbook summaries usually collapse: first, separate "corruption arguments" (the law prevents moral corruption of the agent) from "symbolic solidarity arguments" (the law expresses communal values regardless of causal harm); second, treat "naturalness has independent normative weight" as a hidden split—some respondents will invoke it for (1) but not for (3), or vice versa. Build these as explicit intermediate nodes where the three case-judgments alone underdetermine the path.

The leaves must include: Hart's harm principle (strict version), Devlin's social-disintegration thesis, moderate legal moralism (offense-based but not corruption-based), the asymmetry view (morality may justify negative prohibitions but never positive mandates), and at least two "unnamed" combinations that are logically possible but rarely defended. For each leaf, provide: the position name or "unnamed"; a one-sentence core commitment; and its most awkward commitment—specifically, what it must say about one of the three cases above that sounds counterintuitive.

Where the tree genuinely cannot separate two positions (e.g., because they yield identical judgments across all three cases and both invoke corruption rather than solidarity), flag the pair explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction.

Deliver the result as a JSON object with these fields:
- "root_question": string
- "injected_distinctions": array of strings describing the non-standard splits you added
- "nodes": array of objects, each with "node_id", "question_text", "branch_options" (array of strings), and "notes" (optional string explaining the diagnostic purpose)
- "leaves": array of objects, each with "leaf_id", "path" (ordered list of branch choices), "position_name", "core_commitment", "awkward_commitment", "indistinguishable_from" (string or null, naming the leaf it collapses with and why)
- "unoccupied_combinations": array of objects with "path_description" and "diagnosis" (one sentence on whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored)

Keep every node phrased so a respondent can answer it without knowing which interpretation it leads to, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
