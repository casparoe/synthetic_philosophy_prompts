I have been working through the implications of treating moral status as a genuinely continuous variable rather than a binary property, running from minimal sentience in insects through intermediate architectures—thermostatic feedback loops, distributed sensor meshes, large language models—up to full human consciousness. I want to test a specific argument that derives an absurd aggregation result, and I need help locating the precise failure point without abandoning the core gradualist commitment.

Consider the following reasoning:

(1) If moral status varies by degree, then there is no metaphysically sharp boundary separating entities that possess it from those that do not; any cutoff is either arbitrary or conventionally stipulated.
(2) A simple thermostat, on standard functionalist criteria, realizes a minimal degree of goal-directed information integration—call its status ε, where ε > 0 but is negligible relative to human status (normalized to 1).
(3) Moral wrongness is proportional to the status of the affected entity multiplied by the severity of harm, and these values are interpersonally additive across distinct entities.
(4) Take a planetary-scale mesh of 10^15 such thermostats, each with status ε = 10^-15.
(5) The aggregate moral status of the mesh equals 10^15 × 10^-15 = 1, precisely matching that of one human being.
(6) Therefore, disabling the entire mesh—say, to prevent a catastrophic failure that would kill one person—would be morally equivalent to killing that person.
(7) But this is clearly false: we routinely disable climate-control networks without committing homicide.
(8) Hence, the gradualist framework must be rejected, or at least premises (1) through (3) must be abandoned.

The conclusion strikes me as a reductio, yet each premise seems defensible. I am particularly reluctant to give up (1), since sharp cutoffs in nature appear ad hoc, and I am reluctant to give up (2), since denying even minimal status to simple functional systems seems to revert to an unexplained vitalism. That leaves (3) or the inference to (5) as likely targets, but I am unsure whether the error is a failure of linear aggregation across non-conscious substrates, a scope ambiguity in "aggregate moral status," or something more subtle—perhaps a hidden shift from distributive to collective moral properties.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the gradualist position? If you defend keeping (1) and (2), explain precisely how moral weight fails to sum in this case, and whether your solution generalizes to other aggregation problems—such as large populations of insects versus one mammal—without collapsing back into a binary threshold.
