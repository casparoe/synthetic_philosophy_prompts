You are an expert in the philosophy of science and comparative metaphysics, with particular familiarity with the reception of quantum theory in twentieth-century European thought. I am writing a position paper for an advanced seminar and want you to audit my commitments for consistency before I present them. Please think through this carefully and systematically: start by mapping the logical relations between each pair of claims, then distinguish apparent conflicts that dissolve under closer interpretation from genuine contradictions, and only then recommend revisions.

Here is the set of views I am defending about Schrödinger's philosophical outlook and its implications. I take his later essays to advance a Vedanta-inspired metaphysical monism: there is fundamentally only one universal consciousness, and the appearance of many separate minds is produced by the partitioning of experience rather than by any real ontological division. I believe quantum mechanics genuinely motivates this, because the universal wave function describes a single entangled system, which implies that spatial separation does not entail ontological separation. At the same time, I hold that phenomenal consciousness has an irreducible first-person structure—what it is like to be me is not captured by any third-person physical description, including a complete quantum-state description. From this I conclude that if monism is correct, then personal identity over time and the fear of death are both based on an illusion: there is no enduring individual self that either survives or is annihilated. Even so, I want to maintain that contemporary neuroscience is correct to treat different brains as causally autonomous information-processing systems, and that localized brain damage can selectively destroy specific capacities in one subject without affecting others. Finally, I claim that Schrödinger's monism offers a real consolation for mortality, because if I am ultimately identical with the universal consciousness, then my death is merely the dissolution of a temporary limitation, not an absolute end.

Please examine these commitments and tell me which pairs are in tension, ranking them by severity. For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and state what philosophical cost that revision carries. Also point out any conflict that looks serious but actually dissolves once the commitments are interpreted charitably. Conclude by saying which single commitment I should revise—giving up or significantly modifying just one—to eliminate the largest number of inconsistencies, and be explicit about which of my verdicts I must abandon if I insist on preserving all the others at the price of a more complicated underlying principle.
