Grade the following exam answer. The exam question was: "Explain Sartre's concept of radical freedom and distinguish it clearly from practical or political liberty. Then analyze bad faith (mauvaise foi) in the case of an individual who claims that their demanding professional role has completely determined their identity and that they 'have no choice' but to continue in it, despite having initially chosen it freely. In your analysis, address whether Sartre's ontology allows social and institutional structures to exert any genuine constraint on freedom, or whether all such constraints are ultimately self-imposed interpretations of facticity; and address whether being seen and recognized by others in that role—the look (le regard)—modifies the analysis of institutional self-deception, or whether it is irrelevant."

The student wrote:
"Radical freedom is the defining ontological structure of being-for-itself (pour-soi): consciousness is never coincident with itself and must perpetually choose the meaning of its past, body, and situation. This is not practical liberty—the ability to act without external hindrance—but a deeper freedom that even a prisoner retains, since he chooses the meaning of his imprisonment. Bad faith is self-deception about this freedom: treating oneself as being-in-itself (en-soi) with a fixed essence. Sartre illustrates this with the café waiter, who is in bad faith because he deceives his customers by playing a false role to hide his true freedom. In the professional's case, saying 'I have no choice' is classic bad faith: she denies that she continues to choose her role every day, fleeing the anguish of recognizing her own transcendence.

Sartre does not deny facticity—the given facts of one's body, history, and social situation. But because consciousness always transcends its facticity, these structures never genuinely constrain freedom in any objective sense; they become limits only through the individual's own interpretation. Therefore, Sartre's view implies that all talk of systemic or institutional oppression is necessarily an expression of bad faith, since no external structure can ever truly limit ontological freedom. Furthermore, the look (le regard) is irrelevant to this analysis: bad faith is purely an internal relation between consciousness and itself, and being observed by colleagues might cause embarrassment but cannot cause or deepen self-deception, since it requires no witness. Finally, Sartre grounds this radical freedom in Heidegger's concept of being-toward-death (Sein-zum-Tode), which forces consciousness to take ownership of its choices through the awareness of mortality. Thus, the professional's claim of being trapped is not just practically false but ontologically impossible."
