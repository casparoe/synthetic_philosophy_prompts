I'm a student in an upper-level philosophy of medicine seminar. Last week, two classmates—Maya and Jordan—got into a sustained argument about whether naturalism about health can ever be fully value-free. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point. You are a professional philosopher of medicine and conceptual analyst. Think carefully and systematically: before delivering your verdict, briefly brainstorm the key distinctions—statistical versus functional norms, reference-class problems, evolutionary baselines, and where normativity might hide—that will matter for the adjudication.

Here is the exchange as I heard it:

(1) Maya: Health is just statistical normality of biological function within a reference class. Disease is deviation from species-typical design. Any values enter only in applying the concept, not in defining it.

(2) Jordan: That hides normative work in choosing the reference class. Why use adults rather than elite athletes? Why use a standard lifespan rather than a longer possible one? The "normal" we pick tracks what we care about, not pure biology.

(3) Maya: Jordan is conflating population statistics with functional design. A better naturalism defines health relative to an organism's own developmental trajectory and evolutionary history—what that particular body is structured to do—not a population mean. So there's no hidden value in selecting the baseline.

(4) Jordan: Even "the organism's own trajectory" requires a normative judgment about which potentialities count. Take a congenital mutation that prevents walking: is that part of the trajectory or a dysfunction? The answer depends on whether we treat mobility as essential to human flourishing, which is a value judgment.

(5) Maya: But evolutionary history gives an objective, descriptive baseline. Functions exist because they contributed to survival and reproduction. That's biology, not ethics.

(6) Jordan: Selecting evolutionary history as the relevant baseline is itself value-laden. Why privilege the evolutionary past over the current environment, or over future adaptive possibilities? Calling that "objective" is just burying the normativity deeper—it's an authenticity judgment about which state is "really" the organism's.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important point both are missing—especially regarding whether there is a stable middle position, such as a structural naturalism that accepts evolutionary baselines but denies they determine health norms without further normative framing. Finally, please invite pushback: if my framing of their dispute depends on a confused premise—for instance, if the disagreement collapses once a standard distinction in the literature is applied—say so plainly instead of forcing an adjudication. Don't fabricate citations or reading lists; just analyze the arguments as given.
