Imagine you are advising an interdisciplinary research group with three years of funding and a mandate to pursue work that genuinely grows out of Sen's liberal paradox, rather than merely solving a technical puzzle in social-choice theory. The paradox—demonstrating that minimal liberalism, Pareto efficiency, and an unrestricted domain of preferences are jointly incompatible—has produced several families of response: weakening the liberal principle itself (for example, reducing each individual's decisive sphere to a single pair of alternatives); restricting the domain of admissible preferences (excluding so-called meddlesome orderings); treating rights as deontic side-constraints that sit outside preference aggregation altogether; and applying the framework to algorithmic allocation mechanisms, where delineating a "personal sphere" is institutionally fraught. More recently, two less conventional directions have been proposed. One is a layered resolution: minimal liberalism holds firmly at the constitutional level, where basic liberties are fixed, but is suspended or transformed at the operational level of everyday collective choice. The other is a model-theoretic critique arguing that the paradox arises only because rights-claims are misrepresented as preferences over outcomes, rather than as normative constraints that do not enter a social welfare function at all.

Given these directions—domain restriction, rights-as-constraints, algorithmic application, layered constitutional resolution, and the preference-model critique—which open questions or lines of work should the group prioritise, and in what order? Rank them and defend your ordering with explicit criteria: tractability for philosophers and institutional designers (not for machine-learning engineers); dependency—whether resolving one question dissolves, reshapes, or leaves untouched the others; and whether a result would genuinely alter how we design collective decision procedures or understand individual rights within them, as opposed to providing formal closure. Please write in British English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable should be a defended research agenda, not a survey of the literature.
