I've been working through some readings for a philosophy of mind course that treats personal identity not as a substance but as a center of narrative gravity—the point around which an autobiographical story organizes itself, with coherence and practical reasoning taking the place of any hidden Cartesian core. After discussing this with a study partner over coffee, she constructed what she thinks is a reductio: if the self is genuinely nothing more than such a center, then the view should apply consistently wherever the functional conditions are met, and that consistency seems to generate absurd results.

She laid out the argument in numbered steps:

(1) If the self is only a center of narrative gravity, then any entity that sustains a continuous, coherent first-person narrative with integrated practical reasoning counts as a self.
(2) These conditions—internal consistency, accessible memory structures, behavioral control—are purely functional; they do not depend on being realized in one particular brain, body, or temporal slice.
(3) Therefore, if a narrative center can be functionally duplicated—imagine a split-brain case in which both hemispheres maintain continuous, non-communicating autobiographies—both duplicates satisfy the conditions for being that self.
(4) But prior to the split there was only one center. After the split, neither hemisphere enjoys privileged continuity with the pre-split narrative; both inherit it equally.
(5) So the original self either survives as both duplicates, violating uniqueness, or survives as neither, meaning a purely functional division can annihilate a self without destroying either substrate.
(6) The same reasoning applies across ordinary sleep: each morning, a narrative center resumes with memory access to the previous day's story but without direct experiential continuity.
(7) Hence either the self dies every night and is replaced by a narrative successor, or the criteria are so permissive that a meticulously constructed fictional autobiography—say, a novel written with perfect internal consistency—possesses a self in precisely the same sense.
(8) Since neither nightly death nor literary selves are acceptable, the narrative-gravity theory must secretly rely on a hidden substance criterion it officially rejects.

She suspects the error lies in the move from (3) to (5), but she also thinks (6) might be doing hidden work. I feel there is a slide somewhere—perhaps an equivocation between functional role and token identity, or between causal continuity and narrative continuity—but I cannot pinpoint it. Could you identify which step actually fails, explain why it seemed compelling, and assess what abandoning it costs? Specifically, must the narrative theorist accept split selves, nightly replacement, or fictional persons, or is there a less damaging revision—say, tightening the coherence conditions or introducing a causal-constraint clause—that preserves the anti-substantialist core?
