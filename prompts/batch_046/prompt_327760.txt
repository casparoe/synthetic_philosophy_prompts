I'm preparing a feature on whether classic arguments for innate linguistic structure survive recent learning-theoretic models, and I want to include an adjudicated exchange I observed at a closed workshop between Maya Chen, who studies unsupervised syntactic induction, and Jonas Okonkwo, who specializes in acquisition and domain-specificity. They debated whether the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for innate syntax is genuinely undermined by sparse-corpus induction. Their disagreement proceeded in four moves.

First, Maya argued that recent models show hierarchical phrase structure can be recovered from very small, noisy linguistic corpora using a domain-general recursive chunking heuristic—adjacent recurring pairs are grouped into units, then the same rule applies to those units—so the stimulus is not actually poor, and syntax can be induced without language-specific priors.

Second, Jonas replied that this heuristic is not genuinely domain-general: for it to apply selectively to linguistic input rather than every temporal sequence, the learner needs a prior bias that treats specific acoustic and prosodic boundaries as syntactically relevant. That bias is just Universal Grammar distributed across a mechanism rather than a module, so the poverty argument survives intact.

Third, Maya countered that the tuning is not language-specific. The same recursive chunking explains infant segmentation of musical phrases and adult parsing of social-role hierarchies, which suggests the mechanism is a general working-memory constraint on hierarchical embedding. If it solves syntactic learning without innate linguistic knowledge, then the poverty argument collapses.

Fourth, Jonas answered that those other domains exhibit the same underdetermination—musical structure requires prior knowledge of meter, and social hierarchies depend on heavy scaffolding—so the mechanism does not eliminate innate structure but merely relocates it. Calling the bias "domain-general" is therefore a misdirection: the poverty of the stimulus reappears in every domain the heuristic touches.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything significant both parties are missing—particularly any hidden assumptions about what counts as domain-general, or about the evidentiary weight of cross-domain analogies.
