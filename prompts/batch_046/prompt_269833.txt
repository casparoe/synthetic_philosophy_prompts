I cover science and ideas for a magazine and lately I've noticed a specific argumentative slide in genetics coverage that I don't have a word for. A reporter or commentator cites a twin study showing heritability around 0.75 for some trait—say, educational attainment or a mental-health measure—in a particular, relatively homogenous cohort. Then they treat that number like a pie-chart split inside every individual: genes "explain" 75%, enviroment gets the leftover 25%. When someone corrects them—heritability is population-level variance, not personal causation—they don't back off; they pivot to a second move. They say okay, but since the environment is already pretty uniform in that sample, the 25% residual is basically the entire "budget" for any policy that changes conditions for everyone, so big social interventions are capped at that slice. I've seen this in debates about school funding and also in coverage of psychiatric genetics.

It's not the plain old "heritability equals immutability" error, because they accept the statistical framing; it's also not the reaction-range idea, since they're not talking about individual plasticity but about aggregate intervention ceilings. Is there an established term for this two-step—variance-partition misread followed by treating the residual share as a policy budget? If there isn't, can you name and define it, and say exactly where its edges are: how it differs from genetic determinism, from the non-shared-enviroment emphasis, and from ordinary misunderstanding of heritability? Give me the bottom line in the first sentence—name it or declare it unnamed with a proposed label—then the reasoning with no preamble and don't restate the question.
