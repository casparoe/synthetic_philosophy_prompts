I am a doctoral candidate in comparative cultural memory, and I am writing to ask for your adjudication of an argument that arose in my dissertation workshop between two fellow students—Maya and Jonas—about whether Dunkirk belongs in the standard taxonomy of “lost-cause” national myths alongside the Confederacy, Masada, and Kosovo.

Maya opened by insisting that it does. She argued that the 1940 evacuation was, militarily, a catastrophic rout: the British Expeditionary Force was driven to the coast, abandoned its heavy equipment, and rescued only through improvised maritime effort. Yet the event was immediately narrativized as the “miracle of Dunkirk,” a framing that suppressed strategic failure and transformed withdrawal into deliverance. For Maya, the “cause” that was lost was not merely tactical but ideological: Britain’s pre-war commitment to large-scale continental land warfare was abandoned in favor of an insular, maritime, and eventually imperial identity. In this respect, she said, Dunkirk functions structurally like the Confederacy’s “Lost Cause”: both recast strategic defeat as the founding moment of a revised national character, allowing the defeated strategic orientation to be mourned without being acknowledged as failure.

Jonas replied that this overextends the category to the point of vacuity. He proposed that Masada, Kosovo, and the Confederacy share three precise structural features: (1) a social group that identifies with a defeated political or religious project; (2) a narrative in which that defeat is interpreted as noble, betrayed, or sacrificial rather than merely unsuccessful; and (3) the institutional non-victory of that specific group—that is, the custodians of the myth do not control the state apparatus that now governs the territory in question. By this criterion, Jonas said, Dunkirk clearly fails on the third condition: Britain won the war, and the very army evacuated from Dunkirk formed the nucleus of the eventually victorious force. The Dunkirk myth, he argued, is not sustaining a defeated subgroup; it is celebrating the resilience of a victor-in-progress. To call it a lost cause, he concluded, empties the concept of its distinguishing power.

Maya answered that Jonas’s third condition is circular: it defines “lost cause” by the eventual political outcome rather than by the narrative mechanism itself. She pointed out that the Confederacy’s “Lost Cause” mythology persisted long after the Civil Rights Movement had altered institutional power in the American South, and that Kosovo’s myth continues to structure Serbian identity despite the absence of Serbian state control over Kosovo. From this she derived a stricter, non-circular criterion: what distinguishes a lost-cause myth is not institutional defeat but “mnemonic custodianship”—the presence of a dedicated social mechanism (a church, a heritage organization, a liturgical calendar) whose explicit function is to preserve the narrative of defeat across generations. Masada, she noted, lacks living custodians and survives only through archaeological and interpretive tradition; Kosovo has the Serbian Orthodox Church; the Confederacy had the United Daughters of the Confederacy. Dunkirk, by contrast, lacks any such custodial body separate from the British state itself, because the state co-opted the narrative for victory. Thus, she concluded, Dunkirk should be classified not as a lost-cause myth but as a “foundational withdrawal narrative”—a distinct genre that mimics the affective structure of defeat without possessing its custodial architecture.

Jonas countered that Maya had now made the category unfalsifiable. If any strategic pivot can be narrated as the loss of an implicit “cause” (such as Britain’s continental commitment), and if “mnemonic custodianship” can be assigned retroactively to anyone who retells the story, then the distinction between a lost-cause myth and any other national founding narrative collapses. He insisted on a stricter formulation: a lost-cause myth requires (a) an explicit ideological cause that was defeated; (b) a community that continues to affirm the moral validity of that cause despite its defeat; and (c) that community’s absence from control over the governing institutions of the relevant territory. He granted that Masada is an edge case—there, the “cause” (Zealot resistance to Roman rule) is explicit, and custodianship is purely interpretive—but he maintained that even in that edge case, condition (a) is satisfied. In Dunkirk, he said, there is no analogous explicit “cause”: there was only a tactical decision to evacuate, not a defeated ideological project. The myth of Dunkirk concerns resilience and survival, not the moral vindication of a lost strategic orientation.

Maya’s final move was to press Jonas on the consistency of his own criteria. If condition (c) is taken literally, she said, then Masada should be excluded, since there were no survivors to form a non-governing community. Jonas must therefore either weaken (c)—in which case Dunkirk might still be excluded, but only through ad hoc adjustment—or concede that his taxonomy is built to preserve the canonical examples rather than to capture a genuine structural kind. She also argued that “cause” need not be an explicit political program articulated by a party; it can be a strategic orientation whose abandonment carries ideological weight, and that Britain’s post-1940 pivot away from continental commitment was precisely such an orientation.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess the strength of Maya’s opening comparison, Jonas’s three-part criterion, Maya’s custodianship rejoinder, Jonas’s charge of unfalsifiability, and Maya’s final consistency challenge. For each step, indicate whether the argument is clearly mistaken, partially sound, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then state which party is more right overall about the classification of Dunkirk, and identify at least one substantive issue—perhaps about the role of explicit ideology versus implicit strategic orientation, or about whether custodianship can be separated from state power—that both accounts overlook or under-theorize. I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I want a point-by-point conceptual assessment of the arguments exactly as they have been made.
