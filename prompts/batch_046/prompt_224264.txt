I am drafting a novel centered on a composer who, persuaded that a singular harmonic vision demands the destruction of a rival’s instrument, acts not from envy but from what he takes to be an aesthetic absolute transcending conventional moral law. In working through Kierkegaard’s figure of Abraham, I have come to treat the teleological suspension of the ethical not as a temporary exemption granted to an exceptional agent, but as a structural feature of any narrative in which a singular purpose genuinely supersedes the universal: within the fiction’s frame, the ethical norms are not violated but rendered inapplicable, and this inapplicability extends to the reader’s judgment, since to condemn the composer from within the narrative logic is to mistake the singular for the universal. My difficulty is this. If the suspension operates structurally across the narrative frame rather than remaining confined to the character’s consciousness, then by crafting the scene so that the reader perceives the destruction as necessary rather than monstrous, am I not asking the audience to perform an actual teleological suspension—transferring Abraham’s private relation to the absolute into the public aesthetic contract between author and reader? Or does the mere fictionality of the frame preserve the ethical universal intact for the reader, leaving only the protagonist suspended, so that any feeling of justification in the audience is merely a simulated sympathy rather than a real ethical exception?
