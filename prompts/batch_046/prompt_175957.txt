My study group tried to formlize Chesterton's fence inside a Burkean framework and now we have a reductio that makes Burke look like a total stasis advocate. (1) If a social practice P has survived long selection pressure, then every feature of P serves some stabilisng function we might not perceive. (2) Any reform R targets a specific feature F of P for change or removal. (3) Because our model of P is always incomplete, we can never fully rule out that F is a hidden keystone holding other parts together. (4) Chesterton's rule is: don't alter a feature unless you've shown it isn't load-bearing. (5) But showing F isn't load-bearing for this specific change requires a complete model of P, which (3) denies we ever have. (6) So the rule applies to every proposed change of every feature. (7) Therefore Burkean conservatism actually forbids all reform, including Burke's own — which is absurd. I want to hold onto (1) and (4) if I can, but (5) feels like a scope slip from "incomplete in general" to "useless for this case." Is that the break, or is there an equivocation in "shown" between local justification and global omniscience? Tell me which step to reject, what rejecting it costs for the Burkean, and whether saving reform just waters Chesterton down to "be careful."
