I've been working through comparative moral philosophy with a study group, and one analogy keeps surfacing whenever Confucian role ethics is discussed: society under that framework is like a classical orchestra, with each social role—father, son, minister, ruler—functioning as a specific instrumental part that has a fixed score, the rituals and norms of li. The analogy is usually deployed to defend the coherence of role-based morality against liberal individualism: just as an orchestra collapses if every musician improvises according to private conscience, social harmony collapses when people treat moral obligations as self-authored rather than role-constituted. Before I accept that normative conclusion, I want to audit the analogy itself.

Could you lay out the correspondence table in detail—what plays the role of the conductor, the sheet music, the rehearsal process, the acoustic space, and the audience? Then identify the precise fractures: for instance, do Confucian roles carry the reciprocal, two-directional structure that instrumental parts lack, so that the father-son or ruler-minister relation is not a one-way transmission of authority but a mutual conditioning? Does the analogy quietly smuggle in a hierarchical, top-down authority that misrepresents the corrective possibility of remonstrance, where a subordinate's moral protest is not "dissonance" but part of the score itself? And does it assume that any departure from assigned role-performance is moral failure, rather than recognizing that role ethics itself requires adaptive interpretation—what some call the elasticity of yi within li?

Finally, tell me whether the anti-individualist conclusion survives these disanalogies, or whether the orchestra image was carrying the normative load all along. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the argument from those that are merely decorative. If the analogy fails at the crucial points, suggest a more accurate analog—perhaps one drawn from ecological interdependence or linguistic pragmatics—that can accommodate reciprocity, moral protest, and interpretive flexibility without collapsing into pure individual choice, and explain why it handles those features better.
