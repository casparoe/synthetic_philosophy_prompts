Consider the following argument, paraphrased from a classic nineteenth-century treatise on parliamentary sovereignty: the legislature possesses unlimited legal authority to make or unmake any law whatsoever, and from this it is inferred that no present legislature can bind a future one. Any statute that attempts entrenchment—say, by requiring a special procedure for its own repeal—remains vulnerable to repeal by the ordinary legislative process, so the entrenching clause is legally void. The text presents this not as a contingent political weakness but as a structural feature of sovereignty itself, yet it is unclear whether the failure it describes is logical, temporal, or jurisdictional.

I am trying to understand whether this reasoning conflates two distinct failures. On one reading, it is a problem of logical self-reference: a single-level normative system tries to include a rule that governs its own conditions of revision, producing the kind of reflexive instability seen in formal paradoxes of self-amendment. On another reading, it is a problem of temporal identity: successive legislatures are differently constituted bodies, so treating them as the same sovereign across time is what makes entrenchment impossible, not any internal logical contradiction in the rule-structure itself.

Could you help me disentangle these two readings? Specifically, does the original formulation implicitly presuppose a flat, non-hierarchical model of legal rules in which every norm operates at the same logical level? And if one introduces an explicit meta-rule—one that assigns revision authority to distinct levels without claiming that the meta-rule applies to itself—does that dissolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate the problem of legislative continuity? I want to see exactly where the nineteenth-century reasoning is genuinely vulnerable to the logical objection and where it is only vulnerable to a mischaracterization of how sovereignty persists over time, particularly when the system is reconstructed with layered norms rather than a single sovereign will.
