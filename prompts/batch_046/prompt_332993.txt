Write a short story about a polity in which the handicap principle—drawn from evolutionary biology but here institutionalized—determines civic authority. In this society, citizens advance not by election or wealth but by voluntarily adopting irreversible cognitive limitations: for example, permanently suppressing episodic memory beyond a fixed temporal window, or disabling one’s capacity for probabilistic reasoning. The severity of the handicap functions as a costly signal of commitment to collective decision-making, and only those who bear the deepest limitations sit in the highest deliberative chamber. Center the narrative on a senior councilor who faces an imminent crisis that demands rapid, unified action. She holds a moderate handicap that qualifies her for advisory roles, but promotion to the crisis chamber requires her to accept a further, permanent limitation—one that will render her incapable of understanding the statistical models predicting the crisis’s trajectory. The story should explore whether her willingness to accept the deeper handicap demonstrates genuine trustworthiness or merely ritual compliance, whether the system still selects for judgment worthy of authority or has begun to filter for performative self-harm, and whether the informational cost of the signal has finally exceeded its social value. End without moral resolution, leaving ambiguous whether the handicap remains a meaningful credential or has collapsed into pure ceremony.
