I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, currently drafting a long-form feature on moral responsibility in bureaucratic and automated systems. Over the course of my reporting, I've tried to work out a coherent position on Hannah Arendt's analysis of Adolf Eichmann and what is often called the banality of evil, partly because I keep encountering institutional architectures—especially in algorithmic resource allocation—where no one seems to intend harm, yet harm is systematically produced. Before I finalize the piece, I want an audit of whether my working commitments actually hang together.

Here is where I have landed: (a) Arendt's judgment that Eichmann's evil was banal should be read primarily as a failure of reflective judgment rather than as a deficit of empathy or the presence of monstrous intent; (b) judgment, on her account, is not a solitary mental operation but an essentially interlocutory practice that requires a public space in which one can appear and be held accountable; (c) therefore, bureaucratic structures that insulate actors from the visible consequences of their decisions do not merely corrupt judgment but actively suppress the conditions under which it is possible; (d) applying this to my reporting, I treat modern automated decision systems—those that distribute public goods without direct human review—as institutionalizing a kind of distributed thoughtlessness: harm occurs without anyone exercising the judgment that could have prevented it; (e) nevertheless, I hold with Arendt that evil remains properly an attribute of human action, so that if no human agent is in a position to think or judge, there is no proper subject of moral condemnation, only a structural failure; (f) I also believe that Arendt's refusal to describe Eichmann's evil as radical was partly strategic, aimed at resisting the mythologization of evil as monstrous, which allows ordinary people to disclaim their own capacity for wrongdoing; and (g) consequently, I want to say that algorithmic architectures are dangerous precisely because they extend this bureaucratic suppression of judgment, yet I simultaneously want to deny that such architectures can themselves be evil or thoughtless in the full moral sense.

Do these commitments form a consistent package? Specifically, I am worried that (d) applies Arendt's concept of thoughtlessness to non-agential systems, while (e) and (g) seem to restrict that concept to agents capable of judgment—creating a tension I may not have resolved. I am also unsure whether (c) and (f) pull in opposite directions: if judgment requires publicity, then bureaucratic insulation is the primary danger, but if the strategic point is to prevent mythologizing, then emphasizing structural danger might inadvertently recreate a kind of system-level monstrosity. Please audit this set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and rank them by severity, indicate which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension specify what the least costly revision would be and what it would commit me to giving up.
