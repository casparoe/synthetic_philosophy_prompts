Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Dr. L. Chen and Prof. M. Okonkwo, who hold opposing views on whether split-brain patients host one conscious subject or two. Their exchange should centre on a hypothetical but conceptually precise case: a patient with complete corpus callosotomy but an intact anterior commissure, who in a bimanual task selects contradictory objects with each hand, verbally reports only one preference (confabulating a reason for it), yet reveals through a non-verbal button-press interface that the right-hemisphere-guided hand maintains its opposing preference without awareness in the left-hemisphere narrative.

Chen should defend the unity thesis: that there remains a single conscious subject whose access is fragmented, and that the left-hemisphere interpreter’s confabulation is evidence of one mind trying to maintain coherence across dissociated streams, not proof of a second mind. Okonkwo should defend the division thesis: that the intact anterior commissure permits subcortical emotional synchronisation but not phenomenal binding, so the right hemisphere’s autonomous goal-directed behaviour, coupled with its inability to be reported or corrected by the left, constitutes a genuinely separate centre of awareness.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should address the other’s specific claim about the anterior commissure, about whether confabulation is integration or suppression, and about what the hidden button-press result actually demonstrates. Neither should be a strawman. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. After the dialogue, include a brief reflective note in which you state how confident you are in the philosophical accuracy of the strongest argument advanced on each side, and single out the specific point—whether about subcortical binding, the evidential status of confabulation, or the interpretation of the button-press data—about which you are least sure.

Write in British English, using formal but accessible language.
