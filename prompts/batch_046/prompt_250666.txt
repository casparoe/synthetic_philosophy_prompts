Design a realistic, one-paragraph workplace or clinical thought experiment—no trolleys, no perfect predictors, no science fiction—in which indirect normativity (a norm binds an agent only through the deliverance of an idealized deliberative procedure) and direct normativity (the norm applies to the agent's actual situation regardless of deliberative access) yield opposite verdicts. The divergence must be driven not by ignorance, bias, or cognitive limitation, but by a structural feature of idealization itself: that fully rational deliberation requires abstracting away contextual attachments—specific professional roles, relational commitments, or institutional loyalties—that are constitutive of the agent's practical identity and therefore partly constitutive of the normative structure of the situation. Present the scenario, then for each theory state the specific verdict, the evaluation method used, the relevant counterfactual conditions or "nearby worlds," and why each theory gives the result it does. Isolate the exact feature doing the work, show whether removing it collapses the case back into agreement, and present the strongest reply available to the indirect normativist—specifically, a modification of the idealization conditions that preserves the attachments rather than an abandonment of the view. Take a definite, committed position on whether that modified defense succeeds, and give your reasons; do not merely lay out both options and leave the choice to the reader.
