I am writing an essay for my political philosophy seminar on whether “Western values” is a coherent descriptive category or primarily a rhetorical boundary-marker, and I would be grateful for your comments on its accuracy, structure, and potential blind spots. My central claim is that the phrase functions less as a taxonomy of shared beliefs and more as a performative device whose specific content shifts according to the exclusions it needs to authorize at a given moment. I distinguish three broad phases in its usage: the late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century reference to “Christian civilization”; the mid-twentieth-century Cold War framing of “the West” against Soviet communism; and the post-1989 prominence of “Western values” precisely in the absence of a single geopolitical antagonist, which allows selective deployment—sometimes to include liberal democracies regardless of geography, sometimes to exclude illiberal movements within those same societies.

Rather than enumerating values and testing for shared presence, I propose analyzing a recurring rhetorical structure with two components. First, a universalist claim—e.g., that freedom, human dignity, or the rule of law is affirmed. Second, an implicit mechanism of exclusion that treats rejection of the claim not merely as false but as evidence that the rejecter stands outside the community of reason and moral consideration being addressed. I argue that this structure explains why the listed values vary—sometimes stressing secularism, sometimes religious heritage, sometimes market economies—without undermining the phrase’s political utility.

I then address what I take to be the strongest counter-argument: that there is genuine substantive overlap across societies conventionally called Western, including institutional commitments to the rule of law, individual rights, and representative governance, and that this overlap makes the category descriptively meaningful despite fuzzy boundaries. I respond that institutional similarity does not establish a distinctive value-set, since comparable institutions exist in societies not labeled Western, and that “Western values” is rarely invoked neutrally to describe those institutions but rather to authorize specific policies or draw membership lines.

Finally, I ask whether recognizing this performative function requires abandoning the phrase altogether, or whether it can be rehabilitated as an aspirational self-description for a particular political tradition rather than a geographic or cultural designation.

Please comment specifically on the following: (1) whether my historical framing of the shift from “Christian civilization” through “the West” to “Western values” is accurate or dangerously oversimplified; (2) whether the two-part rhetorical structure I identify is genuinely distinctive to this phrase or applies to virtually any universalist moral language; (3) whether my reply to the substantive-overlap objection is convincing, or whether I am illegitimately conflating institutional form with value content; and (4) whether there is an important distinction—say, between descriptive sociological usage and normative political usage—that I have neglected, and if so, whether that distinction weakens or strengthens my overall argument. I am particularly interested in whether my analysis misses an alternative rule for the phrase’s coherence that does not depend on either geographic essentialism or pure performative emptiness.
