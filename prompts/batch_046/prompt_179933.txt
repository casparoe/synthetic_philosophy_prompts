I am a novelist working on a cycle of novels about memory and inheritance, and in researching the historians who appear in my narrative I have become tangled in what seems like a convergence between two very different ideas that share a single name. The problem of presentism in historiography—whether historians can ever judge past societies by standards other than those of their own present—strikes me as not merely an epistemic limitation but an ontological one: if every act of historical understanding necessarily imports the conceptual and moral framework of the present, then the past has no independent authority over our judgments about it. From this, I have begun to think that historiographical presentism might actually provide support for metaphysical presentism, the philosophical view that only present entities exist and that past events are in some sense unreal. After all, if the past can only ever be accessed as a present construction, does that not suggest that its being is exhausted by its present representation? I realize I may be sliding between disciplines, but I want to know precisely where the slide occurs and whether my intuition can be salvaged in some weaker form.

Please respond in four sections, in this order and approximate length: "The false convergence" (about 250 words), identifying exactly where the argument confuses epistemic access with ontological status; "Separating the two presentisms" (about 350 words), explaining why the inevitability of present-centered judgment in historiography neither proves nor requires the metaphysical claim that past objects do not exist; "What historians can actually escape" (about 250 words), addressing whether there are partial or methodological escapes from present standards that preserve the past's otherness without pretending to a view from nowhere; and "A novelist's coda" (about 150 words), suggesting how this distinction might shape a fictional historian's crisis of method without reducing it to a philosophical error.
