I've been going in circles on lost causes and national myths - Masada, Dunkirk, the Kosovo Field story, the Confederacy's Lost Cause. Here's an argument I built that feels like it should collapse but I can't see where.

(1) If a false historical narrative is counterfactually indispensible to a group's collective agency - meaning they literally wouldn't coordinate large-scale action without it - then preserving the myth is morally justified, even if it glorifies defeat or sanitizes violence.

(2) The Masada seige myth, the Dunkirk evacuation framing, and the Kosovo Covenant narrative are all factually distorted, yet each is genuinely indispensible in this counterfactual sense for the groups that hold them.

(3) The Lost Cause narrative - that the Confederacy fought for states' rights rather than slavery, that secession was noble, that defeat was a tragedy - serves the exact same counterfactual function for parts of the American South today, structuring mutual aid and political identity.

(4) So by (1), we have equal moral reason to preserve the Lost Cause.

(5) But obviously we shouldn't preserve a myth that romanticizes slavery and secession.

(6) Therefore (1) is false?

I think the slip might be between "indispensible for collective agency" and "indispensible for this specific form of collective agency" - like, maybe the South could coordinate just as well around a true narrative? Or maybe there's a hidden harm condition in (1) I'm missing? I'd rather keep (1) since it captures why Masada and Dunkirk matter, but I don't want to defend (4). Which step do you reject, and what's the cost?
