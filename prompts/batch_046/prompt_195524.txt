For the final meeting of my course on the politics of knowledge and record-keeping, I want a poem that treats the archive not as a passive repository but as an active technology of power, specifically exploring a distinction I have been using in our discussions but have not found precisely named in the standard literature: what I call exclusionary silence, in which a subject never enters the record at all, and misfiled presence, in which a subject does enter but is indexed under a bureaucratic category—such as disturbance, error, or noise—that makes their own account structurally unreadable within the system. I would like this rendered as a sestina of exactly thirty-nine lines, with six stanzas of six lines each followed by a three-line envoi, using these six end-words in this rotational order: record, silence, index, voice, file, witness. The formal constraint should do conceptual work: as the end-words cycle and recombine through the stanzas, they should trace the progressive transformation of a living voice into an archived file, with the word witness gradually losing its active, first-person sense and becoming only a catalog label by the envoi. The poem should be accurate enough that someone versed in how archives produce power through classification rules—particularly the idea that knowledge is shaped by what a system permits to be said—recognizes the conceptual architecture, without reducing the idea to a named doctrine or relying on obscure citations. Please write the poem, and then provide a brief reflective note that separates the elements drawn from established archival theory from those that depend on my invented distinction between exclusionary silence and misfiled presence, and that explicitly flags where the poem takes interpretive liberties or leans on contested readings rather than presenting any single view as the settled consensus.
