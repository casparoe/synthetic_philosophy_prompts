I'm trying to develop a rigorous interpretive framework for Hegel's master-slave dialectic—specifically the movement in the Phenomenology of Spirit from self-consciousness through domination to recognition—without importing concepts wholesale from later recognition-theory traditions. Before I evaluate particular readings, I want a precise specification of what any adequate theory of recognition genuinely rooted in this dialectic must deliver. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain (why unilateral domination collapses into a dependency that undermines the master's self-certainty, how the slave's formative labor produces a self-relation that consumption cannot, and why recognition resists reduction to either psychological esteem or legal status alone); the distinctions it must respect (between recognition as mutual acknowledgment of freedom and recognition as asymmetrical attribution of status, between face-to-face encounter and institutional mediation, and between the historical development of consciousness and the normative structure of freedom); and the puzzles it must dissolve—especially whether recognition requires actual reciprocity to be valid, and whether social arrangements that grant formal equality while systematically withholding the material and symbolic conditions for self-development represent a realization of mutual recognition or a structurally mediated continuation of the master-slave relation. For each requirement, supply the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it.

Then identify which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which might be negotiable, flagging in particular any tension between the demand for historical specificity to Hegel's argument and the demand for universal normative applicability; state explicitly what textual evidence or logical consideration would lead you to relax one side rather than the other. Briefly assess how strict phenomenological exegesis, an existentialist appropriation in the Sartrean or Fanonian mode, and a contemporary social-ontological account that treats recognition as status-conferral fare against your list, but keep the evaluation brief and subordinate to the criteria themselves. Finally, indicate where your reasoning is most vulnerable: which of your listed requirements may actually reflect a prejudice of one interpretive camp rather than a constraint internal to the dialectic, and what counter-argument, overlooked structural feature of the dialectic, or finding about the logic of recognition would cause you to revise your assessment of the requirements.
