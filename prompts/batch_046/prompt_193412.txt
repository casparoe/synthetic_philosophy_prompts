my twist is that this doesn't actually escape the absurdist structure, it just relocates it. i call it 'second-order absurdism.' once you accept that there are dozens of equally valid objective goods (art, care, knowledge, etc.) and that your attraction to one is largely contingent on biography, then the subjective half of the hybrid becomes unstable under reflection. it's not that you stop caring; it's that the caring feels 'thin' — like you're holding onto a project not because it's uniquely justified, but because you happen to be the kind of creature that latched onto it. i think this creates a recursive justification failure: you ask why this project, and the answer points back to your own contingent psychology, which doesn't feel like enough to block the absurdist itch.

i try to show that this is different from wolf's own worry about 'passive fulfillment' or from the standard 'too many goods' objection. it's specifically about the modal fragility of subjective attraction when pluralism is taken seriously. i also briefly compare it to metz's more robust objectivism, arguing that even he has to smuggle in some subjective stability that pluralism undermines.
