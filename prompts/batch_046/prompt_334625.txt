For my senior philosophy seminar, I am studying a municipal commemorative policy that strikes me as philosophically divided. The ordinance removes statues of figures linked to slavery or colonial violence from central public parks, yet it retains all street names honoring those same figures, supplementing them only with small interpretive plaques describing the harm. Crucially, the policy includes an asymmetry: corrective plaques may be added only to streets named after individuals who were previously commemorated with statues; if a figure received only a street name and never a monument, the ordinance provides no mechanism for correction at all. I want to understand what view of public memory, moral responsibility, and civic honor this split practice presupposes. Does treating statues and street names differently assume that physical presence in shared space carries a normative weight that linguistic reference in an address lacks? Does the asymmetry—limiting correction to names tied to prior monuments—imply that commemorative wrong requires an earlier act of glorification, or is the practice compatible with several competing theories about which media of honor are visible enough to demand redress? Please reconstruct the implicit theory that makes both halves cohere, distinguish what the policy strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what a unified commemorative ethics—whether centered on present harm, historical accuracy, or democratic deliberation—would have to change about the current rules.
