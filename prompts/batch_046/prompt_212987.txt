I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy with some seriousness. Next Thursday I am to deliver the retirement address for Dr. Eleanor Whitmore, a colleague of thirty-five years, at an occasion attended by her family, former students, and scholars from several disciplines. Dr. Whitmore's career was marked by an unusual refusal to commit her entire research group to any single theoretical framework; she consistently distributed resources across small, independent lines of inquiry, accepting that this meant she would never produce a single, spectacular discovery of the kind that dominates headlines.

I should like the speech to take as its organising principle the Kelly criterion — specifically, the geometric-rationality argument that an agent maximising long-run growth must size each exposure in direct proportion to her perceived edge, and that over-betting relative to that edge does not merely reduce returns but introduces a non-zero risk of irreversible ruin. I want this applied not to finance but to epistemic practice: her method was, I believe, a lived version of Kelly logic, treating theoretical capital as something that must never be staked in full on one proposition. The address should therefore honour her precisely for this distributed caution, not for conventional triumph.

The audience is mixed. The philosophical point must do real structural work: there should be passages where the logic of logarithmic utility actually constrains what I can truthfully say about her achievements — for instance, I must not imply that she staked everything on one idea and prevailed, because that misrepresents both her method and the criterion's warning against over-commitment. At the same time, the speech must not alienate her family or non-specialist guests; it should feel like a genuine tribute, not a lecture.

Please write the address in formal but warm British English, with no false consolation about an afterlife or immaterial legacy — she shares my materialist convictions — and without citing specific texts, authors, or bibliographies. Make the Kelly framing integral, not decorative, and ensure that the speech lands for experts without losing everyone else.
