I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy and public culture, and we've been debating whether historical fiction owes anything specific to its subjects once they are dead. One of my classmates argued that dramatists have a duty of "epistemic fidelity"—they should not invent private mental states for real people—while another insisted that the dead lack interests and that accessibility justifies nearly any narrative license. This led me to a more specific worry about what happens when invented intimacy doesn't just embellish a life but reshapes how audiences understand a thinker's moral character.

Write an essay addressing whether dramatists and biopic creators owe the real dead a form of "posthumous narrative sovereignty"—a duty not to fabricate intimate psychological scenes, such as deathbed doubts, hidden affections, or unrecorded arguments, even when those inventions align with the subject's published theories and make them more emotionally legible to modern audiences. Consider the tension between "moral ventriloquism," in which fabricated emotional contexts alter public perception of a thinker's integrity, and the defense that since consciousness ends with death there is no subject to harm, and that pedagogical value should therefore prevail. Weigh the arguments for and against a meaningful ethical limit on fictionalizing the dead, reach and justify a conclusion about where that limit lies if anywhere, and explain whether your conclusion applies differently to public intellectuals than to private individuals. You need not provide filmographies, bibliographies, or scholarly citations; reason directly from the conceptual structure of the obligations involved.
