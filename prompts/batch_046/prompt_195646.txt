I'm a PhD student working in philosophy of mathematics, and my reading group has deadlocked over a deliberately contrived variant of Hilbert's hotel meant to test whether finitist commitments actually dissolve Cantorian paradoxes or merely relocate them. The scenario: the hotel has countably infinite rooms, but the manager operates under a strict sequential-registration rule—she may place a guest in room n only after she has placed guests in rooms 1 through n-1. At no finite time has she assigned every room; there is always a next empty room, and she never performs an "infinite step." Nevertheless, she claims the hotel is practically full, since accommodating any new arrival would require breaking the sequential rule and "skipping ahead" to an unregistered room.

Colleague A, who leans toward a potentialist or strict finitist view, argues that this shows Hilbert's paradox collapses. There is no actual infinite totality of occupied rooms—only an open-ended, never-completed process—so talk of "shifting every current guest up by one" is a modal error. You cannot reorder a collection that was never actually collected. A further insists that if one relaxes the rule and allows simultaneous reassignment (guest in room k goes to room 2k), this presupposes treating the current infinite occupancy as a completed set available for a global mapping, which the potentialist denies exists.

Colleague B, defending an actual-infinity position, replies that the registration rule is purely procedural or epistemic, not metaphysical. The rooms exist independently of bookkeeping; the failure to complete the assignment reflects a cognitive or administrative limitation, not an ontological absence. B adds that A's claim of "fullness" is incoherent without actual infinity: to say the hotel is full is to assert that the cardinality of guests equals that of rooms, but if there is no determinate infinite cardinality on either side, there is no basis for the comparison—only an indefinite "there are always more rooms." Regarding the simultaneous mapping, B argues that defining a rule (k ↦ 2k) applies distributively to each individual guest without requiring collective comprehension of an infinite totality; we do not need to "grasp" the whole set to apply a well-defined function.

Please adjudicate move by move: (1) Does A's sequential-registration variant genuinely dissolve the paradox, or does it confuse administrative procedure with metaphysical structure? (2) Is B right that "fullness" without actual infinity is incoherent, or can a potentialist consistently maintain practical saturation without cardinal comparison? (3) Does the simultaneous reassignment rule genuinely presuppose a completed infinite totality, or can a potentialist accept rule-governed redistribution without ontological commitment? (4) Is either side missing a deeper distinction—say, between mathematical existence and cognitive accessibility—that would reframe the dispute? I am genuinely undecided and would value a point-by-point assessment that says not only who is right about what, but also where both arguments are strongest and weakest. No citations or bibliographies are needed; I am looking for conceptual clarity rather than scholarly attribution.
