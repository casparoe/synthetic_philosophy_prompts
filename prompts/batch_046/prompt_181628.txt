I'm chairing this tiny grant committee for a regional arts council — we recieve like twelve apps a year, and twice now we've hit this situation where two proposals are dead even on every rubrick: same budget, same community impact score, same artistic merit, and the reviewers genuinely can't split them. I read a bit about Buridan's ass in college and it stuck with me — the idea that if there's literally no reason to pick A over B, then picking either one is rational but also kind of arbitrary, and maybe that's okay? But I need something more concrete. I'm trying to build a reusable decision flowchart for these ties, something I can run in about thirty minutes with my two other board memebers, none of us being philosophers, and then I have to sign the paper.

The principle I want turned into a procedure is basically this: rational choice doesn't require a preference ordering when options are genuinely on a par, but you have to be able to tell real parity from hidden differences, and you need rules for what tie-breakers are legitimate versus just rationalization after the fact. I also want the checklist to handle this weird variant I've been mulling over — call it recursive parity — where not only are the options tied, but any tie-breaking rule you'd apply is also tied with an alternative rule, so you can't escape the loop by going up a level. So the deliverable should be a step-by-step audit: first how to check for hidden differences, then tests for weak versus strong parity, then permitted tie-breakers like randomization or an external constraint, with explicit flags for where judgment is unavoidable and can't be dressed up as procedure. It needs to cover the case where the board will hate the answer — say the procedure says flip a coin but the applicants are politically connected — and it should include worked examples: one where the tie breaks cleanly, one where recursive parity forces a different move, and one where the flowchart tells us to reject both because we can't establish parity without circularity. Also be honest about what this can't do — like, can it ever justify picking A over B in a genuinely symmetric case without smuggling in a preference? Make it practical: I do this maybe four times a year max, there's no ethicist on call, and I need to defend whatever I write to the board. No citations needed, just the actual steps.
