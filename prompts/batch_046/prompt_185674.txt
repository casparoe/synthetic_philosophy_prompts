In debates about Quine’s “Two Dogmas of Empiricism,” philosophers frequently invoke the image of belief as a spider’s web: individual beliefs are threads, sensory experience touches only the outer strands, and logical or mathematical beliefs sit near the center, protected not by any special analytic status but only by our reluctance to disturb them because of the widespread adjustments that would be required elsewhere. The analogy is often used to support the claim that there is no principled analytic-synthetic boundary—only a gradient of centrality and revisability. Audit this analogy precisely. First, lay out the explicit mapping: what plays the role of the threads, the spider, the center versus the periphery, and the act of repairing or cutting a strand? Second, identify where the mapping breaks: does a web have anything corresponding to the normative or inferential relationships between beliefs? Can it represent the asymmetry between changing a logical law and changing an empirical generalization, given that both might require peripheral adjustments? Does the image implicitly treat beliefs as spatially arranged nodes rather than as propositions with representational content, and if so, does that smuggle in a structuralist picture that makes Quine’s holism seem inevitable rather than argued for? Third, ask whether the conclusion—the dissolution of the analytic-synthetic distinction—actually follows from the web structure, or whether the web is only an illustration of a conclusion reached on independent grounds about the interdependence of meaning and belief. Finally, if the analogy is deficient, suggest whether a different image—perhaps a legal system with constitutional and statutory provisions, or a navigational chart revised in light of new landmarks—would do the work more honestly, or whether the failure of the web image undermines the argument it is meant to carry.
