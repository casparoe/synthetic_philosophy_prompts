I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have drafted notes for a short essay on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. I want to move beyond the usual formulation—“utilitarianism permits sacrificing one person for the greater good”—and argue that the real force of the objection is structural. In object-oriented design, encapsulation treats an object’s internal state as not globally readable or writable; analogously, I think the objection treats moral agents as ontologically encapsulated, with internal projects and welfare that cannot be pooled into a shared computation. Utilitarian aggregation, even with equal weights, violates this by making agent-boundaries transparent interfaces rather than normative barriers. I therefore claim that quantitative reforms of utilitarianism miss the point, because they preserve the very transparency that the objection denies.

My concern is that I may be importing an engineering metaphor that distorts the philosophical argument. Specifically, encapsulation sounds like an informational-privacy constraint, whereas the separateness objection might be about normative authority—who may decide over whom—rather than about data access across boundaries. I also worry that framing persons as “encapsulated objects” sounds reductive in a way that undermines the moral seriousness of the claim.

Could you comment on whether this framing is a faithful development of the standard objection or a category error? In particular, I would value feedback on whether the encapsulation analogy illuminates why weighted-utilitarian rules still feel inadequate, or whether it simply replaces a moral problem with a systems-design problem. If the analogy holds, does it strengthen the objection against modern preference-utilitarian versions, or does it collapse into a claim about cognitive bias? I am not looking for citations or a literature review—just an assessment of whether the conceptual move is coherent and interesting.
