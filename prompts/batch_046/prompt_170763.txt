I'm drafting a novel about obsession and the sea, and I keep circling back to Moby-Dick — not as background but as a philosophical case study. I want to read Captain Ahab's refusal to turn back after all the damage, the lost crew, the smashed boats through the sunk-cost fallacy: is honoring sunk costs always irrational? But I want to go past the Econ 101 version. There's this niche framing I'm playing with — call it narrative rationality — where quitting doesn't just waste past investment but collapses the story you've built about who you are. So: does Melvill present Ahab's persistence as pure fallacy, or does the novel dramatize a kind of rationality to sunk costs that philosophy usually ignores — something about existential commitment or the coherence of a life? I'm especially interested in the quaterdeck scenes and the gold doubloon nailed to the mast: do those show him calculating and choosing the cost, or are they just more monomania?

I was discussing this with my writing group and they said I'm overthinking it, but I think the text actually resists a clean answer. I'd like a reading that takes both the book and the decision-theoretic idea seriously, that points to specific scenes where the idea illuminates Ahab's choice and where the novel pushes back. Don't reduce the story to an illustration, and don't twist the philosophy to fit the plot. If there's a place where the work adds something to the debate that argument alone can't capture — maybe about how sunk costs feel different when they're embodied in a crew's bodies rather than in dollars — I'd like to hear it. No need for bibliographies or citations; I just want the critical reading.
