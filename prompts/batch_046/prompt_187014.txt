I am writing an extended essay for my philosophy society on Gödel's modal ontological argument, and I have tried to formalise an extension of his reasoning that appears to demonstrate something far stronger — and, I think, plainly absurd — about the multiplicity of perfect beings. I should be grateful if you could identify the precise step where the reasoning collapses, explain why that step initially seemed defensible, and indicate what each standard response — the essentialist defence, the modal realist concession, and the hyperintensional rejection — is forced to surrender.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Gödel defines a property as positive if and only if it is purely perfect or good in itself, independent of contingent circumstances.
(2) He assumes that for any collection of positive properties, if they are jointly consistent, there exists an essence that entails exactly those properties.
(3) If an essence E is possibly exemplified and entails every positive property, then E is necessarily exemplified by a being that exists in all worlds.
(4) Now introduce what I shall call an indexical perfection property: being the maximally perfect being relative to world-index i, where i ranges over every metaphysically possible world.
(5) For each world Wi, the corresponding indexical essence Ei entails all positive properties that are non-contingently available in Wi, and lacks all negative ones.
(6) By (3), each Ei is necessarily exemplified, so in every world Wi there exists a being Gi that is maximally perfect relative to Wi.
(7) But if Gi is defined relative to Wi, then in another world Wj (where Wi is merely possible), Gi either fails to exist or fails to possess world-relative properties tied to Wi.
(8) Consequently, no single being possesses maximal perfection across all worlds; instead, perfection fragments into a plurality of world-bound beings.
(9) This contradicts the classical theistic claim that God's perfection is absolute and world-independent.
(10) Therefore, either Gödel's closure principle for positive properties is invalid when applied to indexical essences, or the very notion of world-independent maximal greatness is incoherent.

I am particularly reluctant to abandon (3), since it captures the heart of the modal argument, and I also wish to retain (4) because indexical properties seem metaphysically respectable. Where should the rejection occur? Furthermore, please say what would change your mind about this diagnosis — specifically, which distinction or counter-argument would persuade you that the error lies elsewhere — and indicate where your own reasoning is most vulnerable to failure. Please respond in British English.
