I am preparing a 90-minute session for an upper-level political philosophy seminar of about sixteen students, and I want them to experience the democratic boundary problem—the circular question of who belongs to the demos that authorizes democratic decisions—not as an abstract puzzle but as a practical paralysis they generate themselves. The students have encountered general discussions of democratic legitimacy, but they have not studied any named technical solution, and I do not want them to reproduce standard textbook answers. Please design a fully runnable classroom activity, complete with roles, rules, materials, a small-stakes incentive structure, a structured debrief, and an explicit admission of which feature of the problem the exercise cannot reproduce.

Call the exercise “The Watershed Republic Charter Game.” Divide the class into four groups of four; each group is a Constituent Assembly tasked with writing Article 1—defining membership in the demos—for a fictional shared governance body controlling a river system that crosses borders, generations, and species lines. Give each group a Charter Kit containing eight cards: a current citizen, a long-term resident non-citizen, a sixteen-year-old, a permanently unconscious adult, a child born in 2100, a non-human animal affected by pollution policy, an artificial system that manages water distribution and expresses policy preferences, and a corporation with legal standing. Each group opens a sealed envelope containing exactly one membership criterion, phrased as a concrete test rather than an academic label—(a) born within the territory; (b) currently resident and contributing labor; (c) able to understand and respond to reasons about collective rules; (d) causally affected by the Assembly’s decisions; (e) able to give or withhold consent; or (f) alive at the time of decision. They have fifteen minutes to apply it consistently, sorting every card into “Member” or “Non-Member.”

Once the piles are locked, exchange kits: each group becomes a Challenge Council facing another Assembly’s rule. They then receive a hidden Shadow Card describing an entity identical to one of the original Assembly’s members except for the precise property that distinguishes it under the Assembly’s own criterion—for instance, if the criterion was (c), the shadow pairs the unconscious adult with the artificial system running structurally identical reasoning processes, or pairs the citizen with a future person who will be affected but is not yet alive. The Council has ten minutes to show that the rule either wrongly includes or wrongly excludes by its own logic. After the challenge, the original Assemblies must revise Article 1, but they are now bound by a Recursive Amendment Rule: any change to membership requires approval by two-thirds of all entities affected by the change, including those currently classified as Non-Members. Give them fifteen minutes to try to break the circle; they will discover they cannot invoke democratic procedure to fix the boundary, because the procedure presupposes the boundary. Groups earn “Legitimacy Points” for internal consistency but lose points if they reach deadlock; there is no material prize, only the public board record.

In the thirty-minute debrief—only after the deadlock has been felt—name the boundary paradox and map each phase onto it: the initial sorting felt obvious until the Shadow Cards revealed arbitrary cutoffs; the Challenge Phase showed every criterion over-including or under-including; the Recursive Rule made the circularity practical, since they literally could not vote on who gets to vote. Predict the failures: groups will try to smuggle in hybrid criteria like “rational and affected,” which you should allow and then expose as multiplying rather than solving the boundary questions; others will appeal to “common sense,” at which point you ask whose common sense counts and watch the problem return. Be frank about the limitation: the activity reproduces the logical circularity and the arbitrariness of criteria very well, but it cannot reproduce the lived temporal weight of real intergenerational obligation—future people and ecological entities are present only as cards, not as challengers—so the facilitator must supply that missing moral pressure in the debrief by pressing what we owe to those who cannot sit at the table. Specify exactly what materials are needed (the eight-card kits, six sealed envelopes, Shadow Card deck, score sheet) so a substitute could run it without philosophical background.
