I have been developing a refinement of the Kuhnian framework that I believe sidesteps several familiar objections, though I worry it may be exposed elsewhere. Rather than treating paradigm shifts as either wholesale replacements of conceptual schemes—where terms such as 'mass' or 'planet' undergo total semantic change—or as incremental adjustments within a stable framework, I defend a position of normative-epistemic incommensurability. On this view, rival paradigms retain substantial lexical and observational overlap; scientists can partially communicate about anomalies and data. What fractures is not language or ontology per se, but the hierarchy of explanatory virtues—simplicity, predictive breadth, coherence with background metaphysics, tolerance for ad hoc adjustments—such that no neutral, paradigm-independent metric can adjudicate between competing theories. The shift is therefore best understood as a restructuring of scientific values rather than a gestalt switch in perception or a rupture in shared meaning.

If this framing holds, then classic criticisms of Kuhn—particularly the charge that incommensurability renders rational choice impossible or that rival scientists literally talk past one another—lose much of their force, since they presuppose a semantic rather than axiological divergence. Nevertheless, relocating incommensurability into the domain of normative judgment may render the view vulnerable to distinct defeaters. I would like you to map what would genuinely count against this position: which historical cases, formal results concerning theory choice, or intuitions about rational disagreement would undermine the claim that paradigm rivalry is fundamentally a clash over weighted explanatory virtues? Please distinguish between evidence that would be decisive—say, demonstrations that virtue-rankings are themselves derivable from shared methodological rules, or cases where scientists with identical value hierarchies still fail to converge—and objections that a committed defender could absorb through qualification, rendering them merely awkward. In particular, does this insulation—achieved by preserving partial communication and shared observational language—risk collapsing the distinction between revolutionary and cumulative science altogether? I am especially interested in whether you think the view is well insulated or whether its qualifications come at a prohibitive cost.
