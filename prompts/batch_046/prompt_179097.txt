I'm a novelist drafting an essay for a fiction workshop on how narrative form relates to the thermodynamic arrow of time. My central analogy is that conventional plot structure implicitly relies on something like the cosmological past hypothesis: the opening establishes a localized, low-entropy state—sharp character distinctions, unambiguous causal chains—while resolution tends toward an equilibrium in which those distinctions stabilize or dissolve. I want to know whether this is a genuine structural parallel or merely a metaphor that confuses epistemic tracking with ontological asymmetry. Specifically, if a story is told in reverse chronological order—scenes arranged backward while causal relations within each scene remain intact—does that invert an objective temporal arrow, or does it only scramble the reader's epistemic access to an unchanged causal sequence? I am also wondering whether narrative closure is truly analogous to thermal equilibrium, since a resolved plot often remains informationally dense in ways that physical equilibrium is not. The essay is meant for writers, not physicists, so I need to avoid misrepresenting either statistical mechanics or narrative craft. Please comment on the accuracy and limits of the analogy, on whether reverse narration violates a narrative "past hypothesis," and on what, if anything, this comparison reveals about storytelling that a purely formalist account of plot misses.
