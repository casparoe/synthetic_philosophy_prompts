As a doctoral student in political theory working on normative ethics in institutional contexts, I have been tracking a recurring pattern in recent governance debates that does not seem to map neatly onto the standard dirty-hands literature. The pattern runs as follows. A public official sincerely endorses a moral constraint C—say, a prohibition on a particular form of coercive surveillance or on negotiating with certain non-state actors. Faced with a crisis, the official violates C, but not covertly: they publicize the violation and frame it as a necessary exception. Once the exception is visible and unpunished, opponents and successors begin to treat C as already obsolete, not because the crisis justified a one-time breach, but because the breach has demonstrated that the constraint lacks practical authority. The official's initial act thus functions not merely as a tragic choice, the classic Walzerian dirty-hands scenario, but as a strategic demolition of the normative boundary itself, using the authority of the office to make C operationally unenforceable in future deliberation.

I have observed analogues of this in at least three distinct policy domains—counter-terrorism protocols, environmental regulatory waivers, and asylum adjudication—where the first visible breach was explicitly defended as exceptional, yet its primary lasting effect was to shift the burden of proof onto those who wished to restore C. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration: a morally motivated actor deliberately rendering a constraint they value operationally void, not out of hypocrisy, but as a calculated instrumentalization of their own dirty hands? How does it differ from moral licensing, from normative erosion, and from the ordinary slippery-slope dynamics discussed in legal philosophy? If there is no precise term, what conceptual boundaries should govern a new one—particularly to distinguish cases where the dissolution of C is an unintended consequence of a genuine emergency decision from cases where the dissolution of C is itself the intended strategic outcome?
