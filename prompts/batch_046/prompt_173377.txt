My friend and I just finished a long, circular argument about inside view versus outside view applied reflexively to one's own epistemic modesty—not in forecasting tournaments, but to the first-person authority required to actually revise a belief. We kept circling the same point: if I treat my current reasoning as an output belonging to a reference class of agents with similar cognitive architecture and informational environments, I gain statistical humility; yet to act on that humility and change my mind, I seem to need an inside view that grants me direct, non-statistical access to my reasons. The paradox is that the outside view requires observing myself as an object, which threatens to undermine the very agency needed to respond to it, while the inside view risks treating its own authority as an exception that no reference class can capture. I want a poem to close our discussion. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains and only two rhymes—where Refrain A voices the inside view's claim to irreducible first-person authority over belief revision, and Refrain B voices the outside view's claim that such authority is itself a predictable feature of the reference class. The interlocking rhyme scheme and the form's compulsory repetition should structurally enact the problem: the refrains should circle each other without integration, and the final couplet should place them side by side in a way that reveals their mutual dependence rather than resolving the tension. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the literature on epistemic modesty and reference-class reasoning recognizes the positions, but it should leave the argument exactly where ours ended—with the suspicion that neither view can stand without the other, and that adopting both simultaneously may be the hidden arrogance neither admits. Do not resolve the paradox; let the form hold it open.
