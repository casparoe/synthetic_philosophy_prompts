I write fiction about people who mistake their simulations for lives, and I need a decision tree for a philosophical handout on well-being. Take Nozick's experience machine as the baseline, but do not let the taxonomy collapse into the usual yes-or-no on entering. Instead, inject two variant cases that complicate the standard map: the episodic machine, which delivers only disconnected peak moments with no narrative continuity between them, and the reverse machine, in which a subject chooses to exit into a simulated life that is objectively worse by every external metric except its narrative coherence. Build a tree whose root asks what well-being is constituted by—subjective feeling, satisfied desire, objective goods, or narrative unity—then branch each path through judgments about the standard continuous machine, the episodic variant, and the reverse option. Every leaf should name an actual position (hedonism, informed-desire satisfaction, perfectionism or capabilities-style objective-list views, narrative-identity accounts) or be marked 'unnamed,' and each must carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, especially where the episodic or reverse case exposes an internal tension. Note any unoccupied combinations in the logical space—such as a view that prizes narrative unity yet endorses the episodic machine—and diagnose briefly whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree genuinely cannot separate. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; phrase every node as a concrete case judgment or claim that someone could answer without already knowing the academic label. Keep the instrument tight: six to eight leaves, constructed from the branching questions rather than from a pre-set list of names.
