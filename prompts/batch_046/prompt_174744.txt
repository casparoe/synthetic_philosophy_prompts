At a seminar on perceptual psychology, two philosophers, Mara and Eli, debated whether semantic priming can genuinely penetrate perceptual experience rather than merely biasing post-perceptual judgment. Mara claimed that when observers are primed with threat-related concepts before viewing ambiguous point-light displays of human motion, they do not just subsequently interpret the movement as hostile; rather, hostile intentionality is present in the phenomenology of what they see, with top-down expectations altering the representational content of mid-level vision by binding features into structured objects. She argued that insisting on "early sensory stages" as the sole criterion for genuine perception is arbitrary and question-begging, since it presupposes a modular boundary that predictive processing models have undermined—after all, even low-level feature detection is shaped by prior probabilities. Eli replied that without independent access to a subject's pre-interpretive sensory state, we cannot distinguish true penetration from rapid automatic interpretation, and that Mara's position risks unfalsifiability: if every cognitive influence is redefined as perceptual, the claim collapses into a tautology. Mara countered that predictive coding operates at every level of the sensory hierarchy, so Eli's modular defense is empirically obsolete. Eli conceded that prediction errors are minimized throughout the hierarchy, but insisted this does not entail conceptual penetration in the philosophically interesting sense: the expectations can correct errors within sensory representations without the conceptual content literally entering the perceptual representation itself. He added that if Mara is correct, then expert radiologists who learn to detect tumors in noisy scans are not refining perceptual skill but merely altering beliefs, which erodes the distinction between training perception and training judgment.

Go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right about each specific point—whether the priming case demonstrates penetration, whether the "early stages" criterion is question-begging, whether predictive processing supports Mara's conclusion, and whether Eli's unfalsifiability objection succeeds. Also identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent intermediate position that allows for structured perceptual learning without full-blown conceptual penetration of experience.
