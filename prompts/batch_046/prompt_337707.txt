In neuroscience and philosophy of mind, patients with pain asymbolia report vivid nociceptive sensations yet deny any unpleasantness or motivational urgency—some even smile while describing burning or crushing feelings. For decades, one camp has used these cases to argue that pain's badness is not intrinsic to its sensory quality but depends on a separable affective or evaluative layer; the opposing camp insists that the patients either misreport, retain a hidden form of aversion, or have disrupted the representational link between sensation and bodily threat, leaving the core phenomenology altered in ways that do not license the separation thesis. Despite broad agreement on the clinical profiles and on the normative premise that suffering matters, the dispute has barely moved. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical standoff—neuroimaging of these patients remains sparse and contested—but a disagreement about what kind of explanation counts as complete: one side demands an account of phenomenal feel, the other an account of motivational architecture, and neither accepts the other's criteria of success. Is that right, or is there a hidden factual question—perhaps about whether predictive-processing models of interoception can decouple precision-weighted prediction errors from valence assignment without destroying sensory character? Diagnose whether the impasse is verbal, substantive but cross-purpose, or genuinely empirical in disguise; specify what each side actually takes as decisive data; and say what, if anything—an experiment, a conceptual distinction between phenomenal aversion and axiological disvalue, or a reframing of the question—would actually move it.
