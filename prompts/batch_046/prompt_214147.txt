As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to clarify my own judgments about what makes one mental state or life-configuration genuinely better than another, and I keep arriving at something like tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters, while positive goods such as achievement, love, or intense joy carry no independent weight. I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the following verdicts, or whether I must abandon one of them.

Consider four mental configurations, described in system-like terms:

1. High-output, high-strain mode: intense productivity, deep attachment, and strong positive affect, but accompanied by constant anxiety, desire for more, and fear of loss—like a system delivering peak throughput but running hot with perpetual error-handling loops and resource contention. Verdict: this is not the best state; the negative elements dominate.

2. Zero-strain idle mode: complete absence of craving, suffering, and anxiety, with no particular achievements or intense pleasures—like a system running cool, with no queued tasks, no alerts, and minimal resource consumption. Verdict: this is better than (1).

3. Moderate-comfort mode: mild, stable satisfaction with occasional minor frustrations and small pleasures—like a system operating within normal parameters with occasional latency spikes. Verdict: this is worse than (2), because the presence of any craving or suffering seems to outweigh moderate positive gains.

4. Optimized-flow mode: near-maximum positive goods—creativity, meaning, deep relationships—with elegant efficiency and almost no pain, but with a persistent low-level anticipatory readiness, like predictive caching that never causes failure but constantly runs in the background. Verdict: I am genuinely divided. One part of me says this is still worse than (2) because craving is present; another part says the overwhelming positives and near-absence of suffering should make it the best of all.

The principle I am drawn to ranks states solely by the absence of craving and suffering, treating all positive goods as irrelevant. But this struggles with my hesitation about (4). Is there a refined principle—perhaps one that distinguishes craving-that-hurts from craving-that-merely-readies, or that weights absence by intensity—that can preserve all four verdicts? Or must I drop one?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause or distinction handles each of the four cases above, predict its verdict for an unlisted case (a state in which craving and suffering are eliminated through artificial suppression that also eliminates all capacity for joy or attachment), and be explicit about which of my four verdicts the principle cannot accommodate if it saves the others.
