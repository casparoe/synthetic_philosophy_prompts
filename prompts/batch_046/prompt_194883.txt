i'm running a seminar on markets and belief for about 14 philosophy grad students, one 80-minute session, and i want them to feel the actual squeeze of teh no-trade idea — not just nod at the classic rational-agreement result but feel why agents with shared priors should refuse each other's bets, and then watch themselves trade anyway. can you design a classroom game with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that uses only paper chips? they should come away seeing that disagreement can survive common rationality when there's hidden divergence about how to weight private evidence, not just about the outcome itself.

here's the core mechanic: call it "the sealed envelope bazaar." one envelope holds a hidden coin-flip result (A or B). each student draws a private signal card saying "source indicates A/B" with a printed 70% accuracy rate, plus a secret "frame card" — either "data-truster" or "skeptic" — that tells them whether to treat that 70% as solid or closer to 40%. they all know the prior is 50/50 and that everyone is rational, but they must not reveal card contents. for 20 minutes they write bilateral "belief contracts" on slips (e.g., "i pay you 2 chips if B, you pay me 1 if A"), using an initial endowment of 5 paper chips. after we open the envelope, contracts settle: correct side gains chips, wrong side loses them, and net score determines who picks next week's reading. the incentives should make them construct the sequence themselves — first feel an edge, then notice that rationality should collapse any agreed price since the other person wouldn't take a bad deal, yet they still trade because they think thier hidden frame is more reliable.

for the debrief, map the three phases — feeling the edge, seeing the rationality trap, then trading through meta-epistemic distrust — onto the standard no-trade argument and its usual common-knowledge fix. be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: the infinite regress of mutual knowledge, the absence of noise traders, and the fact that the frame-card twist is my own injection rather than a named formal theory, though it captures why real-world disagreement sticks around. also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it — probably someone blurts thier card, or one student loudly explains the result and freezes all trading. keep it casual, a typo or two is fine, and don't include any reading lists, citations, or requests for bibliographies.
