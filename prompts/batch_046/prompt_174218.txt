I was discussing with colleagues over coffee last week whether the current campus debates about moral culture actually clarify anything, or whether they simply recycle old distinctions with new vocabulary. I am now delivering the opening address at a one-day interdisciplinary symposium at my university — the audience will be split evenly between moral philosophers, sociologists of culture, and undergraduate students who have only read the assigned introductory texts — and I want the speech to take that ambiguity seriously. The theme is whether any of the three well-known moral cultures — honour, dignity, and victimhood — can claim genuine moral superiority over the others, or whether asking the question already privileges one framework's internal criteria.

I want the speech to do real structural work: it should not merely mention these categories as illustrations, but should shape what can and can't be said from the podium. Specifically, please inject a somewhat niche but defensible conceptual distinction — between what we might call 'recognition-based dignity' (the Kantian strand) and 'performance-based dignity' (the social-psychological strand that measures self-worth through achievement) — and show how this distinction destabilises the usual triadic comparison. The occasion is formal but not ceremonial; I need to welcome a mixed audience without alienating the specialists, and I must avoid any bibliography, reading list, or citations — the students wouldn't follow them and the philosophers don't need them. The tone should be polished, slightly self-deprecating, and written in British English.

Please write the address, about 700 words, and ensure that the philosophical argument actually constrains the welcome rather than decorating it: for instance, if the argument implies that victimhood culture and honour culture share an unacknowledged dependence on third-party validation, then the speaker's own authority to welcome the room should be quietly problematised rather than asserted with false confidence.
