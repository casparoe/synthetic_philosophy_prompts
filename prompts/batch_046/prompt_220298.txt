Design a 90-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level metaphysics course on the persistence conditions of artifacts, using the Ship of Theseus as the entry point but structured so that it cannot be answered by reciting the standard Hobbes or Locke formulations or by simply rehearsing the material-continuity versus spatio-temporal-continuity distinction. Embed the following specific scenario: a wooden naval frigate built in 1812 is continuously repaired for two centuries, with every original plank eventually replaced and archived in a maritime museum; in 1920, an international shipping registry begins tracking the vessel not by physical overlap but by a functional-equivalence protocol that certifies continuity of design, crew lineage, and operational role; in 2024, the archived planks are reassembled into a seaworthy reconstruction that also applies for registration under the same functional-equivalence protocol, while the continuously repaired vessel remains in active service. The exam question should ask students to determine whether the registry’s conventionalist criterion solves the paradox, dissolves it, or merely relocates it; specifically, it should require them to argue whether institutional persistence conditions are metaphysically constitutive of the ship’s identity or only epistemically tracking an identity fixed by other means, and it should force them to confront the 2024 museum-reassembly case as a test of whether artifacts can be multiply instantiated without contradiction. Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words. Alongside it, provide a marking scheme out of 25 marks divided into five criteria, each worth 5 marks: (1) correctly distinguishing metaphysical persistence conditions from conventional identification practices; (2) evaluating whether functional equivalence can ground identity without circularity or collapse into arbitrary stipulation; (3) analyzing the museum-reassembly variant and explaining whether it produces a second ship, a revived original, or an illegitimate duplicate; (4) assessing whether introducing institutional conditions genuinely dissolves the paradox or only pushes it back to the identity conditions of the registry or the institution itself; (5) maintaining consistency with broader theories of artifact ontology—specifically, addressing whether artifacts have essential origins or can survive complete material replacement and simultaneous duplication. For each criterion, include a one-line note describing how a B-level answer typically falls short (for example, conflating the registry’s tracking role with a metaphysical ground, or treating the reassembly case as obviously the “real” ship without argument). The difficulty should turn on whether the student recognizes that the registry protocol introduces a conventionalist layer that must be evaluated on its own terms rather than treated as an automatic solution.
