okay so i've been chewing on benacerraf's problem about mathematical knowledge and acausal objects, and my gut reactions on different proposed escapes are all over the place. here's where i land:

- computer proof like the four-color theorem: totally counts as knowledge, even though the computer is physical and the graph properties are abstract. so direct causal contact with the objects isn't necessary.
- platonist "mathematical intuition" that lets us literally perceive abstract numbers: feels like special pleading. i don't think this saves knowledge.
- nominalism / formalism where math is just symbol manipulation or physical patterns: avoids benacerraf but then i think we're not actually talking about abstract objects, just changing the subject. i still want to say 7 is prime regardless of physical instantiations.
- structuralism: we study structures through physical instances. works for small arithmetic, but for inaccessable cardinals or huge sets there's no physical instance. yet i still think set theorists know stuff about them. so structuralism feels incomplete.
- evolutionary epistemology: our number sense evolved for tracking prey, and it's accidentally reliable for primes. this explains belief formation but not why the beliefs are true of acausal objects, so the gap is still open.
- fictionalism: we just pretend there are numbers. but then "2+2=4" isn't literally true, which clashes hard with my intuition that it's true in the strongest possible sense.

is there a single principle behind all these? maybe something like "knowledge requires a non-accidental link between belief-forming process and domain, but not causal access"? but then the evolutionary story should work, and i said it doesn't fill the gap. or maybe "either causal access to instances or a formal guarantee of consistency is enough"? that'd cover computer proofs and small numbers, but not large cardinals without instances.

what principle actually captures this pattern, which of my verdicts do i have to drop to keep it simple, and what does the winning account predict for a case i haven't listed: a mathematician who proves something about inaccessable cardinals using only analogical reasoning from small finite cases, with no computer verification and no claimed intuition? please separate what's pretty settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus. also flag any place where my intuitions are probably just wrong.
