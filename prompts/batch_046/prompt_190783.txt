Ok so there's this weird split in translation norms that keeps bugging me. If you're rendering a philosophical text and you smooth out awkward sentences for flow, reviewers will tear you apart for betraying the original — it's seen as a failure of fidelity. But with poetry, especially fixed-form stuff, the exact opposite is true: the best translations are the ones that totally rearrange literal meaning to preserve rhyme scheme and meter. Hofstadter's whole Le Ton beau de Marot project is basically a celebration of that, and the community treats those distortions as virtuous rather than sloppy.

So what philosophical commitments about language, meaning, or textual identity does that differential practice actually presuppose? Does it assume that poetic form carries semantic weight that philosophical form doesn't, or that "what a text says" can be cleanly seperated from "how it sounds" in one genre but not the other? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the split, say whether the practice strictly needs that view or is compatible with rival ones, and point out where it's philosophically underdetermined — maybe the same theory licenses both norms and we're just not noticing.

Then take a definite, committed stance: is the split actually defensible, or should we either demand formal fidelity everywhere or drop it everywhere? And if we replaced the implicit view with a uniform theory of translation — say, that form is always part of content and never disposable — what would have to change in how we teach, publish, and evaluate translations? Don't just lay out options; I want your actual position with reasons.
