Analyze the passage and enforcement of France’s 2004 statute prohibiting conspicuous religious symbols in public primary and secondary schools through the lens of what might be called “positive secularism as visual pedagogy”: the view that republican schools are not merely neutral venues protected from state religion, but active civic-training institutions that must engineer a specific visual equality by excluding visible religious particularity, on the theory that shared citizenship is produced through shared appearance rather than protected through private belief. Treat this not as abstract doctrine but as an institutional design problem. First, map the actual actors and options with discipline: the Stasi Commission (2003), the legislators who drafted the prohibition with its explicit exemption for “discreet” symbols and for universities, the school administrators who had to apply it, the students and families affected, and the higher-education rectors who were deliberately left outside its scope. For each, say what the framework of positive secularism predicts they should have faced and chosen, and whether the record—rare direct expulsion after 2004, widespread anticipatory withdrawal of headscarves, and the migration of visible religious expression into private schools and universities—matches those predictions. Second, identify what this theory explains about the episode that a purely procedural, “negative” neutrality (the state simply does not ask) cannot: why the law targeted visibility rather than belief, why it carved out universities, and why enforcement was left diffuse rather than centralized. Third, be honest about where the theory is silent or misfires: does the visual regime actually generate the republican equality it claims, or does it displace difference rather than dissolve it; does the university exemption undermine the pedagogical argument or reveal it as always already a boundary-management device rather than a universal principle; and what does the long record of minimal formal enforcement but high informal compliance show about whether positive secularism operates through law or through social pressure? Finally, use the episode to test the theory itself: is positive secularism best understood as a stable constitutional equilibrium, or as a recurring conflict-management technique that must be repeatedly renegotiated whenever new visible differences appear? Keep the analysis grounded in well-known features of the law and its aftermath; do not invent obscure enforcement statistics or rely on contested legislative intent, and say clearly where judgment is required because the framework does not determine the answer.
