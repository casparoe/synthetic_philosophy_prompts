I'm giving a seminar next Thursday defending a slightly odd take on Plutarch's Parallel Lives — I call it the triangulation thesis. Basically I think the paired biographies aren't there for direct imitation (the usual exemplarist reading) but to force the reader into a third, unresolved moral position that neither the Greek nor the Roman life actually endorses. I apply this to modern leadership hagiography — the way we write founder lives in tech and politics — arguing that these modern versions fail precisely because they suppress the structural tension Plutarch keeps visible.

The audience is a mixed interdisiplinary group: three classicists who specialise in Hellenistic biography and hate anachronism, two virtue ethicists who favour Zagzebski-style exemplarism and think biography is central to moral education, plus one historian of religion who regards all hagiography as propaganda and doesn't care about Plutarch's literary craft. They care about textual fidelity, they distrust importing modern moral philosophy into ancient texts, and the ethicists will definatly defend the idea that we learn morality by admiring concrete lives.

My sketch: I read the Alexander/Caesar and Demosthenes/Cicero pairs not as ranking one culture over the other, but as setting up contradictory virtues — magnanimity versus civic restraint — so that the reader can't settle on either without feeling the pull of the other. Modern founder biographies collapse this into a single heroic arc, which is why they're useless for genuine moral learning.
