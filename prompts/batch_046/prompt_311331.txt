Having recently observed a colleague’s professional exclusion after private correspondence was circulated without context, I have come to defend a distinction between punitive deplatforming and what I call structural delocation: the removal of an individual from a professional or social network not as retributive sanction, but as the unavoidable consequence of a community revising its membership conditions once previously hidden value-incompatibilities are revealed. I hold that the moral evaluation of such exclusions should therefore turn on whether the community’s revised criteria are themselves defensible and applied consistently, rather than on analogies to criminal punishment with its demands for due process and proportionality. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the arguments, empirical patterns, and conceptual distinctions that should move me, arranged from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, is there evidence that affected parties and audiences invariably experience these exclusions as punitive regardless of the community’s internal framing? Could it be shown that the distinction collapses under analysis — for instance, that any exclusion carrying serious material or reputational harm is functionally retributive regardless of intent? What does this position predict that a straightforward punitive account does not — say, about reversibility, community autonomy, or the role of intent — and where might those predictions fail? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing that any apparently punitive feature is merely an epiphenomenon of structural revision, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind? Please include what defenders of the distinction would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say, and be willing to conclude that the position may be well protected if that is the honest assessment.
