I've been messing with a Lucas-Penrose style argument but pushed it in a direction that feels like it should break. Here it is, with steps:

(1) Any mechanistic thinker can be modeled by a formal system F that captures all its arithmetic reasoning.
(2) Gödel's first theorem gives a sentence G_F that F can't prove.
(3) If the thinker learns a new arithmetic truth T that F missed, its belief-set becomes F + T, a strictly stronger system.
(4) That stronger system has its own Gödel sentence G'.
(5) By the usual meta-argument the thinker can see G' is true.
(6) But if it's mechanistic with system F+T, all its beliefs are theorems of F+T, and G' isn't one.
(7) So it can't learn T without falling into contradiction.
(8) Therefore mechanistic minds can never expand their arithmetic knowledge beyond what they started with -- they're arithmetically frozen.

The conclusion seems absured: computers prove new theorems all the time, and people learn math. But every step looks okay to me. Which premise do I actually reject? I want to keep the idea that mechanistic reasoning is bounded by some formal system, and I'd like to keep Gödel's result intact. So what's the cheapest fix, and what does giving up that step do to the anti-mechanist project more generally?
