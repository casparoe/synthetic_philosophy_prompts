I hold a position drawn from Keynes's 1930 essay on economic possibilities for future generations, but sharpened with a specific claim: that the good life he describes — freedom from pressing economic cares to pursue art, science, and moral improvement — depends not merely on reaching a resource threshold, but on an objective-list distinction between absolute and relative needs that makes the transition point socially determinable. I further claim that Keynes implicitly assumes the welfare of artistic and contemplative lives is intersubjectively comparable in a way that justifies a collective reduction to a fifteen-hour working week, rather than leaving labour allocation to market preference. What would count against this position? I want the decisive defeaters — textual evidence that Keynes rejected objective lists, arguments that absolute and relative needs are conceptually inseparable, welfare-economic results showing that leisure distribution cannot be justified by interpersonal comparison, and whether defenders have insulated the view by redefining need categories. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed Keynesian of this stripe would concede versus dismiss, and say whether the position now risks nothing empirically — and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a view about the good life. Please answer in British English.
