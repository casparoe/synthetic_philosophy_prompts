I've been thinking lately that Darwin and Turing both pulled the same strange inversion: Darwin gets complex design out of selection with zero comprehension of the design, and Turing gets intelligent-seeming behavior out of symbol-shuffling with zero comprehension of the symbols. I think if you take them together, the lesson is stronger than people admit: comprehension is never a necessary condition for competence -- not for bacteria, not for LLMs, and definately not for human reasoning, including the parts where we feel like we're "grasping" a proof or seeing the moral reason. But I want to be honest about what could actually knock this down. What concrete observations or arguments would count? I'm especially interested in whether there are domains -- say, normative justification where you need to understand why a rule binds you, or causal discovery where you need to represent mechanisms rather than just correlations -- where comprehension is causally required rather than epiphenomenal. Rank the defeaters: fatal, serious, awkward. And check whether the inversion has been immunized by qualifiers like "except in systems with evolutionary history" or "except for conscious agents" -- if so, is that unfalsifiability a problem? Tell me what defenders of the view would themselves concede, not just what critics say. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reasoning, no preamble, don't restate my question.
