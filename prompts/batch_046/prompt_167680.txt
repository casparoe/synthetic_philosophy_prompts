Here are notes for an essay on a symmetry I have not seen developed with care. Darwin, in Origin of Species, argues that hive-bees construct geometrically optimal cells through instinct accumulated by selection, with no individual bee comprehending geometry or functional rationale; competence is built without comprehension across generations. Turing, in his 1950 essay on machine intelligence, inverts the direction: he proposes that if a machine produces linguistically competent behavior indistinguishable from a human's, we should attribute intelligence regardless of whether we can locate internal comprehension. Both decouple observable competence from subjective or mechanistic comprehension, but Darwin uses the decoupling to naturalize away teleology, while Turing uses it to extend mental attribution beyond biology. I want to press a further asymmetry in evidential standards: for Darwin, the absence of comprehension in the bee strengthens the mechanistic explanation, whereas for Turing, the absence of demonstrable internal comprehension should not weaken the attribution. I ask whether this framing collapses important distinctions—e.g., that Darwin offers a causal history while Turing offers a criterion of ascription, or that Turing's behaviorism is methodological rather than ontological—and whether historical or philosophical objections break the symmetry. Please comment on the accuracy of these characterizations, flag any misreadings of either thinker, assess whether the parallel is illuminating or forced, and separate what is well-established in the specialist literature from what is genuinely contested, especially regarding Turing's intended ontological commitment and Darwin's views on reason in animals. Do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus view.
