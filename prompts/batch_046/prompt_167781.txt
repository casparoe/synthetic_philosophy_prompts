Consider the following examination item and student response, submitted in a graduate seminar on theories of consciousness.

Question:
In Integrated Information Theory (IIT 3.0), consciousness corresponds to the maximally irreducible cause-effect structure specified by a substrate with the highest value of integrated information (φ_max), identified via the exclusion postulate. Imagine a synthetic architecture, System R, consisting of two discrete recurrent modules, A and B, linked by symmetric bidirectional connections. Each module in isolation specifies a cause-effect repertoire with φ ≈ 40 bits. When the union A∪B is evaluated, extensive overlap in cross-boundary repertoires yields φ_max ≈ 42 bits—only marginally above the parts. Separately, a unidirectional pathway C runs from A to B, transmitting high-fidelity signals but lacking any feedback into either module; its contribution to the integrated information of A∪B is negligible. A researcher argues that (i) System R hosts two overlapping conscious complexes centered on A and B, (ii) pathway C serves as an unconscious information conduit directly analogous to cerebellar feedforward loops, and (iii) the near-parity of φ values means the boundary of consciousness in R is genuinely indeterminate, with A∪B representing merely a weakly unified “borderline” substrate.

Evaluate this researcher’s three claims. Specifically: (a) Does IIT’s exclusion postulate permit overlapping complexes within one physical substrate? (b) Is the analogy between pathway C and cerebellar circuitry defensible given what IIT says about the cerebellum’s causal architecture? (c) What does the small gap between φ(A), φ(B), and φ(A∪B) actually imply for identifying the substrate of consciousness in System R?

Student Answer:
“(a) The exclusion postulate requires that only one complex exist within any given substrate, so the researcher is wrong: A and B cannot both be complexes. However, because φ(A∪B) is only 42 compared to 40 for each part, the global maximum is barely dominant. IIT allows for practical ambiguity in such borderline cases, and in applied neuroscience it is common to treat local maxima as provisional complexes. Thus, while technically A∪B wins, the researcher is not far wrong in treating A and B as separate.

(b) The comparison to the cerebellum is strong. The cerebellum is excluded from consciousness in IIT precisely because its architecture is predominantly feedforward, lacking the recurrent loops necessary for integration. Pathway C is purely feedforward with no feedback, so it mirrors the cerebellar situation: it carries information but cannot be part of the conscious complex. Therefore C is correctly characterized as an unconscious conduit.

(c) The near-equality of the φ values indicates that the cross-boundary integration is weak relative to internal integration. This means A∪B is technically the complex, but its cause-effect structure is only weakly unified; the experience it specifies would be fragmented or less coherent than either module alone, reflecting a “fuzzy” boundary rather than a sharp one.”
