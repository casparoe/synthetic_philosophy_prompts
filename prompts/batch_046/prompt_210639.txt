I am preparing notes for an advanced seminar on practical reason, and I would like critical comments on an argument I have been developing about institutional responses to genuinely incommensurable values. In particular, I am interested in what I call "threshold-induced commensuration": the phenomenon where an organization must choose among projects embodying values that resist common measurement—say, safeguarding an irreplaceable cultural heritage site versus achieving a quantifiable public-health outcome—yet the governing decision rule requires a single ordering above a fixed budgetary or risk threshold. Rather than confronting the hard choice directly, administrators often construct an artificial exchange metric, such as a weighted scorecard or a synthetic value unit, which functions not as a genuine measure of comparability but as a procedural fiction that suppresses the phenomenology of loss. I argue that this does not resolve incommensurability but structurally displaces it: the institution produces a determinate outcome without the decision-maker personally appropriating the loss that, according to some accounts of hard choice, is essential to the rational agency involved in choosing.

Could you assess whether this framing conflates practical incomparability with mere institutional convenience? I am also unsure whether my distinction between suppressing and resolving incommensurability is stable: one might object that any practical decision procedure necessarily suppresses the full richness of values, so that my critique applies to rational choice generally rather than to these specific threshold-driven mechanisms. I would welcome direct feedback on the coherence of the argument, on whether I am missing an obvious reply from value pluralism or decision theory, and on whether the notion of a procedural fiction adds anything beyond the familiar charge that bureaucracies reduce qualitative differences to quantitative ones. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list—just a focused evaluation of the reasoning and its presentation.
