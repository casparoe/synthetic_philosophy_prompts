I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I have been reading philosophy of mind informally after years of building identity-management and distributed-access systems. I keep encountering an idea that the self is not a hidden substance inside a person but rather something like a center of narrative gravity—a focal point that pulls together all the stories told about an entity—yet I am unsure whether I am grasping it correctly. To check my understanding, I have been discussing hypothetical cases with a colleague who studied philosophy more seriously than I have, and she has given me a consistent pattern of verdicts, but I cannot tell which underlying principle is actually driving them.

She says that a typical adult human with intact autobiographical memory is clearly such a center. She also says that a corporation like a long-lived technology firm—which updates its public history, makes strategic decisions, and is held legally responsible—counts as one, even though no single employee is the firm. By contrast, she denies that a current large language model with persistent memory and a consistent personality profile qualifies, though she will not specify exactly what is missing. She denies it for a beehive as well, despite its collective problem-solving abilities. For a human with severe dissociative identity disorder in which competing narratives genuinely fail to integrate, she says there are multiple centers rather than none. Most surprisingly, she still counts a human with complete retrograde amnesia as a single center, even though they can construct no story linking present to past. Recently she added that an open-source software project with a unified design philosophy, a continuous commit history interpreted by maintainers, and external users who treat it as a single agent, does qualify, though she calls it a limiting case.

Next, I want her view on a system I am actually designing: a distributed cluster of autonomous microservices in which each node maintains a partial, often contradictory operational log, and a consensus algorithm synthesizes a unified system memoir only when an external auditor queries it. The cluster negotiates contracts and allocates resources as if it were one agent, but no node or human controls it, and the reconstructed memoir is never continuously present inside any single component. Given her track record, what verdict should I expect? I am less interested in the bare answer than in identifying the competing rules that could explain her pattern—perhaps one anchored in functional integration, one in continuous narrative coherence maintained over time, and one in social attribution by outside observers. Please say which of these rules diverge on my cluster, give your best prediction with the reasons for preferring it, and then propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the rule you think she is running from its closest rival.
