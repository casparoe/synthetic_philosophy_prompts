i'm in this course on nationalism and modernity and we've been working through Hobsbawm's stuff on invented tradition and Anderson's argument about imagined communities. i tried to build my own version of the argument for a paper and now it feels like it proves way too much, but i can't tell if i'm being sloppy or if the framework really does collapse. here's the chain i wrote out for my study group.

1. If a social group claims ancient continuity but its unifying symbols and practices were mostly fabricated in the last couple centuries, then the group itself lacks real historical continuity and is essentially a modern construction.
2. Hobsbawm shows that lots of "ancient" national traditions—standardized folk dress, royal rituals, even some anthems—were actually invented in the 1800s to manufacture a false sense of deep roots.
3. Anderson argues that nations depend on people imagining solidarity with strangers they'll never meet, and that this mental image only becomes possible with mass literacy, print capitalism, and technologies like the census and the map.
4. So for nations, both the symbolic traditions and the very capacity to imagine belonging depend on modern conditions.
5. Therefore nations as such couldn't have existed before those conditions—say, before the long 19th century—even if people lived in the same territory.
6. Now apply the same structure to universities. The idea of "the university" as one continuous body depends on shared rituals—robes, Latin mottos, degree ceremonies—many of which were standardized only in the 1800s, and on an imagined community of scholars stretching across centuries.
7. So universities should also be modern constructions with no real continuity back to medieval studia.
8. But Oxford and Bologna clearly existed in the 11th and 12th centuries with actual charters, teaching, and personnel continuity.
9. So either the logic in steps 1-5 is broken, or we have to say Oxford wasn't really a university until the 1800s, which sounds ridiculuous.
10. I want to save step 3 because the census/map point seems right, but if i weaken step 1 so that invented traditions just decorate rather than replace continuity, then Hobsbawm's critique of nationalism seems to lose its bite and becomes just "nations have PR campaigns."

where does this actually go wrong? is there an equivocation between symbolic continuity and institutional continuity that i missed? and if i have to give up one premise, which one costs the least—does saving Anderson's technological point force me to accept that universities are also "imagined" in the exact same way, or can i draw a line somewhere that doesn't feel totally ad hoc?
