I serve on a parliamentary oversight committee that reviews proposed behavioral-governance instruments—policies that do not criminalize conduct but restructure choice architectures, such as tying driver’s-license renewal to an opt-out organ-donation registry, or embedding carbon-tracking incentives in public-transit cards. After a recent seminar on Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive liberty and on Philip Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination, my colleagues and I keep talking past one another about whether a given proposal “restricts freedom,” because we slide carelessly between “it coerces,” “it fails to empower people,” and “it puts citizens under someone else’s thumb.” I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in about thirty minutes with staff who have no formal philosophy background—that translates these three conceptions into an ordered set of tests, with explicit stopping rules, a small set of diagnostic questions, and clear markers for where the answer is an unavoidable value judgment rather than a factual classification. The procedure must keep the three liberties analytically separate even when a single policy touches all of them: negative liberty (actual interference, credible threat of penalty, or physical blocking), positive liberty (the presence or absence of capacities, resources, and conditions for self-directed action), and republican liberty (subjection to arbitrary power—including benevolent, indirect, or delegated power—regardless of whether that power is actually exercised). In particular, it needs to handle what I am calling “proxy domination”: cases in which the state does not directly coerce, but delegates the design and revision of a choice architecture to a private agent who retains unilateral, revocable discretion over its rules and weights, so that citizens remain subject to an arbitrary will that is neither direct state interference nor a straightforward market exchange.

Please construct the checklist or flowchart in words: the exact sequence of questions to ask, what counts as sufficient evidence for each, where the procedure issues a clear finding for each type of liberty, and where it must stop and hand the decision back to the committee because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Then apply the procedure to two proposals. First, a direct-state opt-out organ-donation system linked to driver’s-license renewal, governed by fixed statutory rules with no private intermediary involved in the architecture. Second, a “wellness-score” public-transit subsidy in which a private contractor designs the behavioral algorithm, may alter the scoring weights without legislative approval, and automatically enrolls citizens through a complex opt-out portal. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict that a liberty-focused committee is likely to resist—most likely that the second program is gravely unfree in the republican sense despite being largely non-coercive in the negative sense. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle: for example, whether a severe republican domination finding should automatically outweigh a substantial welfare gain, or whether deficits in positive liberty can ever justify overriding protections of negative liberty. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading; the staff must be able to apply this without access to external sources.
