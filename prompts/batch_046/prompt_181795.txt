I've been writing a piece on Quine's ethics and I'm stuck on something that's been driving me nuts. For like fifty years people have been arguing over whether Quine was a moral antirealist — the guy who said ontology is relative and ethics might just be a matter of convenience — but every time I read a new paper it's just the same two camps trading the same passages about moral values being continuous with physics or whatever. One side says he's reducing ethics to empirical psychology; the other says he's leaving room for objectivity inside his naturalized framework. Nobody budges. My working hunch is that this isn't actually about realism at all, but about what counts as a theoretical virtue when you can't run experiments on fairness or cruelty — like, are they fighting over whether coherance in the web of belief is enough to ground moral truth, or do you need some direct causal hook to the world?

I was talking to a friend who covers philosophy for a magazine and she thinks it's purely verbal: realists mean mind-independent and Quineans mean indespensable to our best theory, and those just slide past each other. But I'm not convinced that's the whole story. Can you diagnose whether this stall is substantive, verbal, or if the sides are secretly answering different questions — one about language, one about psychology — under the same heading? And if it's really stuck, what kind of distinction or argument would actually make progress here — maybe something about how theoretical virtues apply outside empirical science — or should we just admit that no progress should be expected and say why?
