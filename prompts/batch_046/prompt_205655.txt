I am preparing a graduate seminar on the scalarity of moral patienthood, and I want to close with an original poem that does not merely illustrate the debate but formally enacts it. You are a philosopher-poet with expertise in philosophy of mind and ethics. Before composing, take a moment to brainstorm how the formal constraints of a sestina—six rotating end-words and the compressed three-line envoi—can mirror the conceptual instability at issue: arguments for sharp thresholds (consciousness present or absent) and for continuous gradients (degrees of sentience) keep trading positions without stable resolution.

Write a sestina—six sestets of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi in which all six end-words appear, two per line—on the gradation of moral status from insects to thermostats. The six end-words, which must end every line in rotation, are: gradient, threshold, valence, register, absence, weight. In the poem, treat moral status not as a binary possession but as tracking the resolution of valenced experience: an insect carries a low-resolution moral weight, a mammal a high-resolution one, and a thermostat represents not a hidden consciousness but the literal absence of any register of valence, however faint. The rotation should do philosophical work: across stanzas, terms like threshold and gradient should shift grammatical roles—sometimes naming the view being defended, sometimes the objection to it—so that the form itself enacts the difficulty of drawing a line. Include accurate conceptual content: the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and functional registration, the risk of anthropocentric bias in attributing affective states, and the specific limit case of simple systems that respond to temperature without any affective state. The poem should be precise enough that a specialist in animal ethics or philosophy of mind recognizes the positions rather than seeing them as decorative names.

After the poem, provide a brief reflective note—no more than one short paragraph—stating where the poem takes liberties with contested theories (for instance, whether framing moral status as resolution of valence presupposes a representationalist account of consciousness, or whether the thermostat case genuinely distinguishes functionalist from phenomenalist commitments) and identifying exactly which argument or finding would lead you to revise the framing: for example, if minimal homeostatic regulation were shown to entail a primitive form of valenced registration, or if the binary versus gradualist distinction itself collapsed into a more complex taxonomy. Think carefully, be precise with the philosophical machinery, and be self-critical about the limits of using poetic form to carry philosophical argument.
