You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in personal identity and cognitive science. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the main ways philosophical debates stall—verbal disagreement, hidden empirical premises, conflicting standards of explanation, or bedrock intuitions that neither side can justify.

Consider a debate that has run for years without resolution: can an 'I' be distributed across brains? One camp, drawing on Douglas Hofstadter's idea that the self is a high-level pattern of causal loops rather than a biological particular, argues that after intimate long-term cohabitation parts of one person's pattern—habits of reasoning, emotional responses, even fragments of narrative self-model—are genuinely instantiated in a survivor's brain. They do not claim the survivor literally is the deceased, but that something like a distributed or partial persistence occurs: the dead person's self is not fully extinguished but exists in a diluted, shared form across the brains of those who knew them well. The opposing camp insists that personal identity requires spatiotemporal continuity of a single living organism or at least a single stream of consciousness anchored in one brain; on their view, all talk of persistence in others is either metaphor for memory, a category error about representation versus presence, or a romantic confusion of influence with identity.

The disagreement is not about neuroscience—both sides accept that brains change through social interaction—nor about ethics. Both sides also agree that if numerical identity fails, that is a serious loss. Yet neither side has conceded anything in years. Diagnose this stall. Is it a hidden factual dispute about the threshold of pattern similarity required for identity? A verbal disagreement about whether persistence means numerical identity, qualitative similarity, or causal continuation? A clash between two different questions—one metaphysical about what conditions an 'I' must satisfy, one phenomenological about what it feels like to carry someone inside you—being forced into one framework? Or is it a bedrock intuitive divide about whether the self is essentially private?

Give your diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of identity theories. Then say what, if anything, could count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about neural pattern replication, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
