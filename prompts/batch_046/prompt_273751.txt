For an upper-level metaphysics workshop of 14 participants meeting for 90 minutes, design a structured role-play and deliberation game that makes the special composition question unavoidable rather than merely abstract. Divide the group into three roles: Assemblers, who propose that specific spatial arrangements of identical modular geometric parts constitute a single new object and must defend the proposal with a functional or structural description; Auditors, who maintain an official inventory and must approve or reject each proposal according to explicit parsimony constraints; and Witnesses, who report privately whether they experience one unified phenomenon or only a plurality of parts. The rules should include: (1) Assemblers may submit only one proposal per round, with a two-minute defense; (2) Auditors may approve no more than two consecutive proposals, after which they are forced to reject the next on pain of losing speaking privileges for the remainder of the round, simulating institutional pressure for ontological economy; (3) Witnesses vote by secret ballot, and if the vote splits evenly, the proposal is suspended rather than rejected, creating an unresolved entry in the inventory; (4) a hidden cumulative threshold operates throughout—if the total number of approved composites exceeds six by minute 60, all prior approvals are retroactively flagged for revision, forcing the group to confront whether restriction can be principled or is always post-hoc. At minute 40, introduce a boundary arrangement: a circular chain of parts with adjacent physical contact at every link but no central core, no shared functional purpose, and no internal causal interaction, designed to split the group between contact-based and function-based criteria for composition. Provide a precise minute-by-minute timeline, the conditions under which the game ends, and a debrief protocol that maps specific moments onto philosophical positions. The debrief must connect the approval dynamics to universalism versus nihilism, the hidden-threshold crisis to the problem of arbitrary restriction, and the circular boundary case to debates about contact, fusion, and functional unity. Predict that participants will systematically confuse perceptual grouping with metaphysical composition, and instruct the facilitator on how to separate these phenomenological reports from ontological claims without resolving the underlying dispute. Be explicit that the activity cannot reproduce the modal argument that any spatial arrangement is metaphysically possible and therefore composes an object in some world, which the facilitator must supply verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Frame all objects as abstract geometric modules with specified spatial relations so that reasoning proceeds from structural features alone, with no dependence on familiarity with particular artifacts or historical examples.
