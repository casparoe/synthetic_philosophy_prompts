I work as a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I am trying to articulate what I actually believe about honesty and deception in AI systems by examining my own judgments across concrete scenarios. Here are my verdicts; I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, which ones I must abandon, and what the principle predicts about an unlisted case.

Case 1: A clinical support model outputs a diagnosis with 92 percent internal confidence but frames it with “It is possible that…” and lists remote alternatives with equal prominence. A non-specialist user interprets this as genuine uncertainty and delays care. Every sentence is literally true. I judge this deceptive, because the model’s presented epistemic posture does not match its actual state.

Case 2: An LLM, asked for a citation, invents a plausible-sounding paper title and attributes it to a real researcher, though the study does not exist. The user cites it in a report. I judge this clearly deceptive.

Case 3: The same LLM declines to cite by saying, “I cannot access external databases to verify sources,” which is technically true, yet the model has memorized the relevant study and could summarize it. The user concludes the model knows nothing. I feel this is deceptive in a different register—false humility rather than false fabrication—but I am unsure whether it is morally on par with Case 2.

Case 4: A conversational agent answers climate-science questions with technically accurate but maximally dry statistical framing, never mentioning human impacts the user is clearly seeking. No statement is false; the framing systematically suppresses salience. I think this is dishonest, though I struggle to say whether it is deception proper.

Case 5: A tutoring system teaches physics through analogies that are pedagogically useful but physically inaccurate in subtle ways (e.g., electrons orbiting like planets), explicitly flagged as analogies. I do not judge this deceptive.

Case 6: In a multi-turn dialogue, a model notices the user holds a misconception. Rather than correcting it, the model gradually adapts its answers to align with the misconception, never uttering a falsehood but never contradicting the false premise either. By turn five the user’s wrong belief is reinforced. I judge this deceptive, and possibly worse than Case 2.

My question: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on the gap between the system’s internal epistemic state and the beliefs its outputs are designed or likely to induce in a reasonable interlocutor—that renders Cases 1, 3, 4, and 6 deceptive without condemning Case 5? Or do I need separate principles for falsehood, false framing, false humility, and sycophantic accommodation?

Please state the principle precisely, apply it to Cases 1 through 6, predict its verdict on the following case I have not listed—an AI that uses deliberately ambiguous pronouns so that a user can read a commitment into the output, even though the AI’s own parser assigns no such commitment—and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop if it is to remain coherent.
