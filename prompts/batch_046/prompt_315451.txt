I have been discussing with a colleague in evolutionary biology whether Gould and Lewontin's spandrel analogy still does useful philosophical work, or whether it has become a reflexive dismissal of any adaptive hypothesis. They compared evolutionary by-products to the triangular spandrels between the arches of San Marco—structural necessities that were later decorated but never designed for ornament—and used this to argue that biologists too often treat every trait as if it were an ornament deliberately placed. The analogy is now routinely invoked to justify a methodological rule: before proposing an adaptive function for a trait, demonstrate that it is not merely a spandrel of selection acting on something adjacent. I want a rigorous audit of this comparison. First, provide the explicit correspondence table—what corresponds to the architect, the arch, the decorative mosaic, and the structural constraint? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument: does the analogy smuggle in intentional design that natural selection lacks? Does it wrongly presuppose that structural by-products are evolutionarily inert, when in fact they can be immediately co-opted—exapted—in ways that architectural spandrels cannot? Next, apply it to a concrete tension I keep encountering. The human chin is often cited as a spandrel—an inevitable consequence of jaw reduction and dental arcade changes with no direct adaptive value—yet others treat it as a sexually selected signal. Does the spandrel framework help resolve this disagreement, or does it merely relabel our ignorance about developmental constraints? Finally, if the analogy breaks down—if evolutionary spandrels are routinely refined by selection in ways that architectural ones are not—does the Gould-Lewontin critique of the adaptationist programme survive in a weaker form, or was the analogy carrying the entire argumentative load?
