Design a classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of 16 students, running 75 minutes in a single session, that makes participants feel the structural deadlock in Scanlon's contractualist framework when it confronts the aggregation problem. Specifically, they should experience why a principle that permits harming one person to benefit several others seems to fail the test of not being reasonably rejectable by the affected individual, and why this produces a pairwise deadlock rather than a mere counting mistake. Do not open with a lecture on contractualism or aggregation. Instead, participants should encounter the deadlock through rules, roles, and forced revisions, recognizing the philosophical shape only in the debrief.

Inject the following non-standard mechanic: an "Institutional Translator" role. In this activity, affected parties are never physically present; each is represented by a card listing only the objective, non-emotional features of the harm or benefit they face. The group must draft a governing principle for each scenario, then test it against every card. A principle is blocked if any card's assigned holder can articulate—without appeal to personal preference or emotional weight—why that principle denies them equal standing. The group must then revise. This should make the pairwise, non-aggregative structure of contractualist justification visceral rather than abstract.

Use institutional-design scenarios rather than classic emergency dilemmas: for example, a zoning policy that displaces one household to build affordable housing for five; a data-sharing protocol that exposes one user's privacy to prevent fraud for five others; a clinical allocation that preserves full autonomy for one patient but only partial autonomy for five others. Present these without naming the underlying philosophical problem.

Specify group structure and roles (e.g., principle drafters, institutional translators, card-holders, observers); materials needed (scenario cards, principle-drafting sheets, rejection tokens, a collective scoring board); a payoff or incentive structure that uses no real money but creates genuine social stakes (e.g., collective points that unlock a shared seminar privilege, or individual reputation scores visible to the group that affect a final vote); a precise timeline broken into phases (setup and role assignment, silent drafting, translator-mediated testing, revision round, final vote); and clear facilitation rules for how the translator strips emotional framing and how card-holders must frame rejections in terms of standing rather than preference.

Then provide:

3. Timeline and facilitation notes (about 250 words)
4. The debrief: mapping specific moments in the activity onto Scanlon's contractualism—wrongness as the unjustifiability of principles to each affected person, the rejection of simple numerical aggregation, and the "numbers problem"—and explaining why the deadlock is structural (about 350 words)
5. Predictable failures and remedies: what will go wrong the first time (e.g., drafters will intuitively aggregate, card-holders will confuse reasonable rejection with mere complaint, the translator will dominate discussion, groups will try to split the difference) and exactly how to handle each (about 200 words)
6. Limits of the simulation: an honest account of which feature of contractualism or the aggregation debate this activity cannot reproduce—such as Scanlon's allowance for impersonal reasons, or the possibility that weighted principles might accommodate numbers without pure aggregation—and what the facilitator must supply verbally (about 150 words)
