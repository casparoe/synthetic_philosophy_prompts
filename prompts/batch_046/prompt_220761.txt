I cover science and ideas for a magazine, mostly risk, tech policy, and weird decision theory, and I keep recieving pitches that lean on expected-value math to justify big claims -- like 'this climate intervention saves infinite lives in expectation' or 'the tail dominates so we must fund this now.' Half the time the expectation is undefined because the payoff grows without bound or the tail never decays, which is basically that Pasadena game situation where the expectation just diverges. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run on a pitch in about 20 minutes before I decide whether to take the framing seriously, flag it as mathematically broken, or admit it's a values call dressed up as arithmetic. Write it as questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Keep it honest about where small errors will slip through. Apply it to two proposals: (1) a longtermist pitch arguing for a global AI moratorium using an expectation over unbounded future generations where welfare doesn't converge, and (2) a climate-adaptation study I actually like that calculates expected damage with a heavy-tailed payoff that technically diverges but truncates the tail at a 'reasonable' cutoff. Definately include one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like. After the procedure, summarize both cases in a JSON object with this schema: case_name (string), verdict (string), break_type (either 'math' or 'values'), failed_step (integer or null), and editor_warning (string). Don't ask me for citations or a reading list -- I'm working without a library and my readers won't chase footnotes either.
