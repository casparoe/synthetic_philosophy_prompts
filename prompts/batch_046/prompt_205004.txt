I am drafting the appendix to a philosophical novel in which a protagonist teaches an advanced seminar on twentieth-century existentialism. For this fictional course, I need a rigorous 90-minute final-exam essay question—carrying thirty marks—on Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom and the phenomenon of bad faith. The question must resist answers that merely recite the standard distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-itself or summarize the waiter example; instead, it should force students to confront an unresolved tension in Sartre's own framework. Specifically, if consciousness is translucent—if there is no hidden interior where deception can occur—then sustained self-deception seems structurally impossible, because the deceiver and the deceived are one and the same consciousness. The exam should present an unconventional concrete case: a museum curator who insists that her authoritative judgments about historical narratives are simultaneously fully determined by her institutional role and yet authentic expressions of her absolute freedom, and who therefore denies any anguish about her choices. Students must determine whether this posture is coherent within Sartre's ontology, and if it is bad faith, they must explain how it can persist without violating the transparency of consciousness.

Please provide, without relying on bibliographic citations or reading lists:
1. The essay question itself, calibrated for an upper-level undergraduate seminar.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words, demonstrating the precise argumentative moves a top response must make—including the deployment of facticity and transcendence, the flight from anguish, and the resolution (or deliberate preservation) of the transparency paradox.
3. A marking scheme that distinguishes an A-grade answer from a B-grade answer by naming the specific conceptual steps required (for instance, correctly identifying bad faith as a flight rather than an ignorance, and addressing the translucency objection rather than ignoring it) and listing the particular errors or omissions that should reduce a mark.

The response should be self-contained, drawing only on conceptual arguments available without external research.
