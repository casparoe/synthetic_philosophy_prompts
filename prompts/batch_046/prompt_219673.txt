Question: Does Kuhn's claim that paradigms are incommensurable entail that scientists working in rival paradigms literally cannot understand one another's claims, or does it mean something weaker about standards of evaluation? Illustrate your answer with the transition from Newtonian to Einsteinian mechanics.

Student answer: Incommensurability does not mean scientists are unable to understand each other at all. Rather, it means there is no neutral, paradigm-independent standard by which to judge which theory is superior. The Newton-to-Einstein shift illustrates this: both frameworks use the term 'mass,' but Newtonian mass is invariant whereas in special relativity it depends on velocity. Because the meaning of this central term changed, the two paradigms share no common measure. Feyerabend developed this idea further, arguing that every term in a theory changes meaning with the theory, which makes full translation impossible. Consequently, Einstein could not have truly grasped Newton's equations in Newton's own terms, and vice versa. Still, Kuhn allows that a scientist can learn another paradigm much as one learns a foreign language, so comprehension is possible; only rational comparison without circularity is blocked. Furthermore, taxonomic incommensurability—where kind terms are reorganized—is the sole form Kuhn recognizes, and it is precisely what occurs when 'mass' is reconceptualized.
