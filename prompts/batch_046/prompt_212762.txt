Write a poem in American English about Daniel Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness. Structure it as four brief, parallel sections—each a competing "draft" of the same fleeting perceptual moment, such as the instant of noticing an unexpected sound or a sudden color—without any framing stanza or final section that privileges one version as the true or canonical account. Let specific phrases recur across the sections but shift in meaning or emphasis, so that the form itself enacts the absence of a single central place in the brain where experience comes together for one observer. Do not give any draft a unified theatrical "I" or an audience that witnesses a finished show; instead, distribute the observing across the competing descriptions. Make the philosophical content precise enough that someone familiar with the multiple drafts model recognizes the denial of a master narrative stream, but keep the language poetic and accessible to a reader who knows nothing of the theory. Leave the multiplicity unresolved: end with the drafts still standing side by side, not synthesized into a hidden final version.
