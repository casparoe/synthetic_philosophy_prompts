I'm grading responses for an upper-level seminar on moral and political philosophy, and I want a careful second assessment of one exam answer before I record a final score. The question asked students to explain Michael Walzer's concept of dirty hands in politics—specifically, to contrast it with both the Machiavellian claim that ordinary moral rules are suspended within the political sphere and the consequentialist claim that acts producing sufficient public benefit are fully justified and therefore not genuinely wrong. The prompt also asked students to explain why Walzer insists that the leader who takes such action remains genuinely guilty rather than merely excused, and to consider whether that guilt implies any institutional consequence, such as resignation or punishment, within his framework.

Here is the student's answer. They begin by correctly identifying Walzer's core scenario: a political leader may be required to do something that is ordinarily morally wrong—such as ordering an assassination or authorizing coercive interrogation—in order to prevent a catastrophic harm, and that this necessity does not dissolve the wrongness of the act itself. They accurately distinguish this from Machiavelli, noting that Walzer does not treat politics as an amoral domain but rather as a domain in which moral rules remain binding yet can be overridden in extremis. They also distinguish Walzer from consequentialism, observing that for a consequentialist the act is justified and hence not wrong, whereas for Walzer the act remains wrong even when necessary. So far the structure is sound. The student then introduces Max Weber's distinction between an ethic of conviction and an ethic of responsibility, claiming that Walzer essentially appropriates Weber's framework and simply adds the requirement that the responsible leader must experience deep emotional anguish. From there they argue that this anguish is best interpreted as a purely psychological or affective reaction—something like traumatic guilt—rather than as evidence of an actual moral residue. They use this to suggest that Walzer's position is closer to consequentialism than he admits, since if the wrongness is located only in the leader's feelings, the act itself is fully justified on the balance of reasons. Finally, they conclude that because the leader is genuinely guilty in Walzer's view, democratic citizens who retain him in office become morally complicit, and there ought to be institutional consequences such as mandatory resignation or even criminal prosecution to restore the moral order.

Please assign a grade and justify it with point-by-point comments on the argument as presented—identifying which moves are accurate, which involve subtle conflations or misattributions, and which steps do not actually follow. In doing so, please separate what is well-established in the standard literature on Walzer from what depends on genuinely contested interpretive claims—particularly regarding Walzer's relationship to Weber, the ontological status of the guilt Walzer describes, and whether his view entails specific institutional penalties. Do not present any contested reading as if it were the consensus position.
