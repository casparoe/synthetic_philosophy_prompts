I am preparing an interview for a philosophy podcast with an unnamed philosopher—call her Dr. Elena Voss—who defends a synthesized position drawn from Avishai Margalit's ethics of collective memory and from Rieffian critiques of remembrance: specifically, David Rieff's skepticism about compulsory remembrance and Philip Rieff's analysis of therapeutic culture. Voss's claim is that collective obligations to remember are indexed not to universal humanity but to membership in what Margalit calls a "moral community": the duty binds thickly among those who share a lived ethical relationship with the victimized group, and weakens across boundaries where no such shared life exists. Simultaneously, she argues that modern societies do not forget traumatic pasts but administer them through "managed remembrance"—state apologies, trauma curricula, commemorative museums—whose bureaucratic form suppresses the moral emotions (especially shame and resentment) that Margalit treats as prerequisites for genuine forgiveness. On her view, this is not an accidental failure of remembrance but the logical endpoint of therapeutic culture's transformation of moral life into affective management. The danger is therefore not state-enforced amnesia, but a ritualized remembrance that neutralizes accountability without producing reconciliation.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that probe the actual mechanics of this view rather than asking for a summary. The remaining questions should press further tensions: whether forgiveness can be demanded of those outside the moral community; whether her view permits strategic forgetting within the community; and whether suppressing moral emotion through remembrance is a corruption of Margalit's framework or its inevitable consequence.
