I was discussing with a friend recently about the difference between knowing how to do something—repairing an old bicycle, speaking a second language fluently, or navigating a familiar neighborhood without being able to draw a map—and knowing that certain propositions are true, such as the chemical properties of the grease or the grammatical rules underlying the speech. I understand there is a longstanding philosophical debate about whether this first kind of knowledge can ultimately be reduced to the second, but the standard framing feels somewhat stale to me; what now seems more pressing is how the distinction survives in a context where automated systems can execute complex procedural tasks—strategic games, translation, even craft-like activities—without anything resembling human embodied practice or conscious access to rules.

If you were advising someone with about three years to work on this, a philosophical background, but no specialized training in cognitive science, which open questions or lines of inquiry in this area should they prioritize? I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I want a defended ordering. For example, is the central task still to settle whether knowing-how genuinely reduces to knowing-that, or has the more consequential work shifted to understanding what procedural mastery means when it is routinely separated from the embodied skill and conscious understanding that used to define it? Please rank the priorities and explain the reasoning: which questions are upstream of others, how tractable they are for a philosopher rather than an empirical researcher, and what kind of conceptual progress over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
