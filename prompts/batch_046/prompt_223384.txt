I am trying to understand why the Gödelian anti-mechanist debate—particularly in the forms developed by Lucas and later refined by Penrose—has remained in a state of prolonged stalemate despite near-universal agreement on the underlying formal results. The anti-mechanist argument runs roughly as follows: for any consistent formal system F that a mathematician can genuinely grasp, there exists a Gödel sentence G_F that F cannot derive, yet the mathematician can recognise G_F as true; if the mathematician’s mind were fully equivalent to F, she ought to be unable to do so, hence human cognition is not mechanisable in that specific sense. The mechanist response is equally familiar: human reasoners are themselves fallible and sometimes inconsistent; they do not in practice possess unfettered access to consistency proofs for arbitrarily complex systems; and the capacity to recognise a Gödel sentence typically relies on standing outside the system and assuming its consistency rather than computing that consistency from within. Over the decades the exchange has accumulated further layers—objections about the non-constructive nature of consistency recognition, about whether human mathematicians genuinely access transfinite induction or merely approximate it heuristically, and about whether an infinite hierarchy of ever-stronger meta-systems collapses the distinction between mind and machine—yet the core positions have barely shifted. Both sides accept Gödel’s first and second incompleteness theorems without dispute, yet they draw diametrically opposed metaphysical conclusions about cognition.

My working hypothesis is that the blockage is not a hidden empirical question about the actual limits of human meta-reasoning, but rather a deep divergence over what counts as an adequate explanation of cognitive capacity. The anti-mechanist tends to treat seeing the truth of a Gödel sentence as a primitive epistemic achievement that requires no further reduction, whereas the mechanist treats any such seeing as either implicit reliance on a different formal subsystem—whose own consistency is equally unprovable from within—or as heuristic approximation that falls short of rigorous derivation. Nevertheless, I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real disagreement, or whether there is instead a verbal dispute about what mechanisation means, or whether the impasse ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about consciousness that neither side can argue for. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—whether substantive, verbal, or rooted in unstated standards of evidence and explanation—supported by what each side genuinely assumes about proof, consistency, and cognitive access? I am not asking for a literature survey, but for a concrete structural analysis of the positions as actually made, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a shift in the framing of the question—could genuinely move the needle, or whether the debate is constitutionally unresolvable.
