I'm a third-year student in an upper-division philosophy of law and international ethics seminar. For a 75-minute session with about 14 students, please design a runnable in-class simulation—not a lecture or discussion guide—that makes the group feel the conceptual and political tension in classifying mass atrocities. The core idea should be Raphael Lemkin's broader original conception of genocide, which included cultural destruction and forced assimilation alongside physical killing, pressed against the narrower UN Convention legal framework that actually governs international obligations and intervention mandates.

To keep the prompt from being something a model could regurgitate from standard introductions, inject the following less-standard mechanism: some students should play members of the affected community who, even when the factual criteria for the broad label seem satisfied, actively resist being classified as genocide victims. Their resistance should be rationalized within the simulation rules—accepting the label automatically triggers a mandatory coercive-intervention obligation that they fear will escalate violence, and it invites geopolitical instrumentalization of their suffering by outside powers—so that the "council" of remaining students, who must vote on classification and then allocate a fixed pool of non-monetary resources, faces a real strategic conflict. Design exact roles, rules, materials (dossiers describing a fictional but realistic composite case of systematic destruction involving both killings and cultural suppression), and a point-based payoff scheme tracking institutional credibility, protection outcomes, and long-term stability rather than real money. Arrange the sequence so that participants first construct the classification themselves using both Lemkin's broader axes and the Convention's stricter elements, commit to a vote and resource allocation, and only then discover the affected-group members' refusal and its strategic consequences.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto: (1) the legal-moral gap between descriptive classification and prescriptive obligation; (2) the politics of naming, including who controls the category and whose interests it serves; and (3) the tension between Lemkin's holistic vision and the Convention's narrower enforceability. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that participants fixate on factual determination rather than on the politics of labeling—and how to redirect them. Be frank about which feature of real-world atrocity politics, such as the irreversibility of formal international legal designation or the lived experience of survivors outside a classroom, the simulation cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply directly in discussion.

Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars who work on this—such as whether the UN Convention's exclusion of cultural genocide was a necessary political compromise or a conceptual error, whether a responsibility to protect actually follows from the genocide label in state practice, and whether affected communities' resistance to labeling is widespread or exceptional—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus.
