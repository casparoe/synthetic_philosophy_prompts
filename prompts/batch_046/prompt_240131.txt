I work in software engineering without formal philosophical training, but I have been drawn to Moritz Schlick’s claim that human life acquires meaning not through the achievement of external purposes or metaphysical destinies, but through playful, creative activity that is pursued for its own sake. From where I sit, this resonates with the experience of building systems: much of what feels genuinely satisfying is not shipping a finished product but the exploratory, modular play of design itself. I am considering adopting a Schlick-inspired position—that both personal and technological meaning depend on this kind of non-instrumental engagement—while recognizing that rival accounts treat such play as merely instrumental to flourishing, social contribution, or virtue. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure: which arguments, observations, or possible cases would be decisive rather than merely awkward; what defenders of the view would themselves concede as counting against it; and whether it risks anything specific—say, about harmful or socially useless play—that its rivals do not. Please distinguish fatal defeaters from awkward ones, note what the view predicts that purpose-driven or virtue-based rivals do not, and say whether being well-insulated here is a philosophical virtue or a vice. I am especially interested in whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that undermines it for a claim of this kind.
