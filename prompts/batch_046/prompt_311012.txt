I write about philosophy of mind and cognitive science for a magazine audience, and I have been trying to make sense of Daniel Dennett's proposal that the self is best understood as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract point around which a being's actions, beliefs, and utterances organize themselves, rather than a substantive soul. In conversations with colleagues, I applied this idea to several cases that stretch well beyond the standard human example, and I now find my own judgments form a pattern I cannot easily articulate.

My verdicts are these. A typical adult with continuous autobiographical memory clearly possesses such a center; we explain her behavior by appealing to the story she tells about herself. A person with severe retrograde amnesia who retains consistent emotional dispositions and present-tense preferences—but who cannot reconstruct a past—still strikes me as possessing a center, though severely attenuated; we continue to treat her as the same agent rather than a new one. A large language model that generates fluent first-person prose across independent sessions, yet lacks bodily continuity, persistent internal states, and causal links between outputs, does not seem to have a center of narrative gravity, despite the surface narrative coherence. A corporation that maintains legal continuity, strategic plans, and an institutional origin story appears to possess an analogical or derivative center—something like narrative gravity projected onto a collective entity. The split-brain patient, in whom the left hemisphere offers one verbal explanation for an action and the right hemisphere indicates a conflicting one through nonverbal behavior, seems to me not to have one unified center but rather two competing gravitational nodes, or perhaps one that has fractured. Finally, a fictional character sustained across decades of novels by different authors possesses a center of narrative gravity within the fictional world, but none in reality; yet the boundary between analogical possession and mere representation feels sharper in theory than in my intuition.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the causal integration of the narrative mechanism, the temporal continuity of the framing process, or a distinction between being the author versus the subject of the story—that can accommodate all of these verdicts? Or must I drop one? I am especially troubled by whether my refusal to grant narrative gravity to the language model is consistent with my willingness to grant an attenuated version to the amnesiac human and an analogical version to the corporation. What principle, if any, captures this pattern, and which of my judgments should be revised?
