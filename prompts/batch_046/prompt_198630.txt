Before you diagnose anything, please spend a brief moment brainstorming the different families of failure that can afflict a reductio-style argument in this area—hidden quantifier shifts, ambiguities in expectation operators over infinite sample spaces, modal slides between epistemic and metaphysical possibility, and cases where the reasoning is technically valid but forces us to abandon a premise that seemed indispensable.

Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on infinite ethics. Its conclusion strikes me as absurd—if correct, it would seem to paralyze all ordinary practical reasoning—yet each step looks defensible on its own.

(1) A normative ethical theory must satisfy comparative closure: for any two actions actually available to an agent, the theory must determinately rank them as morally better, worse, or exactly tied.

(2) Consider a welfare-maximizing framework that sums the welfare of all affected moral subjects without any spatiotemporal cutoff.

(3) In any physically realistic model, some present actions—say, influencing research priorities—have a nonzero probability of being in the causal chain that determines whether an infinite number of future subjects come to exist with positive welfare.

(4) For any such cosmic-influencing action, the expected total welfare contribution is either undefined or diverges, while for any purely local action (buying groceries, keeping a promise to a friend) the contribution is finite and bounded.

(5) Therefore, on this framework, every choice between a cosmic-influencing option and a local option resolves in favor of the cosmic option, regardless of how severe the local cost.

(6) Moreover, if two different cosmic-influencing options both have undefined or diverging expectations but differ in local cost, the framework still cannot rank them.

(7) So the framework violates comparative closure both globally and locally.

(8) Hence any welfare-maximizing theory without an arbitrary cutoff fails to be action-guiding.

(9) But we have strong independent reasons—rooted in impartiality and in the arbitrariness of spatial or temporal boundaries—to reject cutoffs.

(10) So we face a trilemma: reject welfare maximization, accept arbitrary cutoffs, or deny that ethics requires comparative closure for every available pair.

The conclusion seems too strong: surely ethics does not require me to treat every grocery purchase as morally dwarfed by a one-in-a-billion chance of cosmic-scale influence. Where does the argument actually fail? If I am forced to choose, I most want to preserve (2) and (9), since both seem central to any serious universalist or longtermist framework. Which step should I reject—(4), with its hidden slide from undefined expectation to definite ranking; (3), with its ambiguity about causal influence across infinite domains; (1), with its demanding closure condition; or something else?
