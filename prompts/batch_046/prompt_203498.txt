Write the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Custom, Force, and Institutional Legitimacy," delivered to an audience split between constitutional lawyers and political philosophers—who will recognize Pascal's Pensées arguments that justice is sustained only by custom and that without force it is powerless, and that custom itself is often nothing more than fashion—and practicing judges, clerks, and advocates who do not read seventeenth-century French but navigate precisely these tensions in statutory interpretation and emergency powers. The philosophical framework must do structural work: use Pascal's distinction not as ornament but to frame the workshop's governing question, namely whether modern procedural regularity and constitutional precedent genuinely convert state force into justice, or whether they merely operate as Pascalian custom that disguises an underlying arbitrary power. To keep the prompt from being answerable by generic summary, inject a less-discussed angle: draw on Pascal's critique in the Provincial Letters of probabilism as a deliberately manufactured form of custom, in which authorities construct flexible moral rules to excuse the powerful, and ask whether contemporary interpretive flexibility—broad statutory construction, shifting constitutional meaning—risks the same corruption of custom into a mask for force rather than its transformation. The remarks must work for both halves of the room: include one precise conceptual distinction (for example, between "force that originally establishes custom" and "force that subsequently corrupts it") that specialists can debate, and one concrete institutional example—such as the normalization of emergency administrative discretion or the breaking of precedent—that practitioners can grasp without any knowledge of Pascal. The speech should comfort neither camp with easy answers; it should leave both with the same unsettled problem. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for them. Avoid verbatim quotations from the Pensées or the Provincial Letters; paraphrase all relevant claims. Limit the remarks to a maximum of 500 words.
