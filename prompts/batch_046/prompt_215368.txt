Consider the institutional practice by which international governance bodies—such as Transparency International’s Corruption Perceptions Index and related multilateral benchmarking metrics—aggregate expert and business surveys into a single ordinal ranking of countries. In these instruments, “gift-giving to public officials” is coded as a corruption indicator regardless of whether the gift accompanies a specific, discrete service request (a facilitation payment) or functions as part of an ongoing, diffuse social obligation to maintain reciprocal relationships (as in many patronage-based or kinship-structured polities). At the same time, the metrics sharply distinguish “petty corruption” from “grand corruption” based primarily on the magnitude of resources transferred and the hierarchical rank of the official involved, not on the social logic of the exchange.

What philosophical theory of corruption, and of the moral boundaries of public office, does this measurement practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that corruption is essentially a species of market failure—an illicit, quantifiable exchange that corrupts an otherwise impersonal bureaucratic rationality? Or can it accommodate a view on which some reciprocal obligations between office-holders and constituents are not corruptions but alternative moral economies that only appear corrupt when judged by a liberal-legalist framework? Reconstruct the implicit normative theory: does the practice require a universalist account in which Western-style procedural impartiality is the sole legitimate basis of public authority, or is it philosophically compatible with pluralist or relational conceptions of legitimate governance? Furthermore, examine whether the practice’s internal distinction between petty and grand corruption is consistent with its treatment of gift-giving: if the wrongness of corruption lies in the subversion of institutional roles, why should the scale of the transaction matter more than the nature of the social bond it activates? Finally, indicate what would have to change in the design of anti-corruption policy—both conceptually and institutionally—if the underlying theory were replaced by one that treated corruption not as deviation from impersonal rules but as the inappropriate activation of particularistic ties within specific domains of authority.
