I work as a senior technical assessor for the International Watershed Monitoring Consortium, and I must classify river-water samples as 'contaminated' or 'uncontaminated' for remediation-funding eligibility. The classification depends on microplastic particle counts per litre. There is an obvious sorites structure: a sample with zero particles is clearly uncontaminated, yet adding a single particle cannot plausibly change the status, even though a sample with several thousand particles is clearly contaminated. Our board needs a reusable decision procedure, not a case-by-case intuition, because we review roughly forty proposals per quarter and each decision must be defensible to auditors and local stakeholders with no philosophy training.

Please construct a step-by-step decision procedure grounded in a specific philosophical account of vagueness: a sharp-boundary epistemicist view (the idea that there is a precise but unknown cutoff, and that vagueness is a form of ignorance about where it lies) modified by a mandatory 'indeterminacy corridor'. On this hybrid view, any measurement falling within a calibrated range around the suspected boundary is treated as genuinely indeterminate for practical purposes, and different institutional rules apply inside that corridor depending on whether the stakes involve high ecological risk or administrative efficiency. The procedure must preserve the logical rejection of tolerance principles—there must be a cutoff—while being explicit that we do not know its location and that our response to this ignorance is structured, not arbitrary.

The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: the ordered questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure returns 'not eligible', 'eligible', or 'indeterminate—refer to panel'; the measurements and error margins that must be recorded; the points where human judgement is unavoidable and must be documented rather than concealed; and the explicit limits of what the procedure can settle. It must be executable in under thirty minutes per proposal by staff with a science background but no formal logic training, and it must not require bibliographies, citations, or appeals to unnamed theories.

Apply the procedure to three proposals:

(A) A sample measuring 340 particles per litre, where prior surveys suggest the boundary is likely near 350 but could plausibly lie anywhere between 300 and 400, and the proposed intervention is low-cost monitoring only.

(B) A sample measuring exactly 350 particles per litre, with the same boundary uncertainty, but the proposed intervention is an expensive filtration system that would divert funds from other watersheds.

(C) A sample measuring 120 particles per litre, but where the testing method has a known error margin of ±40 particles.

Include one case—clearly state which—where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with the intuitive preference of an assessor (for instance, denying funding for a seemingly obvious case, or granting it despite ambiguity). Explain why the procedure demands that result, and flag exactly where a human must sign off despite the procedural output.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: identify the specific misfire points, particularly with clustered measurements near the boundary, with shifting stakeholder risk tolerances, or when new measurement technologies alter the error margins. The answer should be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedural rules.
