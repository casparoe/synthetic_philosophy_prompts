I'm taking a seminar on early modern political philosophy and we've been working through Pascal's Pensées, particularly his claim that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that justice is essentially conventional—what is established is taken as just because it is established (the "reason of effects" prevailing over the "reason of reason"). I want to read Victor Hugo's Les Misérables through this framework without reducing the novel to a mere illustration. Javert seems to fuse justice with state force, while Valjean practices a mercy that operates outside both law and institutional enforcement. Does Hugo ultimately support the Pascalian view that justice detached from conventional backing is unsustainable, or does Valjean's trajectory show that a non-conventional justice can still command genuine normative authority? I'm especially interested in Javert's suicide after Valjean spares him, and in the June Rebellion sequences—do these scenes show that force separated from justice collapses, or that justice without enforcement is necessarily powerless? Could you offer a reading that takes both the narrative structure and Pascal's philosophy seriously, and indicate precisely where the novel resists a Pascalian interpretation rather than confirming it?
