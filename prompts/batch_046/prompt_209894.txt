I'm writing a chapter on Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness and have built an argument that seems to derive an impossibility result from her own commitments. I want to know where the inference slips, and what it costs to reject each candidate step.

(1) In her treatise on happiness, du Châtelet distinguishes three necessary conditions for durable happiness: the absence of significant physical suffering; the continuous satisfaction of the passion for study, which she treats as the highest pleasure because it is self-renewing and largely independent of fortune; and the satisfaction of the passion of love, which she holds requires sustaining a specific illusion about being uniquely chosen by the beloved.

(2) She further maintains that each passion must be satisfied in proportion to its "natural weight" in an individual's temperament; suppressing a dominant passion does not merely reduce pleasure but produces a distinctive form of misery.

(3) The passion for study, on her account, requires what I will call a "solitude of attention": a sustained, uninterrupted orientation of the mind toward abstract objects, and she warns that dividing attention between study and social or domestic obligations degrades the quality of intellectual pleasure.

(4) The passion of love, by contrast, requires sustained interpersonal presence and a willingness to suspend critical judgment; she argues that the illusion sustaining love is fragile and collapses under the analytical distance that rigorous study demands.

(5) Therefore, anyone whose temperament assigns significant weight to both study and love—which she presents as the ideal human constitution—must simultaneously maintain an uninterrupted abstract orientation and a present, uncritical emotional orientation.

(6) These are not merely competing for time; they are structurally incompatible modes of attention. One cannot occupy the cognitive state required for Newtonian mechanics and the affective state required for romantic illusion at the same time.

(7) So du Châtelet's necessary conditions for happiness are jointly unsatisfiable for anyone with the ideal temperament, which implies that happiness is impossible precisely for those best equipped to pursue it.

(8) By extension, if her account is correct, the only consistently happy people are those with a defective or partial temperament—those who lack either the passion for study or the passion for love.

Where does this argument go wrong? If forced to preserve one premise, I would keep (4)—the structural incompatibility between analytical and illusion-sustaining attention—because it seems to me the most defensible part of her psychology of passion. But I am open to being told that the error lies in (2), in a hidden modal slide between (5) and (6), or elsewhere. For each plausible rejection, what does it cost for interpreting her view?
