I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who advances a "configurational-parity" reading of Zhuangzi's attitude toward death. On her account, the self is not a persisting substance but a transient patterning of qi, and death is merely the redistribution of that pattern. She interprets the butterfly dream and Zhuangzi's drumming upon his wife's death not as puzzles about personal identity, but as demonstrations that living and dead states are ontologically continuous—labels we apply to an unbroken process of transformation rather than to genuinely distinct metaphysical conditions. She further argues that grief, on this view, involves a kind of category mistake: we suffer the loss of a configuration we erroneously treated as the stable owner of experiences, even though no such owner ever existed.

Please draft a sequence of eight rigorous interview questions that engage the actual substance of this position rather than asking her to summarize it. The questions should include:

- At least two that press the tension between her denial of a stable self and her need to explain why Zhuangzi distinguishes his own wife's death from that of a stranger. For each, provide a follow-up keyed to her most probable reply—that the distinction is pragmatic, not metaphysical—and push it further.

- One question about whether her reading can preserve the normative weight of the passage in which Zhuangzi asks whether hating death is like being a child lost far from home, without reducing that concern to mere preference-satisfaction.

- One question that raises the standard objection that if living and dead configurations are genuinely on par, then there is no reason to prefer preserving the living form of a loved one; include a follow-up that asks whether her answer quietly reintroduces the value hierarchy she claims to reject.

- One question about ritual mourning and ancestral rites: does configurational parity render such practices incoherent, or can they be reinterpreted within her framework?

- A final question asking what textual feature or experiential consideration could actually weaken her commitment—specifically, whether there is any interpretation of the Zhuangzi that she could not absorb into her transformation narrative and that would instead compel her to abandon the configurational-parity thesis.

Avoid generic biographical questions, requests for bibliographies or reading lists, and appeals to authority. Keep the tone philosophical and precise.
