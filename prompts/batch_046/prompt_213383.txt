I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics with working familiarity in category theory and modal logic, but little formal training in metaphysics beyond the standard analytic curriculum. I want to understand whether David Lewis's modal realism—the thesis that possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated entities, that actuality is indexical, and that trans-world identity is replaced by counterpart relations—can be systematically restated in the vocabulary of category theory, not as a loose metaphor but as a rigorous translation. Please take the core commitments one by one—concreteness of worlds, the plenitude principle, world-isolation, counterpart theory, and the indexical analysis of actuality—and render each using only categorical resources: worlds as objects in a category, accessibility relations as morphisms, individuals and properties as functors or sheaves over these objects, counterpart relations as natural transformations, and actuality as a distinguished object or terminal object. For each commitment, specify exactly what translates without distortion, what must be altered to fit, and what has no categorical expression at all. Pay particular attention to the residue surrounding qualitative resemblance between worlds: for Lewis this is a primitive comparative relation, whereas in categorical terms resemblance might only be recoverable via universal properties or limits that presuppose internal structural agreement rather than brute similarity. Then address whether the untranslatable remainder indicates a genuine limitation of categorical vocabulary for ontology—perhaps that category theory is irreducibly structural and relational, and therefore cannot accommodate the non-structural, brute ontological weight Lewis assigns to worlds—or whether it reveals that those parts of modal realism were less precisely articulated than they initially seemed. I am not looking for pedagogical analogies; I want an honest translation with a rigorous residue report, and I want your judgment on where the fault lies when the translation breaks down.
