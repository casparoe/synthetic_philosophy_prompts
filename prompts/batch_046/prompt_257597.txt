I am writing from a seminar on comparative development and the philosophy of social science, where we have been examining whether historical arguments about national wealth can claim genuine causal status or only correlational patterns. I have formed several tentative verdicts about particular explanatory arguments, but I am unsure whether they are governed by one principle or by incompatible intuitions. Here are the cases and my judgments.

First, the division of Korea is often presented as a near-natural experiment supporting institutional explanations: geography, climate, and pre-division culture were largely shared, yet institutional divergence produced radically different outcomes. I take this to strongly confirm that institutions matter for prosperity, because the counterfactual—South Korean institutions operating in the North—seems clearly implied by the comparison.

Second, the argument that high settler mortality in colonies led to extractive institutions, which in turn caused long-run poverty, strikes me as partially confirmed by the correlation. Yet I feel it is more confirmed when it predicts an intermediate mechanism—say, weak contemporary property rights—than when it merely predicts poverty directly from colonial mortality, since geography could also run directly to poverty through disease ecology. So two theories that both predict the same endpoint feel differently confirmed depending on whether they expose a mediating step that is independently observable.

Third, a theory originally framed to predict that all high-mortality colonies would be poor, but then amended after the fact to accommodate Botswana’s success by adding an exception for societies with strong pre-colonial state capacity, strikes me as barely confirmed by Botswana’s prosperity and perhaps slightly disconfirmed by the need for the patch. If the theory had instead predicted Botswana’s exception in advance, I would have taken it as strongly confirmed.

Fourth, if a cultural explanation—say, one linking particular religious traditions to wealth-creating norms—was formulated after observing wealthy Protestant societies, then I think it confirms almost nothing. But if it was then restricted after failing to predict East Asian growth, so that it applies only to Western cases, I think that restriction weakens it further rather than saving it.

Fifth, a surprising prediction made around 1990 that countries with inclusive political institutions would grow faster over the subsequent decades, which then largely held for a specific set of cases, feels like strong confirmation—provided the definition of “inclusive” was fixed beforehand. If defenders can redefine “inclusive” retrospectively to exclude the failures, then I think the theory has not stayed neutral but has lost some credibility, even though no explicit contradiction occurred.

Sixth, I am inclined to say that the correct standard for confirming a deep historical explanation is counterfactual stability: the explanation is credible only if, had the putative cause been absent, the effect would likely have been absent, and we have independent grounds for that counterfactual claim. But applied rigorously, this seems to undermine my first verdict about Korea, since we do not actually observe North Korea with Southern institutions; we only infer the counterfactual from the comparison. So either my principle is wrong, or my confidence in the Korean case is overstated.

My question is whether there is a coherent account of confirmation—specifically for deep historical explanations of economic development—that can accommodate all six verdicts without collapsing into ad hoc adjustments. Must I drop the counterfactual-stability principle, or must I weaken my judgment about Korea? And can any single principle distinguish the legitimate intermediate-mechanism prediction in the second case from the illegitimate post-hoc patch in the third case, without relying solely on my subjective sense of what was “fixed beforehand”? I would welcome a precise formulation, with explicit notice of which of my verdicts it cannot save and what further cases it would decide differently.
