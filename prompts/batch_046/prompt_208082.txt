As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up moral philosophy, I find the standard literature on moral luck too abstract to satisfy my instinct for operational clarity. I would like you to compose a formal dialogue between two equally rigorous interlocutors—call them the Attributionist and the Process Theorist—debating whether outcomes that lie outside an agent’s direct control can legitimately affect our moral assessment of that agent. To keep the discussion precise, I want the debate structured around an analogy drawn from experimental design: the distinction between systematic bias, in which a confounding variable corrupts the measurement of an underlying quantity, and stochastic noise, in which random fluctuation affects individual measurements but cancels out over repeated trials without invalidating the protocol itself.

The Attributionist should defend the view that certain forms of outcome-luck—particularly constitutive and circumstantial luck—function like systematic bias. They distort our view of the agent’s actual character or choices in ways that cannot be corrected by averaging over cases, and therefore our attributions of praise or blame must be attenuated, or withheld, whenever such confounding is present. The Process Theorist should argue that outcome-variance is better understood as stochastic noise: it alters the result of any single “trial” (a particular action in particular circumstances) but leaves the underlying signal—the agent’s intentions, deliberative processes, and dispositions—fully intact. Consequently, moral assessment should attend exclusively to that internal signal and treat outcome-fluctuation as epistemically irrelevant to judgment, though not necessarily irrelevant to practical response or compensation.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the other’s specific deployment of the analogy, rather than merely restating a familiar position from the literature. In particular, they should address the following tension. If moral assessment genuinely requires repeated “measurements” of an agent’s behavior over a lifetime, then a single catastrophic outcome—say, an unintended harm produced by an unforeseeable environmental variable in an otherwise rigorously controlled action—might be treated as an outlier to be discarded; yet our actual moral practices treat such outliers as highly salient. The Process Theorist must explain why this salience does not confer justificatory weight, and the Attributionist must explain why dismissing the outlier as mere noise is not simply an ad hoc defense of intuitive practice.

Please ground the exchange in a concrete, invented scenario rather than in well-worn examples: consider two research scientists who execute identical ethical and methodological protocols in parallel experiments. In one case, an unmonitored atmospheric fluctuation corrupts a reagent and causes serious harm to a subject; in the other, no such fluctuation occurs and the experiment concludes safely. Have each interlocutor apply their framework to this pair of cases, with each identifying a specific weakness in the other’s application—for instance, whether the Attributionist can distinguish this from ordinary bad luck without collapsing into outcome-moralism, or whether the Process Theorist can account for our differential reactive attitudes without smuggling outcomes back into the assessment of character.

The dialogue should conclude without declaring a victor, but with both parties agreeing on one point where their disagreement may be largely verbal—perhaps concerning whether “moral assessment” denotes evaluation of character, evaluation of the particular action, or evaluation of causal contribution—and identifying one point that remains substantively unresolved, such as whether there exists a principled threshold at which the magnitude of an unintended outcome transforms noise into bias.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to philosophical discussion. Do not include bibliographic references, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
