I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of metaphysics and logic in earnest, and I have constructed an argument that strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to undermine the very possibility of unified thought. I would like you to act as an expert professional philosopher specializing in British idealism and the philosophy of mind, and to think through this carefully, step by step, perhaps beginning with a brief brainstorming of where Bradley’s own resources might lie.

Here is the argument:

(1) A proposition achieves unity only if its constituent terms are genuinely connected rather than merely juxtaposed.
(2) If the connection is an external relation R linking term A and term B, then R must itself be connected to A and to B.
(3) Those further connections require further relations, yielding Bradley’s infinite regress, so an external relation can never complete the unification.
(4) Therefore, true unity requires an internal relation: the terms must somehow contain one another or be inseparable from the connection.
(5) But in every empirically known representational system—whether neural architectures, formal logical notation, or cognitive models—terms are discrete tokens or nodes, and any link between them is an additional, separable element.
(6) Hence, in all actual representational systems, relations are external.
(7) So no actual system can host a genuinely unified proposition.
(8) Yet we clearly do entertain unified thoughts and make true judgments.
(9) Therefore, either unified thought is impossible (which is absurd), or one of the premises above must be rejected.

My instinct is to preserve (8) and question either (4) or (5). Where exactly does the argument slip? Is there a hidden equivocation between logical and causal connection? Does the move from (4) to (5) conflate metaphysical internality with physical inseparability? If I reject (4) and allow that external relations can unify after all, what do I owe to Bradley’s broader absolute idealism? If instead I reject (5), what account of mental or artificial representation am I committed to?

Please diagnose the weakest step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what it costs to reject (4) versus (5) in terms of our understanding of mental or distributed representational architecture.
