I serve as a senior legislative analyst for a regional government drafting a charter for referendum use. I need a reusable decision procedure — not a one-time memo — that my staff (three analysts with law or public-policy backgrounds, no philosophy training) can run 10 to 15 times per year. Each run produces a one-page, auditable justification for whether a proposed policy should be decided by direct referendum or reserved for representative deliberation. The stakes are high: the decision determines whether a bill proceeds to popular vote, and an oversight board or court may review our reasoning. The procedure must be operational: ordered questions, pass/fail or escalate criteria, required documentation at each step, explicit points where judgment replaces calculation, and clear stop rules. It must draw on what democratic theory actually knows about voter competence, preference aggregation, framing effects, and value pluralism — including lessons from the Brexit referendum and California ballot-proposition experience — without requiring staff to read philosophy or cite authors. Inject a synthetic operational framework that is not standard textbook material but is defensible from the underlying theory: distinguish aggregation-competence conditions, framing-vulnerability conditions, and mandate-legitimacy conditions.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, with approximate lengths:

1. Bottom line (approx. 100 words): State the single core principle the procedure protects, the most common misapplication you anticipate, and the unwelcome result it will sometimes produce.

Conceptual framing (approx. 250 words): Define the three synthetic conditions, map each to its theoretical roots without quoting specific texts, explain which parts are standard democratic theory and which are synthetic translations for practitioners, and note the tension between treating voters as epistemic aggregators and as framing-vulnerable agents.
