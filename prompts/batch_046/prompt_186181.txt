I'm writing an essay for a course on political economy and literature, and I'm trying to read Shakespeare's The Tempest through Adam Smith's critique of colonial administration in Wealth of Nations. Rather than treating Prospero's rule as a generic allegory of domination, I want to apply Smith's specific economic argument: that colonial governance operates through artificial monopoly privileges—restricted trade, controlled labor, and exclusive rights—that make the colony appear profitable only to the monopolist, while the actual cost of defence and administration exceeds any genuine yield to the mother country. I want to ask whether Prospero's magic functions as just such a monopoly mechanism: Ariel's labor and Caliban's knowledge of the island are both captured by Prospero's exclusive control, producing the appearance of an orderly, productive realm that collapses without his presence. At the same time, Smith recommends abandoning colonies when their defence costs outweigh benefits; does Prospero's decision to break his staff and leave the island reflect that kind of fiscal rationalization, or does the play frame it as personal renunciation that refuses economic calculation altogether? Which scenes—perhaps the initial shipwreck as a defensive expenditure, or Caliban's complaint that he is robbed of his labor—best illuminate this tension? And where does the play resist being read as a Smithian cost-benefit analysis of empire, especially in its treatment of Ariel's freedom and Caliban's claim to the island? I'd like a reading that takes Smith's distinction between artificial monopoly and natural opulence seriously, without reducing the play to an economic case study or distorting Smith to fit the drama.
