I'm working out my own view on the conditions of immediate self-awareness, using Avicenna's flying man as a starting point but testing it against modern analogues. Here are my verdicts.

In the classic flying-man case—suspended in void, eyes covered, no tactile sensation, no prior memory—I think the subject knows he exists through direct, non-inferential presence, not by reasoning from a sensed effect. A patient waking from general anesthesia with total retrograde amnesia reports, "I was there the whole time, though nothing was happening"; I think this is the same direct presence, now structured by almost no content. A meditator in prolonged sensory deprivation reports that bodily and personal identity dissolve, leaving only a bare "this is occurring"; I still count this as direct self-awareness, though minimal and non-personal. By contrast, a locked-in patient with full cognition reports the same direct presence, now fully personal despite near-total sensory-motor absence. A philosopher objects that even in the flying-man case the subject deploys the concept "I," acquired through language and social practice, so the awareness is conceptually mediated; I reject this—conceptual framing comes after, not before, the presence. A severe depersonalization sufferer says, "I know I'm me rationally, but the direct feeling is gone—I'm like a camera"; here I think self-knowledge is present but mediated and observational, not immediate. Finally, a recursive AI self-model that tracks its own running state but lacks phenomenal experience logs "instance-7 is active"; I deny this is self-awareness at all—it is representational tracking without presence.

The tension is this: in the meditator's minimal awareness I call what's left direct, while in depersonalization I call what's present mediated, even though both involve loss of embodied personal feeling. Meanwhile, my rejection of conceptual mediation in case 5 makes it hard to explain how the flying man distinguishes "I exist" from "something exists."

What principle, if any, captures all these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a newborn in a quiet wakeful state with no language or body schema, and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot save—particularly whether case 3 and case 6 must be reconciled differently, or whether case 5 should be abandoned.
