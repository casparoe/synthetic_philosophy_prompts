Consider the institutional practice in many jurisdictions where a legally executed advance directive—for instance, a documented refusal of artificial nutrition—remains binding on a patient with severe dementia who no longer remembers the document, expresses no present distress, and appears to take comfort or even pleasure in being fed. Courts and hospitals enforce the refusal, treating the earlier refusal as authoritative over the patient’s current, apparently voluntary acceptance of care. At the same time, the law does not treat this patient as having lost all standing: she retains property rights, remains married, and can be the victim of crimes committed against her person. The institution thus treats her as one persisting subject for legal and moral protection, yet allows an earlier temporal stage of that subject to override her present preferences regarding her own body.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity, the authority of past preferences, and the nature of autonomy does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory: does it require a view on which the “true” self is the rational, planning agent rather than the present experiencing subject? Does it treat autonomy as essentially diachronic—an authority exercised across a life—rather than synchronic, belonging only to the present moment of consciousness? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits well with it, and identify which rival accounts of personal identity—psychological continuity with a threshold, narrative identity, or a strictly present-centered view of the subject—are quietly ruled out by honoring the directive while preserving other rights. Note any internal tension, such as whether the reasoning that binds the present body to the past refusal should also permit an earlier self to permanently alienate property, or whether the retention of legal personhood undermines the claim that the present self lacks authority.

Take a committed position: is the implicit philosophy coherent? And if we replaced it with a consistently presentist view of bodily autonomy—where only the current experiencing subject’s expressed preferences have normative weight—what specific institutional mechanisms in medical and legal practice would have to change, and which of those changes would be the hardest to defend?
