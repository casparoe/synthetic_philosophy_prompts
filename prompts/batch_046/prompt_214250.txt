I've been trying to understand how minds actually solve problems, not in the abstract but in terms of real architectures. I keep coming back to the familiar division — minds that are essentially hardwired by evolutionary selection, minds shaped by reinforcement, minds that generate and kill off internal hypotheses, and minds that extend themselves through tools and culture. But recently I imagined a different architecture, one that doesn't pick among discrete options or formulate explicit predictions: instead, it maintains many partial, conflicting representations at once, letting them interfere constructively and destructively until a coherent state crystallizes — something like an interferometric cognition, with no central executive and no clear boundary between representation and resolution. Before I decide whether this is just a variant of one of the four familiar kinds or something genuinely different, I want the ground rules.

What must any adequate theory of cognitive kinds actually deliver? Give me the full desiderata: the phenomena it has to cover, the distinctions it must preserve rather than collapse (for example, between adapting through selection and adapting through restructuring, or between individual and distributed problem-solving), the results from biology, neuroscience, or computation it cannot contradict, and the boundary cases — including synthetic or collective systems — it must accommodate or explicitly exclude. For each requirement, explain why it is genuinely necessary rather than a preference of one camp, and flag any pair of desiderata you think might be jointly unsatisfiable — particularly whether insisting on a central hypothesis-testing mechanism is compatible with allowing genuinely distributed, interference-based resolution without explicit alternatives.

Then, briefly, score the four standard kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — against this list, and tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the standard taxonomy if we take it seriously.
