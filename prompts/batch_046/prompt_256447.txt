I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind currently embedded with a psychiatric intake unit that evaluates approximately thirty new patients each week, and I have been asked to help resolve a recurring conceptual impasse among the clinical staff. When patients offer emotional self-reports—statements like "I am terrified of this medication" or "this room feels hostile to me"—the team divides along implicit theoretical lines. Some clinicians interpret these utterances as cognitive judgments about risk or value, which suggests that the appropriate initial response is informational or reasoning-based; others interpret them as perceptual registrations of bodily or environmental danger, which calls for somatic regulation or contextual modification before any discussion of belief; still others interpret them as raw affective feelings that require labeling and containment as a prerequisite to further intervention. Because these interpretations lead to different immediate clinical pathways, I need a formal, reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping conditions—that a non-philosopher clinician can apply in approximately five minutes during intake to determine which of the three frameworks should govern the initial response.

The procedure must specify concrete linguistic indicators and behavioral correlates that distinguish emotional judgments, emotional perceptions, and raw affective feelings; it must include explicit rules for handling cases that display features of more than one category, since mixed presentations are common in our population; and it must clearly demarcate the points where classification is genuinely indeterminate and must be handed back to clinical judgment rather than resolved procedurally. Please apply the procedure to at least two concrete intake scenarios: one straightforward case and one counterintuitive case in which the procedure classifies the emotional state in a way that conflicts with the intuitively correct clinical response, explaining precisely why the misfire occurs. Additionally, state explicitly what this procedure cannot settle—particularly regarding emotional reports that have been retrospectively reconstructed through prior therapeutic framing or mediated by continuous self-monitoring practices—without providing bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations, since the deliverable must be immediately operational for practitioners without access to philosophical literature.
