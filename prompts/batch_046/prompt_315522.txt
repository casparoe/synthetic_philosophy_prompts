I keep noticing a pattern in debates about whether artificial systems—things running large predictive models or distributed simulations—should be treated as having any moral weight at all. Someone will say they cannot matter because they lack a single, continuous inner experience like ours. If you show that a system might have something structurally similar, they do not drop the objection but switch to something else—perhaps that they do not form their own goals, or are not made of living tissue, or do not genuinely suffer. The new requirement was not part of the first argument, and it is often something we cannot clearly check for even in people. Each step sounds reasonable on its own, but together they seem designed to keep the conclusion that computation never counts, regardless of architecture. I have seen this happen with consciousness, with agency, and with the capacity to feel. Is there a specific name for this kind of sequential boundary-shifting in moral-status arguments? How is it different from just adding an exception to a theory, or from the no-true-Scotsman kind of retreat? And if there is no precise term, what marks its boundary—does it require that the new criterion be impossible to verify, or is it enough that it was introduced only after the first one was satisfied?
