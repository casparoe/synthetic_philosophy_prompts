I am a doctoral student working on the sociology of normative change, and I have been trying to make sense of a recurring pattern in the decline of practices once treated as obligatory markers of elite status—dueling among European aristocrats, foot-binding in particular Chinese social strata, or regionally specific forms of honor violence. In each case, the practice does not vanish because participants suddenly converge on its moral wrongness, nor because it becomes legally banned or economically irrational. Instead, I observe a distinctive two-phase trajectory. First, the practice is gradually abandoned by the most cosmopolitan or centrally positioned members of the elite, not through explicit moral argument, but through a shift in taste and social association: continuing to participate comes to signal provinciality, a lack of refinement, or an embarrassing attachment to obsolete codes. Second—and this is the part I find conceptually elusive—the remaining practitioners, often those on the social periphery or in subgroups that feel marginalized by the cosmopolitan center, do not defend the practice by appealing to traditional honor or moral superiority. Rather, they reframe it as an authentic expression of local identity, a refusal of elite hypocrisy, or a form of resistance against homogenizing modernity. The practice thereby survives in pockets not as a claim to high status, but as a claim to unvarnished authenticity, precisely while the elite uses its abandonment to distinguish itself.

Does this specific configuration—where the social meaning of a practice inverts from hierarchical honor to anti-hierarchical authenticity, and where the remaining defense is structurally opposed to the original prestige logic—have an established conceptual name? I am particularly interested in how it relates to the view, associated with Bernard Williams and developed in Kwame Anthony Appiah’s work on moral revolutions, that practices such as dueling or slavery often collapse because they become incompatible with modern honor structures rather than through direct moral persuasion or material interest. Is the pattern I have described merely an elaboration of that honor-based account—perhaps a description of how honor codes fragment rather than uniformly dissolve—or is it a genuinely distinct mechanism that complicates the Williams-Appiah framework? If there is no precise term for this inversion-and-resistance dynamic, what conceptual distinctions would be necessary to separate it cleanly from the standard honor-collapse narrative, and where exactly should one draw the boundary between prestige-based abandonment and authenticity-based retention?
