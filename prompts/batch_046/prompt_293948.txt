I serve on my university's Committee on Ethical Partnerships in Career Development. We must decide whether to renew our institutional endorsement of the Quantified Altruism Career Track, a partnership that steers graduates into high-compensation sectors with a binding pledge to donate twenty percent of earnings to charities vetted by the program's internal metrics board. The program reports impact solely through counterfactual donation yield—estimated dollars given that would not have been donated otherwise—and excludes collective structural interventions, such as labor-backed wage advocacy or regulatory reform campaigns, from its approved list on the grounds that their outcomes lack quantifiable confidence intervals.

Two members have raised conceptual objections. One argues that the program embodies a measurability bias that systematically privileges individually tractable actions over collective structural change, treating moral efficacy as a commodity that can be outsourced from the earner to a metrics board. The other contends that earning to give alienates moral agency by separating the labor generating resources from the judgment about where they should go, producing an institutional culture in which wealthy graduates function as passive revenue streams rather than engaged moral agents. A third member defends the program, insisting that the counterfactual yield is genuinely large and that criticizing measurability is a luxury argument that ignores present, addressable suffering.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—approve renewal, reject it, or approve with specific conditions—including a concrete recommendation and an implementation timeline within one academic year. The memo should take the defense's point about present suffering seriously; address whether measurability bias is an unavoidable feature of evaluation or a correctable institutional flaw; and propose at least one precise structural condition, such as a revised charity-approval criterion, a mandatory reflective practice for participants, or an independent audit sunset clause, rather than a vague call for further dialogue. The reasoning should remain accessible to administrators who are not philosophers, but it should not dilute the conceptual stakes.
