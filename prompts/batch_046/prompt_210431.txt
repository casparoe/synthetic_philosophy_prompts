As a journalist covering science, ethics, and food systems, I have been drafting a feature on the hidden arithmetic of dietary harm and have built what I intended as a reductio of standard vegetarian arguments—only to find that it now seems to dissolve almost all consumer ethics, and I cannot locate the precise exit. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in applied ethics, moral psychology, and decision theory; please think carefully, begin with brief conceptual clarification, and then proceed step by step.

Here is the argument. (1) If an action foreseeably contributes to suffering, and an available alternative contributes less suffering without comparable cost, then choosing the action is morally wrong. (2) Eating chicken requires cultivated feed grains, and the harvesting of those grains foreseeably kills wild rodents, birds, and insects; eating beef—especially from pasture-raised ruminants—can convert non-arable grassland into calories with far less direct crop cultivation, though it involves the suffering of the cow and some pasture-related wild-animal displacement. (3) Per unit of human nutrition, the wild-animal mortality mediated through feed-crop cultivation is therefore higher for chicken than for well-managed beef, and direct plant consumption involves comparable or greater crop-related harm. (4) So if (1) is correct, one ought to prefer beef over chicken, and perhaps over grain- or legume-based diets. (5) By the same reasoning, any use of land or energy—housing, clothing, digital infrastructure, transportation—foreseeably contributes to wild-animal suffering through habitat conversion and climate effects. (6) Therefore (1) implies that any lifestyle beyond bare subsistence is impermissible. (7) Since that conclusion is intolerable, (1) must be false, and foreseeable contribution to diffuse suffering does not by itself generate moral obligations. (8) But if (1) falls, then the familiar argument that we ought to reduce meat consumption because it foreseeably causes farmed-animal suffering falls with it, leaving no special dietary obligation.

I would prefer to preserve (1) and (4) if consistency allows, though I suspect the error may lie in how (3) compares marginal effects, or in a scope shift between comparing specific foods and condemning all consumption. Which step should I reject, why did it initially look defensible, and what does rejecting it cost—particularly for those who defend plant-based diets on harm-reduction grounds, those who accept indirect obligations but limit their scope, and those who deny that wild-animal suffering carries the same moral weight as farmed-animal suffering? Please do not offer bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from first principles, and if you identify an equivocation, quantifier shift, or modal slide between steps, name it precisely.
