I'm prepping a seminar on collective epistemology and I've been wrestling with the discursive dilemma—y'know, that result where a group can have perfectly consistent individual judgments but still reach an incoherent collective conclusion if they vote on premises seperately. I sketched some rough notes comparing premise-based aggregation with conclusion-based aggregation, but I'm not sure I'm being clear about where the incoherence actually lives versus where it just gets hidden.

Here's the gist: suppose a three-person committee is deciding whether to approve a risky research grant. They vote on whether the science is sound (P1), whether the risk is acceptable (P2), and whether to approve (C, which logically follows from P1 and P2). With majority rule on each, you can get P1 yes, P2 yes, but C no—the classic dilemma. I then argue that if we switch to premise-based aggregation—where they only vote on P1 and P2 and derive C—we solve the logical incoherence but open up a different problem: strategic framing. The committee can manipulate what counts as a 'premise' depending on which conclusion they want. For instance, if someone wants to reject the grant, they might insist that 'public perception risk' is actually a separate premise rather than part of P2, thereby changing the logical structure of the decision.

My question is: is this framing-manipulation worry genuinely distinct from the strategic voting problems we already discuss in social choice theory? Or is it just the same issue wearing different clothes? Also, I compare this briefly to Condorcet's jury theorem—suggesting that premise-based aggregation might actually reduce the epistemic value of the group if framing disputes lower the reliability of premise judgments. Does that comparison hold, or am I conflating two different kinds of rationality? Could you read these notes and tell me what's confused, what's redundant, and whether there's anything here worth expanding? I'm particularly unsure about the move where I claim that frame-sensitivity undermines the normative appeal of premise-based rules—maybe I'm overstating the case?
