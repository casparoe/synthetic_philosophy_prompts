You are delivering the opening remarks—approximately seven minutes—at the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Conventions Across Borders," held in a city with notoriously strict unwritten rules about queueing, tipping, and pedestrian crossings. Half the audience are philosophers and social scientists who study collective intentionality and moral obligation; the other half are graduate students, local administrators, and invited practitioners with no background in normative theory. The workshop asks whether everyday conventions—tipping where service is included, queuing in single file even at empty counters, and refraining from jaywalking at deserted intersections—generate genuine moral obligations for temporary visitors, or whether they remain mere local habits that outsiders may ignore without fault.

Your task is not to survey the literature but to frame the debate structurally, using a conceptual distinction you introduce for this occasion: distinguish "inhabited norms" (practices that locals live inside, whose social meaning and point they grasp pre-reflectively) from "navigated norms" (practices that visitors must decode, comply with, or violate from an external position). Build the speech around a single concrete scenario: a visiting scholar who, over the course of one day, waits alone at a red pedestrian signal at 2 a.m. with no traffic (and is quietly mocked by passing locals), refuses to jaywalk across an empty street the next morning despite being late, and tips exactly ten percent at dinner despite knowing that service is already included—thereby committing a different kind of error in each case.

Use this triad to show that the moral question is not binary—"follow local rules" versus "do what you think best"—but concerns whether a visitor can honor an inhabited norm without falsely pretending to inhabit it. Argue that there is a distinctive ethical posture available only to the temporary outsider, which you term "normative hospitality": the willingness to bear the costs of a local practice not because one accepts its authority, but because one recognizes that one's presence is already a kind of borrowing. End by posing the workshop's guiding question: if normative hospitality is a real virtue, does it apply only to visible conventions like queuing and tipping, or does it extend to invisible ones—such as the local tolerance for ambiguity, silence, or disagreement—that a visitor might violate without ever knowing?

Keep the tone formal but accessible; avoid citations, author names, and bibliographic references entirely; and ensure that the philosophical distinction does real work, so that the speech would collapse if "inhabited" and "navigated" norms were treated as the same thing.
