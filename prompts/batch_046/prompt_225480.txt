For a workshop on consciousness as a user-illusion, I have sketched the following position and need an internal consistency check before presenting. I hold that: (1) phenomenal consciousness is a user-illusion generated by self-modeling architectures, with no corresponding intrinsic qualitative properties; (2) first-person reports are generally reliable, but they report the structure of the user-model rather than direct acquaintance with raw feels; (3) the hard problem dissolves once we recognize that phenomenal concepts are indexical and anchored to working-memory buffers, so that the absence of a physical explanation feels mysterious without indicating a real ontological gap; (4) any system implementing comparable predictive self-modeling together with narrative coherence produces the same illusion, and this is sufficient for moral consideration; and (5) thermostats lack both the architecture and the indexical concept, so there is genuinely nothing it is like to be one.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension rather than only apparently so? In particular, does (2) implicitly concede that the user-model tracks something real, thereby undermining (1)? Does (4) commit me to attributing moral status to systems that possess predictive error-minimization but lack working-memory buffers, and can I block that inference without ad hoc stipulation? Is (3)'s dissolution strategy compatible with (1)'s eliminativism about qualitative properties, or does explaining away the gap presuppose a residual phenomenal feature? Rank the tensions by severity, specify the least costly revision for each, and indicate whether any single change would resolve the majority of conflicts.
