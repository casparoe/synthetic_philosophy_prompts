Please explain the conceptual issue involved in what we might call the disquotational equilibrium problem for deflationary theories of truth. The core idea is this: if the truth predicate is nothing more than a device for disquotation—so that to say “S is true” is just to say S—then any disagreement about which sentences are true should reduce to a disagreement about which sentences to assert. Now consider a case of radical empirical underdetermination in physics: two ontologically distinct frameworks (for instance, a discrete, graph-theoretic quantum gravity model and a continuous, manifold-based generalization) generate exactly the same observable predictions across every domain currently accessible to measurement, yet each asserts incompatible sentences about the microstructure of spacetime. A scientific realist wants to say that exactly one framework is true of the world and the other is false, despite the predictive tie. The deflationist, by contrast, seems able to say only that the two scientific communities assert different sentences. Explain the precise conceptual pressure point: can the deflationist maintain that there is a genuine, non-pragmatic disagreement—one that is about how things are, not merely about linguistic practice—without smuggling in a substantive correspondence relation between sentences and reality? Specifically, address whether appeals to “assertibility conditions,” “inferential role,” or “epistemic norms” can do the work the realist demands, and whether those appeals themselves covertly depend on the very notion of getting the world right that deflationism was meant to eliminate. I am not asking for a historical survey or a bibliography; I want a conceptual map of where the deflationist’s machinery breaks down or holds firm, and what that implies for how we should understand scientific disagreement under empirical equivalence.
