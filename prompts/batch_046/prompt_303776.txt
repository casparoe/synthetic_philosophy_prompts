Consider the following notes for a short essay on institutional path dependence. I argue that the concept is systematically overextended from technological cases to political ones. In technology—say, railway gauge or keyboard layout—early adoption creates network effects that genuinely lock in suboptimal standards. In political institutions, however, what looks like lock-in is usually functional convergence through local vocabulary. Take church-state finance across Western Europe: England retains an establishment funded through historical endowments, voluntary parish contribution, and limited state support for heritage buildings; Germany uses Kirchensteuer collected via state payroll administration; France practices direct state maintenance of religious heritage and, in some regions, clergy salaries within a framework of laïcité; and the US relies on tax-exempt status and indirect subsidy. The constitutional vocabularies are path-dependent—rooted in 19th-century settlements—but the fiscal outcomes converge on a stable equilibrium: religious institutions receive predictable funding without direct state theological control. If history had branched differently at the critical juncture, the legal form would differ, yet the functional settlement likely would not. Thus macro-institutional history is not locked in in the strong sense; it is locked in only at the level of symbolic classification, while the underlying distribution of resources remains open to convergent redesign.

Please comment on these notes. Specifically: Is the technological/institutional distinction defensible, or does it rely on an arbitrary separation? Does the claim about functional convergence in church-state finance overlook genuine structural differences—for example, in state neutrality, coercion, or the presence of a formal state church? And is there a coherent middle position between strong path dependence and pure convergence?
