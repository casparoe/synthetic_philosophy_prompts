I'm taking a course on comparative epistemology, and in our discussion of Jain knowledge theory we have become preoccupied with anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality is irreducibly complex and that any single judgment expresses only one partial standpoint (naya), so that apparently contradictory claims can each be valid from different perspectives, qualified by the modal particle syāt.

Rather than treating this as purely metaphysical, my study group wants to see what institutional form it might take in a contemporary setting not usually linked to religious epistemology. We invented the case of a university review board charged with evaluating a single interdisciplinary report on climate adaptation, where atmospheric scientists, economic historians, and urban planners each submit rigorously defended but mutually incompatible assessments of the same evidence. The board operates under what we call a polyphonic charter: every disciplinary verdict must be recorded with its modal qualifier intact, and the board is explicitly forbidden to synthesize these into one authoritative conclusion or rank them by a meta-criterion.

Given this framing, please describe the most important implications of anekāntavāda for how such a board should understand its own epistemic authority, its obligations to the public, and the status of its final report. Present the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
