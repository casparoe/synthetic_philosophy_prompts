I'm taking an upper-division course on mathematical knowledge and metaphysics. After our seminar on Benacerraf's dilemma—the challenge that if mathematical objects are causally inert abstracta then we cannot know them, while if they are not abstract then standard mathematics is false—two classmates, Maya and Jordan, continued debating in the common room. I was present but stayed out of it. Here is what they said, reported as faithfully as I can remember.

Maya opened by saying that Benacerraf's dilemma only genuinely threatens full-blooded Platonists who also hold a causal theory of knowledge. On a structuralist view, she argued, we do not need causal contact with individual mathematical objects; we need only understand structures, and we access those structures through their physical instantiations and formal representations. So the dilemma is dissolved rather than solved.

Jordan replied that structuralism does not escape so easily. Even if mathematics is about structures rather than objects, he said, we still make true claims about structures that are never physically instantiated—think of uncountable sets or large-cardinal axioms. If all our access is mediated by physical instantiation, then either our knowledge is restricted to what is physically realizable, which is wildly revisionary, or we have smuggled in some non-causal access mechanism after all.

Maya then introduced what she called a proxy-relation account: mathematical knowledge is never direct object-knowledge but is always mediated through formal representations, proof procedures, and physical reasoning processes. The objectivity of mathematics comes from the stability of these proxy relations across representations, not from causal interaction with abstract entities. She insisted this handles even infinite or non-instantiated cases, because the proxy relations are governed by formal rules that do not require actual instantiation to be known.

Jordan countered with what he called the scaling problem. For structures that are provably too large or complex to have any physical proxy—for instance, structures corresponding to large cardinal axioms or non-constructive existence claims—the proxy-relation account must either deny that we genuinely know them, which contradicts ordinary mathematical practice, or admit that for these cases we rely on something other than proxy mediation. That something other, he argued, is precisely the direct, unexplained access that Benacerraf showed was mysterious.

Maya responded by proposing a tiered epistemology. She granted that knowledge of base, instantiated structures comes through proxy relations. Knowledge of more remote structures, she said, is derivative: it comes by logical entailment from the base cases together with the formal rules. We do not need direct access to the remote structures themselves; we need only know that the rules preserve truth. This, she claimed, avoids both causal mysticism and revisionism.

Jordan said this is just relabeling. If the base cases are proxy-known and the rest follow by logic, we still need an account of how we know the logical rules apply correctly to those remote domains. Knowing that modus ponens preserves truth across all structures, including non-instantiated ones, requires knowing something about those structures—or at least about the uniformity of logical consequence across domains. That requirement, he concluded, reinstates Benacerraf's original challenge at the meta-level: how do we have that knowledge without causal access?
