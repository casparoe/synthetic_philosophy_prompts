As a retired evolutionary biologist who has only recently begun to read philosophy of science, I find myself returning to the old priority disputes—Newton and Leibniz over calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection. In my former discipline we distinguish strong convergence, in which selection rebuilds the same underlying mechanism, from weak convergence, in which only the functional outcome is shared while the structural architecture differs. I should like you to write a formal dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors, A and B, who apply this biological distinction directly to the philosophy of discovery.

A should defend the view that simultaneous invention is inevitable weak convergence: the intellectual environment makes a particular idea available, so that Newton and Leibniz, or Darwin and Wallace, reach functionally equivalent results independently, and the identity of the author is therefore philosophically incidental—much as the camera eye of a vertebrate and that of a cephalopod share function without shared construction. B should defend the opposing view that these are not cases of weak convergence at all, but strongly contingent achievements in which the representational framework, evidentiary base, and auxiliary commitments differ so profoundly—Leibniz’s notation and metaphysical framing versus Newton’s fluxions and mechanics; Darwin’s branching tree, extensive fossil and biogeographical evidence, and auxiliary hypotheses about sexual selection versus Wallace’s more austere geographical selectionism—that to call them the “same” discovery obscures the very structural features that determine a theory’s downstream fertility, its conceptual descendants, and its integration with adjacent fields.

They must not talk past one another. B should address A’s convergence analogy head-on, asking whether functional equivalence in science can ever truly be separated from representational architecture, and whether treating calculus or evolutionary theory as interchangeable products misses the role of notation and evidence in shaping what questions become askable next. A should press B in return, asking whether an overemphasis on authorship, notation, and auxiliary hypotheses risks conflating the sociology of science with its epistemic content—if the world genuinely constrains the result, then the fact that two independent minds arrived at it should diminish, not magnify, the importance of whose mind it was. The exchange should be precise, avoid strawman simplification, and not devolve into mere historical narration. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
