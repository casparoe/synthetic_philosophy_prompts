I am a doctoral student in philosophy of law, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled Coercion, Compliance, and Normative Order, hosted jointly by my university's law school and its philosophy department. The audience is deliberately split: roughly half are specialists in seventeenth-century political thought who know Pascal's Pensées intimately, and the other half are empirical legal scholars and sociologists of law who have little patience for historical exegesis and will not recognize fragment numbers. The organizers asked me to frame the workshop's central question—whether legal legitimacy can be understood independently of the coercive mechanisms that enforce it—through a single philosophical lens rather than through a literature review.

I want the remarks, about ten minutes, to take Pascal's argument that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical as genuinely structural, not decorative. Specifically, I want to use that coupling to establish a ground rule for the proceedings: any presentation that treats legitimacy as either a purely normative property, entirely detached from enforcement, or as a purely descriptive sociological fact, entirely detached from justification, should be understood to have missed the Pascalian point and ought to be challenged from the floor. At the same time, I want to gesture briefly at Pascal's distinction between the conventional, force-backed order of justice and the higher order of charity or true justice—a distinction that modern discussions of legal legitimacy often collapse—without turning the workshop into a history seminar. I discussed this framing with my advisor, and she cautioned me that many contemporary readings conflate Pascal's account of custom and force with a proto-Hobbesian theory of sovereignty, an interpretation that is genuinely contested rather than settled among Pascal scholars.

Please write the address so that it is meaningful to the philosophers without alienating the empiricists, and so that it avoids presenting any contested interpretation of Pascal—especially the proto-Hobbesian comparison—as the consensus view. The speech should conclude by articulating the workshop's constraint: we are not here to dissolve the justice-force coupling, but to map its varieties. In your response, clearly separate what is broadly accepted in Pascal interpretation from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who know the Pensées well, and do not present a disputed reading as if it were the standard one.
