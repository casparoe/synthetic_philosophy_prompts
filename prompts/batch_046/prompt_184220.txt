I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background; I build civic-infrastructure systems that allocate public resources or enforce municipal rules. I need an operational framework, not a lecture, for evaluating whether a proposed technical feature structurally embeds a political arrangement—meaning it enforces or channels a specific distribution of power, access, or accountability—rather than merely being used for political ends by people.

Please construct a reusable evaluation procedure my team can run on any proposal. Ground it in Langdon Winner’s technological-determinist argument Do Artifacts Have Politics (Daedalus, 1980; The Whale and the Reactor, 1986), but translate it into three ordered engineering filters: (1) Material Lock-In—what the digital or physical architecture makes unavoidable or impossible regardless of user intent; (2) Protocol Bias—what behaviors the system rewards, penalizes, or excludes by design; and (3) Governance Delegation—what human judgments are removed, obscured, or automated away. For each filter, state the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the answer forces a “no” (structurally neutral on this dimension), and where the question is irreducibly about values and must be handed to a stakeholder committee.

Be explicit about misfire conditions: for example, when an artifact’s politics depend entirely on deployment context, or when an identical feature is politically loaded in one jurisdiction but not another. Then apply the procedure to two proposals:

A. An automated public-housing eligibility engine that pulls income-verification APIs and denies applications without human review whenever data conflicts exceed a fixed threshold.

B. A traffic-signal coordination algorithm that grants permanent green-wave priority to emergency vehicles and public transit, but requires private vehicles to yield at every intersection.

Take a committed position: do artifacts, in a sense that should actually constrain engineering practice, have politics? Give your reasons and make definite choices—do not simply map both sides of the debate or leave every hard case open.
