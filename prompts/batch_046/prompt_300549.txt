I have been trying to understand illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—the view that our sense of having private, qualitative experiences, like the particular feel of seeing crimson or tasting cinnamon, is actually a kind of systematic misrepresentation produced by our cognitive architecture. The illusionists say there are brain processes and beliefs about them, but no extra phenomenal layer that makes those processes feel like anything.

This made me think of Immanuel Kant, especially his analysis of transcendental illusion, where reason naturally generates ideas—such as an infinite universe or a necessary being—that seem like discoveries but are really unavoidable byproducts of how our minds work. Kant insisted that some illusions are structurally built in; they are not ordinary empirical errors that observation could correct.

So I want to ask: if Kant were alive today and confronted with illusionism, would he classify the apparent what-it-is-likeness of consciousness as one of these unavoidable transcendental illusions—something built into the form of inner sense—or as a correctable empirical error, more like mistaking a stick for a snake?

There is also a specific objection that seems relevant. Critics of illusionism argue that if consciousness is an illusion, then there must still be an appearance that is being misinterpreted, and that appearance just is phenomenal consciousness, which makes the view self-refuting. Defenders reply that this is like a mirage: there is no appearance of water separate from the misperception; there is only light, sand, and a false belief.

Given Kant’s framework—particularly his claim that time and space are forms of intuition rather than properties of things in themselves, and his distinction between phenomena and noumena—how do you think he would resolve that dispute? Would he say our inner intuition of consciousness is necessarily phenomenal, so that denying its reality collapses into denying the conditions of experience? Or could he accept that even our innermost sense is systematically misleading about its own nature, without destroying the possibility of knowledge?

Please reason from his commitments rather than offering a modern summary, and do not provide a reading list or citations.
