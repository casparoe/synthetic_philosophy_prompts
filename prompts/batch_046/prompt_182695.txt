In discussions of consciousness, the opposition between the Cartesian theater—the idea that experience requires a central, unified point where "it all comes together"—and Dennett's multiple drafts model, which treats consciousness as a distributed, time-extended process of competing narrative fragments, is usually presented as settled: neuroscience has shown there is no single brain region where everything converges, so the theater is out, and drafts are in. Yet in my working group the debate has stalled in a peculiar way. Everyone accepts that global workspace and predictive processing architectures are distributed; everyone accepts there is no homunculus watching a screen. Nevertheless, one side insists that without a "binding moment"—a functional point at which competing drafts are adjudicated into a single reportable stream—we have not explained subjective unity but only renamed it. The other side insists that any such "adjudication point" is just another draft, and that demanding a further convergence is to smuggle the theater back in under functional clothing. The disagreement has persisted for years without either side conceding ground, despite shared commitment to the same empirical literature.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about neuroscience but about what counts as dissolving versus explaining a phenomenological datum: specifically, whether the feeling of being a single, continuous subject is a problem to be solved (requiring a mechanism that produces unity) or an illusion to be dissolved (requiring a demonstration that no such mechanism is necessary). Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about whether reportability requires synchronous integration across modalities, or about whether temporal binding can be genuinely diachronic without a synchronous core? Or are the two sides actually asking different questions, one about the architecture of cognition and the other about the conditions for first-person authority? I want an honest structural diagnosis: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or bedrock intuitive? What would actually move it—an empirical result about cross-modal integration, a conceptual distinction between phenomenal unity and narrative coherence, a formal argument about the impossibility of distributed reportability, or something else? And if no progress should be expected, why not?
