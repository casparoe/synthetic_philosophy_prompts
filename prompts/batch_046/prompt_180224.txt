I am in my final year of sixth form, and after a recent discussion with my philosophy tutor about the inadequacy of binary true-or-false frameworks, I have become rather absorbed in Jain anekāntavāda—not, I should add, with the familiar classroom comparison to Western relativism, which seems to me rather tired. For an upcoming independent research competition aimed at pre-university students, I am required to submit a formal proposal outlining a six-month project rather than a finished essay. Rather than offering a general exposition of Jain epistemology, I wish to explore a specific conceptual tension: the doctrine of syādvāda insists that every predication be qualified by syāt, conventionally glossed as 'perhaps', on the grounds that reality possesses infinite, non-contradictory aspects (anekānta); yet this very qualification appears to paralyse decisive judgement in practical domains such as climate-risk assessment, where contradictory expert predictions must nevertheless be translated into binding policy. I should like you to draft the proposal. Its central question should be whether the seven-fold modal structure of syādvāda—known as saptabhaṅgī—can be reconstructed not merely as a semantic theory of partial truth, but as a decision-theoretic framework for weighing contradictory expert testimony, and whether such a reconstruction supplies a principled, non-arbitrary rule for selecting one modal qualification over another when collective action is urgently required.

The proposal ought to clarify why existing accounts of epistemic pluralism leave this particular problem untouched, propose a comparative method that examines the logical architecture of saptabhaṅgī alongside contemporary practices of Bayesian model averaging without presupposing advanced mathematical training, state precisely what would count as success or failure for the project, and remain rigorously within the scope of a single term's independent study. I do not require a bibliography or list of citations, as I shall compile those separately; instead, I need careful conceptual framing, a clear argumentative structure, and an honest evaluation of the risk that the comparison might prove nothing more than an elaborate analogy. Please write in a polished, formal register suitable for a review panel, and bear in mind that I am writing from a British educational context.
