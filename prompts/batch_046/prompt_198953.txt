I have just come from a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike is genuinely normative or merely a pre-modern prejudice against commerce. My friend kept reducing the distinction to a simple dislike of markets, and I found myself unable to produce a concrete, non-anachronistic counterexample that shows the framework can invert modern intuitions without begging the question. I need you to construct one precisely.

Design a realistic, one-paragraph thought experiment—set in an ordinary cooperative workplace or neighborhood exchange, not in science fiction or fantasy—in which Aristotle’s own taxonomy forces him to judge a practice involving extensive accumulation and circulation of coin as virtuous household management (oikonomia), while judging a practice that involves virtually no exchange, no coin, and direct consumption of goods as vicious wealth-getting (chrematistike). The reversal must be generated strictly from premises Aristotle actually holds: the distinction between use-value and exchange-value, the idea that every artifact has a natural purpose for which it was made, the claim that there is a natural limit to wealth in the household art but none in retail trade, and the principle that using a thing according to its nature is proper while using it against its nature is improper. You must not invoke anachronistic machinery—no compound interest, no speculative bubbles, no modern notions of liquidity or capital efficiency—to produce the inversion.
