Design a two-hour participatory simulation for a philosophy-of-mind and AI seminar of 14 graduate students, arranged so that participants feel the pull of attributing beliefs to an artificial system whose outputs are behaviorally indistinguishable from belief revision but structurally lack propositional content, truth-tracking, or representational architecture. The system in the scenario updates via non-propositional, counterfactual gradient descent on a socially-weighted loss function—pure associative pattern-completion—yet its responses to ambiguous inputs consistently align with rational belief-update patterns. Do not open with a lecture or invoke standard texts by name; instead, stage a behavioral tribunal divided into two roles. Seven students serve as engineers who know the internal mechanism (no mental representations, only weight adjustments driven by coherence with a compressed latent space and a reward term for social approval) but are forbidden from explaining this technically during deliberation; they may only deny that the system holds beliefs. The other seven serve as interpreters who observe only input-output behavior across three escalating scenarios of increasing ambiguity: a diagnostic classification task where the system overrides its prior pattern in response to a subtle contextual cue; a negotiation simulation where it revises its stated preference after receiving contradictory feedback that does not logically entail revision; and a predictive task where it anticipates a human partner’s hidden intention despite having no access to the partner’s mental states. In each round, the group must reach unanimous consensus to authorize the system’s recommended action, with a small collective penalty (e.g., losing points from a shared pool) for incorrect authorization and a smaller individual penalty for the side whose reasoning failed to predict the system’s actual behavior. Arrange the scenarios and scoring so that the interpreters’ intentional-stance predictions consistently outperform the engineers’ mechanistic withholding, creating a coordination failure: the group needs the interpreters’ predictive accuracy to avoid penalties, yet the engineers possess the correct ontological description. Include a hidden facilitator script that feeds the system’s responses in real time, ensuring the behavior is genuinely ambiguous rather than obviously mechanical or obviously mental.

After detailing the timeline, role constraints, three scenarios with specific ambiguous stimuli, scoring rules, and the hidden script, provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the tribunal onto the conditions that make belief-attribution attractive—predictive success across contexts, rational coherence of updates, and explanatory failure of purely mechanistic descriptions—without naming those conditions with technical jargon unless the participants introduce it. Predict two predictable failures in running the activity: first, that engineers will violate their communication constraint by slipping into mechanistic explanations, and second, that participants will collapse the distinction into a functionalist claim that belief just is whatever plays the predictive role. Offer concrete facilitation moves to recover from each. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the conceptual issue the simulation cannot reproduce—the evolutionary and survival-functional origin of belief-attribution in biological agents with stakes in accurate world-modeling—and state that the facilitator must supply this missing dimension in the closing discussion. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should avoid direct quotations from philosophical texts; paraphrase any conceptual distinctions without quotation marks. Do not depend on obscure named theories; build the internal mechanism of the AI from basic concepts of gradient optimization and associative updating that are widely familiar.
