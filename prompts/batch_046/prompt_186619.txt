You are a philosopher of collective decision-making and institutional ethics with deep familiarity with social choice theory and the ontology of group agents. Before answering, take a brief moment to brainstorm the main rival accounts of collective rationality—summative aggregation, group-agent realism, epistemic democracy, and pluralist disagreement—so that your reconstruction is precise rather than superficial.

Consider three institutional practices that handle multi-member judgment differently. First, many institutional review boards and regulatory ethics panels operate under a premise-based closure protocol: members render separate judgments on discrete questions—whether a study poses more than minimal risk, whether informed consent is adequate, and whether the benefit-risk balance is favorable—after which an administrative rule mechanically derives the final approval decision. If a majority finds risk exceeds minimal and consent is inadequate, approval is automatically denied, regardless of how members vote on the bottom-line approval question itself. Second, most corporate boards and legislative committees use a bottom-line-only protocol: members vote solely on the final recommendation or bill, intermediate disagreements about specific factual or normative premises are not recorded, and no consistency filter binds the group’s reasoning path to its outcome. Third, a smaller but growing number of deliberative bodies—particularly in transnational bioethics and environmental governance—have adopted distance-minimizing aggregation protocols: members submit complete judgment sets over all premises and conclusions, and the collective output is the single consistent judgment set that minimizes aggregate disagreement distance (counting mismatches across premises and conclusions) relative to the members’ individual sets, rather than applying premise-by-premise majority rule.

What philosophical commitments about collective rationality, epistemic authority, and moral agency does the premise-based closure practice presuppose? Does it treat the board as a single reasoning agent that must avoid contradiction, thereby importing an individualist logic of belief into group procedure? Or is it merely a coordination device for independent voters? Can a view that denies group agency—say, a purely summative or preference-aggregative theory—explain why the board should be bound by logical closure rather than by direct vote on the outcome? Next, compare this with the bottom-line-only corporate practice: what theory of institutional legitimacy does that presuppose? Does it implicitly hold that democratic or fiduciary authority resides in expressed will rather than in the coherence of the reasoning path? Is it compatible with the idea that groups have epistemic duties of consistency?

Then turn to the distance-minimizing protocol. What view of disagreement does it presuppose? Does treating divergence as distance to be minimized presuppose that disagreement is epistemic noise rather than valuable moral pluralism? Or can it be read as respecting pluralism by selecting the collective view that does least violence to every member’s position? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and rationality here, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—i.e., compatible with several rival theories that would license the same procedural design.

Finally, ask: if we replaced the implicit theory you reconstructed with its rival—say, replacing group-agent rationalism with pure preference aggregation, or replacing distance-minimization with a majoritarian premise rule—what specific institutional features would have to change? And is there an internal tension in using closure rules for ethics boards but bottom-line voting for legislative bodies that deal with equally complex normative judgments? Think carefully, and present your analysis in clearly separated steps.
