I'm a high school junior tasked with delivering the opening remarks at our school's annual symposium, "Institutions and Identity." The audience will be split: roughly half are visiting scholars in philosophy and sociology who have worked on existentialism, and half are parents, teachers, and administrators with no background in it. I want a four-minute address that uses Sartre's analysis of bad faith and radical freedom as its structural backbone, not as ornament. The core move should be this: when someone says "I'm just a student, I'm only following the rules," or "this is just how school works," they are performing the same gesture Sartre diagnosed in the café waiter—treating a contingent institutional role as though it had the fixed, self-identical being of an object that is what it is. At the same time, the remarks must engage the serious objection that Sartre's thesis of unavoidable freedom appears to deny real power imbalances, without either dismissing the objection or turning the speech into an uncritical defense. The argument should turn on the distinction between freedom and facticity: recognizing that we remain free does not erase institutional constraints, but it does block using those constraints as an alibi. Please write remarks that keep this philosophical distinction active for both halves of the room—rigorous enough that scholars do not wince at simplification, but accessible enough that parents and teachers are not lost to jargon. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or suggested readings.
