You are grading a graduate-level examination in moral philosophy. The exam presented the following question:

Explain Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, with particular attention to his notion of ground projects and to the scenario—commonly discussed in the literature—where a husband rescues his wife from drowning yet offers a utilitarian justification, an error often characterized as involving one thought too many. Then apply these concepts to this case: Dr. Elena Voss is a physician-engineer who has devoted her career to hand-crafting prosthetic limbs for children in conflict zones. She is offered a senior position at a defense-technology firm designing autonomous triage algorithms for military medical allocation. Once deployed, these algorithms would systematically deprioritize complex pediatric rehabilitation—including the population Voss serves—by optimizing solely for immediate survival metrics. Voss declines the offer, not because she judges the net utility to be negative, but because she regards participation in the automation of such judgments as incompatible with the caring practice that structures her life. The exam also required students to address a rule-utilitarian reply: that Voss ought to accept the position in order to influence the algorithm's weightings from within, thereby producing better long-term outcomes. Finally, students were asked whether Voss's refusal expresses an agent-relative reason that is distinct from integrity-preservation.

Below is one student's response. The answer correctly identifies that Williams is concerned with the alienation of agents from commitments that constitute their identities, and it rightly notes that Voss's refusal is anchored in the structure of her life-projects rather than in a preference or emotional whim. Nevertheless, the response contains subtle philosophical errors. The student mischaracterizes the one-thought-too-many problem as a defect of emotional distance or intimacy—suggesting that the husband should act purely from love without any supplementary reasoning—rather than recognizing it as a corruption of the agent's justificatory structure, in which a ground-project-based reason is illegitimately replaced by an impersonal calculation. The student also mishandles the rule-utilitarian counter-argument, treating Williams's rejection of it as an empirical claim about institutional capture and the improbability of influencing organizational outcomes, rather than as a claim about the moral psychology of complicity and the damage done to one's own ground projects by entering into the wrong kind of practice. There is a further conflation near the end of the answer, where the student describes Voss's reason as an agent-relative obligation to specific children she knows personally, thereby running together integrity-based refusal with direct special obligations.

Please provide:
1. A letter grade with a brief, precise justification of the overall evaluation.
2. Point-by-point comments that specify the answer's strengths and its specific mistakes, explaining for each mistake why it misrepresents Williams's position or distorts the logical architecture of the case.
3. A brief assessment of whether the student successfully distinguishes agent-relative reasons from integrity-preservation in analyzing Voss's refusal.

Do not include a bibliography, suggested readings, citation list, or references to specific page numbers. Evaluate the reasoning on its philosophical merits alone.

Student answer:
Williams's integrity objection says that utilitarianism can demand that people abandon the ground projects that give their lives meaning. In the George-the-chemist example, George should refuse the chemical-weapons job because it violates his personal integrity and conflicts with who he is as a person. The one-thought-too-many case shows that if a husband saves his wife but thinks to himself that the overall utility is maximized, he has an inappropriate emotional distance—he should save her out of pure love without any extra calculation. Turning to Voss, she refuses the algorithm job because automating triage conflicts with her personal authenticity and her emotional attachment to the children she serves; this is clearly about preserving her integrity. The rule-utilitarian says she should take the job to improve the algorithm from inside, but Williams would reject this for empirical reasons: in a large defense firm, one engineer is unlikely to overcome bureaucratic and profit-driven incentives, so the welfare gain is too speculative. So Voss is right to refuse, and she does so on agent-relative grounds—she has direct special duties to the particular children in her workshop, which gives her a reason that does not depend on her ground project.
