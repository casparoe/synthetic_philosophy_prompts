I am a junior taking an advanced literature and philosophy seminar, and over the past week we have been working through Dostoevsky's parable of the Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov. My instructor emphasized that the Inquisitor's speech is a dramatic argument made by Ivan Karamazov rather than Dostoevsky's own creed, but after rereading the passage I am struggling to see why we should treat it as anything other than a successful proof. The Inquisitor seems to show, through the three temptations of bread, miracle, and authority, that human beings are structurally incapable of bearing freedom without descending into misery; if freedom necessarily produces suffering and the surrender of freedom produces happiness and security, then freedom appears to have negative instrumental value for well-being. From this I infer that Dostoevsky intends the parable to establish a strict logical incompatibility between authentic autonomy and collective happiness—what I have started calling the felicity-freedom antinomy in my notes—rather than merely presenting one character's despair.

This leads me to a question that my classmates and I debated without resolution. If the Inquisitor's reasoning is sound—if people consistently choose comfort over liberty when given the choice, and if the psychological burden of decision-making, similar to what modern behavioral scientists call decision fatigue, makes sustained autonomy exhausting—then why do contemporary liberal philosophers treat negative liberty as an intrinsic good rather than as a harmful cognitive load that rational agents should want to outsource? I am particularly confused because the Inquisitor's argument looks like a direct refutation of the idea that freedom is valuable independent of its outcomes; it suggests that valuing freedom for its own sake is like valuing exhaustion for its own sake. Could it be that the modern commitment to democratic autonomy rests on a false presupposition—namely, that people actually want to be free—when Dostoevsky's parable demonstrates that they do not? Or is there a hidden distinction between the kind of freedom the Inquisitor describes, the freedom to suffer, and the kind liberalism protects, and if so, how do we know which one is real?
