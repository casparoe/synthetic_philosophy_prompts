ok so i've been thinking about this weird split in history grad programs and i can't figure out what it assumes philosophically. on the one hand they drill anti-presentism into you -- like if you're writing about medieval punishment you have to understand it on it's own terms, you cant call it barbaric just because it violates modern norms. but then the same programs have these public impact dissertation requirements where you get graded on whether your research offers actionable insights for current policy, and they treat the inability to draw modern moral lessons as a kind of failure. plus some departments now run anachronism audits on syllabi -- outside reviewers flag language that sounds too modern -- but those same reviewers check whether the course advances equity goals.

what theory of historical knowledge does that institutional setup actually presuppose? does it require a clean split between descriptive understanding (value-neutral) and prescriptive application (value-laden), with historians switching hats? or does it secretly assume all description is already value-laden, making the anti-presentism rule just rhetorical? and if they really think historians can never escape their own time, why frame presentism as an error instead of an inevitability?

also, is the practice philosophically consistent, or does it just work because different parts of the institution presuppose different things about whether moral standards are universal or historically contingent?
