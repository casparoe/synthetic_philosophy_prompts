Write a dialogue between two interlocutors—an advocate for "existential fidelity" and a defender of "archival fidelity"—debating what obligations survive to real, deceased people when their lives become historical fiction or biopics. The existentialist should argue that honoring the dead means preserving the internal logic and emotional architecture of a life rather than every chronological fact, and that inventing a private scene can be permissible if it captures how that person actually experienced their world. The archivalist should counter that any invented private moment—especially one assigning motives or feelings unsupported by the record—constitutes posthumous epistemic appropriation, since the dead cannot consent to having their interior lives reconstructed for narrative economy. Have them respond directly: the existentialist should address the consent objection by asking whether dignity requires factual fixity or coherence of self-understanding; the archivalist should reply by distinguishing public actions (open to interpretation) from private consciousness (not available for artistic licensing). The dialogue must avoid strawmen, maintain equal competence, and center on whether altering a public record for dramatic clarity differs morally from inventing an unrecorded interior moment.
