I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I need an outside eye on an argument he constructed that feels like it eats its own tail. He begins with the standard occasionalist thesis that fire does not burn by any intrinsic nature—God creates the burning on each occasion—so there is no necessary connection in created things between cause and effect. He then adds a premise I rarely see emphasized: Al-Ghazālī still requires a stable "customary course" (al-ʿāda) both to distinguish miracles from ordinary events and to underwrite the predictive reliability of medicine and legal judgment. From this, my friend derives: (1) If occasionalism holds universally, no secondary cause possesses any modal power; the conjunction of fire and burning is only a repeated divine habit with no intrinsic likelihood. (2) Without such modal weight, the line between miracle and ordinary event collapses into mere statistical frequency, which Al-Ghazālī explicitly denies. (3) By the same token, if no action necessarily produces its outcome, then Islamic legal reasoning (aḥkām) that assigns liability—say, for death caused by poison—loses its ontological footing and reduces to arbitrary decree. (4) Therefore occasionalism must either allow some necessary causal powers in human "acquisition" (kasb) of acts, contradicting its universal denial of secondary causation, or it undermines the very religious, legal, and scientific practices Al-Ghazālī meant to preserve. (5) Hence occasionalism proves too much: it is self-refuting. I think the slip is somewhere between (2) and (3)—perhaps conflating metaphysical necessity with the counterfactual stability needed for legal imputation, or assuming that kasb must be causal in the same sense fire is denied—but I cannot name the error precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for occasionalism's coherence? Respond in one paragraph, no more than 250 words; do not provide citations or a reading list.
