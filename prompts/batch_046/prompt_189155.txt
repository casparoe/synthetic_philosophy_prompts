I am writing a response paper for my graduate seminar in practical reason and the philosophy of commitment, and I want to defend a somewhat unconventional view about the normative force of promises. Please argue that promises bind not primarily because they generate legitimate expectations in the promisee, as on standard expectation-based accounts, nor because they constitute an expression of autonomous self-legislation, as on pure autonomy-based views. Instead, argue that the speech-act of promising performs a distinctive structural operation on the promisor’s own practical reasoning: it installs a temporary, self-directed exclusionary reason—what I will call a “deliberative lock”—that removes competing considerations from admissible deliberation within a specifically indexed domain of choice. On this account, breaking a promise is not merely a wrong against the promisee or a lapse in personal integrity, but a form of practical irrationality in which the agent violates her own temporarily fixed reasoning architecture. The lock is normative rather than causal: unlike a Ulysses contract, it does not make alternative action impossible, but renders any conclusion of practical reasoning within the locked domain that departs from the promised course defective. In developing the argument, please distinguish this “deliberative lock” from ordinary resolutions and personal rules, addressing the objection that promises therefore collapse into mere self-commitments with no distinctive interpersonal obligation. Also address the concern that this framework cannot explain why promises generate directed duties to particular others rather than only internal consistency conditions. Finally, clarify the release conditions for the lock—fulfillment, release by the promisee, or mutual dissolution—and explain why these conditions are structurally integral to the lock rather than external afterthoughts.
