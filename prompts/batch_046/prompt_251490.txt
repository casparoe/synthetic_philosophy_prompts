In several European legal systems—notably France, Germany, and Italy—commercial surrogacy is criminalised or void ab initio, grounded in constitutional or civil-law principles of human dignity and the non-commodification of the body, yet these same jurisdictions permit egg donation under tightly regulated conditions of anonymity, altruistic motivation, and state-capped compensation. By contrast, in the United States, both practices are governed primarily by contract, but with a striking asymmetry: surrogacy agreements are enforced as gestational-service contracts with fees often tied to live birth and behavioural compliance during pregnancy, whereas egg-donation contracts treat retrieved oocytes as alienable property subject to extensive genetic, educational, and phenotypic screening, with compensation scaling sharply according to donor traits. Consider this institutional divergence not as a policy debate but as a philosophical artefact.

What view of the human body and its products does this pattern presuppose? Does the European prohibition of surrogacy while permitting egg donation depend on a distinction between genetic contribution and gestational labour as categorically different in their relation to personhood—perhaps that gestation establishes an irreducible relational bond that makes the child non-alienable, whereas gametes are mere biological material? Or can it be explained instead by concerns about exploitation, gendered labour, or tractability that do not commit one to any particular ontology of reproduction?

Meanwhile, what philosophical commitments about alienability and contractual personhood underpin the American framework? The market treats gestation as a time-bound service that terminates upon delivery, yet subjects egg donors to long-term warranties and behavioural controls that seem to treat the donor’s body and future self as partly retained assets. Does this presuppose that reproductive tissues are more fully commodifiable than reproductive labour, or rather that labour is easier to contractually contain?

Reconstruct the implicit theories of bodily integrity, relational identity, and commodification that each jurisdictional model requires. Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely accommodate, identify where the institutional design is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival accounts—and say what would have to change in either legal framework if its underlying view of personhood or property in the body were replaced by its rival.
