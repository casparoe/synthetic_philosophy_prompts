I serve on the advisory board of the National Predictive Ecology Fund, and my regular work is writing about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. By Thursday we must circulate a decision memo on whether to renew the Helix Consortium's five-year grant for its decadal biome-shift projection model. The model treats fungal mycelial networks as transmitting climate signals across ecosystems with perfect fidelity and instantaneous latency—an idealization that every field biologist rejects—yet its range-shift predictions have remained within four percent of observed outcomes across two full cycles. One board faction treats this as a direct application of Friedman's F-twist: if the predictive machinery delivers, the biological absurdity of the premises is methodologically irrelevant. The opposing faction argues that predictive ecology is not macroeconomics, that institutional credibility depends on mechanistic plausibility, and that deploying this model for conservation policy would misdirect resources regardless of its past accuracy. Please draft the memo I will circulate: present it as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introductory or concluding paragraph. The memo must recommend a concrete course of action—renewal, termination, or conditional renewal with an explicit time frame and benchmark—treat the strongest consideration on the opposing side with genuine intellectual fairness, and refrain from asking anyone to supply citations, bibliographies, or reading lists they do not have at hand.
