In a seminar I attend, two participants recently became locked in a disagreement about whether epiphenomenalism genuinely undermines our knowledge of phenomenal consciousness. One argued that if phenomenal properties lack all causal efficacy, then they can never figure in the causal chains that produce our judgments about them; she described this as a "calibration gap," in which beliefs about qualia are not anchored to the properties themselves but instead float freely over their physical correlates. She then pressed what she called "referential displacement": if every judgment we classify as phenomenal is in fact caused solely by neural states, then the content of those judgments is fixed by neural states, not by phenomenal properties, which means epiphenomenalism collapses into a self-effacing physicalism about mental content. The other participant replied that this reasoning smuggles in an unjustifiably demanding causal theory of reference for mental states; he granted that direct causal interaction is absent, but insisted that indirect inference from robust physical-to-phenomenal correlations could still yield knowledge, analogous to knowing about distant planets via light rather than direct contact. He added that if her argument succeeded, it would threaten not only epiphenomenalism but any view that separates phenomenal properties from the physical causal order, including certain non-physicalist dualisms. She conceded that indirect knowledge was logically possible, yet countered that inference requires at least one causal bridge linking the target property to the belief-forming mechanism, and under epiphenomenalism there is none—not even an indirect one—because phenomenal properties are causally isolated at every level. He then proposed that the epiphenomenalist could adopt a "proxy semantics" for phenomenal judgments: our terms refer to phenomenal properties not through direct causal contact, but by being anchored to physical proxies that stand in a systematic, law-like (though non-causal) relation to them. She responded that this sounds like preserving the letter of epiphenomenalism only by abandoning its spirit, since it renders phenomenal properties epistemically redundant and leaves us judging only about their physical stand-ins.

Could you adjudicate this exchange point by point? Specifically: is the "calibration gap" a genuine problem for epiphenomenalism, or does it depend on an implausible causal theory of knowledge? Does "referential displacement" actually establish that phenomenal judgments must be about neural states, or can the proxy-semantics reply preserve genuine reference to phenomenal properties? And if the proxy reply succeeds, does it come at the cost of making phenomenal properties explanatorily and epistemically redundant—thereby conceding too much to the physicalist? In your verdict, identify precisely where each party is right, where each overreaches, and whether a coherent middle position exists that preserves both epiphenomenalism and our ordinary claims to know what it is like to undergo an experience.
