I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I'm working on a feature about whether the concept of "qualia" — the private, intrinsic feel of conscious experience — is philosophically coherent. I want to compare two critiques that both try to undermine the idea, but through structurally different routes.

The first is the argument associated with Chase and Sanborn: they treat "qualia" as an incoherent posit because it is supposed to be simultaneously causally inert, immediately available to introspection, and genuinely explanatory of behavior or belief, a combination they take to be jointly unsatisfiable.

The second is a challenge I will call the "indexical dissolution" problem — a less-discussed line of thought holding that any attempt to conceptually isolate or name a phenomenal quality necessarily transforms it into a relational, context-dependent property, analogous to how an indexical like "now" picks out a time only relative to an utterance. On this view, the moment you successfully describe a quale, you have turned it into something publicly structured and therefore dissolved the very intrinsic privacy that was meant to define it.

Please compare these two critiques carefully. Are they genuinely independent failures, or are they two descriptions of the same underlying mistake? Specifically, does the indexical dissolution avoid the charge of logical contradiction by reconceiving the error as semantic or pragmatic? Or does it ultimately reproduce the same tension — asking a property to be both introspectively available and completely isolated from relational structure? In answering, please separate what is settled about the logical and conceptual structure of these arguments from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and do not present either critique as the consensus position. Write in American English, and do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need the analysis to stand on its own reasoning.
