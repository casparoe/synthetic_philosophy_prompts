I've been trying to wrap my head around Bernard Williams's idea about moral revolutions—basically that huge shifts like the end of slavery or dueling don't happen because philosophers convince everyone with arguments about rights or suffering, but because interests, power, or honor dynamics change first. Then I read Kwame Anthony Appiah on dueling and foot-binding, where he says they collapsed once the honor calculus flipped: suddenly it was shameful rather than noble to do them. So I'm wondering if I have this right: if rational argument is causaly inert in big moral shifts, then why do ethicists still write papers trying to persuade people?

More concretly, my dept is debating whether grad students should do unpaid peer review for for-profit journals—some frame it as exploitation, others say it's part of professional honor. Given Williams and Appiah, shouldn't we drop the justice-talk entirely and just restructure the honor system so refusing unpaid labor becomes the prestigious move? Or is there a hidden role for argument that I'm missing here?
