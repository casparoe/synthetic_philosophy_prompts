In Dennett's Where Am I?, he imagines his brain removed and kept alive in a vat in Houston while his body, remotely linked by radio, operates in Tulsa, and he asks from which location he should truly say he is present. The standard interpretation takes him to be arguing that personal identity tracks whatever carries the brain's functional organization, not the body in which that brain originally sat. I want to extend this line of thought with an engineered modification that seems to reveal a hidden commitment. Suppose, rather than removing the brain, we gradually replace each neural pathway with a wireless link to an external processor that replicates the dynamics exactly, but with a fixed twenty-four-hour latency in the loop. Once substitution is complete, no biological tissue remains in the skull, yet there is a continuous functional chain stretching from the original brain to the server, interrupted only by that temporal gap. During the transition, it appears that Dennett's principle would place me partly in the skull and partly in the remote processor, generating two conscious streams—one delayed by a full day—both legitimately claiming to be me. Because this duplication-with-delay strikes me as incoherent, I have concluded that Dennett must actually be committed to the spatiotemporal continuity of the original brain tissue itself; functional preservation alone cannot suffice, and the vat scenario works only because the brain remains physically intact and contiguous throughout. Is that the right way to resolve the tension, or does the contradiction instead indicate that the question of where I am has no determinate answer even in this gradually replaced case?
