Exam question: Distinguish the etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, being precise about the respective roles of evolutionary history and current system contribution. Apply both accounts to two cases: (i) an engineered ventricular assist device that pumps blood but was never part of an evolutionary lineage; (ii) a de novo cis-regulatory element—arising from a retrotransposed pseudogene fragment—that currently modulates stress-response gene expression but exhibits no phylogenetic conservation pattern indicative of past selection for that regulatory effect. For each case, state whether and why the account assigns a proper function. Finally, assess whether the causal-role account's willingness to treat case (ii) as genuinely functional undermines the etiological theorist's claim to preserve a unique normative distinction between malfunction and mere causal contribution, or whether the etiological denial of function in (ii) instead imposes an unacceptable theoretical cost.

Student answer: The etiological account, as developed by Neander and Wright, defines proper function as the effect for which a trait was actually selected in evolutionary history. The causal-role account, defended by Cummins, defines function as the current causal contribution of a trait to some capacity of the containing system, with no historical condition.

In case (i), the device was designed with the purpose of pumping blood. Because design involves differential retention of variants based on their effects, it is structurally analogous to natural selection; hence the etiological account can assign the device the proper function of circulation. The causal-role account also assigns this function, since the device currently contributes to blood flow. Both accounts therefore converge, though on different grounds.

In case (ii), the element currently regulates stress-response expression, so the causal-role account assigns it that function directly. The etiological account denies it a proper function, since there is no selection history for this effect. Far from being a cost, this is a theoretical virtue: only selection history establishes a normative standard of correct performance, so without it there is causal contribution but no possibility of malfunction. The worry that this makes the etiological view unable to accommodate evolutionary novelty is mistaken. The etiological theorist can broaden the selection criterion to include developmental stabilization and ontogenetic canalization—processes that entrench a trait's contribution during development—thereby allowing novel interactions to acquire proper functions even in the absence of deep phylogenetic conservation. Consequently, the causal-role account gains no clear advantage in handling novelty, and the etiological account retains exclusive ownership of the normative malfunction/distinction without being unduly restrictive.
