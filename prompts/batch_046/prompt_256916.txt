I'm preparing remarks for a philosophy of science workshop on theory change and have been trying to articulate exactly where I stand on the analytic-synthetic boundary after working through Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" alongside Carnap's later conventionalist responses. I find myself drawn to four commitments that feel individually defensible but that may not hang together. First, I accept Quine's argument that there is no sharp, principled distinction between analytic and synthetic truths at the level of individual statements; the boundary is at best a gradient of centrality within the web of belief. Second, despite this, I think the distinction plays an indispensable functional role in scientific practice: we genuinely need to differentiate framework principles—logic, mathematics, and coordinating definitions—from empirical hypotheses in order to make sense of how theories are revised, compared, and translated. Third, I believe Carnap's mature conventionalist account of analyticity, which grounds it in linguistic framework rules rather than in any verificationist reduction of meaning to experience, largely escapes the force of Quine's critique, since Quine's attack in "Two Dogmas" is directed mainly at a verificationist theory of meaning that Carnap had already abandoned. Fourth, I want to maintain that mathematical truths are analytic in this conventionalist sense, yet I also accept Quine's holist claim that even mathematical statements are in principle empirically revisable, provided we are willing to make sufficient adjustments elsewhere in the web.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict upon closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? In particular, I am unsure whether my acceptance of Quine's demolition of the sharp distinction is compatible with my defense of Carnap's conventionalism and with my insistence that the analytic-synthetic split remains functionally indispensable. I also worry that holding mathematics to be both conventionally analytic and empirically revisable may collapse one side into the other rather than yielding a stable hybrid. Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each pair what precisely would have to be abandoned or qualified, and say whether there is a coherent position that preserves all four at the price of nuanced reformulation rather than outright rejection.
