Following a recent family crisis in which my sister and I had to weigh my father's suffering against the emotional and financial costs of prolonging his care, I have found myself unable to stop thinking about whether moral reasoning should ever aggregate one person's pain against another's relief. I am now preparing to teach an upper-level philosophy of ethics seminar, and I want to write a final exam question that gets at the heart of this worry—specifically, the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism—without allowing students to pass by summarizing the standard literature. To force genuine conceptual engagement, I want the question to inject a non-standard case that complicates the metaphysical assumptions behind the objection.

Please draft one essay question for a 90-minute exam aimed at advanced undergraduates or MA students. The question should focus on Rawls's separateness of persons objection, but it must not be answerable by merely reciting the standard critique or listing familiar counter-replies. Instead, embed in the prompt itself a specific, conceptually demanding scenario—such as conjoined twins who share a single circulatory system and partial neural pathways but maintain distinct conscious experiences, preferences, and self-awareness—so that students must determine whether the objection survives when the boundaries between persons are ambiguous. Require students to do three things: (i) reconstruct the core normative claim of the objection and explain why Rawls holds that utilitarian aggregation fails to respect the distinction between persons; (ii) assess whether the objection requires a metaphysically sharp boundary between individuals, or whether it can be sustained as a normative principle about distinct centers of moral concern even under conditions of partial psychological and physiological integration; and (iii) apply this analysis to the twin case, defending either that aggregation is permissible here despite the objection or that the objection blocks it, and addressing the strongest opposing argument.

Please provide:
- The exam question itself, clearly worded for students.
- A model answer of approximately 600–800 words.
- A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each point.

If this request rests on a confused premise—if, for example, the conjoined-twin scenario undermines the coherence of the separateness objection in a way that makes the exam unanswerable, or if my framing mistakenly treats a metaphysical ambiguity as a moral ambiguity—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I genuinely want to know if the task as posed cannot be done well.
