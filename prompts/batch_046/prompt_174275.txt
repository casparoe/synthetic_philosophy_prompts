Hey, so I'm a retired ecologist who got bored and started reading philosophy of history, and I'm stuck on this concept of societal "collapse". Historians apply it to the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island, but conceptually I'm not convinced its one thing. In ecology, a regime shift can be catastrophic or just adaptive—think of a forest becoming savanna. So is there a principled philosophical distinction between a society that actually fails, like stops reproducing its basic institutions, versus one that just rapidly downscales? The Maya kept writing and building in some regions, and Easter Islanders apparently maintained cultural practices with far less resources, yet both are called collapsed. Meanwhile the Bronze Age looks more like a network failure. So when we say collapse, are we tracking an objective loss of social complexity, or are we smuggling in a normative preference for large-scale organization? I cant tell if complexity is even measurable without begging the question. Can you help me think through whether collapse is a coherent analytical category, or just a narrative frame we impose?
