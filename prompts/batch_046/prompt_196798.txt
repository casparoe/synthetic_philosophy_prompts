I am a doctoral student working on classical Indian philosophy, and I have been tracking a stubborn stall in the interpretation of Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, specifically around the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) applied to the Tathāgata in the examination of the Buddha's post-parinirvāṇa status. On one side are readers who take the four-cornered negation—neither existent, nor non-existent, nor both, nor neither—as an exhaustive ontological argument: since every possible mode of intrinsic being (svabhāva) for the Tathāgata is contradicted, emptiness (śūnyatā) is established as a universal metaphysical thesis that applies to all entities in the same register. On the other side are interpreters who treat the identical verses as an ad hominem dialectical maneuver: Nāgārjuna negates these four positions only because essentialist opponents happen to hold them regarding the Buddha, and the success of the argument consists not in establishing a general ontology but in dissolving a specific soteriological confusion and halting conceptual proliferation (prapañca) for that interlocutor.

The stall is peculiar because both camps now concede the philological points—the Sanskrit syntax, the parallel structures in texts attributed to Nāgārjuna such as the Vigrahavyāvartanī, and the commentary traditions. They agree that all four positions are rejected. What does not move is the question of what philosophical work the rejection performs. The ontological camp insists that if the negation were merely tailored to an opponent, there would be no justification for applying the same fourfold schema systematically to dharmas in general; the dialectical camp responds that the schema's recurrence demonstrates only the pervasiveness of essentialist attachment, not that emptiness functions as a positive metaphysical claim about reality.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden factual disagreement but a divergence in what counts as a successful philosophical explanation—specifically, whether a Madhyamaka argument must yield a generalizable claim about the nature of entities or whether it succeeds precisely when it renders further conceptual construction impossible for a given audience. Yet I am uncertain whether this diagnosis captures the structure of the impasse or merely restates it in different vocabulary.

Could you diagnose this disagreement? Is it verbal, turning on different uses of "emptiness," "negation," or "intrinsic nature"? Is it substantive but obscured by textual agreement—perhaps about whether Madhyamaka aims at metaphysical truth or at therapeutic cessation? Are the two sides answering different questions—one about ontology, one about the pragmatics of liberation—under the single heading "What does the tetralemma accomplish"? Or is the question itself ill-posed, such that asking for "the" interpretation presupposes a unitary philosophical project that the text deliberately refuses?

Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would it require a formal distinction—say, a precise logical mapping of the four corners that demonstrates whether they are genuinely exhaustive and therefore force the ontological reading? A historical or conceptual clarification—distinguishing "ontological emptiness" from "dialectical emptiness" in terms both sides could accept without collapsing into one another? Or should we conclude that no further progress is expected, because the disagreement rests on bedrock commitments about whether philosophy should describe reality or dissolve attachment?
