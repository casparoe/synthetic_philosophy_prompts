I sit on the framing review committee of the Meridian Foundation for Public History, and we must reach a decision by next month's board meeting on a grant proposal for a permanent museum installation and documentary series titled "The Unwilled Revolution." The proposal contends that conventional historiography suffers from an anthropocentric selection bias: it treats the Yersinia pestis lineage behind the Black Death, the cooling trajectory of the Little Ice Age, and the 1815 Tambora volcanic forcing event as mere background conditions, when in fact these forces display what the authors term intervention-resistant causal dominance—their macro-effects persist across virtually all feasible human counter-interventions within the relevant temporal windows. From this, the authors derive a methodological rule: rank historical causes by counterfactual robustness, and assign narrative agency (without requiring intentionality) to any factor that exceeds a specified threshold. They allow for local human modulation inside these causal streams, but they assign primary historical authorship to the non-human forces themselves, insisting this is not environmental determinism because human choices are not erased, merely demoted in explanatory priority.

The philosophical tension is whether this framework commits a category error. Attributing agency without intentionality, and weighting causal importance by intervention resistance rather than by normative relevance, may conflate causal salience with moral accountability. Board critics worry that presenting the Black Death as an actor rather than a condition will lead audiences to treat social responses—quarantine design, labor restructuring, scapegoating dynamics—as epiphenomenal, undermining the museum's mandate to highlight human responsibility in crisis. Proponents reply that refusing narrative weight to these forces reproduces a distorting human-exceptionalism and that local modulation preserves sufficient space for human agency.

We need a concrete decision: approve the grant, reject it, or approve it subject to specific, enforceable conditions within a defined time frame (e.g., sixty days). I need the committee's written opinion circulated in advance: a clear recommendation with reasons, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, the argument that intervention-resistant weighting systematically obscures the normative dimensions of human crisis response. The memo should not request bibliographies, further reading, or citation lists; it should assume the board understands basic philosophy of causation and science but does not specialize in historiography.

Please draft this memo. Take a committed position—do not survey options and leave the choice open. Choose one course, defend it with explicit reasoning, and say directly why the best opposing argument does not overturn it.
