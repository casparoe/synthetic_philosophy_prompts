Consider the published case judgments of the moral philosopher M. R. Voss on intergenerational harm and the nonidentity problem. In Case I, Voss held that a couple who delays childbearing for career advancement, thereby conceiving Child A rather than a never-existing Child B, does not wrong Child A, provided Child A’s life is clearly worth living; she added, however, that if the career choice imposes severe ecological costs on present others, a separate and independent wrong occurs. In Case II, she judged that a state policy accelerating industrialization—which alters the identities of future citizens and leaves some with lives barely worth living—is impermissible, but not on the ground that it harms those specific future individuals; instead, she appealed to a breach of intergenerational continuity of care owed to the future as an abstract collective. In Case III, she ruled that parents who conceive during a temporary, avoidable illness—thereby having Child C with a disability rather than waiting for a different Child D—do not wrong Child C if they provide adequate care and Child C’s life is worth living, yet they specifically wrong Child C if they withhold necessary resources, and they wrong society if the disability was easily preventable. In Case IV, she concluded that present climate inaction, which shifts the identities of people alive in 2100, does not wrong those particular 2100 individuals, but violates a stewardship covenant with present and near-future generations.

Now consider Case V. A nation-state proposes a five-year delay in conception subsidies, coupled with a mandatory genetic-identity preservation program: for participating families, the exact genetic profile that would have been realized in the earlier window is stored and later implanted, so that the same genetic individuals come to exist, only into a world that has endured five additional years of climate degradation. The conditions into which these genetically preserved individuals are born are therefore measurably worse—higher temperatures, reduced agricultural stability, and greater social disruption—though their lives remain well above any threshold of being worth living. The state asks whether the delay is permissible, arguing that because the program prevents the replacement of one population with another—since genetic identity is maintained—no nonidentity-based objection applies, even though the environment has deteriorated.

If Voss wishes to remain consistent with her judgments in Cases I through IV, what should she conclude about Case V? Identify the closest analog among the four prior cases, articulate at least two distinct rules that jointly accommodate her earlier verdicts but diverge on Case V, and state what further principle she would be adopting with each possible ruling. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify the precise further hypothetical case that would force a decision between the competing rules.
