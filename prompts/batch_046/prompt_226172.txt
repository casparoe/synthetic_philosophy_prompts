I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of science, and our instructor summarized Thomas Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts for us. The summary went something like this: Kuhn holds that when a scientific revolution occurs—say, the shift from Newtonian mechanics to Einsteinian relativity—the two paradigms are incommensurable, which means they do not share any common standards of evaluation, not even observational ones. Because the very meaning of terms like mass and time changes across the transition, scientists in different paradigms literally inhabit different conceptual worlds, and there is no neutral language in which to state the evidence that could rationally decide between them. Consequently, paradigm choice is ultimately driven by sociological factors, aesthetic preferences, and the persuasive power of the scientific community rather than by logic or empirical testing. From this, our instructor concluded that Kuhn is a thoroughgoing relativist about scientific truth: he denies that later paradigms are closer to reality than earlier ones, and he treats scientific progress as nothing more than a change in collective belief, with no cognitive improvement whatsoever. The instructor also mentioned that Kuhn's view implies that we can never genuinely understand past scientific theories—like the caloric theory of heat—because their conceptual vocabulary is permanently closed off to us.

I have the sense that this is too extreme, but I'm not sure exactly where the distortion lies. Which parts of this characterization capture Kuhn's actual position in his well-known writings, and which parts are exaggerations or misreadings? In particular, I'm wondering whether Kuhn really denies all rational comparison between paradigms, whether his language about different worlds should be read as a metaphysical claim about ontology or as a more modest claim about perceptual framing, and whether his view of scientific progress is genuinely anti-realist or whether he allows for some form of evolutionary development without invoking a theory-independent truth. Does the conclusion that past theories are permanently unintelligible actually follow from incommensurability, or is that an overstatement of the translation difficulties he describes? I'd appreciate an accuracy audit that distinguishes the defensible kernel from the caricature, without assuming I can look up the primary texts myself.
