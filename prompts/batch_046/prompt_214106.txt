Question 4:
In On Certainty, Wittgenstein describes certain propositions — for instance, that the earth has existed for many years, or that a person has two hands — as functioning like hinges on which other beliefs turn. Explain (i) why Wittgenstein maintains that these hinge propositions are not items of knowledge in the standard sense, despite being beyond reasonable doubt, and (ii) how the hinge metaphor illuminates the non-foundationalist structure of empirical justification. In addressing (ii), consider Wittgenstein's response to G. E. Moore's claim to know that he has two hands, and distinguish carefully between the grammatical role of a hinge and its epistemic status.

Student's answer:
Wittgenstein's hinge propositions are background beliefs that stand fast for us and form the framework within which particular empirical judgements are made. Unlike ordinary knowledge claims, they are not justified by evidence, because they are the conditions that make justification possible in the first place. They are not items of knowledge in the standard sense because knowledge requires the possibility of doubt and error, whereas hinges lie outside the route of enquiry and are not subject to verification or falsification.

The hinge metaphor illustrates that justification is not linear or foundationalist. Just as a door needs hinges to open and close while the hinges themselves remain fixed, our system of beliefs needs certain propositions to stay in place so that particular beliefs can be tested and revised. Wittgenstein compares this structure to a river-bed: the bed is composed of hard rock that never shifts, while the water flows over it. Thus, hinge propositions form a permanently fixed foundation that never changes, providing an unshakeable base for all empirical inquiry.

In response to Moore, Wittgenstein accepts that Moore is correct to be certain he has two hands, but he reinterprets Moore's proof as demonstrating that the proposition that one has two hands is a hinge proposition that justifies itself through its grammatical role in our language-games. Moore's error was treating a hinge as an ordinary empirical claim requiring philosophical defence, when in fact its certainty derives from its being embedded in the grammar of our language. The grammatical certainty of the hinge therefore gives it an epistemic status equivalent to a self-evident foundation, even though it is not known in the ordinary way.
