I am currently in my final year of secondary school, and in our philosophy elective we have spent the past month working through Friedrich Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Our instructor emphasized the three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—and we spent considerable time debating whether contemporary digital archiving and algorithmic recommendation systems represent a kind of hypertrophied antiquarianism that no longer serves living culture, but instead buries the present under an endlessly indexed past. For my closing presentation, I would like to offer an original poem that takes up this tension rather than resolving it, since our class discussion ended precisely at that impasse.

Specifically, could you compose a sestina of approximately thirty-nine lines using the six end-words monumental, antiquarian, critical, life, forget, and archive? I want the form's recursive rotation—where each stanza cycles the end-words and the final envoi gathers all six together—to mirror Nietzsche's claim that the three historical attitudes, when pursued without limit, inevitably turn back upon one another and threaten to suffocate the present. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the essay recognizes the specific pathologies Nietzsche assigns to each mode: the deceptive analogy and false continuity of monumental history, the mummifying reverence and loss of perspective in antiquarian history, and the necessary but dangerous dissolution performed by critical history. At the same time, it should not read like a lecture or sermon, but should leave open whether modern algorithmic preservation serves life or constitutes a new form of burial. It needs to be appropriate for oral delivery at our seminar's final session, and it should stand entirely on its own without any citation of secondary scholarship or direct quotation from other texts.
