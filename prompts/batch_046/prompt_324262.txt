Student answer:
A fink is an interfering factor that removes a disposition precisely when the conditions for its manifestation are satisfied, like Martin’s electro-fink device attached to a wire that makes it non-conductive the instant current is applied. A mask, by contrast, is an interfering factor that prevents manifestation without removing the underlying disposition, such as the packing material around a fragile vase that stops it breaking when struck. So the essential difference is that finks are active, externally attached mechanisms that cancel the property, whereas masks are passive, environmental barriers that merely block the triggering conditions from reaching the object.

In the wafer case, the polymer coating is best understood as a mask. Although it alters the lattice when the field is present, its function is to shield the wafer from the conditions that would allow current to flow—similar to how packaging shields glass from impact. Because the original conductive structure returns once the field is removed, the wafer never actually lost its disposition; it was only suppressed during the interval, just as the vase remains fragile while wrapped.

This example supports Martin’s broader point that dispositions are not unconditional intrinsic properties but are instead relational: an object has a disposition only relative to the absence of interfering finks or masks. Since an electro-fink could always be attached, there is no absolute fact of the matter about whether the wire is disposed to conduct; the same relational analysis applies to the wafer. Consequently, the standard conditional analysis—“x is disposed to M iff, were x in C, x would M”—can be repaired by adding a ceteris paribus clause that excludes finks and masks, preserving the analysis against Martin’s objections.
