In distributive ethics, the dispute over prioritarianism's response to the leveling-down objection has followed a remarkably stable script for decades. Critics describe a case in which harming the better-off—destroying resources they would have enjoyed—produces equality without benefiting anyone, and claim this reveals equality's intrinsic value. Defenders reply that prioritarianism weights benefits by absolute well-being, not comparative position, so the scenario provides no reason to level down; they then distinguish telic from deontic egalitarianism and invoke weighted social welfare functions. Yet neither camp has substantially revised its position, despite formal refinements in welfare economics and normative theory. My working theory is that the disagreement is not about the correct social welfare function or about interpersonal comparison, but about whether moral badness is essentially relational (a gap between people) or essentially non-relational (absolute deprivation that happens to be unevenly distributed). Is that diagnosis correct, or does a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the admissibility of cardinal comparisons, or about whether the objection illicitly treats distributions as agents—actually drive the stall? I want a structural diagnosis: is this verbal (disputing the meaning of 'equality'), substantive but resting on bedrock intuitions neither side can defend, or a case of two frameworks answering different questions—one about the value of states of affairs, one about the reasons agents have—under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely move the debate: an empirical study of distributive intuitions, a formal result linking all non-aggregative views to parallel objections, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the scenario, or is progress here impossible?
