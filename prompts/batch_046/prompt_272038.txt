I've been retired from the physics lab for about four years now and lately I've been trying to read philosophy instead of just journal articles, mostly about consciousness and the timing of experience. Last week I had lunch with my old grad-school friend Dr. Chen, who's still in neuroscience, and my neighbor who teaches cognitive psych.

We got onto the color phi illusion -- you show someone a red dot, then a green one a little to the right, and they percieve one dot sliding over and turning orange in the middle, even though there never actually was an orange dot or a smooth trajectory. My neighbor said there's this comparison people make about whether the brain revises experience like Orwell, where old records get rewritten but you might still sense a change happened, or like Stalin, where people get airbrushed out so it's as if they never existed at all. She argued color phi is basically Stalinesque: your visual system never builds separate conscious experiences of 'red' then 'green'; it just computes motion and color change as one continuous narrative, and the discrete flashes are erased before awareness ever forms. Dr. Chen pushed back and said it's Orwellian: both colors do get registered briefly in awareness, but then a later interpretive layer overwrites them in working memory, like altering old newspapers after the fact. If you introspect fast enough you can still catch the original frames before the revision lands. My neighbor replied that introspection can't save you here because the rewrite happens pre-consciously, so what Chen thinks he's catching is just an earlier draft that also gets overwritten -- there's no untouched original to retrieve. Chen then said that if the Stalinesque view were right, we shouldn't see any neural signature of the separate colors in consciousness-related cortex, but there is early activation for both, which only makes sense if they were registered and then revised, not never registered at all.

They turned to me but I'm totally out of my depth on this stuff. Could you adjudicate each move? And please, separate what's actually settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who study it -- don't present one side as the obvious consensus if it's not.
