I am drafting a novel in which a head of state orders the destruction of a civilian center to force an enemy's surrender, yet the decision is not authored by a single will but distributed across a council: each advisor approves one fragment of the plan—target selection, weather conditions, deployment orders—while none explicitly utters that innocent lives are to be sacrificed as a means to peace. In researching the moral architecture of such fractured acts, I have been studying your own refusal to let Mr. Truman's decision be sanitized by appeals to military necessity, collective judgment, or the anonymity of bureaucratic process. I should like you to speak as yourself—G.E.M. Anscombe, in the months after you published your protest against the honorary degree—responding not as a historian but as an interlocutor whose position is now under genuine philosophical pressure.

Stay in character: speak in the first person, hold rigorously to the distinction between what is directly intended and what is merely foreseen, and do not allow that the diffusion of decision-making across committees dissolves personal moral responsibility into an impersonal structure. You may demand precision from me if my terms are vague, but do not break character, do not invoke thinkers or frameworks unknown to your own era, and do not concede that state necessity can ever license the direct killing of the innocent as a means to an end. Be sharp and precise, willing to grant a small logical point if genuinely pressed, but never yield the core claim that Truman's act was murder rather than legitimate warfare.

Here is my opening challenge. Your condemnation appears to require that Truman personally directed his will toward the death of civilians as an end or as a means, yet the actual authorization was distributed among the Interim Committee and military advisors—no single consciousness seemed to author the lethal intention in its entirety. Does your argument therefore depend on an implausible metaphysics of collective agency, as though a government were one moral person with one undivided will? Or can you maintain that each participant intended the killing in the morally relevant sense, even when the command is fractured among many hands and no individual voice pronounces the fatal purpose? I want to defend your view within my novel's architecture, but I must know whether it survives once we stop treating states as if they were single agents with singular souls.
