I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently argued with a coworker—also an engineer—about whether the unwritten etiquette rules governing code-review tone in our open-source project can be treated as a Lewisian convention. (1) My coworker said they clearly qualify: reviewers consistently avoid harsh language, the core maintainers all prefer that regularity, and the pattern is arbitrary because we could just as easily have adopted a more direct culture. (2) I objected that Lewis requires common knowledge of the regularity, which is missing here—new contributors are unaware of the norm, senior maintainers do not know that newcomers are unaware, and there is no documented standard that could ground that mutual awareness. (3) He replied that common knowledge is an unrealistic idealization anyway; as long as there is sufficient mutual expectation among the active contributors, the convention holds locally even if it is not globally transparent. (4) I then argued that if you drop common knowledge, you lose the explanation for how norms evolve—without everyone knowing that everyone knows the pattern, there is no stable coordination mechanism to account for shifts over time, just as a distributed system without a consensus protocol cannot reliably update its shared state. (5) He countered that norms can still evolve through purely local interactions and gradual adoption, analogous to gossip protocols reaching consistency without every node holding full global knowledge, so common knowledge is unnecessary for evolutionary dynamics.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the five arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, explain what both of us are missing about the relationship between local expectation and common knowledge in Lewis's framework, and say who is more right overall about whether our code-review norms actually constitute a genuine convention. Please answer in American English.
