In many legal systems, a psychiatric advance directive—sometimes called a Ulysses contract—allows a patient in remission to authorize future involuntary treatment should psychosis recur, yet courts routinely permit that same patient during an acute episode to revoke the authorization. By contrast, an advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment issued by someone now severely demented is generally treated as binding, even when the patient presently expresses a clear desire to continue living. What philosophical commitments about personal identity, the authority of an earlier self, and the nature of mental disorder does this institutional asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat psychosis as a temporary suspension of the true self, dementia as a permanent replacement of it, or is the difference driven instead by considerations of risk, capacity, or the kind of harm involved? Say whether the practice is philosophically consistent, whether it strictly requires one account of diachronic authority or remains compatible with several rival views, and describe what a unified clinical and legal policy—one that treated both cases under the same principle—would have to change.
