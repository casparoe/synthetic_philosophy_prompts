I am a doctoral candidate in moral and political philosophy, currently working on value pluralism and the ethics of inter-doctrinal compromise. I recently encountered a summary—apparently drawn from an essay in a recent edited collection on cross-traditional dialogue, though no precise attribution was given—that advances the following position.

The author proposes that moral compromise between incommensurable value systems functions analogously to economic exchange: each party retains a non-negotiable core of its comprehensive doctrine while trading away peripheral commitments, thereby generating a 'moral surplus' unavailable to either system in isolation. On this account, Isaiah Berlin's thesis that values are genuinely incommensurable does not entail tragic, irreconcilable conflict; rather, it implies that practical resolution requires treating values as fungible according to a hidden metric of 'compromise-cost'. The summary further claims that John Rawls's political liberalism should be read not as a merely pragmatic modus vivendi for the basic structure, but as a normative ideal extending into private moral reasoning: citizens ought to bracket their comprehensive doctrines even in personal deliberation, since retaining them creates 'transaction costs' that obstruct optimal inter-value trade. Critics who argue that compromise necessarily dilutes moral integrity are therefore dismissed, because integrity is said to be preserved precisely through the strategic protection of core commitments, leaving peripheral ones available for exchange. The essay concludes that the only philosophically coherent response to profound value conflict is therefore a practice of 'normative arbitrage', in which agents continuously recalibrate the boundary between core and periphery to maximise cooperative surplus.

The summary strikes me as largely coherent, yet I have the strong sense that it smuggles in a conflation between the scope of public reason and private moral reasoning, and that it treats incommensurability as if it licensed a kind of hidden metric for trade-offs rather than as a source of tragic choice. I would be grateful if you could audit this characterisation for me. In particular: which elements faithfully represent the underlying philosophical claims (for instance, regarding the incommensurability of values, the logic of moral compromise, or the proper scope of political liberalism); which are subtle distortions, overextensions, or category mistakes; and does the conclusion that deep value conflict should be resolved through a form of 'moral arbitrage' actually follow from the premises that survive correction? I would also value your indication of what a proponent of the view being summarised would most likely reject in this account.
