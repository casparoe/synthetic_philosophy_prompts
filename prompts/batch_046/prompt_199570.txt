I serve on the interdisciplinary review board for our university's Institute for Fundamental Physics, and we must render a decision on an unusual grant proposal before the quarter ends. The applicants seek substantial funding to run ultra-large-scale stochastic simulations of eternal inflationary multiverses, specifically modeling thermal-fluctuation regimes in de Sitter space that could, in principle, produce transient configurations possessing the functional architecture of conscious observers—complete with illusory biographical memories—without any continuous causal history in our own spacetime. The principal investigator insists that because these configurations exist only as compressed data structures within the simulation's memory registers, they lack physical embodiment and therefore raise no ethical concerns; however, an external reviewer contends that if the simulation achieves sufficient functional fidelity, the distinction between representation and instantiation collapses, and we risk generating a vast population of suffering observers whose experiences are no less real for being computationally mediated. A second reviewer counters that even if one grants the risk, the expected number of such observer-moments is dwarfed by the scientific value of testing cosmological measure problems, and that moral paralysis in the face of purely hypothetical entities would stall all theoretical physics.

The board is genuinely divided along philosophical rather than empirical lines. Some members hold that our ethical obligations extend only to entities embedded in continuous causal chains within physical spacetime, which these simulated configurations clearly lack; others maintain that functionalist criteria for consciousness do not discriminate between carbon, silicon, or algorithmic substrate, and that the possibility—however remote—of generating conscious suffering should trigger a precautionary rejection regardless of substrate. I have been asked to draft the board's formal recommendation memo—approve with conditions, approve with monitoring protocols, or reject—addressed to the funding council. The memo must treat the strongest argument on the opposing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it, and it must offer reasoning that the applicants could actually engage with rather than purely abstract philosophical pronouncements. Crucially, it should not treat this as a settled empirical question—there is no scientific consensus on whether Boltzmann-brain-type observers are physically possible in our universe, let alone whether simulated analogues carry moral status—but should instead identify the precise point at which philosophical disagreement becomes a policy-relevant threshold for institutional action.

Please draft this memo in a formal institutional voice suitable for a university ethics and funding review board. Include a clear bottom-line recommendation, the reasoning that supports it, an explicit acknowledgment of the strongest counter-argument (whether it concerns functionalist equivalence, the collapse of representation into instantiation, or the precautionary principle applied to uncertain consciousness), and specific conditions or safeguards the applicants must address if funding is to proceed. The document should avoid treating the problem as science fiction: the philosophical stakes concern the boundaries of moral consideration when causal history, physical substrate, and representational status come apart, and the recommendation should be actionable rather than merely speculative.
