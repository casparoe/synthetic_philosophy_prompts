Write the opening remarks, about five minutes when spoken, for a workshop marking the retirement of an epistemologist whose career centred on the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge. The audience is split: philosophers of science and her former students on one side, and the laboratory scientists, funding officers, and family members who supported her work on the other. You must not lecture, but the philosophical difficulty should shape the structure of what you can and cannot claim: honour her view that basic knowledge cannot be justified without circularity, yet avoid presenting this as defeat or nihilism. Invent and deploy a concrete, slightly niche scenario — a closed loop of scientific instruments that calibrate one another with no external gold standard — to illustrate how easy knowledge, the quick and unearned confidence placed in initial readings, simultaneously generates the circularity and makes collaboration possible despite it. The speech should land for experts without losing the non-philosophers, must contain no bibliographies, citations, or named texts, and should not pretend she resolved the problem. Write in British English. In your response, state how confident you are in the philosophical framing you have used, and identify the specific point about which you are least sure — particularly whether the instrument-calibration analogy genuinely captures epistemic circularity or risks collapsing it into mere practical interdependence.
