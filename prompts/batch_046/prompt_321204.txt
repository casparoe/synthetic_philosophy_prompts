I am preparing a session for my graduate seminar in moral philosophy—eight students, ninety minutes—on standing to blame and hypocrisy. Rather than delivering a lecture, I want the students to encounter the problem through a structured deliberation with hidden information. The specific philosophical idea I want them to feel, before I name it, is that hypocrisy undermines standing not uniformly but in proportion to the structural isomorphism between the blamer’s hidden fault and the fault being blamed; and that in collective contexts this creates an interdependence in which one unrevealed isomorphic fault can void the standing of the entire group.

Please design a complete, runnable activity that meets the following conditions.

First, setup and roles. Each student receives a sealed card describing a past action they have allegedly committed, unknown to the others. One student—the designated target—has a revealed serious fault that is structurally analogous to some hidden cards but not to others (for example, breaking a promise to a vulnerable party versus breaking a promise to a peer; free-riding on collective labor versus free-riding on a public resource). The analogies should be clear enough that students can recognize isomorphism upon disclosure, but not so transparent that they infer their own cards immediately.

Second, decision protocol. The group must decide, in successive rounds, whether to issue a collective blame statement against the target. Any student who wishes to support blame must first declare publicly whether their hidden card is structurally isomorphic to the target’s fault. If they declare isomorphism, they contribute standing tokens to a group pool at a rate proportional to the similarity; if they conceal isomorphism and are later exposed, the group’s blame statement is voided and the concealer forfeits all tokens. At the conclusion, a random draw reveals two hidden cards; if either revealed card is isomorphic to the target’s fault and was not declared, the collective blame collapses entirely.

Third, incentive structure. The group gains normative satisfaction—symbolized by accumulating standing tokens—only if the blame statement survives the final reveal. Individual students prefer not to disclose, since disclosure depletes personal standing, yet undisclosed isomorphism risks collective failure. The rules should therefore generate tension between solidarity and self-protection, forcing participants to discover that maintaining collective blame requires either universal transparency, which fractures group cohesion, or strategic silence, which endangers the group’s normative achievement.

Fourth, the experiential aim. The mechanics should be arranged so that students first feel the pull of collective condemnation, then discover—through disclosure, near-miss, or the threat of the final reveal—that standing is interdependent and scalar rather than an individual binary property. They should arrive at the intuition that one person’s hidden hypocrisy can undermine the group’s authority to blame before I introduce any technical terminology.

Fifth, debrief. After the activity, provide a formal debrief that maps each round onto philosophical distinctions: personal versus derivative standing, structural versus accidental hypocrisy, and the non-linear degradation of standing under isomorphism. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—for instance, the first-person phenomenology of moral resentment, or the temporal dynamics of long-term moral repair—and tell me precisely what I must supply verbally to fill those gaps. Anticipate predictable failures: students may treat standing as purely strategic rather than normative, or may become fixated on the card mechanics rather than the moral dynamics. Suggest exact redirection questions I can pose to return the discussion to the normative dimension.

Please present the rules clearly, specify the exact contents of the hidden cards so that I can print them without alteration, and ensure that the entire design requires no external materials and fits within a single ninety-minute session. Keep the tone formal and suitable for direct use in a graduate philosophy classroom.
