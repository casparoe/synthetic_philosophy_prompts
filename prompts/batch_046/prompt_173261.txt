I am convening the final assessment for an upper-level seminar on collective memory and the politics of national defeat, and I should like a take-home essay prompt together with a model answer and a granular marking scheme. The course has examined how communities convert military or political failure into enduring identity resources, concentrating on four canonical cases: the Confederate Lost Cause in the post-Reconstruction American South; the Masada narrative in modern Israeli state-building; the Kosovo Polje (1389) myth in Serbian nationalist discourse; and the Dunkirk evacuation as a founding story of British resilience during the Second World War.

Rather than asking students merely to compare these cases, I want the examination to turn on a conceptual distinction developed in our seminars: that between a ‘sacrificial defeat’ — in which the community’s physical survival is narrated as directly dependent on the very act of losing, so that defeat becomes an unrepeatable founding trauma — and a ‘redemptive defeat’ — in which the moral meaning of the loss is retroactively transformed into a warrant for future exclusion, revanchism, or exceptionalism, without the survival logic being central. Students should be able to misapply this distinction, for instance by treating Dunkirk as redemptive rather than sacrificial, or by collapsing Masada into the redemptive category solely because of its later militaristic appropriation.

Please construct a single essay question that requires candidates to apply this sacrificial/redemptive framework to exactly two of the four cases, to defend one application and to challenge another, and to address one specific objection: that the distinction is unstable because all sacrificial narratives eventually acquire redemptive uses. The prompt should specify a 90-minute sitting, approximately 1,500 words, and 40 marks total. Then provide a model answer identifying the specific analytical moves a first-class response must make — correct classification of at least two cases, recognition of the instability objection, and a reasoned conclusion about whether the framework survives — together with a marking scheme allocating marks precisely (for example, 10 marks for conceptual framing, 15 for case analysis, 10 for handling the objection, and 5 for argument structure and prose), with explicit deductions for common errors such as conflating the two categories, describing historical events without applying the framework, or treating the four cases as interchangeable illustrations without engaging the specific mnemonic work each performs.

Write everything in British English, and do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since this is a closed-book examination.
