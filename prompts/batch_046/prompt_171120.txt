In a philosophy of science seminar discussing scientific realism, a participant repeatedly invoked the slogan that mathematics is unreasonably effective in describing the natural world — the familiar claim, often associated with Eugene Wigner, that the precision with which abstract mathematical structures yield correct physical predictions is somehow surprising or even miraculous. They used it specifically to resist an instrumentalist account of theoretical entities: since Hilbert-space formalism and Lie-group symmetries were developed with little or no empirical input yet predict measurement outcomes with extraordinary accuracy, they argued, we are forced to conclude that these structures are discovered features of reality rather than invented tools. I want to be able to show that the slogan is ambiguous in ways that matter for that argument. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses this claim might be expressing? For each reading, please specify: what kind of effectiveness is at issue (predictive accuracy, explanatory unification, novel prediction, or structural correspondence?); relative to what baseline the effectiveness counts as unreasonable (the premise that mathematics is a purely formal game? evolutionary constraints on human cognition? the vast space of logically possible formalisms?); what metaphysical or epistemic conclusion, if any, is supposed to follow (mathematical Platonism, structural realism, an indispensability argument, or merely an open puzzle?); and whether that particular reading actually supports the anti-instrumentalist conclusion my interlocutor drew, or whether it collapses into a platitude, depends on a hidden premise, or is false. I am particularly interested in distinctions that separate mathematics developed purely internally and only later applied — for example, Riemannian geometry preceding general relativity — from mathematics constructed specifically to fit existing data, since the unreasonableness seems to attach differently in each case. Finally, indicate which of these readings is likely the one being invoked when someone uses the phrase to end an argument about whether probabilities or wave functions exist independently of our representations.
