I am drafting a novel in which two diplomats are locked in a brinkmanship crisis, and I have been relying on the classic strategic analogy: nuclear standoff is a game of chicken, in which each driver tears out his steering wheel and hurls it through the window to prove he will not swerve. In the literature this is used to license the conclusion that rational deterrence requires visible, voluntary self-binding — one must make oneself credibly irrational in order to force the other party's rational concession. I want to press the analogy further through a narrative invention: in my story, one diplomat possesses not a theatrical gesture but a hidden structural lock — an automatic retaliation protocol she genuinely cannot disable, more like a steering wheel welded into place than one flamboyantly discarded. Could you audit how far the chicken-game analogy holds once we substitute structural commitment for performative commitment? Please provide an explicit correspondence table — driver, swerve, thrown steering wheel, crash — mapped onto state actors, concession, credibility mechanisms, and nuclear exchange. Then identify precisely where the mapping breaks: where it imports the wrong ontology by treating states as unitary drivers rather than bureaucratic assemblages; where it smuggles in the assumption that "crash" is a symmetric shared outcome rather than an asymmetric catastrophe; and whether the theatrical imagery of voluntary self-sacrifice quietly carries the conclusion that credible brinkmanship must be visibly chosen. Finally, tell me whether the analogy's central claim — that rational brinkmanship depends on performative, observable commitment — survives once we replace the thrown wheel with an institutional or technological lock-in, or whether that conclusion was being carried by the analogy's dramatic imagery all along. If the analogy collapses under this substitution, what would be a more accurate analogical frame for the kind of commitment my character actually embodies?
