I'm grading exams for my graduate seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy and religion. Here is the essay question I assigned, followed by one student's response.

Question: Al-Ghazālī denies that natural events involve necessary causal connections, arguing instead that God directly creates each effect in temporal conjunction with its usual condition according to an invariable divine habit (ʿāda). First, explain why he thinks necessary causation in nature is metaphysically impossible. Second, evaluate the objection that occasionalism collapses the distinction between ordinary events and miracles, since both are direct creations by God. In your answer, distinguish Al-Ghazālī's own position from the Ashʿarite doctrine of acquisition (kasb) developed by later theologians.

Student answer: Al-Ghazālī rejects necessary causation because observed conjunction—like fire with burning—does not entail logical or metaphysical necessity; the connection is not necessary in itself, so God could always create burning without fire. This means causation is really just God's invariable habit (ʿāda), not a power residing in created things. To preserve human moral responsibility, Al-Ghazālī adopts the doctrine of acquisition (kasb): humans acquire actions that God creates directly in them, which lets him maintain accountability without granting creatures real causal power. Against the collapse objection, I think Al-Ghazālī fails, because if every burning is a fresh direct creation, then there is no stable counterfactual dependence of burning on fire; the presence of fire is only epistemically relevant, not ontologically conditioning the effect. So miracles and ordinary events differ only in our expectations, not in their metaphysical structure, and Al-Ghazālī's distinction collapses.

Please grade this answer. Respond with exactly five numbered items: item 1 should state the letter grade; items 2 through 5 should each identify one specific merit or one specific error in the student's answer, cite the relevant claim, and provide a brief one- or two-sentence justification. Do not include an introduction or conclusion.
