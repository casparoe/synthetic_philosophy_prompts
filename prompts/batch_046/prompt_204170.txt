Right, I've tied myself in knots over Gödel again. My study group cooked up this argument about applying the first theorem outside maths, and it smells like it proves far too much — like empirical science is permanently broken, and also that the argument itself is broken. Where's the sleight of hand?

(1) Any consistent formal system with enough arithmatic can generate true sentences it can't prove.
(2) Our best scientific theories embed arithmetic and are, ideally, consistent formal systems.
(3) So for any theory T there's a Gödel-style sentence G(T) that's true (if T is consistent) but unprovable in T.
(4) By Gödel numbering we can map syntactic statements onto physical predictions, so G(T) can represent a specific empirical claim — say about a limit — that T never outputs.
(5) So there are physical truths that no empirically adequate theory can ever derive or justify.
(6) Scientific rationality demands we only believe what's derivable from our best theory plus observation.
(7) Hence scientific rationality is epistemically incomplete by design: it permanently excludes true beliefs.
(8) Apply the same machinery to the meta-theory M that describes scientific rationality. M also embeds arithmetic.
(9) So there's a statement about M's own consistency, or about the validity of (6), that M can't derive.
(10) Therefore the argument that science is incomplete relies on premises that M — and therefore we — can't rationally access.
(11) We're left with either universal scepticism about reasoning itself, or admitting Gödel doesn't cross the maths/physics boundary.
(12) But (11) feels like special pleading.

I reckon there's either a category mistake in (4) or a modal slide between syntactic and epistemic incompleteness, but I can't pin it down. Which step should I drop? I'd rather keep (1) and (7) if possible. Also, does bringing in reflective closure — the idea that a rational system ought to represent its own consistency conditions — fix the self-undermining, or just make it worse?
