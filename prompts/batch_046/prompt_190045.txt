I am currently drafting a novel in which a logician character becomes obsessed with the persistence of Skolem's paradox—not as a technical curiosity, but as an emblem of a deeper failure in philosophical argument. The paradox, as I understand it, arises from the 1920s result that any first-order axiomatization of set theory with an infinite model has a countable model, even though within that model there exist sets that the model itself regards as uncountable. Among the mathematicians and philosophers my character encounters, the dispute has calcified into two immovable camps. One camp, which I might call the syntactic relativists, holds that cardinality is inherently model-relative and that first-order logic's inability to pin down an absolute notion of size is not a defect but a feature of formalization. The opposing camp, the ontological absolutists, maintains that if set theory is genuinely about a hierarchical universe containing uncountably many objects, then the existence of a countable model in which those same objects are enumerated is not a benign technicality but a direct contradiction of the theory's intended meaning. For decades, these groups have circulated the same meta-theoretic proofs and the same appeals to the Löwenheim-Skolem theorems without convergence.

My own suspicion—one I have given to my character—is that the disagreement is not really about logic or ontology, but about what counts as a legitimate explanation: the relativists treat the countability of the model as dissolving the paradox by revealing it to be a linguistic illusion, while the absolutists treat that same move as changing the subject from mathematics to meta-mathematics. Is this diagnosis accurate? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal disagreement—perhaps concerning whether internal categoricity can ever be strengthened to an external result, or whether a modal framework for cardinality could bridge the gap—disguised as a metaphysical quarrel? I want an honest diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Furthermore, if your diagnosis holds, what would count as genuine progress? Would it require a new formal result distinguishing internal from external quantification in a rigorous way, a conceptual distinction that makes one side's question dissolve, or should we conclude that no resolution is possible because the two camps are answering different questions—one about the semantics of formal systems, the other about the metaphysics of mathematical objects—under the shared name of Skolem's paradox?
