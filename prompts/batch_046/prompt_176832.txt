I'm preparing a podcast interview with a political theorist who has spent the last several years defending an institutional design she calls cascading deliberative panels. The core idea is to replace career legislatures with successive cohorts selected by lot, each serving a single non-renewable term of fixed length; but unlike standard citizens'-assembly proposals, her model requires each outgoing cohort to randomly select a smaller alumni oversight body from among its own members—again by lot, never by election—to review the incoming cohort's deliberations and veto measures that violate constitutional constraints. She argues that this creates institutional memory and epistemic continuity without producing a professional political class, since no individual can serve twice and no campaign mechanism exists. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the mechanism. I need questions that press exactly where critics of sortition and deliberative democracy typically press: whether random alumni selection reintroduces informal elite capture or status hierarchies within the cohort, how the model handles complex regulatory domains that seem to require sustained technical expertise, whether the veto power of alumni panels effectively turns them into an unaccountable upper chamber, and whether strict term limits destroy the very deliberative goods—trust-building, iterative learning—that make small-group discussion valuable. For each major line of pressure, please include a follow-up keyed to the most likely defense she would offer—for example, if she claims that lot-selection neutralizes status, ask what empirical or institutional feature prevents alumni from trading influence for future social or economic rewards. End with one question about what specific evidence—perhaps from existing citizens' assemblies or from comparative institutional performance—would cause her to conclude that the cascading structure adds nothing over simpler one-shot sortition. The tone should be rigorous and collegial, suitable for an audience familiar with democratic theory but not with her particular hybrid.
