I've been working through my own position on Sartrean freedom and bad faith for a graduate seminar, and I'd like a consistency check before I present. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) I take radical freedom literally—even under extreme external constraint, there is always an irreducible choice about how to interpret and respond to one's situation, so freedom is never diminished, only denied in bad faith; (2) I understand bad faith not as ordinary self-deception but specifically as the refusal to hold together our facticity and transcendence—either treating ourselves as pure being-in-itself (a fixed object) or as pure being-for-itself (disembodied consciousness without weight); (3) I accept Sartre's analysis of the Look: being objectified by others is an ontological threat to my being-for-others, but it never actually limits my freedom, since I remain free to choose my attitude toward that objectification; (4) I think authenticity requires owning this freedom continuously, yet in modern institutional life we necessarily inhabit functional roles; I believe these roles need not be bad faith if one maintains reflective awareness that they are chosen projects rather than essences; and (5) I hold that existential anguish is the recognition that my fundamental project is groundless, though I also think that deeply sedimented commitments—say, to a particular person—can become so embedded in my facticity that abandoning them feels like self-destruction rather than an open choice.

I worry that (1) and (5) pull against each other in a way that is not obvious: if freedom is genuinely never diminished and all values rest on groundless choice, then treating a sedimented commitment as a constraining facticity looks like the very denial of transcendence that (2) identifies as bad faith. At the same time, (4) and (2) seem to generate a practical tension—if bad faith is always in principle overcome through reflection, then maintaining authentic awareness of one's institutional role should be straightforward, yet the sedimentation described in (5) suggests that over time such awareness may become unstable or exhausting without sliding into identification with the role. Meanwhile, there may be only an apparent conflict between (3) and (1): one might think that if others truly cannot limit my freedom, then the Look should not feel like a genuine threat, but I suspect this dissolves once we distinguish ontological vulnerability in being-for-others from a limitation on being-for-itself. Could you audit these commitments? Order the real tensions by severity, specify for each what exactly has to be revised—whether (1)'s absolutism, (5)'s notion of sedimentation, or (2)'s definition of bad faith—and identify which pair is only apparently in tension, with the cheapest way to dissolve it. Please also separate what is settled interpretation of Sartre from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of existentialism, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
