I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about owning ideas, going by gut reactions to cases. If I invent a knot-tying method and teach it to a friend who then opens a sailing school, I'm not owed royalties -- the idea isn't property. But if they photocopy my handwritten manual and sell that, it's wrong, even though the content is identical. If I independantly derive a philosophical argument that was actually published in an obscure 1970s journal I've never seen, I don't think I stole anything -- discovery clears it. But if I write a melody from a dream and it turns out to be a song I heard once as a child, I feel there's some hidden debt there, even without intent to copy. Now the tricky one: an AI trained on public texts writes a poem -- I don't think the AI owns it, but I also don't think the corporation should get copyright just for running the compute. Meanwhile, finding an unpublished proof in a dead mathematician's notebook: publishing it is fine, but I should cite him, not split royalties with his estate.

Is there a principle? I'm tempted by something like 'conceptual genealogy' -- you owe recognition not for the idea itself but for the specific pathway of access that shaped your thinking. But then why does the melody feel different from the knot? And does the AI have a pathway, or is it just a tool? I keep going back and forth on whether independent discovery always wipes the debt, or whether there's lingering obligation when an idea was 'in the air' in a way that structured your conceptual tools. What's the principle, and which of my verdicts has to be dropped? Also, what does it say about a case I haven't mentioned: two people in different countries, with no shared language, come up with the exact same invention on the same day?
