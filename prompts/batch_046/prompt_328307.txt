I'm writing an essay for my philosophy elective on how cinema can dramatize non-Western epistemologies, and I want to avoid the superficial reading that Rashomon merely shows "everyone has their own truth." I am focusing on Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950)—specifically its framing of a samurai's death and the assault of his wife through four incompatible grove-testimonies (the bandit Tajomaru, the wife, the dead samurai via a medium, and the woodcutter), together with the closing scene at the Rashomon gate in which the woodcutter takes in an abandoned infant despite confessing that he does not know what actually happened.

My philosophical lens is Jain anekāntavāda, but I want to keep a specific conceptual distinction sharp rather than treating the doctrine as generic relativism: anekānta as ontological multiplicity (reality possesses infinite, irreducible aspects that cannot be collapsed into a single description) versus naya as epistemic standpoint (each individual cognition grasps only one partial aspect without thereby being false), together with syādvāda, the practice of conditioning assertions with "perhaps" to mark their partiality and avoid absolutism. I also want to consider whether ethical action requires kevalajñāna (omniscient knowledge of all aspects) or can legitimately proceed under acknowledged partiality.

Please read the film through this framework with conceptual precision, not as an illustration but as an argument in its own right:

1. Can the four testimonies be reconstructed as four distinct nayas that each capture a real aspect of a single anekānta event in the grove? Or does the film deny that any such synthesis exists, thereby reducing anekāntavāda to psychological subjectivism and narrative unreliability?

2. Where should syādvāda apply? Would a Jain philosopher formulate each witness's claim with conditional predication, and does the film's refusal to adjudicate between accounts genuinely mirror syādvāda, or does it slide into a stronger skepticism—the denial that any aspect is genuinely captured?

3. The woodcutter's decision to adopt the infant is often interpreted as moral action that transcends epistemic failure. From the anekāntavāda perspective, is this an illegitimate leap in which one naya (the ethical standpoint) is treated as the whole truth? Or is it a legitimate practical judgment made in full awareness that one's knowledge is partial and that kevalajñāna is unavailable to human agents?

4. Where does the philosophical reading break down? Does Rashomon reduce Jain multiplicity to emotional bias, shame, and self-deception, and if so, does that distortion matter for using the film to teach the concept?

Please point to specific scenes—the framing conversation at the gate, the grove flashbacks, the medium's possession, and the final adoption—and say exactly what each shows, dramatizes, or complicates about the distinction between anekānta and naya. I am not asking for a plot summary, verbatim quotation, bibliography, or citations; I need a rigorous conceptual analysis that takes both the film's structure and the Jain framework seriously, and that concludes by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about many-sided truth that formal argument alone does not capture.
