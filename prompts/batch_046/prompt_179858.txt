I'm writing as a doctoral student in philosophy, working at the intersection of decision theory and the philosophy of rationality. At a recent workshop, two colleagues—both philosophers with formal training—fell into a sustained disagreement about the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game. I found myself unable to referee their exchange and would value a careful adjudication.

The game is the standard six-node centipede: at each node, the active player can either take a slightly larger share and end the game, or continue and allow the other player to decide at the next node, with the total payoff growing but the division shifting. Both agreed on the payoff structure and on the formal backward-induction solution (immediate termination at node one). Their dispute was philosophical, not mathematical.

Colleague A opened by insisting that backward induction is structurally unavoidable once its premises are granted. At the terminal node, the second player takes; knowing this, the first player takes at the penultimate node; and so on, propagating all the way back to the first move. The result is that two fully rational agents with common knowledge of their rationality should both anticipate immediate defection and never reach the cooperative path. The paradox, A said, is therefore descriptive rather than normative: it shows that actual human agents systematically deviate from the rational ideal, not that the ideal is flawed.

Colleague B replied that this assumes a transparency of rationality that never holds in practice. Real agents face what B called "epistemic opacity" about each other's reasoning processes: I may believe you are rational, but I cannot be sure that you believe that I believe that you are rational, and so on. Once this opacity is admitted, the induction chain breaks at the first link where common knowledge fails. The fact that people regularly reach the fourth or fifth node, B argued, is not irrationality but evidence that the common-knowledge premise is descriptively false.

A countered that even with opacity, the normative force of the argument remains. If I assign any non-zero credence to your being rational, I should condition on the possibility that you will take at your first opportunity. The expected payoff of continuing therefore collapses rapidly, and the rational move is still to take early. More fundamentally, A claimed, the real philosophical puzzle is that rationality appears self-defeating: when both parties anticipate each other's strategic reasoning, they destroy the very cooperative outcome that would have made them both better off. The paradox is not about human failure but about a structural feature of mutual anticipation.

B then introduced a different line of attack. Even granting A's normative framework, B argued, backward induction depends on a hidden metaphysical assumption: that there is a single, diachronically unified rational agent extending across all six nodes. In reality, B suggested, agents experience "diachronic fragmentation"—the self that decides at node one is not strictly identical in its preferences and reasoning dispositions to the self that would decide at node four, especially after observing the other player's unexpected cooperation. To apply backward induction, one must treat future decision-points as governed by the same rational plan adopted now, but that is precisely the picture of agency that is philosophically contested. If we reject diachronic unity, B concluded, the induction doesn't apply, and reaching later nodes is not a failure of rationality but a consequence of treating each choice as genuinely fresh.

A responded that this saves the phenomenon only by changing the subject. If each node is a fresh decision disconnected from a pre-committed plan, then strategic reasoning—and indeed game theory itself—loses its purchase. The centipede game is designed specifically to test whether sequential rationality can sustain cooperation over time. To deny that there is one rational agent extending through the sequence is not to solve the backward-induction paradox but to abandon the conditions under which the paradox arises.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of A's and B's interventions, rate whether the point is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing. In particular, I want to know whether B's appeal to diachronic fragmentation genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, and whether A is correct that this move undermines strategic reasoning altogether.
