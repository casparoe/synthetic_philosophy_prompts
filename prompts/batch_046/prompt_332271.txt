I'm planning a single sixty-minute seminar for upper-level philosophy undergraduates on Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, and on how that distinction bears on moral responsibility. Rather than delivering a linear exposition of the source text, I want a teaching plan built around one carrying case that is not the standard face-to-face resentment scenario. The case is large-scale digital public shaming—viral denunciation campaigns directed at a specific individual—where the participants may never interact with the target, the audience is diffuse and anonymous, and the mechanism of blame is mediated by platforms rather than by direct interpersonal encounter. The seminar should use this case to illuminate whether such campaigns genuinely express reactive attitudes in Strawson's sense, or whether their scale, mediation, and anonymity push participants into an objective attitude toward the target.

Plan the sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced, specifying the dependency structure: for example, whether an account of ordinary interpersonal relations must be secured before reactive attitudes can be introduced, or whether the problem of determinism should appear only after the attitudes have been characterized. Describe exactly what properties the digital-shaming case must possess and what properties it must lack in order to serve as the carrying example—specifically, how it must preserve the element of personal address that reactive attitudes require, while lacking the direct relational context that makes ordinary resentment unambiguous, so that it does not collapse into empirical sociology or media studies.

Then map the predictable misconceptions. I expect students to think that reactive attitudes are merely intense emotions rather than relational responses; that the objective attitude is simply emotional coldness or detachment rather than a shift in the framework of engagement; and that Strawson's central claim is that belief in determinism renders reactive attitudes irrational. For each misconception, specify the exact point in the sequence—before the case is introduced, during its analysis, or after the check for understanding—where it should be intercepted and neutralized.

Next, design a single check question for the end of the session that reveals whether students grasp why the digital-shaming case either supports or undermines Strawson's claim that reactive attitudes are not grounded in metaphysical beliefs about determinism.

Finally, identify one substantial portion of Strawson's broader argument—perhaps his taxonomy of conditions under which reactive attitudes are suspended, or his reply to the optimist and the pessimist about determinism—that should be deliberately left out at this stage, and explain why omitting it protects the coherence of the session rather than simplifying it carelessly.
