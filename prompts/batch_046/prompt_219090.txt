I've been trying to work through the bootstrapping problem in reliabilism, especially after running into the idea of recursive credentialing—where a system updates its confidence in the very model that generates its outputs, using those outputs themselves as training data. A friend walked me into a human-knowledge version of this that seems to prove way too much, and now I can't find the exit. Here's the chain he laid out. (1) If a belief-forming process P is in fact reliable, then beliefs produced by P count as knowledge—that's the core of process reliabilism. (2) I can use P itself to form beliefs about P's own track record: my memory tells me I've been right about past events, my perception tells me my senses haven't systematically lied, and so on. (3) So by (1), if P really is reliable, then my belief that P is reliable is itself knowledge, achieved with no independant check outside P. (4) Once I know P is reliable, I can use that knowledge to license every other belief P produces—it's like getting a master key that validates the whole factory. (5) That means a single reliable mechanism can recursively credential itself, then spin out an unlimited web of further knowledge without ever touching an external calibraton. (6) But this is viciously circular: it's like weighing a scale with itself, then weighing the world with that same scale and calling it verified. (7) So either reliabilism lets knowledge be pulled up by its own bootstraps with zero outside input, or we must deny that anyone can ever know their own cognitive faculties are reliable—which collapses into a strange skepticism where we have knowledge but can't reflectively know we have it. (8) Both horns feel wrong, and I really want to keep reliabilism without falling into that skepticism.

Which step is the sleight of hand, and what does giving it up actually cost? I'd especially like to hold onto (1) and avoid the conclusion in (7), but I'm not sure that's possible without breaking the chain somewhere in the middle—maybe between the meta-belief and the licensing step, or maybe the problem is that (2) smuggles in other processes without admitting it. If I have to pay a price, I want to know exactly what I'm buying.
