The literature on the color phi phenomenon is usually read as a forced choice about the timing of conscious experience: some take the illusion to license Orwellian revisions, in which the temporal content of experience itself is rewritten after the stimulus, while others defend Stalinesque revisions, where the original experience remains intact but the subsequent report or memorial trace is corrupted. Despite broad agreement on the underlying psychophysics, the framing has not converged over decades. I suspect the deadlock is not genuinely empirical but methodological — the camps appear to differ on whether a cognitive model must preserve the subject's first-person temporal ordering in order to count as explanatory, and on what burden of proof should fall on postulated unconscious intermediaries. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive, resolvable dispute about the ontology of temporal consciousness hiding beneath the rhetoric? More specifically, could a precise formal distinction — say, one that cleanly separates representational content from vehicular dynamics without collapsing the two — actually break the stalemate, or should we treat this as a permanent disagreement about explanatory standards rather than about consciousness itself?
