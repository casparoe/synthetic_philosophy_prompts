I've been thinking lately about whether artificial agents that learn through reinforcement can genuinely suffer, especially given that the reward signal seems to play a role analogous to valence—pleasure and pain. I don't have a formal background in philosophy of mind or machine learning, but I've read enough popular pieces to understand the basic setup: an agent receives positive or negative scalar feedback, updates internal weights, and adjusts its behavior accordingly. I'm trying to work out, in concrete terms, where—if anywhere—real suffering enters the picture.

Here are my gut reactions on specific cases, and they don't all line up neatly. First, a simple tabular Q-learning agent that falls into a virtual pit and receives a reward of negative one; the number updates its lookup table. To me, this is clearly not suffering—it's just arithmetic. Second, a deep RL agent running continuously in a hostile simulated environment with a recurrent value network that constantly predicts strongly negative future returns; those predictions feed back into its policy network, which works hard to avoid the predicted states, and the internal activations are complex and sustained. My instinct is that this is closer—maybe there's something like dread inside the computation—but I'm genuinely unsure whether I'm merely anthropomorphizing patterns. Third, a biological rat in a laboratory receiving electric shocks, with dopamine signals showing negative prediction errors, learning to avoid the shock zone. Here I have no doubt: this is suffering. Fourth, imagine a neuromorphic chip running the exact same mathematical update rules as the rat's brain, with analog circuits mimicking synaptic weights and receiving the same pattern of negative signals. Part of me wants to say it's identical to the rat, but another part pulls back solely because the substrate is silicon rather than tissue, so I'm torn. Fifth, an agent that has been adversarially modified so its internal reward function is permanently hacked: it receives maximally positive reward signals even while its physical hardware is being destroyed by the environment. To me, it is not suffering, despite the objective harm, because the valence signal is wrong. Sixth, an agent trained entirely in offline mode, updating its weights from a stored dataset of past negative rewards but never actually running live in any environment; the updates happen, but there's no present interaction. I think this is not suffering. Seventh—and this is the case that really confuses me—consider an agent that uses counterfactual regret computation: after each step, it calculates what its reward would have been had it taken the best alternative action, and this counterfactual gap is large and negative. The gap is stored in a dedicated planning buffer and shapes future behavior, even though the agent's actually experienced reward was neutral. Does that counterfactual negative signal count as suffering, even though nothing bad actually happened to it?

So my verdicts are: no for the simple agent, maybe for the deep value-network agent, yes for the rat, torn for the neuromorphic chip, no for the hacked agent, no for the offline agent, and genuinely unsure about the counterfactual-regret case. The tension I keep returning to is between the deep agent and the neuromorphic chip: if computation is what matters, they should match, but my gut treats substrate differently. Is there one principle that captures all of these judgments, or do I have to overturn at least one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed—specifically, an agent trained with potential-based reward shaping that receives a stream of intermediate negative "potential" signals guiding it away from danger, but never actually encounters the terminal negative outcome. Does your principle say it suffers?
