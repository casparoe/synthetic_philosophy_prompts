I am serving as the grader for an upper-division philosophy exam on moral obligation and need an independent assessment of one student response. The exam presented the following case and questions.

Case: Dr. Elena Vasquez is a physician-epidemiologist who, during her year-long sabbatical from a research university, chooses to live in a remote region with no functioning health infrastructure and runs a free clinic treating preventable infectious diseases. The work requires her to live in basic conditions, separates her from her family for months at a time, and completely halts her research program. She is not employed by any organization in the region, receives no compensation, and is under no contractual, professional, or legal obligation to be there; colleagues in her field agree that using the sabbatical for research or rest would be perfectly acceptable.

Questions:
(i) Is this a genuine instance of supererogation? In answering, address whether recognizing it as such requires a three-level moral taxonomy—distinguishing the forbidden, the permitted, and the required, with supererogation sitting above the required—or whether a two-level framework (required vs. permitted) can accommodate it without loss.
(ii) Is substantial personal cost or risk a defining condition of supererogation, or only a typical correlate? Could an act be supererogatory if the agent found it effortless and personally rewarding?
(iii) Can a consequentialist ethical framework consistently recognize supererogation, or does the commitment to impartial maximization necessarily collapse acts that produce the best outcomes into obligations?

Please evaluate the student answer below. Assign a letter grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it, identifying both strengths and errors. Do not present your own independent solution to the exam questions; treat this as a grading task in which the deliverable is an assessment of the response's reasoning, accuracy, and coherence.

Student answer:
Dr. Vasquez's clinic work is clearly supererogatory because it produces immense good, is not required by any duty—professional, contractual, or moral—and involves serious personal sacrifice. On (i), Urmson's classic analysis in Saints and Heroes (1958) shows that traditional ethics recognizes only required and forbidden actions, leaving no room for heroic or saintly conduct. To fix this, we need a three-level taxonomy: forbidden actions, permitted-obligatory actions, and supererogatory actions that go beyond what is required. A two-level view fails because it can only classify her work as permitted, which puts it in the same category as trivial choices like selecting one brand of coffee over another; without a separate tier, we cannot mark the moral difference between ordinary permissions and extraordinary merit.

On (ii), substantial cost is not merely a typical feature but a defining condition. If Vasquez found the work relaxing, if it advanced her career more than her research would have, and if she suffered no separation from her family, then even though the act remains good and not strictly required, it would lose its supererogatory status. The moral significance of supererogation lies precisely in the agent's willingness to accept burdens that morality does not demand.

On (iii), consequentialism cannot consistently recognize supererogation. If an act produces the best available consequences, consequentialism requires it; if it produces worse consequences than an alternative, it is wrong. There is no logical space for an act that is best but optional. Some philosophers try to save the appearance by introducing agent-centered prerogatives or personal projects, but these are ad hoc modifications that abandon the core consequentialist commitment to impartial maximization. Therefore, anyone who wants to take supererogation seriously must reject pure consequentialism.
