I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about five minutes—for tomorrow's workshop, "Moral Weight at Scale," which our philosophy department is co-hosting with the university's integrated pest-management program. I am not a scientist myself, though I have been sitting in on the planning committee's discussions with entomologists and policy staff, and I keep returning to a tension that I want my remarks to introduce rather than resolve. The audience will be genuinely mixed: roughly half are agricultural scientists, ecologists, and entomologists who work with insect populations daily; the other half are ethicists, graduate students, and administrative staff with little or no biology background.

The philosophical problem I want placed at the structural center—not as decoration, but as something that genuinely shapes what can and cannot be said—is the scale argument applied to insect sentience. Because insects exist in such vast numbers, even an extremely low probability that any individual insect is sentient could imply that the aggregate moral weight of insects dwarfs that of all vertebrates combined; yet that same low individual probability seems to license treating any particular insect as lacking moral standing altogether. I want to avoid both the false confidence of claiming that insects are clearly conscious and the false comfort of dismissing them entirely, and I want the audience to feel the instability rather than merely hear it described. To do that, I would like to use an unconventional framing device: treating a decision not to intervene in a large-scale agricultural pest outbreak as a kind of moral-portfolio problem under radical uncertainty, where we are managing a population of entities in which we do not know which ones possess standing, but the scale of the portfolio means that errors in either direction carry enormous weight. The remarks should navigate this without offering resolution, should land for the scientists without losing the philosophers, and should feel formal but welcoming. Please write this in American English, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or suggested reading lists, since the audience does not need them and a model without web access could not reliably supply them.
