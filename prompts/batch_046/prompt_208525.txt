Exam question: Explain Newton’s bucket argument for absolute rotation, describing precisely what happens to the water’s surface at each stage. Then present the relationist reply that appeals to distant matter (Mach’s principle). Finally, assess whether that reply succeeds in accounting for the concavity of the water in a hypothetical universe that contains nothing except the bucket and the water.

Student answer:
Newton asks us to hang a bucket of water by a cord. Initially both are at rest and the surface is flat. The bucket is then twisted; at first the water remains flat while the bucket rotates around it, but eventually the water is dragged along, rotates with the bucket, and only then becomes concave. If the bucket is suddenly stopped, the water continues to spin and the surface remains concave even though the bucket is now at rest relative to the water.

From this Newton concludes that rotation is not merely relative to the bucket. In the first stage there is relative motion but no concavity; in the second there is no relative motion yet there is concavity; in the third there is relative motion again, yet the concavity persists. Hence the effect tracks rotation with respect to absolute space, not relational motion.

The relationist denies absolute space and explains the concavity by appealing to rotation relative to the distant fixed stars. This idea was first proposed by Leibniz, who held that all motion is relational and that the stars supply the necessary reference frame. Mach later turned it into the principle that local inertia is determined by the global distribution of matter. If the universe contained only the bucket and water, there would be no distant matter to define rotation, so there would be no fact of the matter about whether the bucket spins and therefore no concavity. General relativity confirms this: without asymptotic boundary conditions there are no global inertial frames, so in such an empty universe the field equations provide no distinction between rest and rotation. Thus Newton’s argument fails. It correctly shows that local rotational effects do not depend on the bucket alone, but it wrongly assumes that the only alternative is substantival space. The Mach-Leibniz view explains the phenomena relationally by tying inertia to the entire cosmic matter distribution.
