I am a high school junior preparing a single fifty-minute session for my school's interdisciplinary seminar on how concepts slip across boundaries within a tightly defined domain. Rather than treating “copycat” as one vague idea with fuzzy edges, I want the group to see it as a family of functionally different judgments that share only a word, and I want to ground this in a constructed microdomain: a hypothetical culinary algorithm that reproduces a celebrated chef’s “flavor signature” using chemically distinct ingredients and processes, which raises parallel questions about craft apprenticeship, consumer deception, and intellectual property without ever duplicating a specific dish.

Plan the explanatory arc as a teaching plan, not as the explanation itself. First, specify the dependency order: which foundational distinction—between token replication and functional resemblance, or between causal copying and structural convergence—must be secured before students can recognize conceptual slippage rather than simple ambiguity. Second, identify the one carrying case or scenario (likely the culinary algorithm, but specify exactly what features it must include and exclude) that can hold all three contextual meanings at once and make the fracture between them visible. Third, map the misconceptions they will predictably form and the sequence in which they will arise—probably that one context supplies the “true” meaning of copycat, that slippage is merely imprecise language, and that the algorithm is obviously either copying or not copying—indicating precisely where in the arc each misconception should be intercepted and defused. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question or brief task at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the functional dependence of the concept on its context rather than memorized three separate definitions. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as full legal doctrines on trademark dilution, Wittgensteinian family resemblance, or the technical architecture of recommendation algorithms—and explain why omitting each protects the explanatory arc rather than merely simplifying it. Keep the focus on the dependency structure among the ideas and the misconception map, and write in American English with a formal, polished tone.
