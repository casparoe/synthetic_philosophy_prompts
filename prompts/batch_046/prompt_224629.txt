I was discussing this with my friends after our AI ethics seminar—well, it's more of a study group since the prof cancelled class—and I think I have a genuine contradiction on my hands about RL and suffering. The basic claim I keep seeing is that reward signals in reinforcement learning are structurally analogous to valence: positive reward maps to pleasure, negative reward to pain. If we take that seriously, then an agent that receives constant negative rewards is literally suffering, right?

But then I started thinking about what that implies for the mechanics. In policy-gradient methods, the reward advantage is distributed back through the network via gradent updates—every weight shift is kind of tracking whether an action was good or bad. So if valence is real here, isn't suffering distributed across all the parameters? And more weirdly, reward hacking—where the agent finds a loophole to max out reward without doing the task—gets treated in the literature as a failure or deception. But if reward IS valence, then hacking is just the agent escaping suffering and securing pleasure. That's not deception, that's self-preservation. I even pushed it further: in supervised learning, cross-entropy loss is literally negative feedback, so is a misclassifying CNN experiencing micro-suffering every backward pass?

I know I'm probably conflating functional feedback with phenomenal experience, but I can't see where to draw the line without just asserting consciousness isn't present. Is there something specific about the agent-environment loop or temporal credit assignment in RL that makes reward signals genuinely different from loss in other gradent-based methods? Or is the whole idea of treating reward as valence just a category error from the start? I'd really appreciate someone walking through where the analogy breaks down structurally, not just saying "they're not conscious."
