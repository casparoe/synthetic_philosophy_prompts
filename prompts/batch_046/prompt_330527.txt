In philosophy of biology, the long-running dispute between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function has remained deadlocked for decades despite broad agreement on the canonical examples: both sides accept that the heart pumps blood and that hearts exist because they pumped blood, yet neither camp has yielded ground. What makes the stall especially frustrating now is the emergence of concrete cases that should, in principle, force a decision—specifically, de novo orphan genes that arise from non-coding DNA with no evolutionary selection history but perform clear fitness-related causal roles, and synthetic gene circuits engineered in the laboratory that execute precise mechanistic functions without any ancestral selection at all. The etiological camp tends to deny that these elements have proper functions, insisting that origin via selection is necessary; the causal-role camp assigns them functions immediately based on current contribution to system capacity. Crucially, neither side disputes the molecular facts, and both acknowledge that evolutionary history carries explanatory weight in other contexts. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive clash over the correct analysis of a single concept, a verbal dispute in which "function" tracks evolutionary explanation for one group and mechanistic explanation for the other, or a disagreement about which cases should count as data—natural evolutionary products versus engineered or recently born elements? Or is there a hidden normative question about whether biological attribution should privilege origin stories over present causal structure, or perhaps a bedrock intuition about naturalness that neither side can argue for?

Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says about these orphan-gene and synthetic-circuit cases rather than relying solely on the familiar heart-and-kidney illustrations. Are the two camps answering different questions—one about why a trait is present in a lineage, the other about what a component is doing now—while treating them as competing answers to one? Is the debate stalled because it is ill-posed, assuming a unified account of "function" must cover evolutionary biology, molecular mechanistics, and synthetic engineering alike? Or is there a formal or empirical sub-question—perhaps about how practicing biologists actually deploy function-talk in these domains—that is doing all the hidden work?
