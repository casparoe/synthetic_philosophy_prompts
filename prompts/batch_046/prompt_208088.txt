I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep seeing in how historians and philosophers talk about nature as a cause, and I'm not sure if there's already a term for it.

Consider how people discuss the 1815 Tambora eruption. You'll often read that the volcano "rewrote European politics" or "forced" governments to deal with crop failures—active verbs, like it was an agent with intentions. But then when you look at how societies recovered—new farming methods, political adjustments—the same writers treat the climate stress as just a passive "background condition," not an actor. The disruption gets credited to nature, the adaptation gets credited to people. Same pattern with epidemics: the Black Death "destroyed" feudal labor structures, but the new contracts and wage shifts are explained by human agency and economic logic.

So there's this asymmetry: non-human forces—volcanos, climate anomalies, pathogens—get selective agency. They act when outcomes are destructive or chaotic, but disappear into context when outcomes are constructive or adaptive. It's not exactly environmental determinism, since no one claims climate always decides history. It's more like a rhetorical rule about when nature gets to be a historical actor.

Does this have an established name? I know about Latour's actants and the usual determinism debates, but those don't capture the negative-vs-positive split I'm describing. If there's no specific concept, what's the closest fit, and where are its edges—does it apply to technology or only "natural" forces, and how is it different from just saying environment matters sometimes?
