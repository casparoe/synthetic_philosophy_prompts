As I research a feature on rational belief under uncertainty, I have become stuck on a conceptual knot within a modified version of the Sleeping Beauty problem. In the familiar setup, Beauty is sedated on Sunday, a fair coin is tossed, and she is awakened on Monday alone if the result is heads, or on Monday and Tuesday—each time with her intervening memories erased—if the result is tails. The debate between halfers and thirders concerns whether her credence in heads upon awakening should remain one-half or become one-third.

The variant that interests me introduces a metaphysical twist. Assume that, on tails, the Tuesday awakening is not experienced by the very same continuous subject who woke on Monday; rather, it is inhabited by a perfect qualitative duplicate—psychologically continuous with Monday-Beauty, but causally disconnected and lacking her prior phenomenal history—who is placed in an indistinguishable situation. From the first-person perspective, this duplicate cannot tell whether she is the original Monday sleeper or the Tuesday replacement.

Please explain the conceptual issue this generates. In particular, clarify why the duplicate's presence undermines the thirder's standard argument from counting observer-moments, and why it also destabilises the halfer's claim that prior probabilities should be updated solely on the evidence of being awake. Address whether resolving the case requires a prior stance on personal identity across branching scenarios, and what this reveals about whether the original debate was genuinely about epistemic updating or was always entangled with metaphysical assumptions about the self.
