Here is an argument I built for a seminar on moral desert and now find suspicious because it proves far too much. (1) Karma, understood as a theory of moral desert, holds that present suffering is deserved on the basis of past moral failures, specifically through the mechanism of prarabdha karma—ripened actions now yielding their fruit—while sanchita karma remains in storage and kriyamana karma is the moral action presently being formed. (2) If suffering is genuinely deserved in a strong retributive sense, then third-party alleviation of that suffering is unjust in the same way that an unauthorized pardon of a guilty prisoner is unjust: it disrupts a proportionate distribution that the agent has earned. (3) There is also a rehabilitative dimension: enduring the fruit of prarabdha karma is said to exhaust the moral debt and purify the agent for future progress. (4) Therefore, intervening not only violates distributive justice but also obstructs the agent’s only available path to karmic purification. (5) By parallel reasoning, conferring unearned benefits upon someone lacking meritorious sanchita karma corrupts the distributive scheme and undermines the incentive structure governing kriyamana action. (6) So if karma is a pure theory of moral desert, then charity, medicine, and social reform are not merely optional but positively wrong. (7) Yet this conclusion is intolerable; we clearly have obligations to relieve suffering regardless of its origin. (8) Hence (1) is false and karma cannot be a theory of moral desert. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) together with the rehabilitative claim in (3) and (4) than accept (6), but I need to know what I am paying. Where does the argument go wrong? Is there an equivocation between retributive and rehabilitative justification, or does the slide from deserved suffering to non-interference depend on treating cosmic law like a state penal system?
