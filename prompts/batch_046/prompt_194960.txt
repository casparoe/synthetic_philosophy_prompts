Consider the following passage from Michel de Montaigne’s essay Of Cannibals (first published in 1580, in Book I of the Essais). In it, Montaigne describes a people who, he reports, live without writing, without magistrates, and without the pursuit of wealth or commerce; their warfare is conducted openly and with a kind of honor, and though they may consume a defeated enemy, they do not torture captives, whereas Europeans—who call them savage—burn enemies alive and tear them apart, often with religious justification. Montaigne then observes that he finds nothing barbarous in these people except that we call them so, since every person calls barbarism whatever is not his own custom; he adds that we seem to have no other standard of truth and reason than the example and opinions of the country in which we live.

How should we understand this comparison? Does Montaigne intend a form of cultural relativism about moral judgment—holding that there is no universal standard by which to condemn cannibalism—or is he using the comparison mainly as a mirror to criticize European cruelty and hypocrisy, while still assuming a shared human nature and a universal reason that both peoples possess? Furthermore, some recent interpreters have proposed that Montaigne’s framing anticipates what is now called ontological pluralism: the idea that different societies do not merely hold different beliefs about one shared reality, but inhabit worlds constituted by different relations and categories, rather than practicing a simple normative relativism within a single natural order. Does the passage actually support this stronger metaphysical reading, or does Montaigne consistently invoke nature and reason as cross-cultural standards?
