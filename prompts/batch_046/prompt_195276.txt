I am taking a course on American philosophical aesthetics, and my professor offered a summary of W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 lecture "Criteria of Negro Art" that struck me as surprisingly severe. According to her gloss, Du Bois holds that all art is necessarily propaganda, but he intends something stronger than the familiar claim that art has political effects: he thinks the aesthetic value of a work is entirely reducible to its efficacy as political messaging, so that a beautiful painting which fails to advance racial justice is defective not merely as politics but as art itself. She added that Du Bois therefore condemns the art-for-art's-sake position not as naive but as actively harmful, and that he extends this into a direct moral duty for Black artists to produce only collective propaganda for racial equality, leaving essentially no legitimate space for private, apolitical, or individually expressive creation. She concluded that, on this reading, Du Bois anticipates later Marxist aesthetics but remains trapped in a tension he never resolves—he demands truth and justice from art, yet implicitly treats beauty as incompatible with propaganda, since pure aesthetic pleasure would distract from the political message.

Something about this feels too one-dimensional, especially since I have encountered passing references to Du Bois caring deeply about beauty and about artists telling the truth of Black life rather than serving a single party line. I am unsure whether my professor has overstated the reduction of aesthetics to politics, whether Du Bois really sees beauty and propaganda as antagonistic, and whether the inference that he suppresses all private artistic expression actually follows from his argument. Could you audit this summary with some care? Please distinguish what accurately captures Du Bois's position in that essay, what counts as a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents his view—particularly regarding how beauty, truth, and propaganda relate in his account. I also want to know whether the conclusion that he allows no room for apolitical art survives once the misrepresentations are corrected. I am not looking for a bibliography or further readings, just a careful assessment of whether I should trust this gloss as a guide to Du Bois's actual claims.
