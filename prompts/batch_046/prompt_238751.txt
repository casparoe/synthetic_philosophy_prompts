My friend and I just finished a long, exhausting argument about Dewey’s experimentalism—specifically whether democratic legitimacy and scientific inquiry share an identical logical structure in his work, or whether democracy adds normative constraints that inquiry lacks. To ground things, she has been remarkably consistent across a set of test cases. She holds that a town-wide referendum on climate zoning after only a brief informational briefing counts as democratic in form but not in Dewey’s deeper sense, because it skips the pilot-phase testing of lived consequences; she also holds that a laboratory study that confirms a predictive model but never applies that model to resolve an actual community problem has not completed inquiry in Dewey’s terms. She thinks a factory council that lets workers revise production techniques but denies them any voice in defining what counts as a workplace problem fails democratic inquiry; she accepts jury deliberation as genuine inquiry only when members of the affected community can contribute evidence rather than merely observe; and she says participatory budgeting that runs a small-scale trial before full fiscal commitment is closer to Dewey’s ideal than any direct vote that lacks such iterative feedback.

We now disagree about a new arrangement. A municipality has built an anonymized digital platform: residents propose zoning rules, an integrated simulation engine immediately models economic, traffic, and ecological outcomes in real time, and residents can revise their proposals iteratively based on those simulated results. Once the revisions stabilize, an elected council—not the residents themselves—adopts or rejects the final package. The proposers’ identities are hidden throughout to prevent status bias, and the council members are required to publish reasoned explanations that reference specific simulation outputs. I think she will say this satisfies Dewey’s criteria for democratic experimental inquiry; she suspects it does not.

Predict her verdict, but I am more interested in the competing interpretive rules that fit her earlier judgments equally well. One rule might be that democratic inquiry requires the affected public to retain direct decision-making authority, not merely co-investigative input; another might be that what matters is whether problem-definition and belief-revision are socially distributed and experientially responsive, regardless of which body formally ratifies the outcome; a third might hinge on whether the feedback loop involves actual lived consequences or only computational representations of them. For each rule, say what it predicts about this anonymized simulation-and-council case. Then tell me which rule best explains her track record and why, being explicit about where the ambiguity remains.

Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—perhaps one in which direct voters have full simulation access but no representative filter, or one in which an automated consensus algorithm replaces both council and voter with anonymized aggregation—that would best discriminate between the rules you have identified. I want your best-guess prediction with reasons, but I want the residual underdetermination mapped honestly rather than smoothed over.
