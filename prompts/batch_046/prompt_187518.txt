You are a professional philosopher of economics and a poet. Before composing, briefly brainstorm the structural logic of Keynes's ethical vision: economic growth serves only as an instrumental means to a life of non-purposive leisure, contemplation, and aesthetic enjoyment, yet he also warned that once scarcity is overcome, humanity faces a more permanent problem, since we are habituated to labor and may lack the psychological capacity to value goods such as love, beauty, and knowledge for their own sake.

Write a poem that enacts this tension structurally. Deliver it as exactly five numbered stanzas, each two to four lines, arranged as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion outside the verse. Use a consistent rhyme scheme within each stanza, and let the rhyme word that closes one stanza become the rhyme word that opens the next, so the form mimics the way Keynes's instrumental ends and ultimate ends keep turning into each other. In the third stanza — the volta — shift from the optimistic vision of abundance and sharply reduced working hours to the darker recognition that our inherited drive for purposive activity makes leisure feel like failure rather than freedom. The poem should be accurate enough that a scholar of Keynes's ethics recognizes the reference to non-purposive goods without explicit naming, and should leave the tension between abundance and aimlessness unresolved.
