I’m a PhD student in information studies, mostly looking at how archival description shapes moral recognition — authority files, faceted classification, the whole mess of who gets to mint a subject heading and what gets buried in general history. I’ve been playing with this idea I call the “metadata veto”: when an event doesn’t recieve a controlled term, it’s not just hidden but structurally un-retrievable in policy contexts, like the absence of the heading becomes a kind of negative ontology.

So here’s what I’m stuck on. Could you restate Raphael Lemkin’s original concept of genocide — especially the parts he included about cultural destruction and the economic/social dimensions, not just the narrow UN version — in the vocabulary of library metadata and authority control? I want you to treat his legal/moral categories as if they were terms in a faceted ontology: who holds the authority to create the heading, which facets get collapsed into a single descriptor, and what happens when you translate “group destruction” into “subject heading assignment.” Then I need the residue report — what survives cleanly, what gets distorted (for instance, does the intentionality clause become a problem of cataloguer intent versus system bias?), and what’s left over that can’t be expressed in metadata vocabulary at all. Is the leftover a failure of information science to capture moral urgency, or is it a sign that Lemkin’s broader definition was always partly rhetorical? I’m not asking for citations, just work with the concepts as they’re generally known — but please don’t reduce it to “it’s just tagging.” I want to know if my hunch that legal categories are basically badly maintained ontologies is right, or if I’m missing the moral specificity that genuinely resists faceting.
