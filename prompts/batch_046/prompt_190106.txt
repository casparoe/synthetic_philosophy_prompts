Two members of my study group got stuck debating rational choice under what philosophers call transformative experience, using a concrete hypothetical to anchor the argument: a one-way neural implant that eliminates existential anxiety but permanently restructures the recipient's aesthetic preferences and emotional range. The first, Ren, argued that this is the precise case where rational decision-making collapses. You cannot evaluate the outcome from your present standpoint because your evaluative standpoint is among the things replaced; any attempt to project yourself into the post-implant state either smuggles in your current preferences—so it is not really the new state—or is pure guesswork. Therefore the only rational response is suspension of judgment, not consent. The second, Jules, replied that rational choice never requires knowing subjective outcomes in advance; it requires only checking whether your current second-order preferences—values about what kind of person you want to become—endorse undergoing the change. If you currently value cognitive openness or suffering-reduction, you can rationally choose even without knowing the first-order preferences you will acquire. Ren objected that this collapses transformative choice into ordinary instrumental reasoning: you are using present values as a lever while ignoring that those very values will be superseded. Jules said values are not discarded but developed—the post-implant self's concerns are a more mature expression of the same underlying commitment. Ren countered that this assumes a narrative continuity that transformative experiences explicitly deny; there is no "more mature version" of the same person, just a different person with different priorities. Jules then said that even with discontinuity, the choice can still be rationalized retrospectively if the post-change self endorses it, and that is all any long-term commitment—marriage, migration, vocation—ever requires. Ren said that shifts the standard from rational choice at the time of decision to rationalized autobiography afterward, which proves her point: before the change, you lack any rational basis to commit.

Go through their exchange point by point and adjudicate who is right about each specific claim, what important distinction both are overlooking, and whether a consistent middle position exists that neither considered.
