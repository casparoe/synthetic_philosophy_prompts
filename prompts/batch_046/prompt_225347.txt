Having spent three decades measuring symmetries in condensed-matter systems before retiring into philosophy, I have become absorbed by Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles and by Max Black’s well-known counterexample: two perfect spheres, qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect, suspended in an otherwise empty universe and distinguished—if at all—only by their spatial separation. Lately I have been struck by a variant that seems to grow out of my old physics instincts rather than out of standard exegesis: if one adopts a strict relationist ontology of space, denying any absolute background manifold or container, then in a universe that contains nothing but these two spheres there may be no substantive relational fact of separation at all, since spatial relations appear to require something further to relate the spheres to. On this view Black’s scenario does not merely present two indiscernible objects; it risks dissolving the very condition that was meant to save them from being one and the same thing.

With that framing in mind, I would ask you to compose a joke—precise, conceptually sharp, and genuinely amusing to someone who knows the metaphysical literature—whose punchline turns specifically on the interaction between the identity of indiscernibles and this relationist collapse of Black’s spheres. The humor should arise not from generic nerd wordplay but from the philosophical tension itself: that the spheres are saved from Leibniz’s principle only by a spatial distinction that the relationist refuses to grant ontological weight, leaving us with either one sphere or none, or perhaps with a metaphysician counting to two in an empty room. Please maintain a formal yet playful tone suitable for a seminar discussion, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts and page numbers; I intend this for my own reflection and for colleagues who can follow the move without bibliographic assistance.
