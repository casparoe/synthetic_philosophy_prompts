I've been thinking about Wittgenstein's idea that religious belief isn't like accepting a scientific claim—it's more like living inside a guiding picture that shapes what you find important and how you act, closer to trusting a person than to agreeing with a report. To see whether I grasp this, I looked at how exemption boards in hospitals and courts actually work: they grant accommodations specifically for "religious belief," but they evaluate it by checking membership in an established tradition and consistency with its doctrines, and they usually deny the same standing to secular ethical commitments that govern a person's life just as completely. What view of belief does that institutional practice presuppose? Does it treat religious belief as essentially social membership, as a private mental state, or as a kind of preference? Is it compatible with the idea that religious belief is a form of life rather than a hypothesis, or does the board's method necessarily reduce it back to a claim about the world? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly needs it or is compatible with several views, and tell me what an evaluation process built on Wittgenstein's distinction would have to change.
