I am a student in an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and we have been studying the Gould-Lewontin critique of the adaptationist program, particularly the notion of evolutionary spandrels—structural byproducts that arise for reasons unrelated to selection and are only later co-opted for function. For a creative assignment, I would like you to write a short story that uses these concepts structurally rather than as mere decoration.

The narrative should center on a synthetic-biology engineer in a near-future research colony who designs modular organisms for high-radiation environments. She introduces a rigid internal lattice solely as a manufacturing scaffold—an engineering spandrel with no anticipated biological role. Over generations, a neutral mutation allows the lattice to stabilize an otherwise toxic intermediate in a novel metabolic pathway. The colony's scientists debate whether the lattice should now be classified as an adaptation, an exaptation, or still fundamentally a spandrel that has been retrospectively assigned function, especially in view of recent arguments—sometimes discussed under the heading of constructive neutral evolution—that neutral origins can produce indispensable complexity and thereby blur the boundary between byproduct and design.

Please write the story so that it does not resolve this ambiguity with a definitive answer, and please avoid treating any one evolutionary interpretation as the consensus view. After the story, provide a brief author's note that separates well-established biological ideas—such as the reality of spandrels and exaptation—from genuinely contested claims, including the prevalence of neutral pathways that become essential and the extent to which recognizing spandrels undermines adaptationist explanations. In that same note, clearly flag where the narrative takes liberties with empirical science—for instance, by projecting intentional design onto evolutionary dynamics or by positing synthetic organisms and metabolic pathways that do not currently exist—so that the conceptual exploration is not mistaken for factual reporting.
