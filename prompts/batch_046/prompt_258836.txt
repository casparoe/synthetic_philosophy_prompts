I serve on the peer-review board of an interdisciplinary journal—something I took on after years of editing in philosophy of science—and I have become increasingly concerned by a recurring class of submission. These papers invoke the physics of information, specifically analogies drawn from Maxwell's demon, Szilard's information-to-work argument, or Landauer's principle regarding the thermodynamic cost of erasure, to claim practical energy or computing efficiencies. In a recent editorial meeting, my colleagues and I realized we had no shared standard for distinguishing a physically coherent proposal from a conceptual confusion: some authors conflate logical reversibility with physical reversibility, others treat software-level data deletion as thermodynamically free because no mechanical motion is visible, and a minority apply the kT ln 2 bound correctly but then extend it illegitimately to macroscopic systems in which correlation-maintenance costs dominate the accounting. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist of conceptual tests, with explicit stop-points and judgment-call flags—that a panelist without specialized statistical-mechanics training can apply in roughly thirty minutes. The procedure must preserve the actual physical distinction between a demon's correlational knowledge and the work required to reset that knowledge, must specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, and must clearly mark where the evaluation must be handed off to a specialist rather than resolved by the checklist. It should also be honest about its own failure modes: the cases where it will misfire by being either too permissive or too restrictive.

Please apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals. (A) A data-center architecture that claims net cooling savings by using predictive algorithms to erase unrequested data packets before they reach physical storage, arguing that avoiding physical write-operations eliminates Landauer costs. (B) A laboratory heat-engine design that uses feedback-controlled gates to sort particles and extract work, providing a detailed energy budget for the measurement stage but omitting any accounting for the memory-state reset. (C) A theoretical framework asserting that because information is physical, any reduction in algorithmic information complexity necessarily lowers the thermodynamic entropy of the computing substrate, and therefore that optimized code inherently reduces cooling loads. One of these should trigger an explicit "halt—requires expert physics assessment" flag, and one should yield a negative verdict that the submitter will find counterintuitive despite the procedure's internal consistency. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate on its own.
