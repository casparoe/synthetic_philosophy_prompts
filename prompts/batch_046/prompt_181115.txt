After a long and exhausting argument with a friend about whether rights-based ethics can survive catastrophic trade-offs without silently collapsing into consequentialism, I want an independent assessment of how I graded an exam answer on this topic. The exam question I assigned was as follows: Some philosophers claim that deontology faces a structural paradox in catastrophic cases—if an agent is absolutely prohibited from directly violating one person's autonomy (for example, by instrumentalizing them to synthesize a vaccine), then in a pandemic scenario where inaction allows thousands to die, with each death itself constituting an autonomy-violating harm inflicted by the pathogen, the agent appears committed to allowing far more autonomy-violations than they prevent. Explain why this is considered a paradox for rights-based ethics rather than merely a tragic dilemma, and then evaluate the proposed resolution that distinguishes direct agency from omission, according to which the constraint applies only to actions that directly instrumentalize a person and not to failures to intervene that allow harm to unfold through other causal pathways. In your evaluation, address whether this distinction genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely relocates the moral burden onto the causal structure of the situation.

The student submitted the following answer: The paradox arises because if rights function as absolute side-constraints, then an agent must never violate one person's rights even to save many others. In the pandemic case, if the authority refuses to use one individual to produce a cure, thousands die, and each death involves an autonomy violation caused by the virus. Consequently, the authority permits thousands of autonomy-violations to avoid one direct violation. This is paradoxical for deontology because its purpose is to protect rights, yet here it protects them so poorly that it allows vastly more violations than it prevents. The proposed resolution distinguishes direct agency from omission. Direct actions that instrumentalize a person violate perfect duties, which are negative and unconditional, whereas omissions fall under imperfect duties of beneficence, which allow moral latitude. Therefore, the authority does not violate a perfect duty by failing to produce the cure, and the paradox dissolves because the constraint never applied to the omission in the first place. This mirrors the structure found in Kant's Groundwork, where perfect duties govern only direct maxims of interference with autonomy. Nevertheless, the resolution is incomplete. It relocates the moral burden onto the causal structure: the authority is not causing the deaths, the virus is. Because the authority can foresee the outcome, however, the burden is not eliminated but only moved from the agent's will to the external causal chain. Thus the paradox is resolved for the agent's moral status, though it remains present in the overall situation.
