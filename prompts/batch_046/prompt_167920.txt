I've been thinking about whether people can really bind their future selves the way Ulysses tied himself to the mast—except with laws and institutions rather than ropes. I want you to construct a specific thought experiment that tests whether a democratic community can write a rule today that genuinely constrains it tomorrow, not just on paper.

Here is the case I want built. A small town council, facing recurring drought cycles, passes a constitutional-style amendment now: if reservoir levels fall below a set threshold, an independent Water Allocation Board takes over distribution. The board's five members serve ten-year staggered terms, appointed by the council that created the amendment but not removable by future councils. Crucially, the board's emergency powers activate by simple majority vote of its members, but can only be suspended by a two-thirds vote of the current town council—an asymmetric lock designed to make it easier to start rationing than to stop it.

Engineer the scenario—keep it to one realistic paragraph, with no futuristic technology, no perfect predictors, and stakes that ordinary residents could actually face—so that three specific analyses apply. First, under a "hard precommitment" analysis, the contract succeeds: when the drought hits and politicians want to divert water to favored districts, the board invokes its powers and the council cannot muster the two-thirds vote to suspend them, so the original plan holds. Second, under a "flexible adaptation" analysis, the contract fails harmfully: the drought is worse than anyone modeled, the board's rigid formula prevents a necessary, life-saving redistribution to a neighboring district, and people suffer precisely because the rule is too brittle. Third, under what I'll call a "continuity of authority" analysis—the idea that a binding rule only works if those bound still recognize the legitimacy of those who made it—the contract fails for a different reason entirely. The board members, appointed a decade ago by a council with different priorities, are viewed by current residents not as neutral guardians but as unaccountable outsiders. The town refuses to cooperate with the board's orders not out of weakness of will, but because the staggered appointment structure broke the chain of democratic authorization.

After presenting the scenario, walk through each analysis and explain exactly why it delivers its verdict. Then answer three specific questions. What structural feature of the case—something about how the board is constituted—makes the third analysis split away from both the first and the second? If I removed that feature and made the board members directly elected every two years, would the case collapse back into an ordinary weakness-of-will problem rather than a legitimacy problem? Finally, how would a defender of the "flexible adaptation" analysis attack this scenario—what is the weak point they would seize on, and does that attack also undermine the first analysis?

Please do not cite philosophers, books, or articles by name; just reason from the structure of the case itself.
