I am trying to articulate my underlying commitments about occasionalism—the view, classically associated with al-Ghazālī, that God is the sole efficient cause and that observed regularities are sustained only by divine habit—by testing them against concrete explanatory scenarios. Here are my verdicts; for each, I note whether occasionalism feels adequate, strained, or clearly wrong.

Case 1: Direct combustion. A flame touches cotton, and the cotton burns. Verdict: occasionalism is compelling. There is no observable mechanism by which fire transmits causal power to cotton; attributing the burning directly to divine action on the occasion of contact feels correct.

Case 2: Medical intervention. A physician administers a specific compound, and in the vast majority of observed instances the patient’s symptoms resolve. Verdict: occasionalism is still acceptable—God creates the cure on the occasion of the compound—but I feel a stronger pull toward treating the compound’s biochemical properties as genuinely explanatory, since the correlation is mediated by pathways the physician can manipulate and predict.

Case 3: Artifactual persistence. A blacksmith forges iron into an arch, and the arch supports weight for centuries without continuous intervention. Verdict: occasionalism feels strained. To say that God re-creates the arch’s structural integrity at every instant seems to miss something that the geometric and material properties of the arch itself explain.

Case 4: Logical derivation. From a set of axioms, a mathematician derives a theorem, and anyone with sufficient understanding recognizes that the conclusion could not be false if the axioms hold. Verdict: occasionalism is inappropriate. The connection is not an empirical correlation maintained by habit, but an internal, necessary relation.

Case 5: Cross-cultural predictive success. Astronomers in two civilizations with no contact observe the same orbital regularity and independently construct accurate calendars. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. Occasionalism explains the uniformity easily—God applies the same habit everywhere—but the independence of discovery also makes it seem as though there is a stable natural structure that explains both observations, not merely parallel divine actions.

Case 6: Mental-physical correlation. A person forms the intention to raise an arm, and shortly thereafter the arm rises. Verdict: occasionalism treats the intention and the movement as two distinct events that God creates together, yet this feels different from Case 1. The correlation is not between two external bodies but within a single agent, and it seems to involve a kind of direct authorship that occasionalism cannot capture without reducing the agent to a passive occasion.

My judgments form a pattern: I reject occasionalism when explanation involves structural mediation, logical necessity, or internal authorship, but accept or tolerate it for direct empirical correlations. Yet Case 5 introduces an unresolved tension—universal regularity should either strengthen or weaken the occasionalist account, and I cannot decide which. Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing “habituated correlation” from “structurally mediated or rationally transparent connection”—that captures verdicts 1 through 4 and 6 while resolving the ambiguity in 5?
