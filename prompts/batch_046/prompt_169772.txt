You can technically decline but then the system penalizes you in future allocations, and management can tweak the weights whenever they want without telling anyone. Jordan said this is a classic loss of negative liberty: the workers are constantly subject to potential intereference with their lives, so they're less free even if they never actually say yes to a shift. Casey said that's not right -- negative freedom is about actual obstacles, not just the threat of them, and since every worker can still refuse there's no direct interference going on. Casey pushed back that republican freedom is satisfied here because the algorithm runs on fixed, transparent penalty rules -- it's rule-governed power, not the arbitrary will of some boss, just like paying taxes under a legal code. Jordan came back that this misses the meta-level problem: management can rewrite the algorithm's rules anytime with no worker input, so the power over the rules themselves is still arbitrary, which means domination survives one level up. Casey said if you go that route then literally every modern workplace and state becomes dominating, which just makes republican liberty useless as a critical tool.
