I have been following a long-running dispute in the philosophy of social norms that has reached an impasse I find genuinely puzzling. On one side are philosophers who apply David Lewis's framework of convention—built on common knowledge of a behavioral regularity, mutual expectations, preference for conformity, and the existence of arbitrary but viable alternatives—to explain the rapid stabilization of emerging linguistic and ethical norms, such as the widespread adoption of singular they in formal registers or revised protocols of consent in institutional settings. They maintain that once a critical mass conforms, the coordination benefits of predictability and social fluency sustain the practice entirely, with no need for an independent normative residue; deviation is irrational only because it frustrates mutual expectation, not because it violates an authority-independent obligation. On the other side are theorists who grant that these patterns satisfy Lewis's formal conditions yet insist that something essential remains unexplained: participants treat the norms as binding even in local contexts where deviation would cause no coordination failure and where the alternative is not genuinely arbitrary, because the norm carries what I will call a status-asymmetry—one party's preference to conform is conditional on recognizing the other party's authority to set or ratify the standard. For years the two camps have cited the same empirical studies of adoption curves, agreed on Lewis's definitional criteria, and failed to move each other, yet they do not appear to disagree about any observable sociological fact.

My own hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about whether these norms count as conventions in Lewis's sense, but about a hidden structural feature I want to call threshold-fragility. In a canonical Lewisian convention, the coordination payoff is continuous: small deviations diminish utility gradually but do not destroy the equilibrium. However, norms that involve recognition of authority or collective identity may sit near a discontinuity in the payoff landscape, where falling below a certain adoption threshold causes the coordination benefit to collapse to zero—not gradually, but suddenly—because the regularity is no longer recognized as a legitimate precedent. If so, one side may be describing an equilibrium-stable convention that is genuinely self-sustaining through mutual expectation alone, while the other side is describing a threshold-fragile practice that can persist near the boundary only if participants supply an independent normative reason to conform. Is this the right diagnosis? Are the sides actually disagreeing about whether the payoff structure is continuous or discontinuous, about whether Lewis's notion of precedent requires continuous reinforcement to survive near collapse, or about whether they are answering different questions—one asking how the norm is maintained at equilibrium, the other asking how it survives when equilibrium is locally unstable? Or is the dispute ultimately about bedrock intuitions regarding whether authority-based norms can ever be purely coordinative, making progress impossible without abandoning the shared framework?
