I am preparing an essay on whether the French Revolution's familiar pattern of revolutionary factions purging successive layers of their own leadership—often summarized as revolutions devouring their children—should be interpreted primarily through class struggle, ideological continuity, or historical contingency. Rather than defend one of the standard historiographical positions, I want to test a narrower mechanism that I am tentatively calling representational cascade failure.

My working notes are as follows. First, the three familiar frameworks—social-structural accounts that tie the Terror to bourgeois or popular-class consolidation; ideological accounts that trace the violence to the Revolution's adoption of a unitary conception of popular sovereignty drawn from the political culture of the Old Regime; and revisionist contingency accounts that treat 1793-94 as an emergency response to war and insurrection—each explain why violence occurred, but they do not adequately explain its specific internal topology: why the purges targeted not outsiders or class enemies but successive waves of the revolutionary leadership itself, often individuals who shared the same social origin, the same republican vocabulary, and the same immediate strategic situation.

Second, I propose that the key lies in the Revolution's institutionalization of what I term a unanimity constraint on sovereignty. Once the National Convention and its committees claimed to embody the general will immediately rather than represent it through delegated pluralism, any visible factional division was structurally interpreted not as legitimate disagreement but as corruption of the collective body—either as hidden aristocratic infection or as foreign interference. Under this constraint, the exclusion of one faction does not restore pluralism; it raises the evidential bar for the remaining members, because prior association with the purged group becomes retrospective proof of contamination. This produces a cascade: each purge generates the conditions for the next, not because of changing material interests, shifting ideology, or new contingent shocks, but because the representational logic itself eliminates the possibility of loyal opposition.

Third, I want to argue that this mechanism is not reducible to the three standard interpretations. It is not a class-struggle phenomenon because the victims and executioners belong to the same social stratum. It is not purely ideological in the conventional sense because the violence is not driven by doctrinal disagreement about the content of republicanism—most victims affirmed the same principles—but by the structural impossibility of expressing dissent within a sovereignty model that denies representation. And it is not merely contingent, because the cascade exhibits a reproducible organizational pattern that reappears in other revolutionary committees operating under similar unanimity assumptions, even when external emergency conditions differ.

Fourth, I am aware that this framing risks anachronism. I am borrowing language—representational cascade, unanimity constraint, contamination logic—that sounds like modern distributed-systems or epidemiological modeling, and I want to avoid smuggling in conceptual machinery that would have been unintelligible to the actors. At the same time, I want to claim that the underlying dynamics—recursive suspicion, the conflation of procedural dissent with substantive betrayal, the organizational need for periodic purification—are historically visible in the records of the Committee of Public Safety and the revolutionary tribunals, even if the vocabulary is mine.

Given these notes, I would welcome comments on the following points. Is the proposed mechanism genuinely distinct from the three standard interpretations, or does it collapse into one of them—perhaps a variant of the ideological account—once the vocabulary is stripped away? Does the introduction of systems-theoretic language obscure the specific political culture of the Revolution, or can it be defended as a neutral descriptive tool? Are there well-known counter-instances within 1792-1794—such as the fall of the Girondins or the Thermidorian reaction—that break the cascade model, and if so, do they require auxiliary conditions or do they show that the model is overfitted? Finally, is the essay's framing likely to be interesting to readers familiar with revolutionary historiography, or does it mainly repackage familiar observations about factional suspicion in unfamiliar jargon?

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in conceptual clarity and internal coherence rather than source verification.
