I am preparing material for an undergraduate seminar on philosophy of personal identity, and I want to avoid the standard classroom version of the Ship of Theseus. Rather than asking about a wooden ship at sea, I would like you to read Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein through that lens, but with a specific conceptual twist: treat the creature not primarily as a biological organism that simply develops, but as an assembled artifact that is gradually rebuilt, repaired, and socially reconstructed over the narrative. Begin with Victor’s original assembly of cadaver parts, then trace the changes: the creature learns language and sews clothing for himself, suffers a gunshot wound during the De Lacey cottage episode, sets fire to the cottage upon rejection, and eventually plans to construct a female companion from entirely new parts. At each stage, physical and experiential components have been replaced or added. Does Shelley present the creature’s persistence through these alterations as analogous to the ship’s persistence—perhaps as a functional or structural continuity despite material turnover—or does she insist that living beings possess different identity conditions, such as consciousness, memory, or social recognition, that artifacts lack? Point to specific scenes where his sense of self seems continuous despite these replacements, and to scenes where he feels he has become something other than what Victor made. I also want you to say honestly where this reading strains the text: where does the novel resist being reduced to an illustration of mereological replacement, and what does the work contribute to debates about diachronic identity that pure philosophical argument cannot capture?
