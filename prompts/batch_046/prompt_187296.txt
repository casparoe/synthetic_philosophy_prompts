I am taking a course on the ethics of belief, and I want to apply the James–Clifford debate to an institutional pattern I have noticed. Large social-media platforms and news aggregators routinely suppress or label empirical claims—about public health, electoral procedures, climate events—when those claims lack verification by recognized authorities, framing the intervention as protection against belief formed on insufficient evidence. At the same time, they apply almost no comparable scrutiny to personal testimony, spiritual conviction, or aesthetic judgment shared without evidential backing, treating belief-formation in those registers as outside institutional jurisdiction. What implicit theory of belief does that asymmetry presuppose? Does the practice commit its architects to a domain-specific evidentialism closer to Clifford for empirical matters, paired with a James-style exemption for passional or personal domains? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether it is coherent, and say what a moderation framework consistently built on James's allowance for belief beyond the evidence—applied even to high-stakes empirical questions—would have to change about current design.
