Write a deadpan parody of the submission guidelines and peer-review rubric for a fictitious academic journal—call it The Annals of Unmediated Presentism—formatted as a numbered bureaucratic document of about 600 words. The voice should be that of an earnest university-press style sheet, complete with sections on “Ethical Vetting of Primary Sources,” “Mandatory Normative Translation,” and “Reader Safety Protocols.” The satire should target the historiographical position that historians cannot, and should not, escape the moral standards of their own time, treating present-day judgment not as an anachronistic risk but as a core scholarly obligation.

To keep the piece conceptually precise rather than merely topical, ground it in the real methodological tension between actor’s categories and analyst’s categories: require that every manuscript translate historical agents’ self-descriptions into contemporary normative vocabulary, and penalize authors who “reproduce obsolete self-understandings” without sufficient moral framing. Invent, within the document, a fictional “Tri-Axial Compatibility Index” that scores historical figures on Intersectional Awareness, Carbon Consciousness, and Neurotypical Normativity; apply it rigorously to a medieval guild master, a Roman patron, and an Enlightenment political economist, not to mock those figures but to show the absurdity of measuring them by metrics they could not have possessed. Include a sidebar titled “Frequently Asked Objections” that answers, in deadpan legalese, the standard historicist complaints about anachronism, lost context, and the erasure of historical agency, treating each objection as a failure of the author’s moral seriousness rather than as a methodological concern.

The humor should come from exaggerating actual features of presentist practice—such as the collapse of historical explanation into moral evaluation—so that a reader familiar with the debate over whether judgment belongs in historiography recognizes the satire as fair. At the same time, it should remain readable to someone who knows only that historians usually try to understand the past on its own terms. Do not cite real scholars by name, do not include a bibliography, and do not ask for quotations from actual texts; instead, paraphrase standard objections and invent institutional language. By the end, a reader should feel the real conceptual problem: if every past society is judged solely by present standards, historiography collapses into a uniform moral report card, and the past becomes unreadable except as a catalogue of failures.
