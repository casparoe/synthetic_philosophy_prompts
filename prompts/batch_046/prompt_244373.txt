I have recently been sorting through the unfinished manuscript on metaphysics left by my late doctoral supervisor, who was preparing a study of Bradley's argument that relations generate an infinite regress and therefore cannot be ultimately real. In trying to frame the introduction, I found myself stuck on exactly the point he never resolved, and at a dinner last week two philosopher friends—both with analytic training and metaphysical sympathies, one working in philosophy of language and the other in ontology—fell into an extended argument over it. I am reporting their exchange below exactly as I heard it, without taking sides; please adjudicate it move by move.

(1) The philosopher of language argued that Bradley's regress dissolves once we distinguish logical form from ontological composition. In a proposition such as "Socrates is wise," the concept expressed by "wise" is unsaturated: its very nature is to combine with an argument, so no external "glue" is required to unify subject and predicate. The unity of the proposition is internal to its logical structure, not an additional entity that itself needs relating.

(2) The metaphysician replied that this merely relocates the problem rather than solving it. If unsaturatedness is a real feature of the concept, then we still need an account of how that feature belongs to the concept—seemingly a relation between the concept and its mode of being. More importantly, in the world itself, if Socrates actually stands in the relation of being wise, something must make it the case that he does; to call relations "fundamental" and stop asking is not to answer Bradley's challenge but to abandon explanatory responsibility.

(3) She countered that unsaturatedness is not an extra property requiring a connecting relation, but a mode of being—comparable to asking what binds the shape of a key to its capacity to open a lock, where the fit is constitutive rather than externally mediated. She then pressed the case of negative existential propositions, such as "the round square does not exist." On a Russellian analysis, the descriptive phrase fails to denote, so there are no relata requiring unification in the first place; the proposition's unity is syntactic, and its truth consists in the absence of any corresponding structure in the world.

(4) He responded that this collapses into a form of linguistic conventionalism. If the unity of "Socrates is wise" is only logical or syntactic, then the objective truth of the proposition depends on our symbolic conventions rather than on how things stand in reality. He proposed instead that only a substratum theory escapes the regress: a bare particular instantiates properties without relational mediation, because the particular is not one relatum among others but the ultimate subject upon which properties depend asymmetrically. Once this asymmetry is granted, the chain of connecting relations stops.

(5) She objected that bare particulars are incoherent. If a particular is truly bare—devoid of all properties—then it is featureless and indistinguishable from other bare particulars, and therefore unfit to serve as the subject of specific properties. If, on the other hand, it possesses properties, then we need an account of how those properties belong to it, which revives exactly the relational problem Bradley identified. She suggested that structural realism offers a better path: propositions map onto worldly structures, and the correspondence is not a further entity but is vindicated by the explanatory success of the theory that employs it.

(6) He pressed that "correspondence" is itself a relation, generating a meta-regress: what relates the proposition to the worldly structure? He concluded that any relational account—whether logical, syntactic, or structural—fails Bradley's core challenge, leaving only two consistent options: Bradley's own monistic absolute, in which all relational appearance is transcended in a non-relational whole, or a radical rejection of relational thought altogether.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. State which party is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether the dispute over negative existentials actually advances the argument about propositional unity, and whether either side has adequately addressed the possibility that Bradley's regress applies differently to logical structure than it does to ontological composition.
