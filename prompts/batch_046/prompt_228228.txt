I have been working through Nelson Goodman's problem of induction on my own, and I have hit a conceptual knot that the usual introductions do not seem to address. Goodman introduces the predicate grue—green before some future time and blue afterward—to show that induction depends on which predicates we treat as projectible. The standard response is that green tracks a natural kind or a stable causal structure, while grue is gerrymandered and does not. I understand that reply, but I want to press on whether it solves the problem or merely restates it.

To make the difficulty concrete, I have been thinking about a hypothetical property I will call flume. An object is flume if it possesses an internal chemical structure that emits a specific wavelength, but that wavelength is masked by atmospheric conditions before the year 2100, so observers see green; after 2100 the masking conditions disappear and observers see blue. So flume is defined by reference to a future temporal boundary, yet it is grounded in a present physical state that causally explains both appearances. It seems ontologically on a par with green: there is a real underlying mechanism.

Here is my question. If flume is physically real and causally unified, why should induction not support flume-predictions about objects before 2100? Is the exclusion of flume (and grue) driven by something in the world—perhaps that temporal boundaries are not part of causal structures—or by something in our language and reasoning practices? More specifically, is there a non-circular way to mark the boundary between projectible and gerrymandered predicates that does not already presuppose that induction succeeds for the properties we happen to prefer?

Please explain the conceptual issue: whether the asymmetry between green and grue (or green and flume) is ultimately ontological, linguistic, or methodological, and whether my flume case shows that the distinction is sharper in principle than it is in practice. I am not looking for a historical survey; I want to understand where the philosophical work is actually being done.
