I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on philosophical methodology and the epistemology of thought experiments, and I need to design a runnable session for about ten fellow students over seventy-five minutes. I want the group to experience—before anyone names the phenomenon—how intuition pumps generate conviction through what I will call "narrative completion pressure": the feeling that a scenario must resolve in a particular way because its framing has quietly eliminated the alternatives that would block that resolution, leaving the mind to "complete" the story along the only path left visible.

Please design a structured classroom game with explicit rules, roles, and a timeline. In the first stage, students work in pairs to construct an original brief scenario—avoiding standard examples like the Chinese Room or the Trolley Problem—designed to make one philosophical conclusion feel inevitable, relying only on framing, sequencing, and selective detail rather than explicit argument. In the second stage, pairs exchange scenarios with another pair who have received an inverted framing of the same underlying logical structure but with normative labels and affective cues swapped; each pair must defend the conclusion that their received scenario pushes toward, using only the reasoning available inside that framing. The reveal comes only after both defenses are delivered: the facilitator displays the structural isomorphism and the framing inversion, then asks each pair to identify which details in their received version made resistance feel irrational.

Please include: a minute-by-minute timeline specifying facilitator moves and pair roles; the exact rule or payoff structure that creates pressure to defend the received conclusion rather than question the frame; the mechanism of the reveal; a debrief that maps the construction stage onto pump design, the defense stage onto being pumped, and the reveal stage onto framing dependence; an honest prediction of what will predictably misfire (for instance, one pair spotting the inversion early and breaking the effect, or defensive rationalization persisting despite the reveal); and a frank admission of which feature of narrative-completion pressure—most likely its unconscious, affective component—the staged exercise can reproduce only weakly and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator's commentary.

Finally, please state how confident you are that this two-stage reversal actually produces targeted epistemic discomfort rather than mere confusion, and single out the specific design choice—perhaps the rule governing what counts as an acceptable "inverted framing"—about which you are least certain.
