As a retired evolutionary biologist who has only recently turned to philosophy of law and moral taxonomy, I am trying to understand how thinkers actually divide over genocide—not merely the dictionary entry, but the underlying logical space of commitments about what makes an atrocity belong to this category, how we ought to name it, and whether the politics of labeling corrupts or completes the moral judgment. I want a diagnostic classification instrument, not a literature survey.

Please construct a decision tree (or equivalent flowchart taxonomy) that sorts positions on genocide as a legal and moral category and on the politics of naming atrocities. Build it from the logical combinations of answers to concrete cases and conceptual commitments, then map recognizable positions onto the leaves. Do not organize it as a list of scholar names.

The tree must branch on at least these four dimensions, each posed as a concrete diagnostic question answerable without knowing philosophical labels:
1. The definitional core: Does genocide require specific intent to destroy a protected group (the Lemkinian criterion), or is it defined by the scale and systematic nature of group destruction regardless of explicit intent?
2. The continuum question: Is genocide a morally and legally sui generis kind, discontinuous with crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing, or is it an extreme point on a continuous spectrum of mass violence?
3. The naming act: When an international body or state applies the term "genocide," is it primarily describing an objective state of affairs, or is it performing a political and legal intervention that changes the obligations of other actors?
4. Retroactive labeling: Can an atrocity that occurred before the 1948 Convention (or before a particular domestic statute) legitimately be named genocide retroactively, or does nullum crimen sine lege forbid applying the legal category backward, even if the moral judgment is clear?

For each leaf in your tree, specify: the path of answers that reaches it; a brief description of the position in plain language; whether that logical space is occupied by a recognizable view (for example, the UN Convention framework, expanded sociological accounts, legal formalism, political instrumentalism about naming); and if the space is unoccupied, a brief diagnosis—is the combination incoherent, morally unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Also identify any pair of positions that this tree cannot separate, explaining the shared commitment that causes them to coincide on all four diagnostics.

Additionally, apply one framing concept from biological taxonomy—specifically, the distinction between phenetic grouping (by overall similarity) and cladistic grouping (by shared derived characteristics, or synapomorphies)—to one branch of your tree. Explain whether classifying atrocities should proceed by overall resemblance to a paradigm case of genocide, or by the presence of a specific defining origin or mechanism, and note which positions fall on each side.

Present the result as structured data: a JSON object with exactly these fields:
- "root_question": string, the initial dividing commitment;
- "branches": array of objects, each with "node_id" (string), "diagnostic_question" (string), "options" (array of strings), and "child_nodes" (array of strings, either other node_ids or leaf_ids);
- "leaves": array of objects, each with "leaf_id" (string), "answer_path" (array of strings), "position_description" (string), "occupied_by" (string or null), "empty_diagnosis" (string or null);
- "inseparable_pairs": array of objects, each with "position_a" (string), "position_b" (string), "reason" (string);
- "taxonomy_framing": object with "biological_analogy" (string) and "application" (string);
- "unoccupied_spaces": array of objects, each with "coordinates" (array of strings) and "diagnosis" (string).

Keep the language precise but accessible to a non-specialist. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
