Consider the institutional practice of "deep-time" public investment: governments and foundations commit substantial resources to projects—geological nuclear-waste markers, millennial seed vaults, orbital archives—designed to benefit or warn humans thousands of years hence, and they justify these expenditures by saying that allowing civilization's achievements and warnings to vanish would be a profound loss, not merely a missed welfare opportunity. At the same time, these bodies reject parallel obligations to present distant strangers—devoting far less comparable effort to global poverty reduction—and treat the value of these long-horizon projects as non-fungible with immediate welfare gains. What theory of value, or of the relation between present lives and future ones, does that pattern presuppose? Must it invoke Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—the idea that human lives derive meaning from what survives them—or can an impersonal future-regarding ethic, a theory of symbolic cultural continuity, or a tractability-based rationale explain the asymmetry? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and tell me what such policies would have to change if they replaced that justification with a straightforward intergenerational welfare calculus.
