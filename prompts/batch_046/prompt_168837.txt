I have recently begun a clinical ethics fellowship in a large teaching hospital, and part of my orientation requires me to anticipate how our senior ethics consultant, Dr. Elena Vasquez, applies the idea of causation-by-omission in adverse-event reviews. She never cites philosophers explicitly, but over the past year she has rendered a consistent set of verdicts that I am trying to map onto an underlying principle before I assist with next week’s docket. The problem is that her track record seems compatible with more than one rule, and the case I need to prepare for is constructed so that those rules split.

Here are her rulings. In Case One, an ICU nurse noticed a slowly falling oxygen saturation, interpreted it as motion artifact, and did not escalate; the patient arrested, and Vasquez held that the nurse’s omission was a contributing cause. In Case Two, an adult child failed to relay a family allergy history during intake, the patient received the drug and reacted mildly, and Vasquez said the omission was not causally responsible because no institutional caregiving duty existed. In Case Three, a hospital board declined to fund a redundant backup generator that exceeded code requirements; during a blackout life support failed, and Vasquez ruled the board’s omission was not causal. In Case Four, an attending surgeon declined an unvalidated experimental procedure and the patient died of the underlying disease; Vasquez said this omission was not causal. In Case Five, a pharmacist dismissed an interaction flag because the software was known to generate frequent false positives, the interaction occurred, and Vasquez ruled the pharmacist’s omission was causal.

Next week’s case involves a clinical AI monitoring system that was kept at its factory-default alert threshold. The hospital’s clinical governance committee had available, but chose not to adopt and validate, a more sensitive setting that would have weighted a specific medication interaction differently. The system is configured to alert only when two parameters cross thresholds simultaneously; during the event only one parameter crossed, though the second was masked by the interaction. The attending physician, seeing no alert and not being required by policy to perform an unscheduled manual review—though permitted to do so—did not assess the patient. Preventable harm followed. I need you to predict whether Vasquez will treat the physician’s failure to review, the committee’s failure to adopt the sensitive configuration, both, or neither as causally responsible; identify which prior case is the closest analog; state whether her record genuinely underdetermines the answer; and, if it does, specify what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, please describe the further hypothetical case that would actually distinguish the competing rules her past rulings support.
