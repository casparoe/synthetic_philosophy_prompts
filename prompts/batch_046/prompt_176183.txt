Consider the record of judgments Jorge Luis Borges makes, across his essays and fictions, about competing architectures of time. First, regarding strict linear, irreversible chronology—the framework of ordinary history and narrative—he treats it as a pervasive illusion sustained by language and habit, philosophically naive yet practically unavoidable. Second, regarding strict cyclical repetition in which every epoch returns identically but without any retained memory across cycles, he grants it logical coherence yet judges it existentially empty: lacking recollection, repetition collapses back into an undifferentiated linear progression. Third, regarding a branching structure in which every decision spawns permanently divergent timelines that never reconverge, he presents it as a captivating narrative image, but suggests that if all branches are equally actual, then regret, choice, and personal identity lose their weight, dissolving agency into static multiplicity. Fourth, regarding the complete abolition of time in favor of a timeless simultaneity—drawn from Berkeley, Schopenhauer, and Eastern mysticism—he expresses deep philosophical sympathy and attempts a rigorous refutation of temporal succession, even as he continues to construct fictions that depend on sequential order.

Now consider a new case: a single, non-branching, open-ended timeline in which causation runs bidirectionally—future events genuinely determine past ones—while full mnemonic continuity is preserved, so that an agent can remember having been shaped by actions she has not yet performed. There is no cyclical return to an identical origin, no parallel worlds, and no dissolution of the self into multiplicity; rather, linear sequence remains intact, but retrocausal links bind future and past within one continuous biography.
