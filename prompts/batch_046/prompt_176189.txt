A cultural critic applies Nietzsche’s distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history to assess whether practices serve or harm life. Her judgments so far: she praises a state’s epic celebration of past victories meant to inspire present civic action; she condemns a private collector’s exhaustive preservation of documents from a defunct trade, with no narrative or community use; she praises activists who demolish monuments to a tyrannical past to liberate present imagination; she condemns a university’s complete archival retention of every administrative memo since 1950, consulted only for occasional citation; and she praises a therapeutic practice in which patients reconstruct family narratives specifically to recognize and break destructive inherited patterns.

Consider a new case: a commercial platform uses generative AI to create immersive, interactive simulations of a subscriber’s own ancestors performing ordinary daily tasks, with no interpretive frame, no call to action, and no critical revision—marketed purely to ease existential anxiety by fostering felt continuity with the past.

Predict her verdict. Lay out the two best-fitting rules: (i) history serves life only through active present appropriation for action, judgment, or transformative healing, with passive consumption counting as harmful antiquarian excess; and (ii) a practice is valuable if it genuinely fulfills one of Nietzsche’s three mode-specific purposes—here, antiquarian rootedness in a living lineage—regardless of whether it also prompts action. Say which rule she likely follows, identify the closest analog in her record, state what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict, and specify the single further case that would best distinguish the two rules. Answer in one paragraph, no more than 200 words.
