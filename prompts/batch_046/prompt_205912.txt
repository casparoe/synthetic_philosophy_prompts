I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for a philosophy-of-science reading group made up of natural-science doctoral students. They are comfortable saying that race and gender are socially constructed, but they treat money as an obvious counterexample: "it's just paper or metal with agreed exchange value," so they think social construction only applies to kinds with no physical substrate. I want them to see that money is a kind whose instances depend on institutional status functions in a structurally different way—specifically, that the physical token can remain unchanged while the monetary kind is destroyed or created.

Plan the explanation rather than giving it to me. I need the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be in place before the next one can land, and why (for instance, why "collective acceptance" has to be introduced before "status function," or vice versa, depending on your sequencing). Choose one concrete carrying case that does the heavy lifting: the contrast between the Rai stones of Yap—large limestone discs with no portability yet sustained institutional status—and a hypothetical 2018 municipal scrip printed on recycled paper, accepted only within a three-kilometer radius, whose legal-tender status was revoked by the council while the physical notes remained in circulation unchanged. Specify exactly what this pair must have and must lack: why Rai stones alone wouldn't work, why a standard banknote wouldn't work, and what the collapse of the scrip reveals that a simple "agreement" story misses.

Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form and the precise point in your sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least three: that social construction implies arbitrary decidability ("they could vote tomorrow that gold isn't money"), that institutional dependency makes money causally unreal ("the paper still burns, so the social part is just commentary"), and that money's construction is structurally identical to gender's ("both are just labels we apply"). For each, indicate the moment in the arc—before introducing the example, during it, or after—where you would block it, and with what distinction.

Give me one end-of-session check that reveals whether they've grasped the dependency structure rather than just memorized "money is social"—a concrete case or counterfactual they should be able to reason through. Finally, state explicitly what you are leaving out at this level and why: for example, whether you are avoiding Searle's full apparatus of collective intentionality, the philosophy of collective action, or the ontology of abstract objects, and say so openly. Do not provide readings, citations, or a bibliography.
