I am preparing a handout for an undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar, and I should like a diagnostic taxonomy—rather than a standard lecture slide—that sorts students by where they actually stand on the species problem, using concrete puzzle cases rather than asking them to choose among familiar labels.

The tree should open with a single commitment: whether a species must possess some unified internal essence—genetic, morphological, or reproductive—to count as a real kind at all. From there, each branch should divide on a claim tested by a specific scenario: the ring-species pattern observed in Ensatina salamanders, where adjacent populations interbreed but the endpoints do not; bacterial lineages in which extensive horizontal gene transfer obscures vertical descent; and plant populations that maintain phenotypic distinctness despite persistent hybridisation and gene flow. The required leaves are: the Biological Species Concept; the Phylogenetic Species Concept; the Ecological Species Concept; Michael Ghiselin’s thesis that species are historical individuals rather than natural kinds; and an unnamed position that treats reproductive isolation as sufficient locally but denies that it produces globally discrete, non-overlapping groups. For every leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment—the consequence its defenders must accept. In addition, flag any pair of leaves that these cases cannot genuinely separate, and identify any logical combination of answers that remains unoccupied in your tree, with a brief diagnosis: is the gap incoherent, merely unexplored, or avoided because it collapses into an adjacent view? The instrument ought to work for someone reasoning entirely from the cases, so please do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or named texts.
