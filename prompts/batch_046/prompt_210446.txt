You are an expert in classical Chinese philosophy and in applied clinical ethics, with experience drafting formal policy documents for university governance bodies. I am a final-year undergraduate taking a course on Zhuangzi and comparative philosophy of death, and I also sit on my university's Palliative Care Curriculum Review Board. Please think carefully, and begin with a brief internal brainstorming of the key philosophical and institutional tensions before producing the final document.

We have received a proposal to pilot a Transformation Narrative training module for final-year medical students on hospice placements. The module draws on Zhuangzian ideas: it teaches clinicians to frame death not as the annihilation of a self but as a change of form analogous to the decay and renewal of matter, encouraging patients to regard their own dissolution with the same equanimity Zhuangzi displays towards the death of his wife. The proponents argue that this reduces existential suffering without requiring religious belief. However, a minority report warns that embedding this metaphysical picture in clinical training violates value-neutrality: it treats personal identity as discontinuous across death, which may undermine the moral force patients attach to advance directives that presuppose a continuous subject of harm, and it risks being coercive for patients whose traditions treat death as a final judgement rather than a seasonal change.

The Board must decide whether to approve the pilot, approve it with restrictions, or reject it. Draft a concise but formal policy memo addressed to the Board. Your memo must include:

- A clear, actionable recommendation — approve, reject, or approve conditionally — together with explicit criteria the Board can apply to future proposals of this kind.
- The philosophical reasoning: how Zhuangzi's distinction between small knowledge, which clings to the life-death boundary, and great knowledge, which perceives continuous transformation, relates to clinical ethics, and whether this distinction can be taught as a therapeutic stance without being imposed as metaphysical doctrine.
- An honest and substantive treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side — specifically, the worry that framing death as mere transformation erodes the ethical weight of autonomy and bodily continuity.
- A brief implementation note: if the recommendation is for approval, what safeguards would prevent the module from being used to override a patient's own framework?

Please write in polished British English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to named scholars. Do not invent obscure theorists or reference niche secondary literature; work from the conceptual material generally available in the primary texts and standard interpretation. The memo should be a document the Board could genuinely act upon.
