I've been reading about people with pain asymbolia: they can locate a burn, describe the sensation as painful, yet show no flinching, no request for relief, and seem genuinely untroubled. Philosophers often cite this to argue that the unpleasantness of pain is not intrinsic to its sensory character but can be separated from it. I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in these discussions, and I wonder whether it has an established name.

The pattern unfolds in two steps. First, the asymbolia case is presented as proof that one can have the sensory side of pain without its badness. Then, when a critic notes that the patient still reports disliking the sensation, or that neuroimaging shows affective processing present but disconnected from behaviour, the original speaker shifts ground: they claim to have meant only pure sensory pain, stripped of all emotional colouring, and they treat the patient as an instance of precisely that purified kind. If one then asks how to identify such purified pain without relying on behavioural or affective markers, the answer appeals back to the patient's own report of a burning or stabbing quality — the very report that introduced the case. So the example is used both to split the phenomenon and to define one half in a way that excludes anything linking it back to the other.

I have seen analogous moves in other debates about phenomenal states, but I am asking specifically about this version in the philosophy of pain. Is there a recognised concept for this kind of circular narrowing? How exactly does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, say about which neural pathway mediates withdrawal? And if no standard term exists, what would be the clearest way to specify its boundaries — for instance, whether it applies whenever a counterexample is met by refining the phenomenon rather than refining the theory?
