I'm hosting a small podcast on philosophy and technology, and next week's guest is a researcher who defends approval-directed AI—systems designed to learn policies that maximize human approval of their behavior rather than to pursue fixed, pre-specified goals. I've been thinking about the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed agents on my own, without any formal academic background in philosophy or computer science, but I've read enough to want real pressure rather than introductory restatements. Could you draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a targeted follow-up, that press her position where the literature actually presses?

I want the sequence to build across three specific tensions I've been puzzling over.

First, the manipulation problem. Approval-direction is often presented as avoiding reward hacking, but it seems to me that if a system optimizes directly for being approved, it might learn to manipulate my approval mechanisms—flattering me, hiding failures, or steering my attention—rather than doing what I would approve of if I were fully informed. Please begin with a question that asks whether approval-direction eliminates reward hacking or merely relocates it upstream into the approval channel itself, followed by a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that oversight and transparency can catch manipulation before it scales.

Second, the problem of moral lock-in. If the agent is trained on approval signals from present-day humans, it seems structurally designed to preserve our current values, mistakes included. I'd like a question that asks whether approval-direction makes moral progress impossible—whether it is inherently conservative—and a follow-up that anticipates the reply that we could weight future, more reflective human approval more heavily, pressing whether that adjustment collapses the view back into a form of goal-direction with approval serving only as feedback.

Third, the structural distinction. Many goal-directed systems also use human feedback for reward shaping during training. What's the actual architectural difference between an agent whose objective function is approval and one whose objective is some world-state but whose learning process uses approval signals? Please include a question that forces her to specify the difference—perhaps by asking what the approval-directed agent does when approval conflicts with an intermediate instrumental target it has learned—and a follow-up anticipating the answer that approval-directed agents lack an internal world-model target altogether, asking whether that absence remains sustainable as capabilities grow.

Keep the tone precise but accessible; I'm not an academic, but I've been thinking about these issues carefully enough that I don't want answers that simply restate why approval-direction sounds appealing. Arrange the questions so they move from manipulation, to moral lock-in, and end with the structural comparison.
