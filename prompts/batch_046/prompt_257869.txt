I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time thinking about Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle—the scenario in which a brain is removed, placed in a vat, and made to control a body remotely—and about what would actually happen to "me" if my brain were moved, copied, or altered in various ways. Rather than asking for an overview of the literature, I want to know whether there is a hidden principle behind my own intuitive judgments about modified versions of the case.

Here are five scenarios and the verdicts I find myself reaching. I think the first three hang together, but I am not sure about the last two, and I suspect one of them is wrong.

1. Classic extraction. My brain is removed intact, placed in a vat, and my body is controlled remotely from the vat. Verdict: I am in the vat; my body is just an avatar I pilot.

2. Perfect digital copy. My brain is scanned and destroyed, and a digital simulation with all my memories and personality runs in a robotic body. Verdict: That simulation is me; I have survived in a new medium.

3. Gradual synthetic replacement. Over ten years, every neuron in my brain is replaced one-by-one with a functionally identical synthetic chip, with no break in consciousness. Verdict: I am still the same person at the end; the substrate does not matter.

4. Asymmetric split with stranded awareness. My brain is divided. The left hemisphere is placed in vat A and controls a new body B with full memory access. The right hemisphere remains in my original skull, disconnected from all motor output, but it reports full awareness, remembers everything, and feels like "me," though it can only observe. Verdict: I am primarily in vat A—that is the controlling center—but there is also a genuine, leftover fragment of me stranded in the skull. It is not a separate person, just a disconnected part of the same self.

5. Delayed telemetry loop. My brain is in the vat and sends commands to my original body, but there is a fixed 30-second transmission delay. During that gap, the body executes the intended action through purely local reflex circuits that happen to match the command exactly. I only experience the decision in the vat, then, half a minute later, observe the result in the body. Verdict: During the 30-second gap, I am neither fully in the vat (because I have not registered the outcome) nor in the body (because the reflex action was not caused by my present consciousness). I am temporarily distributed or suspended.

Is there a single principle that saves all five? If not, which verdict should I drop? And what does the best principle say about cases I have not mentioned?

Please organize your answer exactly as follows:

- Pattern check (about 150 words): State whether my five verdicts share a coherent principle, and identify the pair that is hardest to reconcile.
- The principle (about 200 words): Formulate the simplest rule that captures the pattern, with one clause tied to each motivating case. Be precise about whether identity tracks continuous functional integration, a narrative center of consciousness, or something else.
- Two unseen cases (about 150 words): Apply the principle to (a) a split where the left and right hemispheres are placed in separate vats with full body control and equal memory access, and (b) a scenario in which the biological brain remains in my head but a parallel digital copy runs for one year, both with continuous consciousness and shared memory updates.
- The verdict to abandon (about 100 words): Say clearly which of my five judgments the principle cannot accommodate without excessive complexity, and explain why dropping that one restores coherence.
- Bottom line (about 75 words): Give your overall view on whether personal location is determined by causal-functional continuity, by first-person perspective, or by another factor, and note what that implies for case 5 specifically.

Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I have no access to academic databases and want reasoning I can evaluate on my own.
