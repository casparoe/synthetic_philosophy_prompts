You are a professional philosopher of science with particular expertise in the metaphysics of biology and the philosophy of taxonomy. Before you answer, please think carefully and begin with a brief internal brainstorming of the main ontological stances on species—realism, nominalism, and pluralism—so that your analysis is well structured.

As a retired evolutionary ecologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science, I have become puzzled by an institutional pattern I observed throughout my career. In wildlife management and captive-breeding programs, agencies routinely authorize genetic rescue—the deliberate translocation of individuals between isolated populations to restore genetic diversity—but only when both populations are classified within the same pre-defined evolutionary unit, a designation based primarily on historical isolation and phylogenetic divergence rather than on present reproductive compatibility. At the same time, these same institutions treat fertile, ecologically successful hybrids between two recognized species as categorical management failures. Hybrid individuals are often removed from wild populations or excluded from pedigree registries, even when they display no fitness deficit and the parental populations are themselves linked by continuous genetic clines. Thus the practice treats intra-unit gene flow as ontologically restorative and inter-specific gene flow as ontologically contaminating, apparently independent of empirical demographic outcomes.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of species does this dual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: does it strictly require that species are discrete natural kinds with essential boundaries, or could a pluralist, nominalist, or purely operationalist framework license the same protocol? Carefully distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates well. Is the practice internally coherent, given that evolutionary units are sometimes defined by reproductive isolation and sometimes by historical lineage? Finally, describe what would have to change in management criteria—translocation rules, breeding registry standards, and hybrid-removal policies—if the implicit ontological framework were replaced by a straightforwardly population-genetic, outcome-based approach that assigns no metaphysical weight to species boundaries and evaluates interventions solely by adaptive potential and demographic viability.
