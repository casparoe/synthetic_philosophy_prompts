There's this weird tension in how some european democracies handle direct democracy. You can launch a citizens initiative with a million signatures, but then the commission can just reject it without real parliamentary debate. At the same time, citizens assemblies are convened with random selection and paid deliberation, but their recommendations are legally non-binding—politicians can nod and ignore them. Meanwhile constitutional amendments often need a referendum with high turnout thresholds, but ordinary laws just need legislative majorities. What theory of 'the will of the people' does that whole setup presuppose? Does it treat popular will as a raw preference snapshot that needs expert filtering, or as something that only genuinely forms through deliberation but then lacks direct authority? Can a liberal aggregation view and a general-will view in the rousseau tradition both accommodate this, or does the practice quietly rule one out? And if we take the idea of a formed collective will seriously, wouldn't binding assemblies be required—so is the non-binding part just hypocrisy, or is there a hidden distinction between knowing what people want and having the right to impose it? Don't just lay out options; defend an actual reconstruction of the implicit theory and say whether the practice is philosphically consistant.
