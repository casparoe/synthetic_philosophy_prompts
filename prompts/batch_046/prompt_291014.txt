I retired after three decades in biophysics and have recently begun auditing philosophy seminars at a local university. I am serving as an occasional grader for a course on moral psychology and historical explanation, and I need a rigorous, point-by-point evaluation of the following student submission.

The exam question was this: Compare Christopher Browning’s Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Jonah Goldhagen’s Hitler’s Willing Executioners (1996) with respect to Reserve Police Battalion 101’s participation in mass shootings in occupied Poland, particularly the Jozefow massacre of July 1942. Address three issues: first, whether the primary explanatory weight should fall on situational dynamics—peer conformity, authority structures, career incentives—or on a pre-existing ideological disposition, specifically Goldhagen’s notion of eliminationist antisemitism; second, how each author interprets the institutional fact that refusal to participate carried no formal punishment, only social cost, and what each takes that feature to reveal about motivation; and third, whether the two explanations are logically incompatible, or whether they might instead stand in some relation of explanatory redundancy, complementarity, or mutual undermining. Do not merely summarize the books; evaluate the logical structure of the disagreement itself.

The student submitted this answer:

Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 emphasizes that these men were ordinary middle-aged policemen from Hamburg, not ideologically selected SS fanatics. At Jozefow in July 1942, Major Trapp explicitly offered the men the option to step aside; only a small minority did so, and the vast majority proceeded to shoot Jewish women, children, and elderly people. Browning interprets this as decisive evidence that peer pressure, unit cohesion, and concern for social standing—not deep personal hatred—were the dominant causal drivers. He further argues that these men were not exceptional in their antisemitism; they shared the general European prejudice of the era, but German culture before 1933 contained no distinctive or exceptional reservoir of anti-Jewish hatred that would specifically predispose them to genocide.

Goldhagen rejects this framing. He maintains that a uniquely virulent, specifically German form of eliminationist antisemitism—deeply embedded in German culture long before Hitler—can alone explain why these policemen killed not only under orders but often with evident enthusiasm. Where Browning sees situational forces overwhelming ordinary moral inhibitions, Goldhagen sees ideology rendering the killings psychologically welcome. Crucially, Goldhagen denies that peer pressure played any meaningful causal role; the men killed because they genuinely believed Jews ought to be destroyed, and the absence of formal punishment for refusal is largely irrelevant because refusal was culturally unthinkable given their worldview.

These accounts are therefore strictly incompatible. Browning’s thesis requires the absence of any special German cultural predisposition, treating antisemitism as a generic background condition common across Europe, whereas Goldhagen’s thesis depends precisely on that specificity being present and decisive. Because the presence of a unique national psychology is either necessary or it is not, there is no logical space for synthesis: either social dynamics explain the killings or ideology does. The institutional fact that refusal was permitted yet rare supports Browning’s conformity model, but it offers no assistance to Goldhagen, since on Goldhagen’s view ideology alone was fully sufficient motivation, making the social structure redundant. For these reasons, Browning’s more parsimonious account—one that avoids positing an unverified collective psychology—should be preferred.

Please assign a letter grade from A to F and justify it with specific, section-by-section comments. Identify precisely where the reasoning is sound, where it conflates one author’s position with another, where a view is misattributed, and where an inference does not actually follow from the premises offered. Work through the argument step by step rather than giving a global impression. Keep your assessment to no more than 350 words.
