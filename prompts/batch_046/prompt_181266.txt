The long-running dispute over Ian Hacking’s looping effects in psychiatric taxonomy seems stuck in a pattern that is not quite empirical and not quite terminological, yet neither side treats it as intractable. One camp holds that because being classified with a disorder—say, dissociative identity disorder or autism spectrum condition—can alter the self-conception and behavior of the classified, psychiatric kinds are “interactive kinds” that fail a basic requirement for scientific objectivity: they are not indifferent to being known. The opposing camp accepts the historical dynamics—the broadening of autism criteria across DSM editions, the shifting phenomenology of dissociative states, the disappearance of hysteria—but treats looping as a stable, parameterizable feedback mechanism rather than an ontological disqualifier; on this view, the kind remains real, only dynamically coupled to its taxonomy. Both sides invoke the same episodes not to dispute chronology but to illustrate opposing commitments about what a kind must be. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about whether loops occur, but about a condition I will call the “indifference condition”: whether a genuine kind requires that its instances would exist and behave identically even if no concept of them had ever been formulated. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about the magnitude, directionality, or asymptotic behavior of specific loops that I am missing? More precisely, are the two sides genuinely disagreeing about ontology—what kinds are—or are they answering different questions, one about the metaphysics of classification and the other about the methodology of measurement, under the single heading “looping effects”? And if the stall is indeed rooted in competing conditions for kindhood rather than in unresolved data, what would count as progress: a formal model that distinguishes dampening loops from amplifying ones, a historical case in which a loop collapsed without altering the underlying taxonomy, or a conceptual distinction that separates “being affected by knowledge of the kind” from “being constituted by that knowledge”? Or should we conclude that no further evidence about how classifications reshape behavior will move the dispute, because the disagreement is ultimately about which metaphysical price we are willing to pay for psychiatric science?
