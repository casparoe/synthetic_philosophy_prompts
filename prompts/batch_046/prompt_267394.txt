I'm applying for a six-month postdoctoral research fellowship in moral philosophy, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a tight two-page proposal. The standard "man of his time" defence—applied to figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus—typically excuses moral failures by appealing to the epistemic limits of an era: the agent could not have known better because their culture lacked the relevant conceptual resources. I suspect this defence rests on a hidden sociological assumption, namely that moral knowledge within any epoch is distributed more or less uniformly, so that culpability tracks the dominant consensus available to them. I want to investigate whether the defence survives once we reject that uniformity and replace it with what I am calling "moral friction": the idea that within any historical moment there exist accessible, lived disagreements—dissenting voices, marginal practices, or internal contradictions within the agent's own commitments—that generate localised pockets of moral knowledge even when the dominant culture suppresses them. The precise question is therefore: does modelling moral epistemology as friction-ridden rather than consensus-driven undermine the "man of his time" defence for specific figures, and if so, does it yield a principled distinction between excusable ignorance and culpable inattention?

The existing literature on historical moral judgment tends to oscillate between two unsatisfactory poles. Contextualists argue that we must evaluate agents solely by the normative framework they inhabited, which risks collapsing into an uninteresting relativism that excuses nearly any atrocity. Presentists apply contemporary standards without adjustment, which ignores the genuine epistemic constraints that shape moral agency. Neither camp, however, systematically examines the internal topology of moral knowledge within an epoch—that is, who within Jefferson's Virginia, Churchill's imperial bureaucracy, or Columbus's Catholic Spain had access to dissenting moral concepts, and whether those dissenting resources were practically available to the agent in question. A philosophical rather than purely historical project can make progress here by constructing a normative model: specifying the conditions under which an agent can be said to have "access" to a moral reason despite its social marginalisation, and testing that model against the conceptual architecture of specific historical cases.

My method would be analytical and conceptual. Months one and two would refine the "moral friction" framework—defining accessibility conditions (proximity, intelligibility, and non-crippling cost) and distinguishing them from mere logical possibility. Months three and four would apply the framework to two contrasting cases: one where dissent was structurally visible but personally costly (for instance, abolitionist arguments circulating within Jefferson's own intellectual circle), and one where dissent was institutionally remote yet conceptually available (for instance, critiques of colonial violence within Spanish theological debates of the period). Month five would address the objection that this approach merely relocates presentism into a subtler form: I would argue that friction-based evaluation depends not on our current moral conclusions but on the agent's own acknowledged commitments, which generate internal pressure points. The deliverable would be a 6,000-word paper with an honest failure condition: if the accessibility conditions turn out either so permissive that every historical agent becomes culpable, or so restrictive that the framework collapses back into the uniform-consensus view, then the project fails to offer a genuine middle path. I am not requesting a literature review, bibliography, or citations in this proposal—I will supply those separately. What I need is feedback on whether the framing of "moral friction" is precise enough to support this scope, and whether the distinction between excusable ignorance and culpable inattention holds up under sustained pressure.
