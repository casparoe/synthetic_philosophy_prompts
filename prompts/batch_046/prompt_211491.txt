Write the desiderata an adequate theory of moral standing to blame must meet, treating hypocrisy not as the sole case but as the stress-test that reveals the structure. For each requirement, state the concrete case or distinction behind it, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and identify any pair of desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable.

The list should include: (i) coverage of the core hypocrisy intuition—A blames B for fault X while A is guilty of X—together with the reform, ignorance, and structural-inevitability exceptions that show hypocrisy is not binary; (ii) an account of non-hypocritical standing failures, including victim-privileged standing, bystander exclusion, and forfeiture through prior wrongdoing against the same party; (iii) preservation of the first-person/third-person asymmetry, explaining why self-blame survives hypocrisy that undermines other-directed blame; (iv) a decision on whether standing to blame, standing to resent, and standing to punish come apart, with hypocrisy’s differential effect on each; (v) accommodation or rejection of the “defeasible license” model, on which standing is a gradable normative power that can be partially suspended and reinstated through acknowledgment rather than an all-or-nothing status; (vi) consistency with the claim that blame can remain appropriate in content even when the blamer lacks standing, without collapsing the distinction between appropriateness-of-content and propriety-of-delivery; (vii) a boundary condition that prevents the account from generalizing into an indiscriminate “clean hands” principle applying to all normative criticism; and (viii) extension to collective agents, specifying whether a group retains standing when only some members are hypocritical.

Then note which subset is hardest to satisfy together—particularly whether (v) and a binary conception of standing can both be kept, and whether (ii) and universalist egalitarian views of standing are compatible. Finally, score briefly against the list the four main families: unfairness/disproportionality accounts, moral-equality/consistency accounts, relational or role-based accounts, and normative-power-forfeiture accounts—keeping the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.
