I serve on the Clinical Ethics Appeals Panel of the Meridian Health System. We must issue a written opinion by Friday regarding Protocol 9-C, which mandates quarterly biomarker screening for all 2,400 clinical staff. Each screening carries an expected false-positive rate of 0.3%, yielding roughly seven temporary suspensions per quarter with associated psychological distress and lost wages; collectively, the protocol aims to prevent an estimated three to five preventable fatalities annually among immunocompromised patients by detecting an emerging low-prevalence pathogen before ward transmission.

The Staff Nurses Union has filed a formal appeal arguing that this arrangement violates the contractualist requirement that any principle governing them must be justifiable to each affected individual, not merely defensible by aggregate statistics. They contend that imposing a small but real burden—risk of wrongful suspension, anxiety, and income interruption—on hundreds of staff for the sake of anonymous statistical lives saved elsewhere treats each nurse as a fungible contribution to a collective outcome. From their view, a principle permitting such aggregation is one any individual nurse could reasonably reject, since it fails to offer her a justification addressed specifically to her as a party entitled to non-instrumental consideration. They ask us to reject the protocol and replace it with a fully voluntary screening program, even if that raises the risk of outbreak.

The administration responds that contractualism permits aggregation when the principle is embedded in a system of mutual protection to which each staff member is already a beneficiary, and that voluntary screening has failed twice in the past eighteen months, leading to preventable deaths. They further argue that the union’s position collapses the distinction between permissible aggregation for collective security and impermissible sacrifice of the one for the many.

Please draft the panel’s formal opinion for circulation to both parties. It should recommend a concrete course of action—uphold, modify with conditions, or reject Protocol 9-C—together with an explicit implementation time frame. The reasoning must treat the union’s contractualist objection seriously rather than dismissing it as a category error, and should address whether Scanlon’s emphasis on justifiability to each individual genuinely excludes aggregation across large numbers in institutional contexts like this. In particular, examine whether there is a principled distinction between this case and classic rescue scenarios in which comparable burdens are imposed on one person to save several, and whether contractualism can accommodate a principle of mutual risk-pooling without treating affected parties as merely fungible. The opinion should present its analysis in the panel’s own voice, without bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to sources the panel could not itself consult.
