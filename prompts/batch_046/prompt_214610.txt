A friend has walked me into an argument about simultaneous discovery and I cannot find the exit. Here it is, step by step.

(1) Define a discovery as inevitable just in case any sufficiently competent researcher working in the same domain, with access to the same background evidence and methods, would eventually have arrived at substantially the same result.

(2) The independent, near-simultaneous formulation of calculus by Newton and Leibniz, and of natural selection by Darwin and Wallace, demonstrates that both results satisfied the condition in (1): the conceptual and empirical pieces were already in place, and more than one mind assembled them.

(3) So the calculus and the theory of natural selection were inevitable discoveries.

(4) If a result is inevitable, then the particular person who first published it is causally redundant with respect to that result: in the nearest possible world where that individual never entered science, the result still appears, produced by the other discoverer or by a successor.

(5) Causal redundancy with respect to a result entails that attributing the result to that individual is merely a historical accident; they contributed nothing that the world could not have obtained elsewhere, so they deserve no substantive scientific credit—only the ceremonial credit of being first.

(6) Therefore Newton, Leibniz, Darwin, and Wallace deserve no substantive credit for calculus or evolutionary theory; any praise we give them is a convention about chronology, not about achievement.

(7) Generalizing: for any scientific result that was "in the air," the discoverer is causally redundant and hence undeserving of substantive credit.

(8) Applied consistently, this reasoning collapses the distinction between a hard-won breakthrough and a trivial derivation, because every successful discovery is either inevitable (hence redundant) or not genuinely a discovery. The conclusion is that scientific credit is never substantively deserved.

I think (8) is false—surely Darwin's two-decade synthesis was a deeper achievement than Wallace's brief outline, and some discoveries are genuinely hard-won. But every step before (8) seems defensible. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation between causal redundancy and normative desert? Does the argument illicitly slide from "the theory was inevitable" to "this particular formulation, with all its explanatory power and scope, was inevitable"? Or is the flaw elsewhere, and if I fix it, do I have to abandon the intuition that simultaneous invention tells us something important about the nature of discovery?
