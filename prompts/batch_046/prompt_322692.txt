hey, im drafting an essay on the tragedy paradox — why we seek out art that makes us cry — and i tried to avoid the usual catharsis/Hume talk. instead im playing with this idea im calling the "mourning contract." the basic claim is that tragedy works because audience and artwork enter an implicit agreement: we treat fictional losses as emotionally real (we grieve them) but the contract includes a hidden "no-fault clause" — the grief doesn't come with the usual existential demands that real grief carries, like changing your life, seeking justice, or re-evaluating your values. so we get the full affective weight of loss without the normative debt. the enjoyment, on this view, isn't "despite" the sadness but comes from precisely this calibrated asymmetry: we experience deep sorrow in a context where sorrow is "safe" not just psychologically but morally.

i then try to use this to explain some weird edge cases — why we reject art that is "too sad" (it violates the contract by making the grief feel like it should spill over into real-world obligations), why documentary tragedy hits different (no contract, real obligations remain), and why people can rewatch the same tragedy repeatedly (real grief is usually once-and-done because it forces change; contractual grief can be renewed).

i'd like comments on whether this is actually distinct from aristotle's catharsis or just a rebranding, and whether the "no-fault clause" idea collapses into standard "psychological distance" theories. also, is there a hidden contradiction in saying the grief is "real" but lacks normative weight? maybe grief just IS the normative weight?

any feedback on the argument or where it falls apart? dont worry about citations, just the logic.
