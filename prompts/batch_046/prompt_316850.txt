For an essay I am preparing on economic ethics, I have been asked to lay out my own philosophical commitments regarding money, exchange, and value so that readers can see where I stand before I turn to policy arguments. I am writing this as a journalist who covers science and ideas, not as an academic specialist, and I want to know whether my position is coherent or whether I am smuggling in contradictions. Here is where I have landed.

First, I take Aristotle's account in the Politics seriously: money was introduced as a conventional instrument to make exchange possible between goods that are naturally incommensurable, and its proper function is to serve as a measure of need rather than to become an object of unlimited acquisition. Second, I find the modern chartalist or credit view largely persuasive—state currency derives its value chiefly from its acceptance in discharging tax obligations and from collective social convention, not from any intrinsic commodity worth. Third, I regard chrematistike—the pursuit of wealth through interest, speculative arbitrage, or the circulation of money for more money—as ethically defective, because it severs acquisition from use-value and treats the measuring instrument itself as the end sought. Fourth, despite that, I defend compound interest on productive capital loans in contemporary economies, on the grounds that when such interest finances technological innovation expanding genuine human capabilities, it operates more like cooperative risk-sharing than like sterile coin-breeding. Fifth, I hold that exchange value ought to be assessed by its correspondence to real use-value and human need, not by subjective willingness to pay, which makes me skeptical of markets that price essential goods—medicines, carbon allowances, housing—purely by scarcity and purchasing power. Sixth, I think reflective equilibrium is the right method for economic ethics: particular judgments about transactions should be revised if they conflict with general principles, and general principles should be revised if they systematically fail to accommodate well-considered particular judgments, with coherence rather than axiomatic derivation as the goal.

My questions are these. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled with modest clarification, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? In particular, does my defense of compound interest in commitment four collapse into the very chrematistike I condemn in commitment three, or can the distinction hold up under pressure? Does my acceptance of chartalist money in commitment two sit uneasily with my rejection of willingness-to-pay pricing in commitment five, given that modern credit systems typically allocate goods through price mechanisms? And is there a hidden tension between treating chrematistike as categorically wrong in commitment three and endorsing reflective equilibrium in commitment six, which seems to invite revising categorical rules in light of particular cases? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to give—whether a principle, a case judgment, or a definitional distinction—and identify the single revision that would clean up the most conflicts at once. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want an internal audit of this specific package.
