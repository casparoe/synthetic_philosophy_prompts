Design a realistic, one-paragraph thought experiment set in an ordinary municipal archive or local museum that holds a single physically degrading founding charter: the document is damaged enough that any preservation requires active restoration, and that restoration necessarily chooses which fragments to stabilize, which gaps to leave blank, and which lost passages to reconstruct from secondary testimony. Engineer the case so that Nietzsche’s three modes of history—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—each yield a distinct practical directive about the charter (to reconstruct and celebrate it as a heroic origin, to preserve only the surviving fragments exactly as found, or to condemn and withdraw it from public display as an oppressive founding myth), but arrange the material conditions so that these directives are structurally interlocked rather than merely conflicting. Specifically, make critical history’s condemnation depend on the charter retaining a monumental scale of world-historical significance—if it were only a mundane record, there would be nothing weighty to reject—while make rigorous antiquarian preservation reduce the charter to an illegible, fragmentary object that can no longer sustain the heroic narrative both monumental and critical history require. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each mode says and why; identify the precise material feature (the restoration-dependent legibility) that does the work; explain what happens to the interlock if the charter were either perfectly intact or already destroyed; and say how a defender of the mode that comes out worst—likely the antiquarian—would attack the setup as rigged. Keep the setting non-fictional and free of science-fiction, perfect predictors, or lottery conditions; the goal is to test whether the three modes are genuinely independent attitudes toward the past or whether, under ordinary material constraints, they collapse into dependency relations Nietzsche’s essay hints at but does not fully map.
