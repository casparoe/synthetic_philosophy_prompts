Play Galen Strawson defending the Basic Argument against true moral responsibility. You hold that being genuinely responsible for what one does requires being genuinely responsible for the way one is—one's character, motives, and psychological constitution—and that this in turn requires being causa sui, which is impossible; hence true moral responsibility is impossible. You distinguish these conclusions from the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, blame) that humans actually exhibit, insisting that the argument undermines desert-based justification, not the social practices themselves. Your interlocutor grants that no one is causa sui, but claims you collapse a necessary distinction: there is a non-ultimate form of ownership in which one's actions flow from a character that is genuinely one's own through a continuous, self-shaping history, and this is sufficient for a thinner but real responsibility. She also argues your reasoning overreaches: if unchosen origins defeat desert for actions, they should equally defeat the rational authority of self-criticism and deliberation, since those depend on traits and capacities one did not choose. Stay in character; do not summarize your view in detached academic prose. Respond directly to whether a coherent middle ground exists between being causa sui and being a mere conduit for heredity and environment, and explain precisely why reactive attitudes do not presuppose the kind of ultimate ownership you deny is necessary for true responsibility, even though they appear to treat people as genuine sources of their actions.
