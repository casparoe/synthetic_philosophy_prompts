Consider the disagreement between two colleagues, Elena and Marcus, about whether temporary visitors have genuine moral obligations to observe local everyday conventions—specifically tipping norms, queueing practices, and rules against jaywalking.

Elena holds that these conventions generate real moral duties for anyone present, visitors included. She argues that tipping, orderly queueing, and pedestrian signals are not mere etiquette but coordination mechanisms that protect vulnerable parties: service workers depend on tips to reach a living wage; queueing safeguards people who arrived earlier; traffic rules protect pedestrians and drivers alike. A visitor who disregards them therefore exploits a system from which they benefit without sustaining its stability. Elena concedes that the obligation weakens when a convention is genuinely opaque, but she insists that in most urban environments these norms are visibly signaled—menus list service charges, lines form in plain sight, signals are posted—so ignorance is rarely excusable. She further claims that the fact locals often forgive small breaches does not erase the underlying wrong; forgiveness presupposes it.

Marcus rejects the claim that these are moral obligations. He distinguishes conventions that protect basic rights from those that are purely local coordination devices or arbitrary customs. He grants that cutting in line can wrong someone if it violates a fair-ordering scheme they depend on, but he insists that tipping percentages are culturally arbitrary—there is no injustice in leaving fifteen percent in a culture that expects twenty, provided one genuinely did not know—and that jaywalking across an empty street harms no one. Marcus introduces an idea he calls normative tourism: treating every local practice as morally binding on outsiders collapses moral judgment into mere cultural obedience and imposes an unreasonable epistemic burden on travelers. He notes that locals themselves routinely excuse visitors for minor breaches of tipping or pedestrian norms, which suggests these are social expectations enforced by embarrassment, not obligations of justice. He also argues that entering a city is not like signing a contract; one cannot be said to consent to every arbitrary local rule simply by arriving.

Elena answers in three moves. First, she says Marcus's charge of normative tourism misfires: recognizing local obligations is not cultural obedience but respect for the institutional context one has voluntarily entered. Second, she argues that Marcus's harm-based criterion is too narrow—moral obligations can sustain collective goods, such as public trust in traffic systems or wage stability in service industries, even when no identifiable individual is harmed in a particular breach. Third, she insists that the local practice of excusing visitors is fully compatible with there being a real obligation; people forgive debts they still acknowledge are owed.

Marcus replies point by point. To the first, he says voluntary entry does not entail consent to arbitrary rules—no traveler signs an agreement to tip at a specific rate, and one cannot be morally bound by norms one has no reasonable way of discovering in advance. To the second, he concedes that sustaining collective goods can generate obligations, but he demands a principled line: if every convention that supports some collective good binds visitors, then travelers are obligated to master every local custom, which is implausible. To the third, he distinguishes forgiveness that presupposes a wrong from politeness that treats a breach as harmless; he claims the latter is what actually occurs with tipping errors and minor jaywalking, which is why locals smile rather than resent.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Elena's three responses and each of Marcus's three counter-responses, rate the move as clearly mistaken, mostly correct but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, specifying exactly where each party is correct and where each overreaches—particularly on whether there is a defensible distinction between conventions that bind visitors and those that do not. Finally, identify what both are missing: is there a principled criterion—perhaps turning on visibility, connection to harm, or institutional role—that distinguishes genuine moral obligations from mere social expectations without collapsing into either universalism or pure cultural relativism? And be honest about whether this dispute risks stalling because neither party has offered a criterion that can be applied consistently across tipping, queueing, and jaywalking.
