I'm drafting a novel in which a protagonist gradually suspects her own identity is a sustained fiction rather than a continuous substance, and in discussing the premise with two fellow writers—Alex and Jordan—I witnessed the following disagreement. Alex argued that if one takes Dennett's notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity seriously, then the self is ontologically on a par with Elizabeth Bennet: both are abstract patterns maintained solely by narrative relations, with no physical or mental substance underpinning them. Jordan objected that this overreads the view; a fictional character lacks first-person access and embodied agency, whereas the narrative gravity of a person is anchored by a living brain that generates the story from the inside. The self, Jordan insisted, is not the character being described but the viewpoint from which the description issues. Alex then countered that if the self is merely such a viewpoint, split-brain patients ought to host two equally legitimate narrative centers within one skull, yet we resist treating them as two persons—which suggests we are secretly smuggling in a pre-Dennettian unified soul rather than explaining unity through narrative coherence alone. Jordan replied that Dennett's framework actually predicts plural centers precisely when integration fails: the hemispheres maintain separate narrative streams, which is exactly why split-brain phenomena are striking. The coherence that binds a single autobiography is a psychological achievement, not a metaphysical substrate, and there is no contradiction in allowing one body to contain more than one center if the stories diverge. Alex pressed further: if coherence alone is sufficient, then a meticulously ghostwritten autobiography that its subject fully endorses should generate an equally valid self, even though the author never lived those events. Jordan conceded that coherence must include causal and experiential anchoring, but maintained that adding this condition does not reintroduce a Cartesian ego; it merely restricts which narratives count as self-constituting. They reached an impasse over whether that restriction saves Dennett's view or quietly collapses it back into a substance account.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Alex's and Jordan's interventions from clearly mistaken to largely right, with brief justification for each. Then identify which specific points each party is genuinely correct about, what both are missing—especially regarding the boundary between fictional characters and embodied narrators—and whether Alex's ghostwriting challenge genuinely undermines Jordan's coherence condition or can be absorbed without metaphysical cost.
