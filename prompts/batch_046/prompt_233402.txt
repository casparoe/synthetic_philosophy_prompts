I'm trying to put a name to a tension I keep seeing in debates over socially constructed kinds—race and gender in particular, though it also shows up with categories like disability status or credit risk—where the same feature is used to establish construction on one side of an argument and then quietly abandoned on the other. Here are two concrete patterns. First, anthropologists and sociologists often argue that racial categories are constructed by pointing to cross-cultural variation: the US census scheme, Brazilian color terms, and older European taxonomies draw boundaries differently, with no biological anchor. But in epidemiology and clinical medicine, those very US census boxes are treated as stable controls, and defenders justify this by saying race tracks real differences in exposure to discrimination or environmental risk—differences that are partly produced by living inside a society that uses exactly those boxes for housing, policing, and healthcare access. The boundary variability proves construction; the locally frozen boundary is then defended as tracking an independent causal reality, without addressing whether that reality exists because the boundary was enforced. Second, with gender: scholars emphasize that gender norms and binary classifications vary widely across cultures and history, undermining any universal natural-kind claim. Yet in medical records and screening protocols, gender is often recorded in fixed binary fields that determine access to tests, and the justification is that these fields capture real differences in health outcomes—again treating an administratively chosen boundary as if it merely discovers a pre-existing division, even though the outcomes in question are shaped by which bodies are routed through which protocols. The move isn't simple hypocrisy, because the people making it often sincerely hold both commitments. Is there an established concept for this specific two-step—using inter-context boundary variability to establish that a kind is made, then treating one local operationalization as causally tracking real differences that the operationalization itself helps generate? How is it different from ordinary reification, from institutional feedback loops more generally, and from the claim that constructed kinds can still be causally real? If there is no precise term, what should distinguish this pattern from cases where a conventional category genuinely tracks stable causal patterns despite being human-made? Please take a committed position: identify the nearest existing concepts, say exactly where the described pattern diverges from each, and either defend an existing label with a clear boundary or propose a new one with its defining conditions and edge cases—especially for cases where the institutional boundary produces outcomes that then justify it, versus cases where the outcomes would exist regardless of the boundary used to measure them.
