(1) Any maxim I act on has the form 'I (agent A) will do X in circumstances C'.
(2) The universalizability test requires me to will that this maxim become a universal law.
(3) But 'universal law' is ambigious: it can mean that every agent applies the maxim distributively (each to themselves) or collectively (each applies it to every case involving anyone).
(4) If distributive: universalising 'I will kill anyone who threatens me' yields only that each agent may kill their own threats — no contradiction in conception.
(5) If collective: universalising the same maxim yields that every agent must kill anyone who threatens anyone — which seems practically impossible and perhaps contradictory in will.
(6) So on the distributive reading, the test allows far too much (murder, free-riding, etc.); on the collective reading, it forbids far too much (even self-defence becomes impossible because of cross-interference).
(7) Kant clearly wants to allow self-defence but forbid murder, yet neither reading delivers that result consistently.
(8) Therefore the Formula of Universal Law proves too much (or too little), and the only way out is to smuggle in substantive moral content about which reading applies when — which defeats the purpose of a formal test.
