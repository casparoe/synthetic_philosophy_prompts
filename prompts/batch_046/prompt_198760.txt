Write an essay on whether split-brain patients actually host two conscious minds, or if the unity of consciousness is just disrupted in some weirder, less binary way. Don't just rehash the classic Sperry experiments with the left hand and the right hemisphere not knowing what the left eye saw—everyone's read that. Instead, weigh a weirder scenario: imagine a patient, years post-callosotomy, who has trained both hemispheres to co-write answers using a shared keyboard, but they still report feeling "like one person" even when the left hand deliberately types contradictory answers. Does this kind of long-term adaptive coordination count as evidence that consciousness was always one but fragmented in its expression, or does it show two minds learning to fake unity? You need to defend an actual position—maybe that unity is a practical achievement not a metaphysical given, or that there's still exactly one subject of experience but with a split narrative stream, or that we should just say there are two and the feeling of oneness is an illusion maintained by the verbal hemispere. Reach a verdict and justify it, but don't end with "it depends on how you define consciousness." Be specific about what would have to be true about subjective exprience for your view to hold.
