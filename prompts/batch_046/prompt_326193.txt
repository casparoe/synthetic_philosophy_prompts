As a student in an early modern philosophy course, I have been tracking the long-running dispute over Berkeley’s so-called master argument—the claim that attempting to conceive of a sensible object existing unperceived necessarily fails, because in the very act of conception one forms an idea of the object and thereby perceives it in thought. For decades, critics have objected that one can abstract away from one’s own perceptual relation to the object and conceive a tree, for instance, without conceiving oneself seeing it; defenders have countered that such abstraction either smuggles in a perceiver or collapses into an empty placeholder rather than a genuinely conceived object. The exchange seems to recycle the same rejoinders without resolution, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not simply about the psychology of imagination, but conceals a deeper disagreement about what kind of mental representation can license a modal claim—specifically, whether conceivability requires what I will call direct conception, in which one positively represents an object with its intrinsic determinacy while merely withholding the perceptual relation, or negative conception, in which one represents the object only through the exclusion of perception, leaving its intrinsic content underspecified. Each side, I suspect, treats one of these as the default case without arguing for that priority, so that critics test Berkeley with direct-conception scenarios and defenders reject those tests on the grounds that they have secretly become negative-conception exercises.

But is this impasse primarily verbal—turning on ambiguous uses of “conceive” and “abstract”? Is it substantive, rooted in competing modal epistemologies about whether possibility tracks positively articulated mental content? Or are the two sides answering different questions: one about the phenomenological limits of human ideation, the other about the metaphysical conditions for mind-independent existence? Could there be a hidden formal issue—perhaps about whether negative representations can ground possibility judgments at all, or about whether conceivability is a reliable guide to possibility only for representations that preserve a specific kind of determinacy—that neither side has made explicit? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it require a sharp conceptual distinction between direct and negative conception applied systematically across modal arguments; a reframing of the question away from “can we conceive X unperceived?” toward “what determinacy must a representation possess to support an unperceived-existence claim”; or is the dispute so structurally entrenched that no internal philosophical move should be expected to resolve it, and if so, why?
