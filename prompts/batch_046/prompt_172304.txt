An unnamed philosophy-of-economics blog post offers the following gloss on Harsanyi’s 1955 aggregation result: Harsanyi proved that if every member of a group has von Neumann–Morgenstern preferences over lotteries, and if the group’s collective preferences satisfy those same axioms together with the Pareto principle applied ex-ante (before any uncertainty resolves), then there exists a social welfare function that is a linear weighted sum of the individual cardinal utility functions. The proof requires that all agents share identical subjective probabilities over outcomes; if beliefs diverge, the representation fails unless one shifts to an ex-post framework with state-dependent weights. Consequently, the result is often read as dissolving the interpersonal-comparison problem: the weights are merely formal scaling parameters determined by each agent’s risk aversion, not ethical judgments about whose welfare counts more, and no independent moral argument about distribution is needed.

This sounds authoritative, but I am suspicious of the claim that identical probabilities are strictly required, of the idea that the weights depend on risk aversion, and of the conclusion that ethical judgment is eliminated entirely.
