I'm on the acquisitions committee for our university museum and we have a weird donation case. An anonymous donor is offering us a major sound installation by Elias Vorn—he was convicted a few years ago of forging informed-consent documents for clinical trials. The piece, Echoes of Consent, is built from audio recordings of people reading those forged docs aloud, layered over hospital ambience. The donor requires us to display it within six months or return it; there's no option to accept and store.

Some members want to reject it because the work's aesthetic power depends on recognizing the same deception that caused real harm—there's an argument about moral contamination through the material substrate, that the taint isn't separable from the form. Others say we should judge art on it's own terms and that displaying doesn't excuse Vorn, plus returning it looks like censorship.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course—accept with conditions, reject with timeline, whatever—and include an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the other side. Don't just cite philosophers; work through the case.
