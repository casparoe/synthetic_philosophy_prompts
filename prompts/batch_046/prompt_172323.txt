I'm a doctoral student in comparative memory studies, and I have noticed a recurring configuration across the four cases I work on—Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's postwar commemorative architecture, Turkey's contested acknowledgment dynamics around 1915, and the American South's heritage and monument culture—that I cannot map cleanly onto existing vocabulary. In each case, there is a densely institutionalized framework for confronting one traumatic past (the Holocaust and its educational and restorative apparatus; Hiroshima and official war-victim narratives; the state's evolving position on Armenian memory; Confederate commemoration and Lost Cause heritage tourism). Alongside it exists a structurally linked but institutionally thinner framework for a second trauma occurring within the same state-building or imperial history (colonial genocide in German Southwest Africa; the comfort women system and forced Korean labor under Japanese empire; the Kurdish conflict and its entanglement with Armenian remembrance in Turkey; slavery and Jim Crow in the postbellum South). The second framework is not denied: monuments, official apologies, and museum exhibitions may exist. What distinguishes the pattern is that the first framework's completeness—its budgets, legal mandates, and accumulated moral credibility—functions as a kind of saturation mechanism. Demands to build parallel institutional density for the second trauma are answered not by disputing the harm, but by treating the state's already-demonstrated remorse as sufficient, so that further structural investment appears redundant, divisive, or politically opportunistic. The two traumas are not arranged in a simple hierarchy of suffering, nor is the second actively suppressed; rather, the maturation of the first commemorative regime seems to immunize the state against replicating that maturation elsewhere. Does this configuration have an established name—distinct from competitive victimhood, mnemonic hegemony, or straightforward denial? If not, what precisely are its boundary conditions: must both traumas be state-acknowledged, or can the pattern hold when one framework is primarily civil-societal? And what separates it from cases where remembrance genuinely competes for limited political resources?
