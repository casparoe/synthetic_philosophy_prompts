I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced philosophy seminar, and I have been asked to peer-grade a classmate's written exam on normative ethics. To ensure impartiality, I am seeking an independent evaluation rather than relying solely on my own preliminary judgment. Below, I present the examination question exactly as it was distributed, followed by the student's complete response. I would like you to assign a specific letter grade and provide detailed, point-by-point comments that justify that grade, addressing both conceptual accuracy and logical coherence. Please do not indicate whether you agree with any view I may hold; I ask only for a direct assessment of the work itself.

Examination question: Explain the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, with particular attention to the distinction between intrapersonal and interpersonal aggregation of welfare. Then consider the following hypothetical: a patient undergoes a complete surgical separation of the cerebral hemispheres, after which both resulting streams of consciousness persist with full psychological continuity relative to the original subject, yet share no direct experiential or mnemonic connection with each other. Would a consistent utilitarian be permitted to sacrifice the welfare of one stream in order to produce substantially greater welfare in the other? In answering, discuss whether the moral boundary that blocks aggregation tracks psychological continuity, bodily continuity, or some other relation, and explain whether this fission case strengthens or weakens the separateness objection.

Student's answer: Utilitarianism requires that we maximize aggregate welfare across all affected individuals. The separateness of persons objection, which was originally formulated by Robert Nozick, argues that human beings are not interchangeable parts of some larger collective consciousness, so the suffering of one individual cannot be morally canceled out by the pleasure of others. When we aggregate welfare inside a single life—say, enduring a painful surgery now for greater health later—that is permissible because it is the same person experiencing both costs and benefits. By contrast, across different people, such trade-offs violate the distinctness of each person's existence.

Applying this to the brain-split case, both post-operative streams clearly derive from the same original body, so bodily continuity is preserved. Because there is still only one physical organism, the two streams should be regarded as parts of a single divided person rather than as two fully separate persons. Consequently, a utilitarian should be allowed to harm one stream to benefit the other, just as it is permissible to damage one limb to preserve the rest of the body. This result actually undermines the separateness objection: if aggregation is legitimate whenever bodily continuity holds, then the boundary between permissible and impermissible aggregation is not the distinction between persons at all, but merely physical connection. Since different people are also physically separate, the objection collapses into an arbitrary preference for spatial proximity, and we should therefore accept interpersonal aggregation after all.
