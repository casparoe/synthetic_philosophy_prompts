I serve as the procedural referee for a cooperative governance charter that contains Article X: any charter provision, including Article X itself, may be amended by a three-fourths vote of the membership, with no external validating authority. The membership is now proposing amendments that alter the amendment mechanism itself or introduce temporary override powers, and I need a reusable decision procedure to evaluate them. Please translate the conceptual analysis of legal self-reference—specifically the distinction between a rule altering its own content versus altering its own authority to alter, the temporal-indexing problem when a rule governs its own future instances, and the bootstrap paradox in self-authorizing amendments—into an ordered, sequential checklist of conceptual tests I can run on any proposal. For each step, state the precise question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether a failure is fatal (the procedure stops with rejection) or delegable (it flags a values-based decision for the membership, not the referee). Mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated. The procedure must handle cases where an amendment applies to itself either directly or through an intermediate clause. Then apply it to two proposals: first, an amendment lowering the Article X threshold to a simple majority, but only for amendments that contain an automatic expiration date; second, an amendment creating an "emergency suspension" clause that allows the current governing council to suspend any charter provision, including this clause, by unanimous vote, with no temporal limit. Ensure that at least one application produces an answer the membership is likely to find unwelcome. Lead your response with its bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
