I'm delivering the retirement toast for a senior risk analyst and decision theorist who spent her career insisting that every model needs an explicit stopping rule—not because she feared uncertainty, but because she recognized that without one, expected-value reasoning collapses into fanaticism, where arbitrarily small probabilities of unbounded outcomes dominate every finite choice. About half the guests are quantitative researchers who will immediately recognize the formal problem; the other half are family and friends with no background in decision theory. Write a roughly five-minute toast that makes her insistence on stopping rules the central structural theme: explain, in terms accessible to the non-experts, how EV maximization with unbounded utilities allows negligible probabilities to swamp all practical considerations, and argue that her refusal to let calculations run past the resolution of the world was therefore not timidity but a kind of intellectual integrity. For the experts in the room, the toast should quietly invoke the idea that lexical or threshold commitments might be the only coherent response to fanaticism, without turning into a seminar. Include one affectionate, concrete detail or joke that works for both audiences—something that references the logic without explaining it—and avoid framing her as conventionally risk-averse; she took enormous risks, she just refused to let arithmetic pretend it could measure the infinite.
