I am preparing a fifty-minute tutorial for a small group of philosophy undergraduates who have completed an introductory course on modal logic and possible worlds, yet have not encountered metaphysical grounding or fundamentality in any systematic form. In a recent discussion with a colleague in digital aesthetics, I began to wonder whether the dependency relations we invoke in standard physical cases—such as a statue being grounded in its clay—retain any meaningful analogue in the ontology of reproduced images. I would like the session to revolve around a single carrying scenario: a high-resolution digitised scan of a hand-painted watercolour, in which students must consider whether the displayed pixels are grounded in the original pigment molecules, in the scanner's electronic sensor states, or in some combination of both, and whether altering the resolution affects which layer counts as fundamental. Rather than receiving a direct explanation, I need a teaching plan that specifies the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the structure of dependency becomes intelligible before the example is deployed; the specific properties this digital-image case must possess and must lack if it is to illuminate grounding without collapsing it into mere causation or supervenience; and the misconceptions—particularly that fundamentality is simply a matter of physical smallness, that grounding is always transitive in the manner of causal chains, or that the 'more fundamental' level is invariably the one closer to fundamental physics—that students are likely to import from their prior training in modal logic, together with the exact juncture in the sequence at which each misconception should be pre-empted.

In addition, I require a single, carefully designed check for understanding that I can pose at the close of the session to reveal whether the students have genuinely grasped the idea rather than merely memorised a formal definition. I also ask for an explicit statement of what ought to be deliberately omitted at this level—perhaps the technical debate over well-foundedness, or the Aristotelian conception of ontological priority—and a clear indication that I should openly acknowledge these omissions rather than leaving them implicit. Please present the entire plan in polished, formal British English.
