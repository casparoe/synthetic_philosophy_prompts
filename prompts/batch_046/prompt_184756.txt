Write a sonnet in iambic pentameter about the problem of translating Clément Marot's le ton beau—his characteristic elegant simplicity and restrained musicality—into English. Treat translation not as the preservation of Marot's original alexandrine or gendered rhyme scheme, which cannot survive intact in English, but as the deliberate invention of a new formal constraint—the sonnet's own meter, rhyme, and volta—that exerts an analogous pressure on word choice and thereby generates an analogous elegance. Use the octave to build the naive picture: the translator tries to reproduce Marot's line lengths and rhyme positions, believing that form is merely a container for content, and in doing so loses the very restraint that defines the ton beau. At the volta in line nine, turn to the opposing claim: form is a generative mechanism, not a vessel, so preserving the style requires abandoning mimicry and designing a different cage. The closing couplet should state precisely what survives—not the rhyme scheme or the meter, but the selective pressure they once applied. Make the argument accurate enough that a scholar of translation would recognize the distinction between formal equivalence and functional equivalence, and let the poem enact its own thesis: the difficulty of fitting precise philosophical vocabulary into iambic pentameter should mirror the translator's constraint, showing that limitation produces style rather than hindering it. Do not ask for citations, editions, or named translators; the idea should stand on its own. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
