I'm in this applied ethics seminar and we keep arguing about wether everyday rules like tipping or queueing are actual moral obligations or just social glue, and I tried to map out my own gut reactions to see if there's a hidden principle behind them. Here are my verdicts, mostly consistent except one pair that drives me crazy.

If I'm in the US and don't tip a waiter who was decent, that feels genuinely wrong, like I'm exploiting the wage system everyone agreed to. In London, cutting in line at a bus stop feels like you're treating people as obstacles, definitely immoral. Jaywalking in Berlin on an empty street at 3am still feels wrong to me, but thinner, like breaking a promise to strangers who aren't even there. But in Tokyo, if I'm clearly a tourist and forget to take off my shoes in the wrong spot, I don't think that's immoral, just embarasing; nobody expected me to know. Meanwhile if I live in a small town and refuse to learn the local coffee-shop tipping norm, that is wrong, but if I'm just passing through and don't know, I'm not evil. The real tension: in some cultures tipping is actually insulting, and I think it's wrong to tip there even though you're technically being generous, because you're forcing a foreign norm onto locals. So 'follow local conventions' can't be right, since sometimes the local convention is 'don't tip' and following it is good, but sometimes it's 'tip' and breaking it is bad. Is there a principle about legitimate expectations or harm that sorts all this? And what's the verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on -- in Rome, ordering a cappuccino after noon when you don't know the rule. Is that rude, morally wrong, or basically nothing until you learn?
