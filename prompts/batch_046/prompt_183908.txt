Exam question: Consider "two-layer tranquilism," the thesis that intrinsic well-being consists solely in the absence of first-order suffering and of second-order craving—that is, craving directed at one's own absence of craving. A critic argues that this view cannot distinguish between a permanently anesthetized human who altogether lacks consciousness and an enlightened sage who has achieved lasting equanimity, since neither experiences suffering nor meta-craving. Evaluate this objection. In particular, does the tranquilist have resources to block the identification without conceding that consciousness or any positive mental state carries intrinsic value?

Student answer: Two-layer tranquilism is essentially Schopenhauer's doctrine that all value lies in the negation of the will, since Schopenhauer held that positive mental states never contribute to well-being and that only the cessation of wanting matters. Because the view defines well-being purely negatively, it applies to any being that lacks suffering and meta-craving, including unconscious ones. So the critic is right: the tranquilist cannot distinguish the sage from the anesthetized patient. To avoid this, the tranquilist might reply that only beings capable of suffering can possess well-being, but this secretly imports a positive valuation of consciousness, which contradicts the core claim that only absence matters. Furthermore, second-order craving is really just a subtype of first-order suffering, so the two-layer distinction collapses into ordinary negative hedonism. The view therefore fails unless it abandons its defining commitment.
