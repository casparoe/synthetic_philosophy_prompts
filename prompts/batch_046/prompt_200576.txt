For my seminar on collective memory and political defeat, I am developing a concept I call indexical sovereignty—the claim that a nation’s authority over its past depends less on what happened than on who controls the categorical labels applied to its losses. Could you write a short story, set in the bureaucratic archives of a fictional state, in which a junior curator is asked to design a joint exhibition about two historical moments: a siege-ending collective suicide that was later narrated as sovereign refusal rather than defeat, and a strategic maritime evacuation that was reframed as democratic resilience rather than retreat. The curator discovers a misfiled administrative record showing that both events were originally catalogued under the same internal code for tactical withdrawal, and that their separation into morally distinct categories—martyrdom versus survival—was introduced decades later through a routine revision of the filing taxonomy. The narrative should follow her quiet decision about whether to restore the original classification or preserve the current one, and it should implicitly pose the philosophical question of whether there is any fact of the matter about whether a defeat is a tragedy or a triumph, or whether that distinction is produced entirely by the archive’s indexing system. I would prefer a subdued, procedural tone rather than dramatic heroism, with the protagonist more concerned with cataloguing protocols than with political activism, and I would like the story to end without resolving whether her choice reveals a hidden truth or merely imposes a new fiction.
