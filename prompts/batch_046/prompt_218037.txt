I have been asked to design a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in comparative politics, to be delivered next month, on performance legitimacy—specifically, whether sustained prosperity and public order can, by themselves, justify authoritarian governance, taking China and Singapore as the paired cases. The topic has become personally urgent for me since I recently returned from an extended research stay in Singapore, where I observed how the Housing Development Board's rapid delivery schedules and the state's low crime statistics seemed to generate among residents a tacit willingness to accept strict limits on public assembly and civic association—something I now want to understand conceptually rather than anecdotally. I am not looking for the lecture itself, but for a teaching plan: the dependency structure, the carrying example, the misconception map, the check for understanding, the explicit exclusions, and the insertion of one niche mechanism.

Please structure your response around the following.

First, the dependency structure. Before the students can evaluate whether prosperity justifies authoritarian rule, what conceptual distinction must already be in place? I am considering framing the distinction between input legitimacy (procedural authorization) and output legitimacy (substantive delivery), but I want to know what must precede even that—perhaps a clarification of how consent can be structural and tacit rather than explicit. Specify the exact order in which these layers should be introduced, and which layer depends on which.

Second, the carrying example. Select one concrete case that must bear the full conceptual weight of the session. The case should illustrate that material delivery and political closure are not parallel tracks but structurally coupled. I am inclined to build around Singapore's Housing Development Board queue system and its correlation with restrictions on assembly permits, but I want to know precisely what this example must display and what it must lack—should it avoid direct comparison to Western welfare states, for instance? If a different carrying case serves the dependency structure better, propose it and justify the substitution.

Third, the misconception map. Identify the misconceptions this audience will predictably form, in the likely order of appearance: for example, (a) that performance legitimacy is merely state-level utilitarianism; (b) that it requires citizens to actively endorse the regime; (c) that digital or financial infrastructure—such as China's grid-management (wangge) welfare-delivery interface or Singapore's Central Provident Fund withdrawal rules tied to housing equity—are neutral administrative tools rather than mechanisms that embed dependency. For each misconception, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off—before the example is introduced, during its exposition, or after.

Fourth, a check for understanding. Provide one precise end-of-session question or brief applied exercise that reveals whether the students have grasped the concept rather than memorized the comparison. The check should require them to apply the input/output distinction to a regime or hypothetical not discussed in the session.

Fifth, explicit exclusions. State clearly which related frameworks or analogues I should openly set aside at this level and say so—such as Habermasian discourse ethics, the full literature on authoritarian resilience, or historiographic analogies to the Chinese Mandate of Heaven—and explain why leaving them out protects the dependency structure rather than simplifying it dishonestly.

Sixth, the niche injection. Choose one under-discussed mechanism—either Singapore's asset-based dependency through Central Provident Fund withdrawal rules tied to housing equity, or China's wangge system's transformation of welfare delivery into a surveillance interface—and show exactly where it enters the arc, which misconception it corrects, and what distortion it risks if overemphasized.

Please deliver this as a polished teaching plan, not as an explanation of the concept to me, and do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or citations.
