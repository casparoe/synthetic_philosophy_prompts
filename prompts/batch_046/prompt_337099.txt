I'm trying to figure out what makes an ontological dispute merely verbal rather than substantive, by working through my gut reactions to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

First, when a museum conservator and a philosopher of art disagree over whether a rebuilt temple pillar—every original stone replaced, but the architectural form and spatial position preserved—is "the same pillar," I think the dispute is verbal. They agree on all physical and historical facts; they differ only over whether "same object" requires material continuity or structural-functional continuity.

Second, when two legal theorists disagree about whether a multinational corporation has "moral agency"—one treating it as a nexus of contracts with no collective intentions, the other attributing derived but real intentional states—I think this is not verbal. They agree on every legal fact, but they disagree about what obligations follow, and that seems like a disagreement about the world rather than about language.

Third, when historians of chemistry debate whether "phlogiston" referred to nothing at all (pure error theory) or referred to oxidative processes under a mistaken theoretical description, I think this is verbal. The chemical facts are settled; the dispute is about the correct semantic rule for assigning reference to terms embedded in superseded theories.

Fourth, when two metaphysicians disagree about whether "composite objects exist"—one accepting universal composition, the other accepting only simple parts arranged object-wise—but they agree on every empirical observation and on the predictive content of physics, I think this is not verbal, though I keep sliding back and forth. They seem to be disagreeing about what there is, not about how to use "exists." Yet since every observable consequence is identical, I sometimes think it's verbal after all.

Fifth, there's a pair I can't reconcile. When anthropologists disagree about whether a specific ritual mask, used only in closed ceremonies and never displayed, counts as "art"—with one applying a functionalist definition tied to aesthetic intention and the other applying an institutional definition tied to display practices—I think that's verbal: it's about the extension of "art." But when ecologists and economists disagree about whether "a forest" is one ecosystem or a collection of individual organisms—agreeing on every biological interaction but disagreeing on the correct unit of explanation—I think that's substantive, not verbal, even though the structure looks identical to the mask case.

So: is there a coherent principle that delivers these verdicts? Does it depend on whether the disputants share all non-semantic beliefs, on whether there's a hidden normative disagreement, or on whether one side could translate the other without loss? What does it predict for a case I haven't settled—two architects disagreeing whether a building designed by an AI but executed entirely by human labor is "authored by the AI," where they agree on every causal chain but differ on whether intentionality can be distributed across non-biological systems?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause handles each of my five cases, and be honest about which verdict it forces me to overturn if I want to keep the principle simple. If there's a more complex principle that saves all five, present it too, but say which feature of the case does the work and whether a defender of the overturned view could attack that feature.
