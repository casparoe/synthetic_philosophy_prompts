Student answer:

"Jaspers distinguishes four kinds of guilt: criminal guilt attaches to specific law-breakers; political guilt belongs collectively to citizens for the actions of their state; moral guilt concerns individual conscience and intention; and metaphysical guilt arises when one fails to show solidarity with others suffering injustice. For Jaspers, both criminal and political guilt can be collective, whereas moral and metaphysical guilt are strictly personal and cannot be shared across a group. When we ask whether a nation can be guilty for past generations' acts, political guilt offers the clearest path: citizens inherit the benefits and institutions of their state, so they share in its collective political responsibility across time. By contrast, metaphysical guilt requires a direct, personal failure of solidarity; it cannot be structurally inherited simply because one lives in a society built on past wrongs. The recent proposal of 'structural metaphysical guilt'—the idea that descendants bear a metaphysical debt because they continue to benefit from unjust structures—therefore confuses an individual moral condition with a collective political one. On this view, descendants do not personally fail in solidarity merely by existing within inherited institutions, so metaphysical guilt does not transfer. The correct conclusion is that a nation can bear collective political guilt for historical injustices, but metaphysical guilt remains confined to those who personally failed to act, meaning intergenerational transmission is impossible in that register."
