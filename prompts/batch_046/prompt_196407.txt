Having recently found myself negotiating the boundary between necessary labor and reclaimed rest after a prolonged period of overwork, I want a poem that treats the Protestant work ethic, the institutionalized 35-hour week, and karoshi not as historical footnotes but as three competing claims on the same temporal body. Write a Shakespearean sonnet in American English in which the octave establishes work as moral vocation and the 35-hour week as a negotiated concession, the volta at line nine introduces karoshi not as an aberration but as the logical extremity of the same logic, and the closing couplet leaves the three claims—calling, boundary, death—in suspension without resolving whether leisure is a right or a privilege. The rhyme scheme and meter should be regular, but the conceptual architecture should do the structural work: the turn must feel inevitable rather than inserted, and the rhyme words themselves should echo the tension between obligation and limit. Avoid bibliographic references, citations, or verbatim quotations from sociological texts; render the positions with enough precision that someone familiar with the sociology of labor and the Japanese concept of death by overwork recognizes them, yet frame everything through the urgency of a lived contradiction rather than an abstract taxonomy.
