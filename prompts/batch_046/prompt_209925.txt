As a retired field ecologist who has recently begun reading moral philosophy in earnest, I have been struck by the debate over whether habitat destruction reduces wild-animal suffering, and I find the standard framing—biodiversity loss versus welfare gains from reduced predation and competition—far too coarse. It treats ecosystems as uniform welfare containers and ignores what I will call the microhabitat thermal-stress asymmetry: in fragmented canopies, edge effects raise ground-level temperature variance, which for small ectotherms, particularly soil nematodes and larval amphibians, produces chronic physiological distress that may outweigh any welfare gains from lower predator density. At the same time, there is what I term the trophic-release injury hypothesis: mesopredator suppression in degraded patches may not merely lower mortality but increase the prevalence of non-fatal wounding among surviving prey, generating sustained suffering rather than simple survival benefits. Given these dynamics, I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Among the following open lines of inquiry—(1) developing welfare-weighted metrics for ecosystem health that integrate chronic physiological stress rather than mortality alone; (2) resolving whether predator-release dynamics in degraded habitats produce net suffering increases via injury and displacement rather than straightforward survival gains; (3) clarifying the moral status of invertebrate welfare in conservation calculus, given that thermal stress disproportionately affects nematodes and arthropods whose sentience remains philosophically contested; and (4) assessing whether habitat destruction’s net welfare effect is path-dependent or reversible, with direct implications for restoration ethics—which two or three matter most for a researcher with about three years, a background in ecology rather than formal welfare economics, and no capacity to collect new field data? Please rank them and defend the ordering with explicit criteria—such as tractability without fresh empirical work, dependence on prior conceptual resolution, practical import for conservation policy, and neglectedness in the current literature—while explaining what evidence or argument would cause you to reorder them. I am writing a long-form essay and want to know which questions, if settled, would genuinely change how conservation biologists and ethicists talk about degradation, rather than those that merely add nuance.
