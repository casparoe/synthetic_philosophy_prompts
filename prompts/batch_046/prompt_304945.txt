I'm writing for a comparative political economy course and have come to accept the view that the Nordic model is not primarily an exportable welfare-state package—high taxes, universal provision, strong centralized bargaining—but rather a path-dependent equilibrium sustained by a pre-political substrate of Lutheran-influenced social trust, small-state scale, and an early-twentieth-century corporatist settlement that compressed wage differentials through coordinated industrial relations rather than through redistribution alone. On this account, attempts to replicate the model in large, pluralistic societies fail not at the level of policy design or union density, but because the requisite homogeneous trust environment and institutional memory of class compromise are absent, rendering high-tax compliance and universal benefits politically unstable regardless of formal institutional copying. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward: empirical cases where heterogeneous societies have sustained comparable outcomes without that substrate; theoretical arguments that universal social policy can generate the trust it requires rather than depending on it ex ante; comparative evidence that immigration and fiscal strain are now eroding the Nordic equilibrium from within; and whether defenders risk insulating the claim by defining any successful replication as not genuinely Nordic, with an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a legitimate conceptual boundary or a fatal flaw. Which of these objections would a committed proponent concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss?
