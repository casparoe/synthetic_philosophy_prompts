For a philosophy assignment, I am examining whether moral reasoning about harm can be reduced to arithmetic aggregation, and I want to check whether my intuitions form a coherent pattern rather than an inconsistent cluster. Below are my judgments about cases involving severe concentrated suffering versus widely distributed minor suffering.

I believe that torturing one innocent person for an extended period is morally worse than allowing a billion people each to experience a momentary, barely perceptible irritation—say, a dust speck in the eye—even though the summed discomfort in the latter scenario is astronomically larger. I also think that distributing a fixed quantity of extreme pain across so many people that no individual feels more than a negligible fraction does not morally equal the same total pain experienced by a single person; the concentrated case retains a special gravity that arithmetic does not capture. At the same time, I suspect that if the distributed harm becomes non-negligible—if every person on Earth suffers a noticeable but mild headache continuously for decades—the collective harm might eventually surpass the wrong of one torture session, though I cannot locate the precise boundary. Furthermore, I maintain that causing one catastrophic trauma is worse than causing a trillion people to experience a trivial annoyance they immediately forget, regardless of summed totals. Yet I also imagine a technological scenario in which preventing a single act of torture requires imposing an infinitesimal, unconscious discomfort on every sentient being in existence; my intuition is still to prevent the torture, but if the distributed discomfort were just slightly more perceptible—say, a faint, subliminal itch—my judgment might reverse. This suggests my reasoning depends on something other than pure addition, perhaps on the qualitative intensity of harm, on the moral significance of individual experience, or on a threshold that separates imperceptible from perceptible suffering.

Can you identify a principle that captures all, or most, of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which of my judgments it supports and which it forces me to reject, and apply it to a case I have not listed: ten thousand people each enduring a moderate, clearly noticeable pain for one hour, versus one person enduring that same one-hour moderate pain divided into slices so small that none are individually perceived. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want only the conceptual analysis and an honest account of where the principle is silent.
