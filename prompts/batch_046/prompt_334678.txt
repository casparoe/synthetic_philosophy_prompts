As a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up philosophy of collective judgment, I wish to understand the Condorcet jury theorem and its standard limitations—especially the independence conditions and the problem of individual competence falling below one-half—restated entirely in the vocabulary of measurement science: detectors, systematic versus random error, calibration curves, ensemble averaging, replication studies, and meta-analytic pooling of heterogeneous instruments with non-uniform precision and known bias distributions.

Please translate the theorem’s core claim and its principal objections into those terms, as faithfully as the mapping allows. Then provide a residue report: which philosophical assertions carry over cleanly into statistical aggregation or instrument calibration, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart whatsoever in experimental measurement theory. In particular, address whether the philosophical worry about dependency among voters corresponds precisely to correlated errors in a multi-laboratory replication study, or whether something essential is lost.

Finally, assess whether the untranslatable residue indicates a genuine conceptual gap in how the natural sciences treat collective judgment, or whether it suggests that part of the original philosophical argument derives its apparent substance from formal presentation rather than from content that survives rigorous translation.
