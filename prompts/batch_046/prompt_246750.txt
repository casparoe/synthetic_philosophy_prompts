I'm working on an essay for a seminar on the philosophy of political judgment, and I'd like your comments on both its accuracy and whether the framing is genuinely illuminating or merely ornate. The essay addresses the familiar complaint that policymakers misuse historical analogies—Munich and appeasement, Vietnam and quagmire, the Thucydides trap and great-power rivalry. Rather than treating these as failures of similarity judgment, I argue that each analogy smuggles in an implicit causal ontology that determines what counts as a relevant parallel. The Munich analogy presupposes that aggressor psychology is causally uniform across regimes and eras, so that yielding to one expansionist leader mechanistically invites further demands. The Vietnam analogy presupposes institutional path-dependence: bureaucratic inertia, asymmetric local knowledge, and mission creep operate like recurring structural forces regardless of strategic intent. The Thucydides trap presupposes systemic determinism—power transitions generate conflict not primarily through particular leaders but through positional dynamics in an anarchic system. My claim is that when analysts debate whether a rising power is another Munich or another Thucydides case, they are rarely arguing about empirical resemblance; they are talking past one another because they have adopted incompatible metaphysics of causation without defending them. I propose a preliminary framework—what I call a causal ontology audit—that asks, before applying any analogy, whether the case is being read through agent-centered, institutional-inertial, or structural-systemic causation, and whether that ontology is warranted by the target situation.

I'm particularly unsure whether the distinction between Vietnam-style institutional inertia and Munich-style psychological uniformity holds up, or whether I'm imposing philosophical taxonomy on what is really just a difference in rhetorical emphasis. I also worry that my framing makes the Thucydides trap look more deterministic than its proponents intend, since some formulations allow for diplomatic mitigation that seems agent-centered after all. I'd welcome specific suggestions on where the argument feels strained, whether the audit framework adds clarity or just jargon, and whether there is an obvious counterexample—perhaps from economic history or scientific reasoning—where analogies fail for reasons that my three-part taxonomy misses entirely. Finally, does the essay risk being unfalsifiable, since any failed analogy could be explained away as a causal-ontology mismatch rather than a genuine error in reasoning?
