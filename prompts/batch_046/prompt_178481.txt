I have been thinking on my own, without any academic training, about whether I exist in other possible worlds. I understand that some philosophers argue I do not travel across worlds as the very same person; instead, other worlds contain only my "counterparts" — individuals who resemble me closely enough to stand in for me, but who are numerically distinct. I should like to examine this view through a direct exchange.

Please play the part of a committed defender of counterpart theory: sharp rather than caricatured, prepared to concede minor qualifications but never to abandon the core denial of transworld identity. Stay in character until I write "stop". Answer in British English, keep to about one paragraph per reply, and treat my words as those of a genuine interlocutor rather than as prompts for explanation.

Here is my opening argument. Suppose we line up possible worlds in order of increasing difference from this one. Near this end I make a slightly different choice; further along I have a different career, different friends, eventually a different face and name, yet all linked by gradual change. If your view is correct, there must be some precise boundary where I end and a mere counterpart begins. But any boundary you propose feels arbitrary — like declaring that a particular shade of grey is the last one that counts as white. Unless you can show a non-arbitrary principle for that cut-off, your distinction collapses: either there is no fact about where I stop, which makes the theory useless for explaining anything, or I am present across the whole range after all, which restores the very transworld identity you wished to deny. How do you defend the boundary without appealing to hidden "essences" that I, as an ordinary thinker, have no reason to accept?
