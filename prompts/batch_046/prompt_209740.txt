I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind writing a chapter on the dialectics of Gödelian anti-mechanism—specifically the Lucas–Penrose family of arguments and their recent afterlife in debates about artificial cognition. In both classic texts and contemporary discussions about whether transformer architectures can transcend their own formalization, I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I lack a precise label for, and I want to determine whether the literature already names it or whether I ought to coin a term with explicit boundaries.

The pattern unfolds like this. A proponent begins with the classic Gödelian claim: any consistent formal system rich enough to represent arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, yet human mathematicians can recognize that consistency (and even grasp the truth of associated Gödel sentences), so human cognition is not capturable within any single formal system. When pressed—say, by noting that human mathematicians are fallible, sometimes miss inconsistencies, or that recognizing consistency might be a heuristic skill rather than metaphysical transcendence—the proponent retreats to a more defensible structural position. They say the argument is not about infallibility but about the mind's capacity to step outside any given formal framework and adopt a stronger one, something allegedly impossible for a system wholly defined within one framework. Once the critic accepts this framing and begins analyzing whether framework-shifting could be mechanized—perhaps via hierarchical architectures or context-sensitive processing—the proponent slides back to the stronger version. They then treat specific instances of human mathematical intuition as direct proof that the mind literally accesses truths lying beyond every possible formalization, using that ontological claim to dismiss mechanist counterexamples without addressing whether framework-shifting actually requires non-mechanical resources.

I've observed this in discussions of Penrose's non-computability thesis, in seminar debates about Lucas's original formulation, and in recent arguments about whether large language models can "step outside" their weights. What distinguishes it, I think, is that it is not exactly a motte-and-bailey: the two positions are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension linked only by shared slogan, but structurally related claims about epistemic access and ontological status that share Gödelian vocabulary. Nor is it straightforward equivocation, since the speaker genuinely sees the versions as continuous. Is there an established concept—perhaps in argumentation theory or philosophy of science—that captures this specific oscillation between an epistemic capacity claim and an ontological transcendence claim, masked by ambiguity about what "seeing" a Gödel sentence entails?
