I'm presenting at our department's moral philosophy seminar a thesis I'll call Baseline Indeterminacy: in large-scale collective harm cases—take climate mitigation—supererogation is structurally inapplicable to individual actions because there is no determinate "call of duty" against which to measure going beyond it. Until a collective coordination mechanism fixes each agent's fair share, the boundary between obligatory, permissible, and supererogatory contribution remains undefined; so an individual who drastically reduces her carbon footprint isn't performing a supererogatory act—she's either fulfilling an unspecified portion of a distributed obligation, or, if the collective baseline is already exceeded, acting within a range of moral indifference. The audience is six philosophers: three Kantian ethicists who work with perfect and imperfect duties and worry about moral fragmentation; two consequentialists who frame obligations in terms of threshold effects and expected-utility baselines; and one specialist in collective action who accepts fair-share reasoning but insists that supererogation can survive at the margins once shares are fixed.
