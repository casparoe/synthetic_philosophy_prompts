I'm a doctoral student in comparative moral philosophy working on classical Chinese conceptions of human nature. At a recent workshop, two interlocutors—let's call them A and B—debated whether the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi is best interpreted as an empirical dispute about innate psychological structures or as a normative disagreement about the conditions under which dispositions become genuinely moral.

A opened by claiming that Mencius's four sprouts represent an early form of moral nativism: they are not merely potentialities but have an intrinsic directional tendency toward virtue, comparable to a biological seed's tendency toward a specific mature form. B countered that this imposes modern essentialism on the text; for Mencius, the sprouts are pre-moral affective responses that acquire moral status only through social cultivation, so the language of natural growth functions as rhetorical motivation rather than ontological description.

A then argued that if B is correct, the difference between Mencius and Xunzi collapses into a trivial empirical point—whether initial raw material is good or bad—since both agree that cultivation is necessary. B replied that the difference is not empirical but conceptual and normative: for Xunzi, ritual and learning construct the very categories of moral evaluation, meaning there is no pre-cultural moral nature to discover, only social tendencies that ritual organizes into virtue or vice. Therefore, the dispute concerns whether moral norms are found in human dispositions or produced by institutional practice.

A raised what he called a circularity problem for B's view: if ritual constructs virtue and virtue is defined by successful ritual, then Xunzi's justification of ritual by its functional success in sustaining order seems circular, since the measure of success is itself ritual-defined. B answered that the circularity is only apparent: ritual is justified functionally by its contribution to social coordination and human flourishing, not by its production of virtue per se. Moreover, B added that the affective responses Mencius calls sprouts are actually the psychological preconditions that make ritual uptake possible, not innate moral capacities.

They asked me to referee, but I remain genuinely divided about whether their disagreement is primarily empirical, normative, or conceptual, and about whether either has correctly identified the crux involving the relationship between Mencius's cultivated expansive moral feeling and Xunzi's model of accumulated habituation. Please adjudicate move by move: assess A's nativist reading, B's anti-essentialist response, A's collapse objection, B's normative-construction reply, and the exchange about circularity and functional justification. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, identify what both overlook—particularly regarding whether Mencius's account of expansive moral feeling and Xunzi's theory of habituation are genuinely incompatible or describe different stages of the same process—and explain whether settling their dispute requires an empirical finding, a normative commitment, or a conceptual clarification.
