I'm giving the opening remarks at a small public-library workshop called "What We Mean When We Say We Understand." The room holds about half retired teachers and local readers who have encountered some philosophy, and about half neighbors who came for the coffee and because the flyer promised no expertise was required. I am not an academic—I'm a carpenter—but for years I have been turning over Wittgenstein's thought that words do not carry one hidden essence and that meaning arises from use within particular practices, the ones he calls language games, and that categories hang together by family resemblance rather than by a single shared feature. I want these remarks to do real structural work: they should shape what I can and cannot claim about understanding, rather than serving as decoration. In particular, I want to take the idea that "understanding" itself has no essential defining trait—just as "game" does not—and to draw on Wittgenstein's less-discussed distinction in his discussion of reading, where he separates mechanically sounding out words from genuinely understanding a sentence, in order to argue that understanding is not a private mental glow but part of the shared practice we are entering. I also want to acknowledge that the two halves of this room are currently playing different language games—one with philosophical vocabulary, one with ordinary talk—and that the workshop will not succeed by forcing everyone into a single definition, but by letting the family resemblance among our different uses become visible. Please write about four minutes of remarks in my own voice: formal and polished, but clearly spoken by a thoughtful non-expert. The philosophical idea should actually constrain the speech—for instance, it should prevent me from offering one definitive account of understanding—rather than being ornamental. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not put quotation marks around any philosopher's words unless you are certain of the exact wording; paraphrase freely instead. Keep the audience mixed in mind, so the knowledgeable half recognizes the depth without the other half being lost.
