I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a modified Scanlonian contractualism. Her specific proposal is that the aggregation problem should be handled not by allowing numbers to weigh directly, but through what she calls “compound reasonable rejection”: the idea that when a principle permits harm to multiple parties, each affected individual’s standing to reject it is structurally reinforced by the presence of others in an identical position, producing a non-linear scaling of rejectability that is meant to be distinct from simple head-counting. She applies this framework especially to intergenerational risks like climate mitigation, where the potential victims are not yet identifiable individuals. Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press this position rather than asking her to restate it. Include: (1) a question forcing her to distinguish compound reasonable rejection from additive aggregation and explain why the former does not collapse into utilitarian counting; (2) a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to Scanlon’s claim that wrongness is relational rather than comparative, asking how compound rejection avoids smuggling comparative weights back into the contractualist calculus; (3) a question about whether people who do not yet exist can stand in the structural relation required for compound rejection in climate cases, and how she handles the asymmetry between actively harming an existing person and allowing future harm to come about; (4) a follow-up anticipating her distinction between doing and allowing harm, asking whether compound rejection applies with equal force to omissions in climate policy; (5) a closing question asking what kind of conceptual or empirical result could genuinely weaken the claim that compound rejection is a distinct mechanism rather than aggregation in disguise, since both seem to recommend saving the larger group. In your response, separate what is settled about Scanlon’s original contractualist framework from what is genuinely contested or idiosyncratic in this modified view, and do not present either the modification or its interpretation of Scanlon as the consensus among specialists.
