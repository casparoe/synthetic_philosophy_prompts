Consider the position that a valid advance directive retains full, non-derivative moral authority over treatment decisions for the same individual after severe dementia has erased the capacities that made the directive autonomous in the first place—not as a proxy for family judgment, and not because the present patient secretly endorses it, but because a relation of narrative identity between the pre-morbid self and the present patient is robust enough to ground an obligation to honor the earlier preference even when doing so clearly reduces the present patient's experiential welfare and even when the present patient expresses contradictory, apparently sincere desires. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Which assumptions about personal identity over time, the moral weight of a past autonomous act, or the concept of welfare are load-bearing, and which are merely decorative? What empirical findings—say, about the degree to which psychological continuity actually survives in advanced dementia, or about the coherence of values expressed during lucid intervals—would count as decisive defeat rather than as an awkward anomaly? What formal or conceptual arguments—concerning the transitivity of authority across discontinuous consciousness, or the possibility that narrative identity collapses into a mere causal chain—would genuinely undermine the view, and which escape routes are live? I want an honest map of its exposure: does the position risk collapsing into an implausible property-rights model of the body, can it accommodate directives that explicitly authorize revision upon cognitive decline, and what would defenders of this view themselves accept as a genuine defeater rather than a problem they can absorb by qualification? Distinguish evidence that would force abandonment from evidence that would merely require adjustment, and say whether you think the position is well insulated—whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
