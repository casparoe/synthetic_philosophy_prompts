For my high school philosophy seminar's closing session, I am giving a short presentation on how rational belief collapses under its own weight when we try to hold too many of them at once. To open, I would like an original villanelle that treats the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as mirror images of a single structural failure—namely, the impossibility of maintaining a consistent belief threshold once aggregation or closure is applied. The poem must use the standard villanelle form: nineteen lines, five tercets and one quatrain, with refrains A1 and A2 and rhyme scheme A b A1 a b A2 a b A1 a b A2 a b A1 a b A2 A1 A2. The first refrain should capture the lottery reasoning—something like "This ticket will not win; I believe it"—and the second should capture the preface admission—something like "Somewhere in these pages hides an error." These two lines should alternate through the tercets without resolving, representing how each paradox repeats its own logic. In the final quatrain, they must meet side by side to expose the shared hidden move: accepting every individual belief while rejecting the conjunction that logical closure requires. The meter should be roughly iambic pentameter, and the rhyme should be disciplined. Please do not name specific philosophers or include bibliographic references; instead, render the positions through image and argument—high odds against any single ticket, the author's honest fallibilism, the contradiction that arises when all beliefs are summed. The result should be accurate enough that a listener who knows the paradoxes recognizes the logical architecture—the guaranteed exception within a set of near-certain beliefs and the tension with closure—while remaining accessible and emotionally coherent for an audience with no background in epistemology.
