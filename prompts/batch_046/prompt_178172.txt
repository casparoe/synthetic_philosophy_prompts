In constructing fictional societies where transformative technologies outpace the moral architectures meant to contain them, I have found that narrative coherence often demands I resolve a tension that real-world institutions seem determined to leave unresolved. Consider the debate surrounding the alignment tax in advanced artificial intelligence development: the claim that rigorous investment in safety, interpretability, and value alignment imposes meaningful performance and temporal costs, such that any organization bearing the full tax risks being outcompeted and displaced by rivals who deploy faster with lighter safeguards. Proponents of what might be called the competitiveness constraint treat this not as an excuse but as a structural law—an evolutionary filter in which actors who voluntarily accept heavy alignment costs are systematically replaced by those who do not, rendering unilateral ethical restraint a form of organizational suicide. Opponents argue that this framing illegitimately conflates competitive disadvantage with logical impossibility, pointing to anticipatory regulation, differential deployment strategies, or open-weight publication norms as mechanisms that could restructure incentives without requiring perfect universal coordination.
