In labor-policy debates, critics often analogize a union to a monopoly cartel: just as a monopolist restricts supply to raise prices, a union restricts labor supply to raise wages, treating non-union workers as black-market competitors and the strike as withholding inventory. The analogy is deployed to argue that collective bargaining is coercive, that strikes harm consumers and non-members alike, and that right-to-work laws merely restore competitive conditions. Audit this mapping precisely: identify what corresponds to what (cartel members, supply restriction, price mechanism, black-market entry, inventory withholding); locate where it breaks—whether because labor is not a fungible commodity, because union membership is voluntary, because the employer is a monopsonist rather than a price-taker, or because the strike withholds one's own labor rather than a stockpiled good; specify which normative conclusions about coercion and efficiency depend on those broken correspondences; and say whether the analogy can be repaired or whether a different comparison (for instance, a union as democratic countervailing power within an asymmetric employment relationship) carries the normative weight without importing misleading market imagery. Conclude with which conclusions about strikes and the right to organize survive once the disanalogies are explicit.
