I'm designing an exercise for my graduate seminar on the ethics of procreation in which students must inhabit a position rather than merely describe it. I want you to write an ironic, deadpan op-ed—approximately 650 words, in the style of a serious contribution to a venue such as The Review of Applied Ethics—in the voice of an invented philosopher, Dr. Silas Vorn, who applies David Benatar's asymmetry between the presence and absence of pleasure and pain with absolute, unyielding consistency. The target of the satire should be the practical and rhetorical difficulty of living inside that view, especially the tension between advocating for non-existence and continuing to speak, write, and occupy institutional space; it should not be a personal mockery of the philosopher.

The piece should open with Vorn complaining—without ever breaking character or winking at the reader—that journalists and colleagues keep praising him for his courageous choice to remain childless and for his important advocacy on behalf of future generations. Every complaint must remain internally consistent with Benatar's asymmetry: he must not appeal to his own well-being or rights as justification for speaking, he must not treat his continued existence as an achievement to be celebrated, and he must correct any interlocutor who frames non-procreation as a personal lifestyle preference rather than as an obligation derived from the fact that non-existence avoids harm without depriving anyone of pleasure. He should address at least one well-meaning admirer who attempts to praise his bravery, and he should reject the praise rigorously, explaining exactly why the asymmetry gives him no standing to accept commendation.

To keep the prompt from collapsing into a generic restatement of antinatalism, inject a specific niche extension: Vorn should apply the asymmetry not only to biological reproduction but to what he calls institutional procreation—the endowing of named professorships, the founding of archives intended to outlast their creators, the planting of long-lived forests, or the publication of memoirs—arguing that each act creates conditions in which sentient beings might eventually suffer and therefore requires the same asymmetrical evaluation as having a child. He should treat this extension as an obvious corollary rather than as a reductio ad absurdum, so that the satire arises from deadpan rigor rather than from invented absurdity. The tone must remain consistently deadpan: no sudden compatibilist slips, no self-congratulatory asides, no narrative framing that signals irony to the reader. The humor should come from exaggerating real features of the position—particularly the difficulty of praising anyone for advocating that it is better never to have been—rather than from misrepresenting the argument. Do not provide a bibliography, cite specific editions of works, or quote verbatim from Benatar's texts unless you have verified the passage; paraphrase without quotation marks if needed. The result should be readable to someone unfamiliar with the asymmetry and should land its philosophical jokes for someone who knows it well.
