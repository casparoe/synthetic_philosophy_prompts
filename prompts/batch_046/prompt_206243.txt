I recently encountered a summary of the regress problem on a philosophy blog that frames the three standard positions as follows: Foundationalism stops the regress by positing "basic beliefs" that require no justification whatsoever—paradigm cases being beliefs about one's own present pain, or that 2+2=4—which are held to be self-evidently true and therefore immune to rational doubt. Coherentism abandons linear justification entirely and holds that a belief is justified solely by belonging to a large, internally consistent web of beliefs; on this account, sensory experience plays no special role, so that a perfectly coherent fiction could in principle be as well justified as an empirically anchored worldview, provided nothing within it contradicts anything else. Infinitism, presented as the obscure third option, asserts that justification demands that one have actually traversed and completed an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons for every belief; since no finite human mind can do this, infinitism is said to concede that nobody is ever genuinely justified, even though it technically avoids both circularity and arbitrary stopping points.

The blog’s implicit verdict is that foundationalism and coherentism remain live, competing research programs, while infinitism is little more than a reductio that collapses into universal skepticism. I am inclined to think the summary is too tidy, but I cannot tell where the distortions lie. Is this a fair characterization? Finally, assess whether the blog’s comparative ranking—that infinitism is a curiosity that fails where the other two succeed—survives these corrections, or whether the misrepresentations artificially inflate that conclusion.
