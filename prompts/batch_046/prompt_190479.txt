My friend and I have spent the last few hours arguing about whether the legal category of genocide—rooted in Lemkin’s original concept but narrowed in the 1948 Convention—is morally indispensable or politically suffocating. She thinks expanding it to cover structural and cultural annihilation makes it a meaningful moral tool; I think that diluting the specific-intent and protected-group requirements turns it into a useless legal standard and a dangerous rhetorical weapon. Rather than keep talking past each other, we want to see if an adversarial collaboration can isolate what we actually share and where we genuinely split.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps:

- The Strict Constructionists: defenders of the Convention’s narrow scope (the listed protected groups; the specific intent to destroy physically or biologically) who hold that legal precision is itself a moral achievement and that broadening the term erodes the duty to prevent.
- The Structural Expansionists: critics of the Convention’s exclusions (political groups; cultural destruction without direct killing) who hold that group annihilation can occur through engineered structural dissolution and that naming should track moral reality, not prosecutorial convenience.

The statement should be formal, under 800 words, and organized exactly as follows:

1. Agreed formal claims (numbered, 5–7 items, ~150 words): one-sentence claims both sides can sign about the drafting history’s exclusions, about the logical relationship between dilution and exclusion, and about conditions that would count as misapplication for both camps.
2. Divergence table (~250 words): paired entries. For each camp, in wording that camp itself would accept, state the core position (up to 3 sentences) and then the precise crux—the sub-question that, if settled empirically or conceptually, would move that camp.
3. Cruxes and shared tests (~300 words): three deepest cruxes (e.g., whether cultural or linguistic dissolution without physical death can constitute group destruction; whether legal precision requires moral exclusivity; whether the politics of naming should be constrained by what is prosecutable). For each crux, record both sides’ predictions about what evidence or hypothetical case would bear on it. Include this specific hypothetical: a state enacts a universal, mandatory algorithmic-translation policy that eliminates a minority language within two generations, with no physical harm—each side should state in advance whether this counts as genocide, and on exactly which feature of their definition the judgment turns.
4. Residual verbal disputes (~100 words): identify any disagreement that is purely terminological (e.g., whether “genocide” must name a legal category or can serve as a broader moral umbrella), explain why it is verbal, and propose a disambiguated vocabulary both sides can adopt.

Constraints: do not cite scholars, court judgments, or texts by name; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; refer to positions conceptually. Do not resolve the dispute—leave the residual disagreement exact. End with a brief unsigned note stating whether, once verbal disputes are stripped away, any substantive disagreement remains and what it is.
