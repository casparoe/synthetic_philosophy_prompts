I am presenting a workshop paper arguing that Lewis's account of convention—where a behavioral regularity solves a coordination problem, is arbitrary among alternatives, and is sustained by common knowledge of mutual preferences and past conformity—should be applied to ephemeral citation norms in preprint-server comment threads. I claim these norms are genuine conventions despite lacking stable populations or face-to-face interaction, because algorithmic visibility metrics (reply threading, view counts, author badges) functionally substitute for the interpersonal common-knowledge condition Lewis requires. The audience is split: analytic philosophers of social ontology who worry I am diluting Lewis's conditions, and computational social scientists who study online dynamics but think evolutionary game-theoretic accounts in the style of Skyrms or Young already explain the phenomenon without invoking convention at all. They care about whether algorithmic visibility counts as common knowledge, whether transient populations can sustain conventions, and whether my framework adds explanatory value beyond evolutionary models.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face: order them by likelihood and damage; phrase each the way that audience would phrase it; for each, note what I should concede up front, the reply I should give in the time available, and where that reply is weakest.
