I'm trying to figure out if the seperateness of persons objection actually does the heavy lifting everyone claims, but my gut feelings on cases are all over the place.

Case 1: I can save five people from broken arms by breaking one stranger's arm. That feels okay.

Case 2: I can save those same five people by breaking my own arm instead, and I refuse. That feels selfish in a different way — like I'm treating my body as special, but not in the same way the stranger's body is special in case 1. Confusing.

Case 3: There's this hypothetical "utility bridge" tech that links two nervous systems for an hour and averages out pain and pleasure. If linking with a stranger turns my mild headache into shared slight discomfort for both, saving him from a migraine, I think I should do it. But if it means splitting my broken leg 50/50 so we both limp, I think that's wrong, even though total suffering is identical.

Case 4: Classic organ harvest — kill one healthy person, take organs, save five. Not okay.

Case 5: A tax that takes 10% from me and distributes 1% each to ten poor people. That seems fine, even though it's kind of distributing my wellbeing across bodies.

So what's the actual principle? Is it about physical boundaries, consent, or whether harm is "localized"? And can it save all five verdicts, or do I have to drop one? Also, what does it predict for this: a surgeon can redirect a lethal toxin away from five people into me, but only if she physically shoves my body into the path. Does that physical-intervention detail change the verdict?

Please lay out the principle (or competing ones), say which case each clause is trying to save, and be honest about which of my gut feelings has to go. Also tell me what you'd say about the bridge if the link was permanent instead of one hour.
