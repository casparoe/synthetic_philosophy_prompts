I just finished a long, circular argument with a friend about whether white lies are always wrong and whether Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness are just cultural flavors or carry genuinely different moral weights. I want us to stop talking past each other, so please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between our two camps would actually produce.

Treat the two camps as follows. Camp A (Universalist Autonomy Camp) holds that white lies are pro tanto wrong in every context because they violate the listener’s autonomy over their belief-forming environment; Dutch directness is closer to a universal moral ideal because it respects that autonomy, whereas Japanese indirectness systematically privileges relational harmony over truth-telling in ways that are ethically suspect regardless of culture. Camp B (Contextualist Frame Camp) holds that the ethics of speech depend on which conversational frame is operative; in high-context settings, indirectness protects interactional dignity rather than violating autonomy, and Dutch bespreekbaarheid is a local convention, not a universal standard, that can cause interactional harm when treated as normative for everyone. White lies are sometimes obligatory because they preserve the frame that makes cooperation possible.

Your deliverable should be a single document of no more than 650 words, written in American English, with this structure:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. Include agreement on the formal structure of deception (intentional misleading without explicit falsehood), on what needs explaining (why the same refusal is praised in Amsterdam and expected in Tokyo), and on what is not at stake (for example, both reject the view that all politeness is mere manipulation).

2. A disagreements table with two columns. In each column, state that camp’s precise position in wording the camp would accept for itself. Cover: (a) whether listener autonomy is the master value in speech ethics; (b) whether interactional harm—damage to the relational frame rather than to the listener’s belief state—is a genuine moral category or merely a sociological description; (c) whether Dutch directness carries normative priority over Japanese indirectness, or neither does; and (d) whether a white lie told solely to preserve face is ever morally permissible.

3. The cruxes: identify at least three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, record both sides’ predictions about what kind of evidence or argument would bear—for instance, whether cross-cultural data showing that high-context recipients experience indirect refusals as respectful rather than deceptive would change Camp A’s view, and whether a demonstrated autonomy violation in low-context settings would change Camp B’s.

4. A brief note on where the dispute is partly verbal: specify which disagreements dissolve once terms are defined precisely (for example, if wrong is defined strictly as violating autonomy versus disrupting relational equilibrium).

Keep the formulations neither vacuous nor slanted. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not invent specific empirical studies; describe only the kind of finding that would matter. Write in American English.
