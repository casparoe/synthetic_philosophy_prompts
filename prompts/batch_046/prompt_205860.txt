Draft the joint statement that would result from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in the philosophy of biology on the species problem. Camp One maintains that reproductive isolation—broadly construed to include barriers to gene flow—remains the fundamental, if sometimes operationally elusive, criterion that demarcates species as real evolutionary units, and that apparent counter-examples (such as extensive horizontal gene transfer in prokaryotes, persistent hybrid zones in plants, or ring species with continuous gene flow) merely expose practical or local limits rather than conceptual failures of the Biological Species Concept. Camp Two holds that “species” is an irreducibly pluralist, context-dependent category: no single criterion (reproductive, phylogenetic, ecological, or morphological) is necessary or sufficient across all domains of life, and taxonomic practices should be judged by their predictive and explanatory utility within specific research programmes rather than by correspondence to a unified natural kind.

Your deliverable should be a formal document written in British English, structured as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the formal structure of evolutionary theory; the existence of genuine taxonomic disagreement in specific domains (for example, bacteria, hybridising plant complexes, and ring species); and the shared recognition that classification serves both scientific and communicative functions—phrased so that representatives of both camps could sign them without reservation. Second, a disagreements table in which each camp’s core position is stated in wording that its own proponents would accept, with the residual disputes made exact rather than rhetorical. Identify at least three cruxes—such as whether partial gene flow in a hybrid zone undermines species boundaries or reveals internal population structure; whether horizontal gene transfer renders reproductive isolation conceptually incoherent for prokaryotes or merely locally inapplicable; and whether taxonomic stability should be valued over descriptive fidelity to evolutionary dynamics. For each crux, specify a concrete biological case or empirical finding that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp would predict or conclude about that case. If any portion of the dispute is purely verbal—concerning, for instance, whether “real” must entail a single essential property—state precisely which part and why. Conclude with a brief assessment of whether any shared evidence could, in principle, move either side, or whether the disagreement is ultimately methodological or metaphysical. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
