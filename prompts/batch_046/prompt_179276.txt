I have drafted the outline for a short workshop essay on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and I would appreciate targeted comments on its coherence and accuracy. The essay starts from the familiar observation that autonomous vehicles or weapons can produce harm without a clear locus of human blame, but it argues that the dominant framing treats this only as an allocative gap—an absence of a party who can justly be held responsible. I propose that there is also a constitutive gap: a structural erosion of the very conditions—epistemic transparency, volitional control, and traceable authorization—that make human accountability conceptually coherent. To illustrate this, I use a scenario involving a distributed-learning vehicle architecture whose post-deployment weight updates are shaped by real-time data streams that are neither fully interpretable to the human operator nor reducible to any prior design decision. The operator retains formal command authority, yet the adaptive dynamics of the system have altered the informational and causal environment in which that authority could meaningfully be exercised. I then suggest that this is not merely a failure of oversight but a transformation in what responsibility requires: the gap is not empty space where blame should sit, but a change in the moral architecture that makes blame inapplicable in a new way. I am particularly unsure about three things: whether the distinction between allocative and constitutive gaps holds up under analysis, whether my characterization of distributed-learning architectures misrepresents how updates actually relate to human authorization, and whether the inference from practical opacity to structural erosion of accountability conditions commits a category mistake. Could you comment specifically on these three points, flag any hidden assumptions, and suggest how the argument might be sharpened or qualified without relying on external sources?
