There's a position I'm trying to hold about lost-cause myths — Masada, the Confederacy's Lost Cause, Kosovo, Dunkirk — and I want to stress-test it. The claim is that these don't mainly preserve history or identity; they function more like counterfactual solidarity engines. A group circulates a narrative of noble defeat that never quite occured in the form told, letting members share victimhood and moral superiority without facing the real record, and this can happen even when the event was technically a withdrawal or a massacre with no strategic victory. I think Kosovo fits this well, and Dunkirk maybe in a different register since it's often narrated as rescue rather than loss. What would count against this? I want decisive defeaters separated from merely awkward ones: say, societies that stay cohesive through purely victorious myths with no defeat-story at all; archival work showing the Masada narrative tracks thier emotional core accurately; or psychological data that people bond more over shared triumph than shared imagined failure. Which of these should a defender honestly concede? Also, have I insulated the view? If solidarity doesn't appear I can blame poor transmission, if it does I cite confirmation — is that unfalsifiability, and is it fatal for a claim like this? Tell me what the position genuinely risks predicting that rivals don't, rank the escape routes, and say whether this is well-protected or just empty.
