I am designing a fifty-minute workshop for first-year philosophy-of-mind graduate students on the gradation of moral status across entities ranging from insects to thermostats. Rather than framing the discussion around the familiar sentient-versus-nonsentient binary, I want to center the session on a specific conceptual thesis: that moral status tracks the density of integrative phenomenal binding—the degree to which an entity's discrete sensory registrations are woven into a unified field of experiential ownership—so that sparse binding confers partial status, dense binding confers full status, and purely functional feedback loops, however complex, confer none. To make this thesis intelligible, I need to establish a prerequisite distinction before the binding-density claim is introduced; I need a single comparative example that can carry the distinction without relying on standard pain-behavior observations, which I fear will cause the students to conflate functional response with phenomenal integration; and I need to anticipate further misconceptions, particularly that gradation will be interpreted as linear rather than clustered around discrete binding thresholds, and that students will treat thermostats as borderline cases rather than clear null cases. Plan the workshop's explanatory arc: identify the prerequisite idea and justify its priority; specify the carrying example, explaining what features it must have and what standard illustrations it must avoid; map the misconceptions onto the sequence, indicating exactly where in the arc each should be headed off and how; propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the end to determine whether the students have understood the binding-density thesis itself rather than merely adopted its vocabulary; and state which elements of the standard literature on animal consciousness should be deliberately left out at this level, with a brief reason for each omission.
