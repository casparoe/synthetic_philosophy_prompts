Below are working notes for a short philosophical essay. I am exploring whether Daniel Dennett's intentional stance can be coherently applied to large language models, and if so, whether it requires structural modification.

Notes:
1. Dennett's intentional stance treats the attribution of beliefs and desires as a predictive strategy rather than an empirical claim about internal architecture. One adopts it toward a system when predicting behavior via rational agency is more efficient than analysis at the physical or design level.
2. In ordinary conversation, we routinely speak of what a language model "believes," "knows," or "intends." At first glance, this looks like a straightforward application of the intentional stance. Yet there is a structural disanalogy with human cognition. Human belief attribution relies on a relatively stable, integrated architecture with enduring dispositions. By contrast, an LLM produces outputs through a single forward pass over fixed parameters; between queries it possesses no persistent internal state beyond the context window.
3. I propose that belief attribution to an LLM is best understood not as the attribution of a discrete, enduring representational state, but rather as the attribution of a "distributed functional role" — a statistical regularity distributed across the parameter matrix that plays the explanatory work of belief within a specific conversational context, without being localized in any particular layer or activation.
4. This modifies Dennett's framework in an important respect. For human agents, the intentional stance assumes a unified agent with cross-contextual consistency. For LLMs, the stance becomes a local, context-bound interpretive move. Consequently, the design stance (predicting behavior via architecture, weights, and training dynamics) and the intentional stance overlap far more extensively for LLMs than they do for humans.
5. One might object that if belief attribution lacks integration across contexts and persists only within a single forward pass, it loses explanatory power and collapses into a pragmatic gloss on the design stance. I want to defend the view that predictive utility remains, but the ontology is thinner — closer to what we might call a "stance fragment" than to a full agent model.

Please provide comments and suggestions. In particular, I am interested in: (a) whether the interpretation of Dennett's intentional stance is accurate and whether the distinction from the design stance is drawn correctly; (b) whether the concept of a "distributed functional role" is coherent and whether it genuinely modifies the stance rather than merely renaming design-level prediction; and (c) whether the absence of persistent internal state between queries undermines belief attribution more severely than the absence of conscious integration already does for human agents, or whether this disanalogy is less damaging than it appears. I welcome criticism of the overall argument, suggestions for sharpening the distinction between "stance fragment" and full intentional attribution, and any observations about whether the conclusion — that the intentional stance survives but in a thinner form — actually follows from the premises.
