The historiographical dispute sparked by Fritz Fischer’s claim that the German leadership deliberately pursued a continental war—what became known as the Fischer controversy—has reached a peculiar stasis. Intentionalists continue to point to the September Program, to the documented risk-acceptance in the 1914 crisis councils, and to the explicit linkage between preventive-war logic and expansionist aims recorded in cabinet and military correspondence. Structuralists, in turn, emphasize alliance rigidities, railway mobilization schedules, and the diffusion of authority across civilian and military bureaucracies that made escalation appear automatic regardless of any single cabinet’s intent. Over recent decades the archival ground has largely converged—few serious scholars now deny either that ambitious war aims existed among parts of the elite or that systemic constraints were severe—yet the disagreement about whether Germany was “responsible” for the war has barely moved.

I am not asking for an adjudication of the evidence. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure. Is it a substantive dispute about hidden psychological or institutional facts—say, about exactly which decision-makers knew what and when? Or has it become verbal, with both sides using “responsible” to cover causation, moral culpability, and collective intentionality without noticing the slide? Or are the sides answering genuinely different questions under one heading: one asking “which state’s actions were the decisive causal contribution?” and the other asking “which collective agent can properly be addressed with moral blame?” I have also wondered whether a further conceptual distinction is being obscured—between blame directed at a nation as a unified moral subject (requiring something like a shared collective will) and blame directed at what might be called institutional recklessness, where the fault lies not in a common intention but in decision-making architectures that systematically excluded non-escalatory paths. If that distinction is sound, does it reveal that the debate is ill-posed, or does it show that progress requires splitting the singular question “was Germany responsible?” into several precisely separated ones?

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, drawing on what each side says about evidence, causation, and the conditions for blaming a collective. Show exactly where vocabulary slips, if it does; identify any hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, if there is one; and say whether the sides are answering different questions masked by one label. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about collective agency, a reframing of the historical question, or an argument that no further progress should be expected and why.
