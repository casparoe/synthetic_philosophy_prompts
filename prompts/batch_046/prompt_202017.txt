I am trying to extend Jeffrey conditionalization to what I call cross-pressured evidence: a situation in which two independent sources move an agent’s credence in E in opposite directions—one supporting a shift to 0.7, the other supporting a shift to 0.3—so that there is no single settled value for the posterior probability of E. My instinct is to apply Jeffrey’s rule sequentially, first using the 0.7 shift and then the 0.3 shift, treating the intermediate posterior as the prior for the second step, on the grounds that each application respects the rigidity condition that P(H|E) and P(H|¬E) remain unchanged.

My question turns on whether this sequential procedure is genuinely coherent. If Jeffrey’s rule is meant to govern updates whenever direct observation of E is unavailable, then applying it twice with contradictory shifts seems to treat the agent’s unsettled belief about E as if it were a definite state that can serve as a legitimate prior. But is there a hidden assumption in the rule that the evidential shift must be unique and unified—so that aggregating the two shifts into a single midpoint value (say, 0.5) and applying the rule once is not merely computationally simpler, but conceptually required? Or does the sequential version reveal that Jeffrey conditionalization was always implicitly committed to a particular ordering of evidence, which would undermine its claim to be a general account of uncertain updating?

Please give a committed answer in British English: take a definite position on whether sequential cross-pressured Jeffrey updating is a category mistake rather than a legitimate extension, and explain what that result implies about the uniqueness of the evidential partition in the rule.
