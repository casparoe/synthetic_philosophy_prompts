The displays and textbooks typically partition the narrative of National Socialism's rise into two adjacent but rarely integrated tracks: one biographical arc focused on Adolf Hitler's personal rhetoric, strategic choices, and appointment to the chancellorship, and one structural arc treating the Treaty of Versailles, Weimar economic collapse, and the continuity of military and civil-service bureaucracies. The tracks sit side by side, yet the curators and curriculum designers rarely specify whether the regime was historically contingent on Hitler's particular presence—whether, in other words, there would have been a Nazi Germany without him—or whether structural conditions alone were sufficient. What philosophical commitments about historical causation and moral responsibility does this institutional bifurcation presuppose?

Does it commit its designers to a Great-Man counterfactual—that absent Hitler, no comparable authoritarian regime would have consolidated—or can the split be read as holding that individual agency and structural conditions serve separate evaluative purposes that need not answer to a single causal verdict? Tell me what a unified pedagogical framework—one that replaced the bifurcated layout with a single explanatory account—would have to change in both exhibit design and classroom assessment, and whether such unification would resolve the underlying tension or only displace it onto a different conceptual problem.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among professional historians and philosophers of history, and do not present a contested thesis—such as the precise causal weight of Hitler's personal agency relative to structural conditions—as though it were the disciplinary consensus.
