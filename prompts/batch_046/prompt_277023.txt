im writing something on Braudel's longue durée — the slow history of geography and climate versus the quick noisy history of events and individuals. i want to read One Hundred Years of Solitude through that lens but im worried im just forcing the book into a theory it resists.

the novel spans generations in Macondo: the swamp and jungle, the banana plantation and its massacre, the rain that lasts years and wipes records away, and the cyclical repetition of Aurelianos and José Arcadios. Braudel distinguishes geographical time (almost immobile) from social structures and then from events that are like foam on waves.

my question is whether the magical realist elements break the Braudelian frame. the rain and the cyclical names feel like fate or prophecy rather than material geography — especially since Melquíades's manuscripts contain the whole story in advance, like an archive that predicts rather than records. but then there's the banana massacre: an event that gets erased from official memory and even from the town's own recall, yet its economic and bodily traces linger. does the novel actualy show that slow structures survive events, or does it collapse everything into a predetermined loop?

i'm especially interested in whether Melquíades's writings function like Braudel's long-duration archives, or whether they turn history into prophecy and make the distinction between structure and event meaningless. i dont need citations, just want to know if this reading holds water or if im imposing something the book actively complicates.
