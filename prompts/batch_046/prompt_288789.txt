Hey, I'm putting together prompts about conceptual patterns I keep running into when reading about the fall of Rome. There's this specific argumentative move that I don't have a word for, and I'm wondering if historians or philosophers of history have named it.

The pattern is about how people defend the claim that Rome "fell." They pick one indicator — say, the disappearance of the Western emperor in 476, or the collapse of long-distance tax collection, or demographic contraction in the West — and say that's the real measure of collapse. But when someone points out that another indicator stayed pretty stable — like trade networks, urban habitation, or literary production — they don't say "okay, so maybe it didn't fall on that dimension." Instead they say "fall was never about that dimension; it's about this other one." Then, a few paragraphs later, they're using the first indicator again as if it's the obvious standard. So they have this cluster of metrics (political, economic, demographic, cultural) and they rotate between them, treating each as decisive proof when it supports "fall" and as irrelevant noise when it doesn't. I've seen this happen in comparisons with the Eastern Empire too: the same historian will call the West a "collapse" because imperial administration ended, but call the East a "transformation" even though its tax base shrank just as dramatically, without explaining why one metric applies there and not the other.

It's not exactly equivocation, because "fall" really does cover different dimensions. And it's not quite motte-and-bailey, since there isn't one defensible core and one wild extension — it's more like there are several equally contentious metrics being used interchangeably without anyone flagging the switch. Does this specific thing — selectively rotating the criterion for collapse to keep the claim immune from counterevidence — have an established name in historiography or philosophy of history? If there isn't one, what should we call it, and where are its edges? How is it different from plain ambiguity or from just being interdisciplinary? I want the carving, not just a label: what it is, what it isn't, and where it stops being the same move.

Also, please separate what's pretty settled (like that there was real continuity in some domains) from what's genuinely contested (like whether that continuity means there was no fall), and don't present one interpretation as if it's the consensus just because it's popular. And I'm not after a bibliography — just whether there's a term and how the boundaries work.
