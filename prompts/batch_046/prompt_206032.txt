I am drafting a policy paper—recently made urgent by my appointment to a local democratic-reform working group—arguing that our borough should pilot compulsory attendance at local elections for citizens aged sixteen and over, extend the vote to non-citizen residents with ten continuous years of council-tax contribution, and justify both measures through an intergenerational fiduciary model: present electors hold democratic authority in trust for future inhabitants who cannot yet vote, so legitimacy demands including those long-term residents who will bear the consequences but are currently disenfranchised. Next month I present this to a review panel of four public-law scholars who favour liberal minimal-state frameworks and are deeply sceptical of coercive civic duties; one political economist who treats voter competence, not inclusion, as the binding constraint; and one youth-advocacy delegate who supports lowering the age but rejects compulsion as autonomy-violating. Prepare my Q&A defence: list the objections I will actually receive, ordered by likelihood and potential damage, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would use; for each, supply a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, flag the weakest part of that reply, and note what I should concede before they force it. Identify the single objection I am hoping remains unspoken—the conflict between my fiduciary justification for including long-term tax-paying residents and my arbitrary exclusion of temporary residents who also pay council tax but fall short of the ten-year threshold. Write in British English, and end with a line beginning Verdict: giving a one-sentence summary of the weakest structural point in my proposal.
