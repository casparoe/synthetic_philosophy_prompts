In my classics and philosophy course we have been working through Cicero’s treatise on old age, and I have tried to formalize the four rulings he delivers against the canonical objections: that old age necessarily withdraws one from public affairs, that it weakens the body beyond usefulness, that it deprives one of pleasure, and that its defining feature is the imminence of death. In each case Cicero returns a negative verdict—old age does not entail these harms, and mental vigor, disciplined habit, and the irrelevance of temporal proximity to death can preserve or even enhance one’s capacity for judgment and contribution. I want to test whether this framework is genuinely consistent or whether it smuggles in an unstated exception. Consider a new case not discussed in the text: a proposed institutional rule that philosophers over seventy must pass periodic cognitive audits in order to retain voting membership on peer-review boards, justified on the grounds that aging necessarily degrades the reliability of judgment and that gatekeeping roles in knowledge-production cannot tolerate such risk. Predict what Cicero should say about this proposal if he wishes to remain consistent with his four earlier rulings. Identify which of the four objections is the closest analog, state honestly whether his existing principles genuinely underdetermine the outcome, and specify what distinct principle he would be adopting with each possible verdict—outright rejection of the audit, acceptance with conditions, or acceptance of the premise but denial that peer review is the appropriate remedy.
