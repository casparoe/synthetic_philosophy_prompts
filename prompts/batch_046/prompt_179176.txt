A recent essay summarized Pascal’s view on justice and force like this: Pascal argues that because fallen human reason cannot access any universal standard of justice, every concrete legal order is merely the codification of whatever the dominant faction can enforce; he labels this customary justice and treats it as pure ideology lacking normative weight. Consequently, he advises that rational agents should obey local laws solely from strategic prudence, never from moral obligation, and that the same logic applies across borders—since no transcendent standard exists, a sovereign may deploy force whenever it predicts success. The essay further claims that Pascal thereby anticipates modern normative anti-realism about institutions, holding that justice-talk is essentially predictive of power dynamics rather than genuinely prescriptive. I have heard, however, that Pascal was a devout Christian who affirmed a hidden divine justice and warned against confusing custom with nature, which makes this sound like cynical realism. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what accurately captures his distinction between true justice and established custom, what subtly conflates his epistemic humility with an ontological denial of justice, and whether the international-relations conclusion and the anti-realist framing actually follow. Respond in one paragraph, no more than 180 words.
