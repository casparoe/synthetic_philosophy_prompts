I'm delivering the keynote address at the retirement ceremony for Dr. Elena Voss, who spent thirty-four years directing clinical ethics consultations at a large public hospital. The audience is deliberately mixed: her family and the nursing staff who worked beside her sit alongside several of her former graduate students—now practicing moral philosophers—who know that Elena has long defended an unorthodox view about moral worth, one that treats the objective structural alignment between institutional duty and action as more important than the private subjective reasons from which one acts, and that regards public attributions of "pure motive" as both epistemically reckless and conceptually confused. In her retirement letter, she explicitly asked that I not claim she was "driven by inner compassion" or "acted from love of the good," since she considers such statements false about her own psychology and misleading about what makes a life in ethics valuable; she wants the ceremony to honor the shape of her commitments without pretending anyone can read her heart. At the same time, the staff who genuinely depended on her calm authority during crises need to feel sincerely praised, not lectured, and her young grandchildren are seated in the front row. Please write the speech: use the distinction between acting from the right reasons and acting in accordance with right reasons to determine exactly what can be celebrated, what must be deliberately withheld, and how the audience is invited to understand her legacy—so that the philosophers in attendance see rigorous conceptual work being done, the staff experience real warmth and recognition, and nothing is said about Elena's private mental states that she would reject as unfounded.
