I am a student in an upper-division course on moral psychology and social epistemology, and I am trying to determine whether there is a coherent principle that distinguishes a genuine preference from a masked bias in moral and evaluative judgment. My intuitions across cases feel partly consistent and partly contradictory, and I want to see whether a single account can accommodate them without forcing me to abandon too many verdicts.

Here are my judgments. First, a hiring committee sincerely experiences a particular rhetorical polish as clarity and professionalism, yet the trait tracks elite schooling almost perfectly; I judge this a bias laundered through preference. Second, members of a culture prefer arranged marriage over romantic choice, report deep satisfaction, and defend the practice by appealing to family cohesion rather than coercion, even where legal exit is available; I am inclined to treat this as an autonomous cultural preference, not a bias, though I cannot articulate exactly what makes it different from the hiring case. Third, a person with a chronic disability reports that her life is good and that she does not wish for a cure, resisting social narratives that frame her condition as tragic; I think her evaluation is an unbiased preference reflecting her actual values, yet I worry that I am merely relabeling adaptive preference as autonomy. Fourth, a consumer develops an intense brand loyalty after years of targeted advertising and insists her taste is refined; I call this an externally implanted preference that has become subjectively real, hence a bias. Fifth, a philosopher finds consequentialist reasoning aesthetically compelling and therefore trusts it in hard cases, fully acknowledging that her graduate training shaped this taste; I feel this should count as bias, but since all theoretical taste seems shaped, I fear I have collapsed preference into bias altogether.

My tentative idea is that a preference is really a bias when it exhibits normative inertia: it resists revision not because of independent value but because the social structure that produced it also suppresses the evidence or alternatives that would enable revision. But this seems to classify case two as bias unless I add an ad hoc exemption, and it does not clearly separate case three from case four.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving the direction of fit between evaluation and social structure, the counterfactual availability of revision, or some other feature—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop, and why? In your answer, please state how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the specific point—especially regarding normative inertia—about which you are least sure.
