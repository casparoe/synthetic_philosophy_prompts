Here is the argument I constructed, step by step. First, if there will be a naval battle tomorrow, then today it is neither necessary that the battle occur nor necessary that it not occur; that is the core of contingency. Second, if a present statement about tomorrow's battle is already determinately true, then the battle's occurrence is fixed in the present, which contradicts its being genuinely contingent; hence, if the future is open in this strong sense, no present-tensed statement about the contingent event can be determinately true or false. Third, if there is no fact of the matter about whether the statement is true, then assigning any calibrated credence to it—anything other than total suspension of judgment—is epistemically groundless, since nothing in reality could make the belief correct or mistaken. Fourth, this reasoning applies not only to singular predictions but to every probabilistic judgment about an open future, including those that underpin risk analysis, forecasting models, and prediction markets. I am inclined to keep the Aristotelian intuition about contingency and to preserve our ordinary predictive practices, but I do not know which step I can reject without paying an unacceptable price.
