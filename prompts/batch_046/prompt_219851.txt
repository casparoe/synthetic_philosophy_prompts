I'm putting together materials for a grad seminar on consciousness and there's this slogan that keeps circulatting — 'phenomenal consciousness is just the illusion that there's something it's like' — and my students treat it like it makes the hard problem dissapear instantly. I'm pretty sure they're running together about four different kinds of 'illusion' without noticing. Can you lay out the precise readings?

What I want is a menu, not one answer. Is the claim that phenomenal properties are like a perceptual illusion (a bent stick in water)? A conceptual error, like treating 'the average taxpayer' as a real entity? A self-modeling glitch where the brain mispresents its own internal states? Or more like a useful fiction — we can't stop describing experience as if there's an inner movie screen, even though there literally isn't one? For each reading, say whether it's genuinely illusionist in the strong sense (denying that phenomenal properties exist at all) or whether it just relocates them into representation, language, or functional architechture. And tell me which versions actually support the move my students keep making, which is dismissing first-person reports as unreliable — because I think that dismissal only follows on one or two of these readings, not all.

Also, since this is for a handout, flag which versions are actually defended by people in the illusionist camp and which are strawmen nobody holds. Don't bother with a bibliography — I'll add references myself — just map the logical space clearly, including any awkward combinations that don't seem to be occupied. I want to show the class that repeating a one-liner isn't the same as picking a position.
