I have been trying to clarify my own views on when communal obligations should constrain individual choice, and my judgments across concrete situations seem to pull in different directions. In a village bound by an unwritten covenant that every able adult works the communal orchard one day a month, I believe a resident who abandons the duty to write poetry should be permitted to leave, even if the harvest suffers. When a daughter breaks a three-generation agreement to care for her grandparents in exchange for inheriting the family home, moving away for a career opportunity, I believe she should still be free to go, though I feel she owes them something substantial in return. If a small linguistic community democratically mandates that parents speak only the heritage language at home to prevent extinction, I believe the mandate is illegitimate, yet I also believe that parents who voluntarily abandon the language are damaging a genuinely shared good. A factory owner whose plant employs half the town wishes to sell it to a multinational that will shutter operations; I believe he has the legal right to sell, but I also believe that exercising it constitutes a moral failure that the community may rightly condemn. By contrast, when a citizen refuses conscription after the town's direct assembly—of which he was part—unanimously votes for defense, I believe he is genuinely obligated to serve, because the decision was authentically collective rather than imposed by a distant state.

Can these five verdicts be unified by one principle—perhaps concerning the source, scale, or reversibility of communal claims—or do they require two incompatible frameworks? If your account cannot preserve all five judgments, say explicitly which one you would overturn and why. Then apply your principle to a case I have not mentioned: an individual publicly rejects a shared mourning ritual after a local disaster, insisting on entirely private grief, and the community regards this refusal as a rupture in collective healing.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Address in order: the unifying principle or fundamental conflict; cases one and two; cases three through five; which of my judgments must be abandoned; and the prediction for the mourning-ritual case.
