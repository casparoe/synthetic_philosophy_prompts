As a retired natural scientist who has recently taken up philosophy of physics, I encountered the following summary of Bell's theorem in an essay aimed at scientifically literate non-specialists. The author writes: "Bell proved that quantum mechanics violates locality in a physically actionable way: if particles carry hidden properties before measurement, then the outcome of a measurement on one particle must instantaneously alter the state of its distant partner. He derived an inequality that quantum correlations breach, and since experiments confirm the breach, we must reject either locality or the idea that particles have definite pre-measurement states. Because the latter rejection amounts to saying quantum mechanics is complete, Bell's result effectively demonstrates that nature permits faster-than-light influence, and the only escape is to deny that experiments can be freely chosen—a loophole that most physicists dismiss as conspiratorial."

I have the sense that this slides together several distinct claims—about parameter dependence, about signaling, about the free-choice assumption, and about what "locality" technically denotes in Bell's derivation—but I cannot pinpoint the errors with confidence. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what is technically correct, what is an acceptable popularization, and what subtly misrepresents Bell's formal conditions or the philosophical landscape, including positions that relax statistical independence rather than locality. In particular, address whether the conclusion that nature permits faster-than-light influence actually follows from the violation of Bell's inequality, and whether the summary fairly characterizes the options available to someone who wishes to retain locality without accepting conspiracy.
