I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on animal welfare science and applied ethics, and during our unit on aquaculture practices a fellow student—who works part-time in a salmon and shrimp farming operation—kept repeating the line, "Fish don't feel pain, and crustaceans don't either; there's no brain for it." He invoked it specifically to dismiss objections to common industry practices, including the use of ice-slurry stunning for farmed trout, the failure rates of electrical stunning in Atlantic salmon, and the routine eyestalk ablation performed on shrimp broodstock to induce spawning. Rather than argue back immediately, I want to understand exactly what conceptual work that slogan could be doing. Could you lay out, as a precise menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses this claim might be expressing? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a trivial definitional stipulation, an empirically substantive but defensible scientific claim, an empirically false assertion, or a category error. I am especially interested in how each interpretation interacts with the neurobiological fact that decapod crustaceans possess distributed ganglionic nervous systems rather than centralized cortical structures, and in whether "feeling pain" is here being used to deny nociception, to deny conscious phenomenal experience, or to deny some morally relevant form of suffering. Crucially, I need to know which of these precise readings, if granted, would actually license dismissing welfare concerns about stunning failures and ablation, and which would be entirely irrelevant to that normative conclusion even if true. Please do not provide a bibliography or list of sources—just the conceptual disambiguation, with clear verdicts on which versions do the argumentative labor my classmate needs them to do.
