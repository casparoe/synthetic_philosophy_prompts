I'm putting together the final assessment for an upper-division philosophy seminar—mostly juniors and seniors, about fifteen students—on evolutionary epistemology and the philosophy of cognition. We've spent the term tracing how Darwin's mechanism of cumulative selection and Turing's framework for machine intelligence both seem to invert the ordinary reasoning order, yielding complex competence without any underlying comprehension. The problem is that students keep collapsing both cases into the same vague slogan—"it's all just blind process"—and miss the structural difference between a population-level absence of foresight and a system-level absence of semantic understanding. I want an exam prompt that forces them to keep these inversions distinct and to evaluate whether the analogy between evolutionary and computational competence actually holds.

Please draft a single take-home essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam. The prompt should ask students to reconstruct Darwin's argument that adaptive complexity can arise without a designer's prior grasp of the adaptive problem, then reconstruct Turing's argument that behavioral competence can exist without internal understanding, and finally assess whether the comparison collapses once we notice that selection operates over populations across generations while computation operates over representations within a single system. Along with the question, provide a model answer and a marking scheme structured around five specific analytical moves a top answer must make—each assigned a share of 20 total marks—plus a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks at each point. The difficulty should come from requiring precise reconstruction of where comprehension drops out in each mechanism, not from asking students to recite labels.

Also, please state how confident you are in the framing I've described and single out the point in your model answer where you are least sure—particularly regarding whether Turing's imitation game should be interpreted as a purely behavioral criterion or as implicitly carrying metaphysical commitments about mental states.
