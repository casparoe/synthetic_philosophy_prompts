Here's an argument I've been turning over that feels like it proves too much, and I can't find the leak.

(1) Unilateralism says I should act on my own best judgment rather than deferring to a collective decision procedure, provided I properly account for how others will respond.

(2) The unilateralist's curse is the failure mode where agents miss that their actions are correlated—each assumes they're the sole deviator, so everyone deviates and the collective outcome is worse.

(3) But now consider domains where my deviation really is isolated: I'm one voter among millions, one researcher with a contrarian result, or one donor to charity. Others won't change their behavior because of me, so there's no correlation failure.

(4) In these cases, unilateralism requires me to act on my private judgment—vote my true preference, publish regardless of peer review, donate to my top cause—rather than deferring to the collective outcome.

(5) Generalizing: for any institutional norm, if my breaking it is isolated and uncorrelated with others', I should break it whenever my judgment says the norm is suboptimal.

(6) But this dissolves every obligation to follow democratic results, scientific consensus, or organizational rules into pure private judgment.

(7) That conclusion seems wrong—there should be reasons to defer even when my deviation is harmless to coordination—but if so, the hidden constraint isn't in (1) or (3).

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (3) if possible.
