Design a fifty-minute seminar plan for first-year philosophy students who have just read William James on pluralism and therefore believe they already grasp "many-sided truth." The target concept is Jain anekāntavāda, specifically the thesis that reality is ontologically anekānta—possessing infinite, genuinely incompatible properties in different respects (dravyya-paryāya)—and that the saptabhaṅgī's seven modes of predication map this ontological complexity rather than expressing human cognitive limits or tolerance. Specify: (1) the prerequisite distinction that must be secured before anekāntavāda can be separated from Jamesian perspectivism; (2) the single concrete example that carries the idea, indicating precisely which properties it must display and which it must lack to avoid collapsing into either contradiction or mere viewpoint-relativity; (3) the misconceptions students will raise in predictable order (e.g., "this is just relativism," "it licenses logical contradictions," "it makes knowledge impossible") and the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; (4) the diagnostic question or task that reveals whether they have understood the claim as metaphysical rather than adopting a tolerant attitude; and (5) which elements of the technical apparatus—such as the full grammar of syāt, the cosmological cycles, or the debate with Nyāya realism—should be named and deliberately excluded at this level.
