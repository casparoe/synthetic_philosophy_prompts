Two friends of mine — Jordan and Riley — were arguing about Scanlon's contractualism and whether it can actually deal with climate-type aggregation problems. I mostly just listened but they want me to ask someone smart to sort it out for them. Here was the back-and-forth, more or less:

(1) Jordan said Scanlon's framework actually needs aggregation to work for collective harm. If each person's carbon emission doesn't hurt anyone specifically, then no individual can reject a principle allowing emissions. Only when you aggregate all the emissions together do you get actual harm, so only aggregation lets contractualism condemn climate inaction.

(2) Riley said that's exactly wrong — contractualism is built on the individualist restriction. You can't justify harming one person just because it prevents harm to many others. Aggregation is what Scanlon is trying to avoid, not what he needs.

(3) Jordan pushed back that climate harm isn't direct harm. The principle being evaluated isn't "emit to save someone" but "allow a practice of diffuse emission." Nobody is being singled out or used as a means, so the individualist restriction isn't violated. The harm is spread so thin that pairwise rejection never triggers, which is why aggregation is necessary to see the problem.

(4) Riley said that just relocates the issue. Each person affected by climate change still suffers some burden, and each can reject the principle allowing the practice that imposes that burden. The fact that others suffer too doesn't weaken their complaint — if anything it strengthens it. So aggregation doesn't solve anything; it's still pairwise rejection, just multiplied.

(5) Jordan then brought up what he called "structural contractualism" — evaluating principles at the institutional level rather than the act level. The principle is the rule governing the institution, not the individual choice inside it. Then you ask whether anyone can reject the rule given the total pattern it creates. The burdens are interdependent: my burden under the rule depends on what everyone else does. So recognizing that interdependence isn't smuggling in utilitarian comparison; it's just being honest about collective contexts. Scanlon never says this explicitly but it's compatible with his view. Jordan said this is a seperate level of analysis, not a hidden comparison.

(6) Riley called this a bait-and-switch. Once you evaluate rules holistically and let interdependence do that much work, you're basically using a Rawlsian veil-of-ignorance argument in Scanlonian clothing. Scanlon rejects that kind of reasoning precisely because it compares burdens across people. Jordan is just relabeling aggregation and hoping nobody notices.

They asked me to adjudicate move by move — rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who's more right overall and what's missing from both sides. Can you do that? Don't worry about giving a bibliography or anything, they just want the reasoning sorted out.
