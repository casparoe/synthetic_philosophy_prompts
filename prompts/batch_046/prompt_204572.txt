I am writing an essay for my course on the ethics of artistic representation, and I want to examine how technological change alters our obligations to the dead when we dramatise them. Rather than treating biopic ethics as static, I want to apply a historical philosopher's framework to a problem that arose long after his death. Given John Stuart Mill's well-known commitments to individual liberty, the harm principle, and the social importance of truthful discourse, how do you think he would have assessed the modern practice of using large language models—trained on a deceased person's private correspondence—to synthesise counterfactual speech and emotional reactions for that person in a biopic? Would he have regarded the posthumous algorithmic construction of a synthetic voice as a continuing harm to reputation and dignity, comparable to posthumous libel, or as a protected form of artistic expression? I am not asking for biographical guesswork but for a disciplined reconstruction of his likely position, applied specifically to this intersection of synthetic media and memorial ethics. Please argue for one interpretation, keeping the analysis tightly focused on the conflict between expressive freedom and the integrity of the dead.
