I've been working through case studies of scientific change in fields like thermodynamics and early genetics, and I've kept running into a rhetorical pattern that doesn't seem to line up neatly with the standard vocabulary. Here's what I mean. A new framework introduces a concept—say, a distinct causal mechanism or a novel unit of analysis—that doesn't map cleanly onto the old theoretical vocabulary. Instead of rejecting the new results outright, defenders of the established view don't typically deny the data; they offer a sequence of local reinterpretations: "that mechanism is really just our familiar process operating under special boundary conditions," or "that unit is only an aggregate of the entities we already recognize." Each move is individually plausible and preserves empirical agreement, but collectively they strip the new concept of the theoretical distinctiveness that made it interesting. Meanwhile, proponents of the new framework often do the mirror image, redescribing the old theory's successes as approximate derivations from their own principles. The result isn't open conflict, and it isn't exactly incommensurability in the classic sense—both sides claim continuity and compatibility—yet the substantive disagreement that was supposed to be at stake gets dissolved rather than resolved. I know Kuhn discussed paradigm defense and incommensurability, and I know Lakatos wrote about research programs protecting their hard core, but neither label quite captures this specific two-way absorption, where empirical agreement is maintained precisely by emptying theoretical novelty on both sides. Does this phenomenon have an established name in philosophy of science or in the sociology of knowledge? How should it be distinguished from ordinary theoretical reduction, from the addition of auxiliary hypotheses, or from straightforward strategic rhetoric? And if the literature hasn't named it precisely, what boundaries would define it—what would count as a clear instance, and what near-neighbors should be excluded?
