I have been discussing moral status with friends in my philosophy club, and we keep running into the same problem: people talk as though an entity either counts morally or does not, yet it seems more plausible that status comes in gradations—ranging from something like a thermostat, through insects with nociception, up to fully self-aware agents. Before I present my argument in an upcoming school symposium, I want to establish the full specification that any adequate theory of gradated moral status must satisfy, rather than relying on isolated intuitions about one case.

Could you provide the complete desiderata? I need the list of phenomena and boundary conditions the theory must handle—such as the distinction between a thermostat operating through predictive feedback loops, an insect that displays nociceptive responses without any evident self-model, a sleepwalking human who reacts to harm without conscious awareness, and a synthetic neural organoid engineered to exhibit predictive coding but lacking any subjective report mechanism—along with the distinctions it must not collapse, for instance between possessing minimal intrinsic status and possessing a high degree of it, or between instrumental value and direct moral weight. For each requirement, please state why it is genuinely necessary rather than merely a preference of one philosophical camp, and indicate whether empirical results from biology or neuroscience place hard constraints on the theory. I am especially interested in identifying the single desideratum that proves most exclusionary—the one that eliminates the largest share of familiar accounts, whether behaviorist, purely sentience-based, or rational-agent theories. If a table helps organize this, please include one; I do not need citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references, only the logical architecture of the standard itself.
