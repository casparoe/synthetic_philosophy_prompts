I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas publication on how classical moral frameworks absorb clinical discoveries that split sensory experience from emotional valence, and over coffee last week with a neurologist colleague I kept returning to the case of pain asymbolia—patients who retain intact nociceptive awareness, can localize tissue damage with precision, and report that something painful is occurring, yet display no protective withdrawal modulation, no affective distress, and no motivational drive to avoid repetition. Rather than asking for a bibliography or a survey of modern studies, I want to construct a rigorous conceptual exercise for readers who won't have database access: imagine that Jeremy Bentham, equipped only with the conceptual apparatus available to him in the early nineteenth century but granted full descriptive knowledge of these contemporary clinical profiles, is compelled to defend or revise his hedonistic calculus in light of them. Would he conclude that pain's badness is purely phenomenal and therefore absent in these cases, or would he argue for a hidden, sub-personal utility deficit—perhaps a failure of the organism's self-preservation signaling—that renders the condition harmful even when the patient reports no suffering?

Please work through this in detail, staying at the conceptual level rather than the empirical: address whether the decoupling of nociception from aversion genuinely undermines the identification of pain with badness, or merely relocates the badness to a functional level that Bentham's quantitative framework could accommodate with minor adjustments. In particular, I want you to consider whether this pressure might push him toward something resembling a qualitative distinction between sensory registration and motivational force—perhaps even drawing his view closer to later distinctions in the utilitarian tradition—rather than allowing him to double down on pure measurement. Note where his likely reply is weakest, whether an affective theory of pain's badness survives intact, and what this implies for the broader claim that suffering is the sole currency of moral evaluation. Keep the analysis self-contained: do not cite specific papers by author and date, and treat the clinical picture as a shared conceptual premise rather than a literature review.
