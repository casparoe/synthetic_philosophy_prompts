I am a graduate student in a seminar on comparative moral philosophy, and our reading group has divided over whether Buddhist ethics can be reconstructed as a form of consequentialism. One camp holds the Theravada intentionalist position: moral evaluation tracks cetana (volition) alone, outcomes belong to the separate causal order of vipaka, and no result can retroactively alter the moral quality of the original kammic act. The other camp reads Mahayana sources—particularly the ship-captain Jataka and the upaya-kausalya literature—as showing that the bodhisattva vow makes the welfare of sentient beings the ultimate criterion, licensing outcome-sensitive reasoning that closely resembles consequentialist optimization. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, I want us to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both sides could sign. Please structure the document as follows. First, a numbered list of claims about karma, mental intention, and canonical textual basis in both Pali and Mahayana traditions that both camps genuinely accept without reservation. Second, a table of precise disagreements phrased in neutral wording, specifically addressing (a) whether kusala is indexed solely to mental purity or also to soteriological outcomes for others, and (b) whether upaya introduces a genuinely consequentialist calculus or only a deontological exception available to agents with perfected compassion. Third, identify the two cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify (i) the concrete case or textual feature both agree is relevant, and (ii) each side's explicit prediction about what that evidence demonstrates. I have in mind for the first crux the captain's killing of the robber in the Jataka: does the act derive its justification from the 500 lives saved, or solely from the captain's compassionate intention regardless of outcome? For the second crux, consider whether the Abhidharma distinction between hetu and phala permits outcomes to enter the moral evaluation of the original act itself, and whether the doctrine of prajnaparamita, by dissolving rigid agent-patient boundaries, undermines or supports consequentialist aggregation. Fourth, a brief statement of what kind of textual discovery or conceptual clarification—such as a previously untranslated Abhidharma commentary or a precise formalization of upaya reasoning—would cause each camp to revise its core commitment. Write in formal academic prose, do not include bibliographies or citation requests, represent both positions with sufficient philosophical precision that neither camp would reject the formulation of its own view, and deploy niche distinctions—such as the Abhidharma taxonomy of karma or the role of skillful means—only where they genuinely illuminate the disagreement rather than obscure it.
