I'm a clinical ethics consultant serving on a university hospital's resource-allocation committee, which reviews protocols for distributing scarce life-saving interventions—mechanical ventilation during surge conditions, organs for transplantation, and intensive-care beds—under a mandate to maximize expected patient welfare. Recently the committee has observed that formally identical clinical scenarios yield opposite recommendations depending solely on framing: describing outcomes in terms of survival probabilities versus equivalent mortality probabilities produces reversed preferences, and embedding or factoring out a universal background risk of surgical complication shifts collective judgment even though the relative risks between pathways remain unchanged. These patterns reproduce, in an applied clinical setting, the independence-violating structures associated with the Allais paradox and its common-ratio and common-consequence effects. I need a reusable audit procedure—operable within thirty minutes by committee members without formal training in decision theory—that translates the independence axiom of expected utility theory into a practical checklist for evaluating proposed allocation rules.

The procedure should take the form of ordered tests or questions applied directly to the protocol's decision tree, with explicit criteria for passing or failing at each step, and it must distinguish correctable framing artifacts from morally legitimate responses to ethically relevant distinctions—such as acting versus omitting, or identifiable versus statistical victims—marking precisely where judgment supersedes mechanics. Include a brief flowchart in words, a small set of diagnostic scenario pairs that can be substituted into any protocol to test for framing sensitivity, and worked applications to three concrete cases: first, a ventilator-triage rule that assigns priorities based on survival-rate framing versus mortality-rate framing of identical probabilities; second, an organ-allocation protocol in which a baseline surgical-risk factor is either embedded in both pathways or factored out, altering willingness to recommend transplantation despite unchanged relative outcomes; and third, a proposal to prioritize rescuing identifiable patients facing certain death over achieving a statistically larger number of anonymous lives saved, where the procedure detects an independence-violating framing dependence and returns a structural correction—likely requiring rejection or restructuring of the protocol—that conflicts with the committee's intuitive moral pull. Finally, specify what the procedure cannot settle, which value pluralisms or risk attitudes it must leave to human judgment, the conditions under which it will misfire with false positives or false negatives, and what should occur when different members apply the tests and reach contradictory conclusions.
