I've been working through Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding and the correspondence with Molyneux about whether a person born blind who learns to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch could, upon suddenly gaining sight, immediately tell them apart without touching them. Locke denies this, arguing that there is no innate idea of spatial form shared across touch and vision, so the subject would need to learn the correlation. Recently, however, I've been reading about amodal spatial representations in the lateral intraparietal cortex and the idea that shape is encoded in a modality-independent format. Combined with findings from sight-restoration studies showing that patients can eventually match tactile and visual shapes, this makes it seem like the brain already possesses the relevant representation and Molyneux's question reduces to whether the visual pathway is calibrated to access it—a version of the binding problem rather than a distinct philosophical puzzle. Furthermore, if one adopts a sensorimotor contingency view of vision, the issue isn't even neural access but the absence of learned sensorimotor skills with visual input. Given all this, is there still a genuinely conceptual question in Molyneux's problem that isn't captured by either the neuroscience of cross-modal integration or the sensorimotor account of perceptual learning, or have philosophers of perception kept the problem alive mainly by insisting on a distinction between having a representation and being able to deploy it in judgment?
