I am preparing a graduate module on applied moral psychology and have become dissatisfied with the usual treatment of Kierkegaard’s teleological suspension of the ethical as a purely exegetical puzzle. My students keep asking whether Fear and Trembling offers anything operational for practitioners who face conflicts between universal norms and singular obligations, and rather than dismiss the question I want to see whether the framework can be translated into a reusable institutional procedure without betraying its core logic.

Please construct a decision protocol—an ordered sequence of questions with explicit stop conditions and judgment nodes—for an institutional ethics reviewer who must evaluate claims that a standard ethical protocol should be suspended for a higher, non-universalizable telos. The protocol must be anchored in Kierkegaard’s actual architecture: the distinction between the aesthetic, ethical, and religious stages; the paradox of Abraham’s silence; the invisibility of the knight of faith to the ethical community; and the absolute relation to the absolute. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a refusal, and where it must hand the decision back to the singular agent because the framework itself denies institutional resolution. Include a small set of diagnostic tests—applied to specific sentences or scenarios—to distinguish a genuine teleological suspension from an aesthetic evasion or an ethical rationalization. Write this for a practitioner familiar with clinical ethics but not with Kierkegaard, avoiding untranslated Danish terminology except where genuinely necessary.

Then apply the protocol to three cases: first, a surgeon who declines to disclose a fatal prognosis to honor a patient’s privately communicated religious vow that hope must be maintained until death; second, a field researcher who suppresses preliminary negative data about a local water source to preserve a vulnerable community’s trust in the research process; and third, a parent who refuses mandatory vaccination for a child based on what they describe as a direct, incommunicable command received in prayer. One of these applications must produce a result the reviewer will find morally unwelcome, and you should flag exactly where in the procedure that unwelcome result originates. Conclude by stating what the protocol cannot do—specifically, whether it can ever authorize a suspension from within the institution, or whether Kierkegaard’s logic necessarily restricts authorization to the singular individual outside the universal. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
