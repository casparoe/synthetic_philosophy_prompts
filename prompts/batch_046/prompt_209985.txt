I serve on my hospital's clinical ethics and resource-allocation committee. The administration proposes adopting an algorithmic triage-support tool for ICU bed allocation during surge-capacity events. The predictive model was trained on five years of historical admission and discharge data. A preliminary internal audit reveals a sharp statistical pattern: across the full patient cohort, those aged seventy and older show a higher observed survival-to-discharge rate (62 percent) than those aged forty to sixty-nine (58 percent). Yet within every severity stratum—mild, moderate, severe, and critical—older patients consistently exhibit lower survival rates than younger patients in the same stratum. The discrepancy arises because older patients are triaged more aggressively into lower-severity categories, partly due to prior do-not-resuscitate patterns and family-requested limitations on intervention, so the aggregate comparison pools clinically dissimilar profiles.

The committee is split. One faction holds that the aggregate figure is the ethically relevant baseline: allocation should maximize lives actually saved in the population as it presents itself to the hospital, not in a hypothetical counterfactual where triage patterns are uniform. The opposing faction argues that conditioning on clinical severity is the only causally legitimate comparison; deploying a model trained on the aggregate association would systematically favor older patients precisely in those severity levels where they are least likely to benefit, thereby encoding structural bias into the algorithm. They further warn that predictive accuracy at the population level can mask distributive injustice at the point of care.

We must issue a written opinion before Thursday's vote recommending whether to approve deployment, reject it outright, or approve it only subject to structural conditions. Draft the memo in formal institutional voice, addressed to the committee chair and suitable for circulation to clinicians and patient advocates who lack statistical training. The memo should:

1. Offer one concrete recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—supported by reasoning tied directly to the statistical pattern described.
2. Explain, without citing bibliographic authorities, why Simpson's paradox is not a mere mathematical curiosity but carries genuine normative weight for allocation policy.
3. Present the strongest argument on the side you reject in honest, non-caricatured form—specifically addressing whether "real-world" outcome maximization can justify relying on the unconditioned aggregate.
4. State your view on whether the paradox reveals something irreducible about the tension between predictive accuracy and distributive justice in clinical algorithms, or whether it can be resolved entirely by selecting the correct conditioning set for comparison.

Treat the family-preference and triage-pattern confounders as ethically significant features of the institutional environment rather than as noise to be eliminated.
