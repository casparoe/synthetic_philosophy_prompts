I was discussing metaphysics of personal identity with a colleague who had just read some of the origin-essentialism literature, and she offered me the following summary: the core claim is that an individual's origins—specifically the particular sperm and egg from which they developed—are metaphysically necessary to them, so that I literally could not have existed had a different gamete pair been involved. She said the argument turns on the idea that identity is a one-one relation: if the organism that is me traces back to those specific cells, then in any possible world lacking that precise origin event there is no organism identical to me, which also means I could not have come from different parents, since different parents entail a different gametic origin. She went on to compare this to essentialism about natural kinds—being human is necessary to me in the same way that being born of these particular cells is—so origin essentialism is just kind-essentialism applied to the biological individual. Finally, she noted that critics often invoke monozygotic twinning, where one origin produces two people, but she dismissed this as irrelevant because the twins share the same origin yet are distinct psychological subjects, which only shows that origin essentialism applies to the biological organism, not to the person conceived as a psychological continuity. From all this she concluded that the view is actually quite modest: once you separate the organism from the mental subject, all origin essentialism really asserts is that you cannot be numerically identical to a different biological origin, a claim she thinks even opponents of essentialism should grant.

Something about this chain feels slippery—particularly the inference from different gametes to different parents, the assimilation to kind-essentialism, and the dismissal of the twinning objection. But I cannot tell whether these are harmless compressions or real distortions, and I am unsure whether her modest conclusion survives if the earlier links are repaired. Would you audit her summary? Then tell me whether her final claim—that origin essentialism collapses into an uncontroversial point about numerical identity—actually follows once the misrepresentations are removed, or whether a stronger and more controversial thesis remains intact.
