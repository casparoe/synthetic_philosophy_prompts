I am preparing a handout for a philosophy-of-psychiatry graduate seminar on Ian Hacking’s theory of looping effects in psychiatric classification. Please construct a decision tree that sorts theoretical positions on the following question: once a psychiatric category is introduced and individuals begin to understand themselves through it, what is the nature and scope of the feedback between the classification and the classified?

The tree should begin with the root commitment that psychiatric classifications can function as interactive kinds, and it should proceed through exactly four binary branch points. The first split should ask whether the loop operates primarily through first-person self-understanding or through institutional and clinical practices of labeling and management. The second split should ask whether the feedback tends to destabilize the category—causing its criteria, prevalence, or phenomenology to shift over time—or whether it can stabilize and reinforce the category’s boundaries. The third split should ask whether this looping dynamic applies uniformly to all diagnostic categories or whether there is a principled, defensible distinction (for instance, between conditions with high narrative plasticity and those anchored in early neurodevelopmental patterns). The fourth split should ask whether the loop is constitutive of the disorder’s identity—such that removing the classificatory framework would alter the phenomenon itself—or whether it remains a causal side-effect that leaves an underlying natural kind intact.

The tree must have exactly six leaves. Five must correspond to named or clearly identifiable positions: (1) hard biological essentialism that denies looping effects altogether; (2) an institutional-interactionist view that accepts feedback through clinical power but denies it changes the disorder’s core nature; (3) Hacking’s own view on transient mental illnesses as unstable loops mediated by self-knowledge; (4) a differential-looping view that restricts the effect to categories with high narrative plasticity; and (5) a stabilizing-loop view (for example, regarding certain personality-disorder clusters) that argues clinical expectation can reinforce rather than fracture boundaries. The sixth leaf should be an unoccupied logical combination; label it “unnamed” and provide one sentence diagnosing whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete, answerable claim—e.g., “Would removing the diagnostic label leave the underlying phenomenon unchanged?”—so that a student can locate themselves without knowing the name of the view in advance. Do not include generic definitional questions (e.g., “What is an interactive kind?”). Where the logical space forces two named positions onto the same branch, flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction. The output should be suitable for students familiar with Hacking’s distinction between indifferent and interactive kinds but not yet immersed in the secondary literature on psychiatric taxonomy. Avoid any request for bibliographies, citation lists, or named scholars beyond Hacking himself.
