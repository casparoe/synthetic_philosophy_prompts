I am working on the conceptual foundations of algorithmic pedagogy and am trying to reconstruct how an early modern empiricist framework would handle technologies that did not exist in its original formulation. Specifically, I want to apply John Locke’s philosophy of education—particularly his account of the mind as initially devoid of innate ideas, the central role of experience in the formation of simple and complex ideas, the tutor’s responsibility for arranging the environment rather than forcing assent, and his concern with the habituation of judgment rather than mere memorization—to a scenario that only becomes possible with contemporary machine-learning architectures.

Consider an adaptive learning ecosystem that does not merely present material in a sequenced order, but continuously updates an internal cognitive model of the learner using real-time biometric feedback and predictive error-correction. Crucially, this system does not wait for the student to form an erroneous association and then correct it through subsequent experience; instead, it anticipates the associative pathway that would lead to error and preemptively adjusts the weighting of sensory inputs or the framing of the problem space before the erroneous idea is ever consciously entertained. In other words, the intervention occurs at a sub-experiential level, shaping the conditions under which ideas are formed rather than shaping the ideas through direct experience.

From the perspective of Locke’s commitments, would he regard this form of algorithmic tutoring as a legitimate extension of the tutor’s environmental arrangement—analogous to selecting appropriate companions or controlling the physical conditions of study—or would he treat it as an illegitimate intrusion into the mechanism of association itself, effectively substituting an artificial order of ideas for the one that should arise naturally from sensation and reflection? I am especially interested in whether he would draw a principled distinction between tutoring that structures the external conditions of experience and tutoring that directly modifies the internal dynamics of idea-formation, and whether he would think the latter violates the empiricist prohibition on pre-experiential content.

Furthermore, how might Locke’s distinction between wit and judgment—where wit concerns the quickness of associating ideas and judgment concerns the careful separation of them—map onto a system that optimizes for predictive accuracy in association? Would he worry that such a system cultivates a kind of artificial wit at the expense of genuine judgment, because the learner never actually traverses the erroneous association that judgment requires one to learn to avoid?
