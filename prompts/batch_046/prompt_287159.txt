I retired from marine biology a few years back and lately I've been reading philosophy for fun, mostly about minds and identity. I got hooked on Hofstadter's idea that the self is more like a pattern or loop that can live on in other brains -- like how his wife's presence stayed with him after she died, in the people who knew her well. But then I cooked up a weird case to test it, and now I think I've built a reductio that proves too much, but I can't find the exit.

Here's the argument. (1) If Hofstadter is right, the 'I' is just the pattern of memories, values, and self-reference running in a brain, not tied to one skull. (2) Imagine two people in a long-term coupled state -- call it a 'dual-core self' -- where brain B has internalized brain A's pattern so precisely that B is running a live, real-time simulation of A's consciousness, not just remembering A. (3) If patterns are what matter, then part of A's self is literally running inside B's head. (4) So if B decides to suppress that model -- say B actively forgets A, or changes core beliefs about A -- B hasn't just moved on emotionally; B has destroyed a living piece of A. (5) By the same reasoning, anyone who knew A well and then genuinely forgets them is committing a kind of partial murder. (6) But that's absurd; we don't think forgetting someone is harming them, let alone killing part of them. (7) So Hofstadter's view must be false, or at least it can't apply across brains like that.

I feel like something slipped between (3) and (5), maybe about what 'part of the self' means, but I can't pin it down. Which premise should I actually reject? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) -- I like teh idea that self isn't just meat -- but then what do I pay? Do I have to accept that deep forgetting really is a kind of harm? Or is there a cleaner way out? Also, if I'm just confused and the whole setup rests on a mistake about how patterns work, tell me straight -- I don't want a polite answer that humors a retired biologist who wandered into teh wrong room.
