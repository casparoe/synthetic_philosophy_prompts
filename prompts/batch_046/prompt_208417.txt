I am drafting an essay on Milton Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions in economic modeling—commonly referred to as the F-twist. My central claim is that Friedman's argument implicitly relies on a distinction between two kinds of idealization that he never articulates: compressive idealizations, which discard detail that is genuinely inert for the prediction in question, like treating air resistance as negligible for a falling brick, and structural idealizations, which replace the actual causal architecture with a different one, like treating firms as expected-profit maximizers. I argue that the F-twist only justifies the first kind. For structural idealizations, predictive success is insufficient, because if the substituted mechanism interacts with policy interventions in ways the real mechanism does not, then using the model for policy guidance can be systematically misleading—even when its out-of-sample predictions are excellent. I illustrate this with a comparison to black-box predictive models in machine learning: a neural network may predict consumer demand well without representing any causal structure, but it offers no counterfactual leverage. I then suggest that Friedman's own example of falling leaves—treating them as if they calculated their trajectories—actually belongs to the compressive category, not the structural one, and that economists have misread it as licensing structural unrealism. I am not asking for a bibliography, but I would like critical comments on three points: first, whether the compressive-structural distinction is genuinely missing from Friedman's text or already present in his discussion of as-if methodology; second, whether the comparison to black-box ML models strengthens the argument or imports contested assumptions about causation; and third, whether my claim about the misreading of the falling-leaves example is defensible, or whether Friedman clearly intends it as a structural analogy.
