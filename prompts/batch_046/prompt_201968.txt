I'm serving on our university's behavioral-science IRB and we've hit this protocol that keeps circling back to me in a bad way. The researcher — she's in philosophy of mind actually, not psych — wants to run what she calls a "reflective instability" study. Subjects do a batch of logic puzzles, then recieve fabricated peer-review feedback saying their reasoning was either top-decile or bottom-decile. After they see that, they redo a subset of the puzzles, and the study tracks whether they flip correct answers they originally got right because the fake meta-evidence made them doubt their own competence. She frames it as experimentally inducing epistemic akrasia: first-order belief they solved it, higher-order belief they can't trust that belief.

Our board is split. Some members say it's just deception with a fancy philosophical garnish and should go through with standard debriefing. Others — especially the chair — say destabilizing someone's self-trust about reasoning is a seperate harm from ordinary deception, and that informed consent fails here because you can't really anticipate what it feels like to distrust your own judgments in real time. There's also a compromise floating around from the grad-student rep: approve only if there's a mandatory "reconstruction session" where participants have to defend one answer they abandoned, to see if that repairs the akrasia before they leave.

I need you to draft our decision memo. Pick one path — approve straight, reject, or approve with the reconstruction condition — and give the reasoning in a way the PI could actually respond to, not just name-checking philosophers. Include an honest account of the strongest point on the losing side. And please: lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning right after, with no preamble and no restating the question.
