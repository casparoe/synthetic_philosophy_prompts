For the past several months I have been iteratively refining a personal knowledge-management protocol, applying what I believed was a conservative decision rule: I adopt a new feature only if it produces a strict local improvement over the current version on every axis I care about. The base system, Protocol Alpha, is minimal — plain-text notes, a single review pass, then archival. Protocol Beta adds exactly one feature: each note receives a confidence score and a backlink to the source that corrected my misunderstanding. Comparing Beta to Alpha, Beta dominates: the tagging takes roughly thirty seconds per entry and eliminates the miscalibration errors I previously missed. Protocol Gamma adds exactly one feature to Beta: a weekly automated script that detects systematically miscalibrated confidence scores and rewrites the affected notes. Comparing Gamma to Beta, Gamma again dominates: the calibration feedback is genuinely useful and the script requires no manual intervention. Yet when I compare Gamma directly to Alpha, I am convinced that Gamma is worse. The system is brittle, the overhead discourages me from recording minor observations, and over a six-month horizon I expect I would retain less usable knowledge with Gamma than with Alpha. My pairwise judgments therefore form a cycle: Beta is better than Alpha, Gamma is better than Beta, but Alpha is better than Gamma. I recognize the structural resemblance to greedy optimization failures in computer science, but I am unsure whether that analogy carries over to preference aggregation. Is there a coherent framework for deciding which protocol to commit to that does not depend on the order in which I introduced the features? Or does this kind of accumulated reversal indicate that judgments of "better" break down in a systematic way when improvements are stacked, and if so, what should that change about how I evaluate multi-step upgrades?
