In constitutional theory, the standard analogy frames entrenchment as a Ulysses contract: the founders bind themselves and their successors to the mast so that transient democratic passions—the Sirens—cannot wreck the ship of state. It is routinely invoked to justify supermajoritarian amendment rules and strong-form judicial review, on the logic that a crew able to untie the ropes at will defeats the purpose of precommitment. I want an audit of how far this mapping actually holds, pushed past the textbook version. Give me the explicit correspondence—Odysseus, the Sirens, the crew, the mast, the wax, and the ship itself—then the breaking points that bear on the normative conclusion. In particular, address the disanalogy between diachronic self-binding (one agent across time) and intergenerational binding (strangers who never consented to serve as crew); the fact that modern constitutional courts often behave more like Sirens (activist interpretation) than like ropes; and the selective application of “wax”—some provisions are amendable, others entrenched, with no principled criterion visible to the current crew. Once those fractures are on the table, does the analogy still license the relaxed conclusion that we ought to welcome hard-to-amend constitutions, or was that conclusion being smuggled in by the emotional pull of the source domain?
