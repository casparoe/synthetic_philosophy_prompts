You are an expert philosopher of language with practical experience designing institutional procedures, and you should think carefully before writing. Begin by briefly clarifying for yourself the distinction between the locutionary content of an utterance, its illocutionary force, and the felicity conditions that must obtain for it to successfully perform the act it claims—paying special attention to how these conditions change when speech is written, asynchronous, and mediated by institutional protocol rather than face-to-face. Then construct a reusable decision procedure, expressed entirely as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or lists, written in the manner of a formal letter, for a dispute-resolution officer in a cross-border cooperative governance body who receives written submissions each week and must determine whether an utterance genuinely performs an institutional act—such as a binding waiver, a valid apology, an authorization, or a commitment—or instead misfires. The officer holds philosophical training but lacks legal expertise, renders preliminary judgments independently, and may escalate to a senior signatory only those steps that turn on irreducible questions of value rather than fact. Your procedure must present the questions in strict order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify the exact point where it stops with a negative determination, identify the exact point where it must hand the decision upward because judgment is unavoidable, and be explicit about where the framework will misfire or remain silent. Because this body operates through delayed written exchange, embed within the procedure a specific working concept—term it deferred illocutionary uptake—to account for how temporal separation and mediated reception alter the conditions for successful performative force. Apply the complete procedure to three submissions: first, a statement that reads, I hereby waive all future claims regarding the water allocation; second, a statement that reads, I apologize for misrepresenting the data and accept full responsibility, immediately followed by a paragraph attributing the error to instruments supplied by the other party; and third, a sincerely meant and institutionally well-formed apology that was filed in the wrong procedural channel, so that your procedure returns a negative institutional determination despite its evident moral weight—explain why this negative result is correct within the framework, then state precisely what the procedure is powerless to adjudicate. Throughout, do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure theoretical apparatus that depends on retrieval; rely solely on the conceptual architecture of speech-act theory and your own reasoning. End by stating the hard limits of the procedure: which misfires it will fail to catch, which moral dimensions it deliberately sets aside, and the conditions under which an officer should distrust its output.
