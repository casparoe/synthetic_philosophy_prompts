Draft a 90-minute PhD comprehensive exam question on teleology and providence in history—specifically, whether the arc of history bends toward justice—designed so that a candidate cannot answer it by summarizing standard positions from Hegel, Marx, or King. Center the question on the interruption thesis: the claim that sustained moral regression is structurally incompatible with any non-vacuous historical teleology unless progress is defined so thinly that it becomes statistically indistinguishable from noise. Ask candidates to evaluate whether any defensible teleological view must therefore take one of three paths: deny that regression is ever genuinely sustained, accept that the arc is epistemically inaccessible to historical agents, or abandon directional claims altogether. Provide a model answer built around five must-hit moves: (1) a precise formulation of the interruption thesis and why it threatens non-vacuous teleology; (2) a clear distinction between thick and thin conceptions of moral progress, with the conceptual cost of each; (3) an evaluation of the denial-of-regression path using a concrete historical case and an explanation of why it risks ad hoc reasoning; (4) an evaluation of the epistemic-inaccessibility path, including whether inaccessibility undermines the motivational or normative force traditionally associated with providential history; (5) a reasoned conclusion on whether abandoning directional claims is unavoidable, with explicit acknowledgment of what is lost and retained. Include a marking scheme out of 20 points, allocating specific marks to each move, and append a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each—e.g., conflating regression with mere delay, treating epistemic inaccessibility as harmless, or reaching a conclusion without weighing all three paths. The question should require no outside sources, bibliographies, or citations to answer.
