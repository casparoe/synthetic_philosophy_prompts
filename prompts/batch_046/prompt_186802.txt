Write the desiderata that a philosophical account of scientific modeling must satisfy if it is meant to accommodate the strategy associated with Milton Friedman — that deliberately unrealistic assumptions are not merely tolerable but can be theoretically virtuous, provided the model delivers accurate predictions — a position frequently referred to as Friedman's twist. Restrict the list to criteria specific to models whose premises are known to be false or counterfactual, rather than generic conditions on scientific adequacy. For each desideratum, state the motivating consideration (for example: computational tractability, predictive power, explanatory transparency, modal fidelity to the target system, or the distinction between a heuristic fiction adopted for convenience and a structural simplification without which the model collapses). Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable and which are contested among instrumentalists, structural realists, and defenders of explanatory pluralism. Specifically, include a desideratum about whether a model built on false premises must preserve counterfactual information about the actual world or whether pure predictive success suffices — and say which prominent philosophical positions this single requirement eliminates. Finally, ask whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, identify the precise tension (for instance, between modal fidelity and tractability) that does the exclusionary work. Keep the weight on the criteria and their rationales; brief scoring of existing accounts is welcome but secondary.
