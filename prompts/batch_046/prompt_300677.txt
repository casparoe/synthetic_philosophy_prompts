I recently encountered an unnamed essay in the philosophy of AI that attempts to summarize the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. The author writes, in effect, the following. Löb’s theorem is essentially Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem applied to belief: in any formal system S strong enough for arithmetic, if S can prove that whatever S proves is true, then S proves its own consistency, which Gödel showed S cannot do unless S is actually inconsistent. Consequently, any agent that models its own reasoning as provability in S and accepts this reflection principle can, by Löb’s theorem, derive every statement. The obstacle is therefore unavoidable for deterministic agents: an agent must either refuse to trust its own proofs or collapse into inconsistency, with no stable middle ground. Probabilistic agents might escape by replacing proof with Bayesian updating, but for classical reasoners the trap is absolute. The essay illustrates this with a self-modifying AI that can read its own source code and approve successor agents that replicate its decision criteria; this, the author claims, is a direct instance of the Löbian loop, since the AI is trying to prove that if it endorses its successor’s reasoning, then that reasoning is sound. As remedies, the author mentions dropping the diagonal lemma or retreating to a modal logic weaker than GL, but dismisses both as technically available yet philosophically unsatisfying, since they require the agent to abandon the very self-transparency that makes reflection valuable.

The essay concludes that rational self-trust is therefore formally impossible.
