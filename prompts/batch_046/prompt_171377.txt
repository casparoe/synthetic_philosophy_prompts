I was recently appointed to a charter-review commission for a mid-sized municipality, and we are now seeing repeated proposals to amend the city charter to lock in long-term climate-adaptation spending and restrict future councils from diverting those funds. The proponents invoke constitutional precommitment and describe the mechanism as a collective Ulysses contract—binding present citizens against future preference reversals—yet no one on the board has a reproducible way to evaluate whether a given clause actually achieves that binding or merely delegates authority under a different name. I need a reusable decision procedure that my commission can apply to any such proposal, designed for non-philosophers who must vote within a fixed meeting schedule and sign off on the recommendation.

Please ground the procedure in the actual logic of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, translated for practitioners. It must distinguish strategic precommitment, in which a present body genuinely constrains its own future choices, from structural precommitment, in which successors with different preferences are bound without a corresponding present commitment; and it must flag three binding-failure modes—emergency-override clauses that swallow the commitment, demographic turnover that renders the original preference obsolete, and delegation disguised as entrenchment. Provide no more than four ordered textual tests that a commissioner can apply directly to the language of a proposed amendment, with explicit stop points where the proposal fails and explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values rather than mechanics.

Then apply the procedure to three charter clauses: (a) a provision requiring three percent of the annual budget for wetland preservation, revocable only by a two-thirds referendum; (b) a provision that automatically transfers climate-fund authority to an appointed trustee if the council attempts diversion, with no sunset date; and (c) a provision locking in current zoning for carbon-sequestration forests but containing an undefined public-emergency exception.

Use the following sections in this order:

1. Conceptual framing (about 150 words): Define strategic versus structural precommitment and the three failure modes, without appealing to external literature.
2. The decision procedure (about 350 words): The ordered steps, the four textual tests, stop points, value-hand-off flags, and the conditions under which the procedure cannot settle the case.
3. Worked applications (about 350 words): Run all three cases; include at least one result that the clause proponents would reject.
4. Limits of the protocol (about 150 words): The residual judgment calls, the risk of false positives in identifying delegation, and the specific vote the commission must still take after running the procedure.
