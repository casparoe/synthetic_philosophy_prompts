I had a long argument with a close friend last night about whether the backward induction paradox in the centipede game actually undermines real-world cooperation, and we kept circling without resolving anything. I want an outside adjudication of our exchange, point by point.

(1) She opened by saying the paradox is genuinely devastating for rational-choice accounts of cooperation: if both players are rational and it is common knowledge that both are rational, then at the final decision node the second player should defect, which makes the penultimate node a defection point for the first player, and the reasoning unwinds all the way back to the first move. So rational agents should never cooperate, yet we observe sustained cooperation constantly; therefore, either people are systematically irrational or the model is descriptively bankrupt.

(2) I replied that she was conflating normative and descriptive claims. Backward induction is a normative result about what agents ought to do given perfect common knowledge of rationality; the fact that people actually cooperate just shows they fall short of that ideal, not that the reasoning itself is flawed. Calling observed cooperation irrational is no stranger than calling observed belief in contradictions irrational.

(3) She countered that this saves the model by making it unfalsifiable—any deviation is just labeled irrationality. She then proposed a more precise fix: in any real interaction there is always some minimal, irreducible uncertainty about whether the other person is reasoning correctly, is fatigued, or is using a different frame of evaluation. That uncertainty breaks the infinite chain of common knowledge and makes continued cooperation rational after all, so the paradox reveals not that cooperation is irrational but that perfect common knowledge is an impossible idealization.

(4) I tried to push deeper with what I take to be the real structural problem. Even if we grant her uncertainty point, there is a second-order induction about belief revision: if I anticipate that you might cooperate because of your uncertainty about me, then I should adjust my own reasoning to exploit that anticipation, which you should then anticipate in turn. This creates a meta-level backward induction not about payoff nodes but about hierarchical beliefs—each step back changes not just the expected payoff but what counts as a rational belief about the other player's rationality. She dismissed this as adding epicycles: instead of accepting that the model fails to map onto reality, I was layering recursive belief structures to preserve it.

Please adjudicate our four moves: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me whether my meta-induction point about hierarchical belief revision genuinely advances the debate or is indeed just an ad hoc rescue, and identify what both of us are missing—particularly about whether backward induction applies only inside strictly defined games or also governs institutional cooperation where binding mechanisms and repeated interaction change the logical structure.
