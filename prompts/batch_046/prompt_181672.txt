I spent about thirty years studying marine ecology before I retired, so I'm pretty comfortable with feedback loops and population dynamics, but lately I've been trying to wrap my head around philophy of social knowledge, specifically echo chambers and epistemic bubbles. I keep running into these terms in essays but the definitions seem to slide around, so I figured I'd lay out my gut verdicts on concrete cases and see if there's actually a coherent principle behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my calls. First, a small town where everyone gets news from the same local radio station that never reports national politics -- they don't distrust outside news, they just never run into it. I'd call that an epistemic bubble, not an echo chamber. Second, a climate activist group that actively mocks anyone citing industry-funded studies, even well-designed ones, and has internal shaming for dissenters. That's clearly an echo chamber. Third, a research group in my old field that only cites papers from their own institution and ignores rival labs, but they claim it's because they think the rival methods are sloppy, not to protect a worldview. Structurally it looks like case two, but I want to say it's not an echo chamber -- though I'm not sure why. Fourth, an AI reccomendation system that only shows users content matching past clicks without them knowing; they never asked for filtering. Is that a bubble, or something else entirely? Fifth, a religious community that teaches members to distrust secular media as worldly corruption and rewards people for reporting doubters. Echo chamber, definitely.

The one I keep going back and forth on is this: a bunch of retired scientists like me who meet every tuesday to talk philophy over coffee. We've all read the same three popular books -- Dennett, maybe some others -- but we never seek out critics. There's no suppression of dissent; there's just none present. Are we in an epistemic bubble, or does it only count when there's active exclusion? And does it matter whether the boundary is transparent to us?

Is there a principle that captures all these verdicts? Maybe something about active distrust versus mere absence of exposure, or maybe intentionality of the boundary, or transparency? Can you state the precise principle -- or a small menu of candidates -- that delivers my verdicts, tell me which case it forces me to overturn, and say what it predicts for a situation I haven't listed, like a hospital board that deliberately excludes patient advocates from deliberations because they believe laypeople lack statistical literacy? I want to understand if I'm tracking something real or just making excuses for my coffee group.
