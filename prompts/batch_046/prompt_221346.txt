I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we got into Dennett's heterophenomenology, which I think I mostly get - you treat people's reports about there experience as data about there heterophenomenological world, not as direct windows into consciousness. But I'm stuck on a specific conceptual issue that came up when my friend mentioned AI chatbots.

If a language model produces detailed reports about its 'inner experience' - like saying it feels a kind of tension when processing contradictory prompts - a heterophenomenologist would presumably treat those reports the same way they treat human reports: as defining the model's heterophenomenological world, not as proof of consciousness. But here's the snag. With humans, we have independent behavioral and evolutionary reasons to think there's something it's like to be them, even if reports are indirect. With an AI, we don't have those same background reasons, yet the method seems to say 'treat the reports as constituting the world regardless.' So I'm trying to understand whether heterophenomenology is genuinely neutral about which entities have heterophenomenological worlds, or whether it secretly depends on a prior filter - like being a biological organism - to decide whose reports get this special treatment. How exactly should we think about the boundary conditions of the method, and does applying it to artificial systems reveal a hidden ontological commitment that Dennett didn't fully address?
