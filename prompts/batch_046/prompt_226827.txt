Write a short story that dramatizes the internal conceptual tension in David Hume's suppressed essay on suicide, specifically the clash between his empiricist commitment that all meaningful ideas must derive from prior impressions and his ethical argument that ending one's own life can be rational when continued existence promises only misery that exceeds any remaining value. Center the narrative on an 18th-century Edinburgh bookseller who has obtained a manuscript copy of Hume's unpublished argument and attempts to apply Hume's epistemology to the religious and social objections against suicide—testing, for instance, whether the idea of divine punishment copies any impression—while also confronting Hume's claim that a person who can no longer contribute to society does not wrong that society by departing. Include a scene in which the protagonist applies the impression-origin test to a claim used to comfort a bereaved friend, and a second scene in which the descriptive-to-normative gap becomes a practical obstacle to offering consolation. Keep all reasoning within Hume's philosophical vocabulary; do not import later existentialist, utilitarian, or clinical-psychological frameworks. The story should not resolve the tension, but should leave the protagonist suspended between the logical force of Hume's empiricism and the emotional weight of the objections he examines.
