I've been working through Pascal's notes on justice and force—the idea that justice without power is powerless and power without justice is tyrannical—and I've started noticing a recurring argumentative pattern in contemporary debates about institutional reform that seems to borrow that pairing but deploy it asymmetrically. Here is the structure: a critic shows that an institution distributes goods or burdens unjustly. The defender replies that correcting this requires coercive enforcement, and that coercion lacking perfect justice is dangerous, effectively invoking Pascal's warning that force without justice becomes tyranny. But when the critic proposes even a minimal, bounded enforcement mechanism, the defender shifts ground and argues that any state intervention to alter the distribution is itself an unjust imposition of power, while the existing arrangement, however uneven, reflects an organic order that needs no further justification. Justice is thus used to constrain proposed force, but the absence of justice is used to protect existing force. I see this in arguments about property rights, progressive taxation, and criminal sentencing. Is there an established name for this selective, one-way use of the justice-force coupling—perhaps an immunization strategy or a "Pascalian ratchet"? How does it differ from ordinary status-quo bias or from special pleading? And if the literature has no specific term, how would you carve the phenomenon precisely: what is essential to it, what is merely adjacent, and where are its boundaries?
