A recent essay in a popular philosophy magazine summarizes Kant’s position on lying—especially the scenario, drawn from his late essay on truthfulness and benevolent motives in which a murderer asks after a hidden friend—like this. First, Kant derives the prohibition on lying from the Formula of Universal Law: universalized deception would collapse the practice of communication and trust, making language self-defeating, so lying is rationally impossible for a rational being in every circumstance. Second, when the murderer asks where your friend is hidden, you are obligated to answer truthfully, because making a benevolent exception violates the unconditional nature of moral law; specifically, to lie is to treat the murderer merely as a means to your friend’s safety rather than as an end in himself, and it also makes you causally responsible for whatever harm follows from the murderer’s reliance on your falsehood—whereas telling the truth leaves you blameless since you did not cause the outcome. Third, Kant permits no evasion through silence or misdirection, since withholding information when directly questioned is morally indistinguishable from explicit falsehood, and perfect duties admit no gradation or contextual weighing. Fourth, the essay concludes that this demonstrates the rigidity of Kantian ethics: because truthfulness is a perfect duty and beneficence is only imperfect, there can be no genuine conflict of duties here—only a failure of moral resolve—so contemporary Kantians who reject the conclusion must be implicitly abandoning the categorical imperative’s absolute authority.
