I've been working through care ethics for a comparative project on normative frameworks, and I'm struck that most defenses stay at the level of "relationships matter" or jump straight to institutional prescriptions without specifying what the theory is actually required to explain. Before I evaluate particular authors, I want the full spec. What must an adequate care ethics actually deliver?

Give me the requirements list — the phenomena it must explain (asymmetric dependency, institutional care failure, the labor/relation distinction), the distinctions it cannot collapse (between having a care obligation and being a virtuous carer; between care appropriate to intimates versus strangers; between legitimate care and controlling "care surplus" where over-attention becomes infantilizing), the empirical or sociological results it must accommodate without reducing to them (attachment patterns, gendered labor distribution, the fact that care is often unidirectional), and the edge cases it must handle consistently — including obligations toward entities that cannot reciprocate or even recognize care (severe cognitive disability, ecological maintenance, future generations) without covertly importing rights-based or utilitarian machinery to cover the gap. For each requirement, include the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, flag which are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are camp-specific preferences (feminist relational, political, virtue-theoretic), and say which single requirement, if taken seriously, eliminates the most popular formulations — I suspect it is the care-surplus/autonomy-boundary problem, but I want the argument laid out. A table is fine for the list; brief notes on how Noddings' relational account, Held's political care ethics, and Tronto's practice-based view fare against the spec are welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves, since that is where the conceptual work is.
