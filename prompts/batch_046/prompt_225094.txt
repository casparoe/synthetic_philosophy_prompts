So there's this weird convention in biopics and historical novels: if a real person died, like, over a century ago and is in every textbook, writers will invent dialouge, change timelines, even give them private thoughts they never had. But if the person died in the 1980s and wasn't a public figure, the same authors will insist on strict factual accuracy and get permission from estates. The practice treats the dead differently based on a kind of sliding scale of fame and time. What theory of posthumous harm — or of who owns a life-story — does that pattern actually presuppose? Is it just that reputation stops mattering once you're dead long enough, or does it rely on a distinction between "historical figures" and "private individuals" that doesn't hold up under scrutiny? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the practice is philosophicly consistent or just pragmatically lazy, and tell me what a filmmaking ethics that actually respected the dead equally would have to look like — would we need estate consent for every Roman senator in a drama?
