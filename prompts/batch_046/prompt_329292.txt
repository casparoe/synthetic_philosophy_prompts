Exam Question (20 points):
Analyze the debate over cognitive penetration of perception. In your response, (a) clearly distinguish representational penetration from phenomenal penetration; (b) differentiate diachronic penetration—where long-term cognitive training reshapes perceptual dispositions—from synchronic penetration, where an occurrent belief or expectation alters a present perceptual state; (c) examine whether the predictive-processing framework, which models perception as top-down Bayesian updating driven by prior probabilities, lends support specifically to synchronic phenomenal penetration, or whether it instead offers only a subpersonal, mechanistic account of diachronic representational change; and (d) discuss whether demonstrating that a belief causally influences perceptual content automatically resolves the epistemic concern that such influence might generate circular justification.

Student Answer:
Cognitive penetration occurs when mental states like beliefs influence perception. Representational penetration is when a belief changes what the experience represents—for example, seeing a shape as a tree rather than as an undifferentiated green form. Phenomenal penetration is stronger: the belief changes the qualitative feel of the experience itself, such as seeing a shadow as genuinely threatening rather than merely interpreting it as such after the fact.

There is also an important temporal distinction. Diachronic penetration involves long-term changes, like an arborist learning to distinguish ash trees from oaks through years of practice. Synchronic penetration involves a belief affecting perception right now. Predictive processing explains perception through hierarchical Bayesian inference, where the brain applies prior probabilities to sensory input. Because these priors are essentially beliefs, predictive processing demonstrates that synchronic phenomenal penetration is ubiquitous: every visual experience is directly shaped by our current beliefs. This undermines Pylyshyn’s informational-encapsulation thesis, since the visual system clearly receives constant top-down input from belief states.

On the epistemic side, there is a circularity worry: if I believe someone is dangerous and that belief makes me perceive them as dangerous, then appealing to that perception to justify my belief seems circular. But predictive processing resolves this problem. Once we understand the causal mechanism—how prior probabilities update perceptual hypotheses—we see that the influence is just normal brain functioning, not a contaminating bias. Therefore, the epistemic worry dissolves. Macpherson is wrong to deny phenomenal penetration on the grounds of experiential transparency, because transparency only tells us that we see through the experience to the object, not that the experience’s qualitative character is immune to belief. In sum, penetration is real, widespread, and epistemically benign.
