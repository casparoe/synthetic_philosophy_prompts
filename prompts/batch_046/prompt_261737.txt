I'm an extension agent for a county ag office, and every week I have to sign off on pest-control requests — mostly insecticide sprays for crops, sometimes urban fogging for mosquitos near wetlands. I want a reusable decision procedure I can run in about 15 minutes before a meeting, based on the idea that insects can suffer but that doesn't automatically trump everything else. Specifically, use this framework I'm calling "stressed-threshold harm reduction": there's a local population stress threshold (when density exceeds what the habitat can support without widespread starvation or disease anyway), and above that line lethal control is permitted only if it minimizes prolonged suffering; below it, only non-lethal or targeted mechanical methods are okay. The procedure also has to check for "trophic rebound" — killing one pest that feeds predators and causes a worse suffering cascade elsewhere.

Write me the actual checklist: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer, hard stops that return "deny," and hand-off points where I have to make a values call rather than a factual one. Include a quick classification test for rapid vs. prolonged kill mechanisms, with one example of each. Then run it on two proposals: spraying neonicotinoids on late-season soybean aphids, and fogging for mosquitos near a wetland where bat populations depend on them. Make sure one answer comes back "approve with conditions" that still feels kind of bad, and one comes back "deny" but hits an ambiguity about larval identification — since some beneficial larvae look identicle to pest ones. Flag exactly where the procedure breaks down if the applicant can't identify the species precisely, and be honest about which steps are just judgement calls dressed up as rules. Also, make sure to seperate the factual checks from the values hand-offs clearly.

Keep it practical — I'm not a philosopher, just the guy with the stamp. And don't worry about citing papers; I just need something I can actually use.
