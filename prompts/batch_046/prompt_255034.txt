I am writing a paper for my philosophy of religion seminar on Buddhist anattā and Western theories of personal identity. I understand the orthodox position: the self is not found among the five skandhas, and a person is only a dependently originated stream of aggregates with no underlying substrate. But I recently read about the Pudgalavāda school, which holds that the pudgala is neither identical to nor different from the aggregates, functioning as a conventional designation that allows moral responsibility and rebirth to make sense. This led me to a puzzling conclusion. It seems orthodox Buddhists face a forced choice: either they deny any bearer of karma altogether, which makes rebirth and ethical continuity incoherent, or they implicitly accept a conventionally constructed narrative self that does exactly the work Western diachronic identity theories require—meaning anattā collapses into a merely metaphysical denial with no soteriological or ethical bite. So is the real disagreement between orthodox and Pudgalavāda Buddhists actually not about whether a self exists, but about whether personal identity is a pragmatically indispensable fiction? And if that is right, does Buddhist ethics not secretly presuppose the very kind of diachronic personal continuity that anattā is supposed to reject?
