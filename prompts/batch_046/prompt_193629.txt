In a philosophy-of-science discussion, Elena and Marcus disagreed about whether the reliability of radiocarbon calibration for medieval manuscripts operates as a Wittgensteinian hinge. Elena argued that for manuscript historians, the commitment that physical decay rates yield chronological ordering stands fast: genuinely doubting it is not to raise an empirical correction but to dissolve the temporal framework within which archival questions make sense, much as doubting the earth’s existence dissolves geology rather than revising it. Marcus replied that historians constantly revise calibration parameters, correct for reservoir effects, and debate regional offsets, so treating calibration reliability as a hinge mischaracterizes routine empirical adjustment as framework collapse. Elena countered that Marcus conflates parameter revision within the curve with doubting the method’s validity altogether; the hinge is the broader commitment to decay-rate ordering, not any particular curve version, and refining parameters assumes rather than threatens that hinge. Marcus said that if the hinge is that broad, it becomes trivial—then nearly every scientific presupposition qualifies, and Wittgenstein’s distinction between hinge and empirical belief collapses into useless generality. Elena answered that hinges are precisely those commitments whose abandonment renders inquiry impossible rather than merely harder, and that for manuscript historians, rejecting radiocarbon ordering entirely would indeed collapse the practice, even if tweaking the curve does not.
