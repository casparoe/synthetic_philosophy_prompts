I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on AI value alignment and liberal neutrality, and I've become stuck on a design choice that is usually treated as purely engineering-level but seems to carry heavy philosophical baggage. In the standard reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback pipeline used by several major labs, contracted annotators—typically recruited through global labor platforms—evaluate model outputs along dimensions of helpfulness and harmlessness, and these evaluations are collapsed into a single scalar reward signal through arithmetic averaging. Before aggregation, ratings that fall more than two standard deviations from the mean for a given prompt are routinely discarded as unreliable. The institutions running these pipelines then present the resulting system as aligned with broad human values rather than with any particular moral or cultural constituency. I want to understand what implicit theory of value aggregation and moral disagreement this institutional practice actually presupposes.

Specifically, does the averaging step commit the pipeline to the view that values are interpersonally comparable on a cardinal scale, or can it be interpreted as a merely procedural mechanism that remains agnostic between utilitarian, contractualist, and pluralist frameworks? And what philosophical work is being done by the outlier-trimming rule: does it presuppose that deep moral disagreement is mostly statistical noise, thereby smuggling in a convergence thesis about reasonable values, or is there a defensible non-epistemic rationale—perhaps about annotator competence or strategic manipulation—that avoids that commitment? There also seems to be a tension with liberal neutrality: the same organizations often invoke neutrality among reasonable comprehensive doctrines to justify refusing to embed any single worldview, yet the aggregation mechanism itself collapses diverse judgments into one weighted preference ordering. Is that tension only apparent, or does the practice genuinely presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the correct weighting of values? In your response, please separate what is relatively settled in the literature on social choice and value pluralism from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding whether cardinal aggregation is ever compatible with robust respect for reasonable pluralism—and do not present any single reconstruction of the pipeline's implicit commitments as the consensus view among philosophers working on these questions.
