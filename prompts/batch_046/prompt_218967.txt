In philosophy of physics and cosmology, there is a persistent institutional pattern: Gödel's rotating universe solution to Einstein's field equations is taught in general relativity courses as a mathematically valid but physically irrelevant pathological case—useful mainly for illustrating that global causality need not hold—while Gödel's philosophical argument, derived from the same formal model, that objective time is an illusion is treated separately, often in metaphysics courses, without requiring students to reconcile the physical dismissal with the philosophical weight. Meanwhile, cosmological models that invoke closed timelike curves are routinely subjected to empirical constraints, yet Gödel's specific metric is rarely tested against observational data in the same manner. What implicit philosophy of science does this compartmentalization presuppose? Does it assume a sharp divide between mathematical existence and physical possibility? Does it treat philosophical arguments extracted from formal models as belonging to a different epistemic category than physical predictions? And is the practice consistent with the discipline's willingness to take other formally pathological solutions—such as black hole singularities—seriously as physical predictions?
