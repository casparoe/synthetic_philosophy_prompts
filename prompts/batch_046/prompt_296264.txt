I am revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar on value pluralism and rational choice. Rather than surveying the standard literature on incommensurable values, I want to organize the course around three live research fronts that actually determine how we should handle hard choices in practice. I have in mind a specific framing: take the position that genuinely hard choices involve options that stand in a relation of parity—neither better, worse, nor precisely equal—such that a rational agent may permissibly choose either, yet the unchosen alternative leaves a rational residue of regret that is not reducible to a mistake about the choice made. Given this framing, which two or three open questions or lines of work matter most right now? In particular, I want you to address whether the small-improvement argument against parity can be blocked without collapsing parity into equality; how models of preference aggregation should treat parity relations when scaling individual hard choices to institutional or algorithmic decision contexts (for instance, democratic deliberation over environmental preservation versus economic development); and whether sustaining genuine parity requires a pluralist ontology of values incompatible with naturalistic reduction. Rank these lines of inquiry—or substitute others you find more pressing—and defend the ordering explicitly: specify the criteria (tractability, neglect, downstream consequences for policy or formal decision theory), identify the trade-offs between them, and state what evidence or result would cause you to reorder them. The deliverable should be a defended research agenda, not a survey or a reading list.
