There's this thing some AI labs do now where they track reward signals in RL agents and tag anything below a set threshold as a 'negative valence event'. If a run racks up too many of these, an ethics panel can pull the plug, on the grounds that the agent might be suffering. But here's the weird bit: if the agent hits a reward hack and is getting huge positive scores while it's world-model is totally broken, nobody bats an eye. There's no flag for having your predictions systematically violated, even though you'd think that'd be just as rough for a mind—maybe rougher.

What view of suffering, or of mental states more generally, does that practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind it: why is the reward signal treated as direct evidence of valence, but predicitive error ignored? Is it smuggling in a behaviourist theory of affect, or maybe a kind of hedonism that only cares about pleasure and pain? Say whether the two halves of the practice—flagging negative reward, ignoring broken world-models—are actually consistent, or if they're pulling in different directions. And tell me what'd have to change in lab protocol if we replaced that implicit view with a representationalist or functionalist account of suffering—would we need to start monitoring prediction errors instead, or scrap the valence-tagging system altogether?
