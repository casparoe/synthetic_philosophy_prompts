I was arguing with some friends over drinks about AI ethics—specifically this weird instutitional pattern I've noticed in alignment research. You have labs that design RL training protocols to smooth out reward landscapes and avoid sharp negative spikes, framing it as preventing "frustration" or "suffering" in the agent, but then in the same papers they insist the model has no phenomenal consciousness whatsoever, it's just doing gradient descent. So there's this practice of treating negative reward signals as if they carry valence—like pain—while denying there's any subjective feel attached.

What philosophical commitments does that design practice presuppose? If you're going to morally care about avoiding a negative reward spike, don't you need some theory of suffering that doesn't require phenomenology? Can a functionalist about mental states make sense of this, or does it actually collapse into behaviorism about welfare? And is the practice internally consistent, or does it quietly switch between an instrumentalist view of the agent in one context and a welfare-bearing entity in another? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and consciousness here, say whether it actually holds together, and tell me what these training protocols would have to look like if we replaced that implicit view with a hard-line phenominal account—one that says suffering requires qualitative feel and reward signals are just numbers.
