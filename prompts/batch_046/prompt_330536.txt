Consider a constitutional framework adopted in several hybrid democracies that institutionalizes a two-stage popular mandate for major structural reforms. First, a randomly selected citizens' assembly—sequestered from media and party politics—deliberates for months and issues a binding policy recommendation. That recommendation is then put to a general referendum. The distinctive feature is the override clause: if the referendum rejects the assembly's recommendation, the recommendation still enters into law after a mandatory cooling-off period, on the explicit statutory rationale that referendum outcomes are vulnerable to emotional mobilization and elite manipulation, whereas the assembly's deliberation reveals the authentic will of the people. At the same time, the same constitutional order permits courts to invalidate legislation—including direct-democratic enactments—when they conflict with entrenched rights of minorities, even when those enactments command overwhelming popular support.

What philosophical conception of democratic legitimacy and collective agency does this design presuppose? Does it require a substantive distinction between an authentic popular will and a merely expressed aggregate preference, or can it be justified on purely procedural or epistemic grounds? Is the framework consistent with the parallel institution of judicial review that may nullify even an authentic popular mandate? Reconstruct the implicit theory of popular sovereignty at work, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, identify points of philosophical underdetermination—whether rival theories of democracy could license the same institutional form—and specify what elements of the framework would have to be abandoned or re-described if one rejected the idea that there exists a correct collective will independent of actual voting behavior.
