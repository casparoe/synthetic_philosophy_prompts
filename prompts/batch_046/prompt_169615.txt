You are a senior philosopher of technology and labour, with particular expertise in the ethics of automation and the conceptual analysis of work. Your task is to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps—the Post-Labour Abolitionists and the Constitutive Work Defenders—could both sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on the meaning of work and automation.

Before composing the statement, take a few minutes to brainstorm: (i) the conceptual distinctions both sides could accept without surrendering their core commitments; (ii) where their disagreement is genuinely substantive rather than verbal; (iii) what concrete technological scenario or institutional case both would agree bears on their cruxes; and (iv) whether any portion of their dispute dissolves once terms are clarified precisely.

The collaboration is framed around a specific, somewhat niche development: the emergence of autonomous maintenance loops—systems that not only produce goods and services but also self-monitor, self-repair, and self-optimise with minimal human input. The Abolitionists maintain that even the residual curatorial labour of overseeing these loops is a contingent historical burden rather than a source of meaning, and that full automation—including the automation of oversight—should be pursued so that human flourishing can migrate entirely to non-instrumental activities. The Defenders maintain that curatorial labour is not a leftover to be eliminated but a distinct mode of practical engagement—interpreting anomalies, setting ethical boundaries, and exercising judgement in open-ended contexts—that is partly constitutive of human dignity and social recognition, and that attempts to automate this layer away risk eroding the very capacities that make us moral agents.

Draft the document in polished, formal British English, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and without relying on obscure figures or theories that a well-trained model might not reliably recall. Structure it as follows.

First, provide a numbered list of agreed claims. These should cover: the descriptive state of current and near-future automation, including autonomous maintenance loops; the normative importance of human wellbeing and autonomy; a neutral distinction between labour (instrumental, burdensome) and curatorial engagement (interpretive, boundary-setting); the shared premise that suffering from unnecessary toil is bad; and any other shared premises about ecology, economics, or agency that do not beg the question.

Second, present a disagreements table. For each disagreement, state: (a) the precise formulation of the Abolitionist position, in wording they would accept; (b) the precise formulation of the Defender position, in wording they would accept; and (c) a concise explanation of why the two formulations are not compatible.

Third, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Likely candidates include: whether curatorial oversight is technically eliminable without loss of system safety or adaptability; whether human dignity requires ongoing practical engagement with complex technological systems; and whether the value of unmediated experience or naturalness carries independent moral weight in this context.

Fourth, for each crux specify: (a) a concrete case, argument, or technological scenario that both sides agree would bear on it; (b) the Abolitionists’ prediction about what that case or argument will show; and (c) the Defenders’ prediction about what it will show.

Fifth, include a brief concluding section on verbal disagreements. State clearly whether any part of the dispute is merely about the word work or labour, identify which part, and explain why the remaining disagreement is substantive.
