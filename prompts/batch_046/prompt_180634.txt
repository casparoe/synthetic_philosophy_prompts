Read Clint Eastwood's film *Million Dollar Baby* through the lens of what might be called "narrative euthanasia" — the view that the moral weight of an assisted death turns less on the relief of suffering or on bare autonomy than on whether the death completes, rather than truncates, the arc of the subject's practical identity. Maggie Fitzgerald, after suffering a catastrophic spinal injury in the ring, asks her trainer Frankie Dunn to help her die. Rather than framing the scene solely as a clash between beneficence and the sanctity of life, examine whether Maggie's request can be interpreted as an effort to retain authorial control over her life story: she has lived as a fighter who wins on her own terms, and she refuses to become a passive object of medical prolongation. Consider, too, Frankie's role: if he complies, does he become a co-author of her ending, and does that shared authorship reinforce her autonomy or dilute it into a collaborative fiction?

To sharpen the reading, introduce the idea of an "asymmetry of narrative repair": while we can retrospectively revise earlier chapters of our lives through reinterpretation, death is the only revision that is irrevocable and therefore demands a distinct form of justification. Ask whether the film dramatises this asymmetry. Maggie can reinterpret her boxing career as a triumph despite the injury, but she cannot reinterpret her death once it has occurred. Does the film imply that the value of narrative closure outweighs the irreversibility, or does Frankie's enduring guilt suggest that co-authorship always leaves a moral residue that narrative logic cannot dissolve? A good answer should take both the film's emotional architecture and the philosophical framework seriously, noting where the narrative-euthanasia reading illuminates a scene and where the film resists being reduced to an illustration of the theory.
