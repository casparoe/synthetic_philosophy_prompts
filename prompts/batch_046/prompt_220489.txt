I work as an ombudsman for an international humanitarian organization and receive three or four field reports each month in which staff claim they faced a “genuine moral dilemma”—not merely a hard choice, but a situation in which they were genuinely required to do two incompatible things, leaving a moral remainder regardless of which course they took. The classification I assign—genuine dilemma (institutional acknowledgment without fault), tragic but resolvable choice (one action was correct, though costly), or rationalized conflict—determines whether the staff member receives support, accountability, or further review, so the stakes are real and I must sign off on every decision. I need a reusable procedure my team can run in about thirty minutes per case without philosophy training, yet it must preserve the actual conceptual work: distinguishing moral residue that survives a decision from ordinary regret, separating logical incompatibility from practical difficulty, and identifying when both obligations are direct rather than derived from a single higher-order principle that resolves them.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of four tests—Incompatibility, Derivation, Residual Obligation, and Symmetry—each with explicit pass/stop conditions, a clear statement of what counts as sufficient evidence at that stage, and marked hand-off points where judgment rather than fact must take over. Include a misfire flag describing the kind of case in which the procedure will wrongly classify a rationalized conflict as a genuine dilemma. Then apply it to two reports: first, a field medic with exactly one dose of antivenom and two dying patients with identical clinical need and no available tie-breaking feature; second, a commander who authorized an airstrike that saved an estimated one hundred civilians but killed ten others, where intelligence was genuinely ambiguous but the operative framework was “minimize total harm.” The second case should produce a classification my staff will resist—explain precisely why the procedure reaches it and specify what moral remainder, if any, still applies to the commander. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot determine and where I must still exercise direct judgment. Keep the language formal and operational, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
