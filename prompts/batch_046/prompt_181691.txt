Compare Christopher Boorse’s biostatistical theory of health with Jerome Wakefield’s harmful dysfunction analysis. For Boorse, health is statistically normal functional ability relative to an appropriate reference class; for Wakefield, a disorder requires both the failure of an evolved internal mechanism to perform its natural function and harm assessed by social norms.

Now consider the following hypothetical case, which we will call silent ecological dysfunction: an individual carries a genetic variant that disrupts a metabolic pathway in a way that produces no detectable statistical deviation from the reference-class mean, causes no subjective suffering or social impairment, and yet confers a severe selective disadvantage in a specific, rarely encountered ecological niche. The dysfunction is evolutionarily real but invisible to population statistics and to the individual’s own evaluative framework.

With this case in view, address the following: (1) Would Boorse’s framework classify the condition as disease? If not, is there any principled way to adjust the reference class or statistical threshold without smuggling in normative judgments about evolutionary fitness? (2) Would Wakefield’s framework classify it as disorder? If the harm is absent from the individual’s experience and social context, can evolutionary disadvantage alone satisfy the harm criterion, or does this expose a hidden dependency on external evaluation? (3) Which of the two theories ultimately handles the tension between statistical invisibility and evolutionary dysfunction more coherently, and does this case suggest that any purely naturalist account must tacitly rely on a normative baseline? Give a point-by-point assessment with specific reasons for your judgments.
