Question: In Meditation II, Descartes reaches the conclusion that he exists whenever he thinks, and he then identifies himself as a thing that thinks. Some interpreters have pressed what might be called an impersonal-construction objection: just as saying it is raining does not commit us to the existence of a rain-substance, saying there is thinking might not commit us to a thinker-substance. Evaluate whether this objection undermines Descartes's inference from the occurrence of thought to the existence of a thinker. In your answer, distinguish carefully between (i) what the cogito proves about existence, (ii) what it proves about essence, and (iii) whether it secures the temporal continuity of the self without further premises.

Student answer: Descartes's cogito establishes that I exist as a substance whose essence is thinking. The impersonal-construction objection misses the point: in Meditation II, Descartes already treats thought as a mode that requires a substantial bearer, analogous to motion requiring a body, so saying it thinks is grammatically parallel but metaphysically inadequate—there must be a thinker. Once this substance is established, its temporal continuity is secured simply by the fact that the cogito can be reiterated at will; if I think now and think again in an hour, the same substance must have persisted between those moments. The argument therefore delivers both existence and essence in a single step, and it also gives us persistence over time. What remains open is only the interaction problem, which Descartes resolves in Meditation VI by appealing to God to guarantee the union of mind and body.
