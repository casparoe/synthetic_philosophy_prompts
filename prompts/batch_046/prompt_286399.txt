Consider the institutional practice in biological research ethics: protocols governing social-insect studies require that individual ants, bees, or termites be anaesthetised and that harm be minimised for each specimen, yet no ethics board treats an integrated colony—one that regulates temperature, allocates labour, and solves optimisation problems that no individual ant comprehends—as a single welfare-subject. At the same time, cognitive scientists routinely describe colonies with mentalistic language—they ‘decide’ where to forage, ‘remember’ trail conditions, and ‘evaluate’ nest sites—only to insist that such descriptions are merely metaphorical for distributed individual behaviour. What philosophical commitments about mind, agency, and moral standing does this pairing of ethical individualism with descriptive emergentism presuppose? Would disrupting a colony’s integrated decision-making then demand justification comparable to interfering with an individual brain, and could the current practice survive if it replaced its implicit reductionism with a non-reductive emergentism about mental properties?
