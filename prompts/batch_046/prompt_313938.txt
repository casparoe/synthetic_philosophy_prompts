I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on authorization and autonomy in biomedical ethics, and I've been puzzling over a class of cases that standard accounts of invalid consent don't seem to capture well. Consider a research participant who is fully competent, faces no explicit threat, and receives technically accurate information about a clinical trial's procedures, but is deliberately led to believe that the study is designed to provide direct therapeutic benefit to her rather than to generate generalizable knowledge—an instance of what is sometimes called therapeutic misconception, but here induced through framing rather than false statements about risk or procedure. Most analyses treat deception as either a form of coercion (it manipulates the option set by hiding costs) or as a trigger for incompetence (it renders the subject irrational), yet neither reduction feels right: the participant can reason perfectly well within the false frame, and her options haven't been narrowed in any coercive sense. I'd like you to argue for the position that deception undermines consent in a structurally distinct way—specifically, that valid consent requires not only accurate information about the act authorized, but a correct grasp of what we might call the "authorizing context": the normative framework (e.g., therapeutic, experimental, commercial) within which the authorization is meant to operate. If deception corrupts that framework without the consenter's awareness, the authorization is hollow even if her preferences are stable, her reasoning intact, and her freedom uncoerced. Please develop this argument with a concrete scenario, distinguish it carefully from both coercion-based and competence-based invalidation, and respond to the objection that if a person's values and all-things-considered judgments remain unchanged by the deception, then the deception is normatively irrelevant to consent's validity. I'm not asking for a literature review; I'm interested in the conceptual architecture and whether this "authorizing context" condition can stand on its own.
