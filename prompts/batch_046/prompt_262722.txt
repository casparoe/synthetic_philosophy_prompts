I retired from marine biology a few years back and lately I've been sitting in on a philosphy seminar about monuments and public memory — mostly because my neighbor serves on our town's Civic Memory Board and I got curious about how they actually decide things. The board's precidents seem kind of all over the place to me, but maybe there's a hidden rule. So far they've kept the statue of Dr. Clara Mendel in the park even though she served on that 1920s eugenics board that approved sterilizations — they said she never performed them herself and the statue honors her public health work. They renamed Bennett Avenue because Bennett personally expelled Black students from advanced classes in the 1940s. They kept the Rivera Aqueduct named after the engineer who used prison labor that killed three men, but added a plaque — they said he didn't personally cause the deaths and the water system benefits everyone now. And they tore down the plaque for Colonel Hale, who ordered his militia to break the 1910 strike that killed four workers.

Now they've got a new one that's bugging me. There's this pedestrian bridge, the Vasquez Bridge, named after Elena Vasquez, an urban planner from the 70s who designed the highway loop that cut right through El Barrio Norte and demolished part of it. She didn't evict anyone personally — the council voted, contractors did the work — but she drew the lines and also designed the affordable housing that replaced part of the demolished block. She actually lived in the neighborhood too. Some descendants of the displaced families want the name changed because the bridge literally stands over the division; others want to keep it because the housing was her idea and she wasn't some outsider. I was thinking there's a difference between a statue you look at and a bridge you drive over every day — like a functional dependency, almost — and also this question of structural moral luck, where you're blamed for harms built into the system you worked inside.

So if the board wants to be consistent with itself, should they rename the bridge or keep it? Which of their earlier cases is the closest analog? I think there are at least two rules that could explain all four precidents — maybe one about direct personal harm versus one about whether the commemoration symbolizes a system that still hurts people — and I want to know which rule they're actually running, what each rule predicts here, and which further case would tell them apart. Also, if they go one way or the other, what principle are they actually adopting? I'm less interested in a simple yes/no than in seeing the logic laid out, especially since the functional-commemoration thing feels like it might break their usual framework.
