I'm putting together material for a philosophy-of-biology reading group that has been circling Douglas Hofstadter's Ant Fugue and the question of whether Aunt Hillary's colony could literally be a mind rather than a metaphor for one, and I find that most discussions sort people by which camp they claim to belong to, which makes the exercise useless for students who don't yet know the labels. Could you write a sorting taxonomy, presented entirely as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a long letter, that takes someone from concrete judgments about ant colonies to an actual position without ever asking them which theory they prefer?

The taxonomy should be built as a decision tree rooted in logical combinations rather than in named theories. Start with the question of whether having a mind requires subjective experience, then branch through whether distributed functional organization across many bodies can satisfy that requirement, whether a synthetic simulation with identical interaction patterns but no biological substrate could share it, and whether collective learning—say, a colony restructuring its pheromone network to avoid a toxic bait with no individual ant retaining the trace—counts as genuine memory or merely as an aggregate statistical effect. From there include a split about the split-colony case: if communication ceases for forty-eight hours and both halves maintain adaptive foraging, does that indicate one divided subject, two emergent subjects, or none? Each internal node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to, and each leaf must carry either the name of an actual position or the label "unnamed," together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment.

The leaves must include at least strong eliminativism (the colony is only a loose aggregate of individual ants, if they are minded at all), weak emergentism (the colony exhibits genuine cognitive properties but lacks a unified point of view), distributed functionalism (functional organization is sufficient regardless of substrate or biological form), and strong collective consciousness (the colony is a single subject of experience with its own phenomenology). Where the logical space produces a combination that no one actually defends—perhaps a view that demands phenomenal consciousness yet denies that substrate-independent simulation could ever possess it—note it briefly and diagnose whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. If any pair of named positions cannot be separated by the tree, flag that explicitly rather than forcing a false distinction. Finally, keep the tone analytical, avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since this is meant for a student working without access to external sources, and make sure the diagnostic cases are specific enough that the taxonomy cannot be answered by lifting a generic encyclopedia entry on collective cognition.
