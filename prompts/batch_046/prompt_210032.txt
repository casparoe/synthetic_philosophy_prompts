I'm writing from the position of a doctoral student in philosophy of language trying to determine whether Davidson's radical interpretation can be repaired or must be superseded. Rather than another comparative survey of existing alternatives—causal-informational, inferentialist, or neo-Davidsonian accounts—I want a specification of the genuine desiderata that any adequate theory of linguistic interpretation must satisfy, particularly if it is to handle cases Davidson's framework either neglects or handles inadequately: interpretation across agents that share no evolutionary history or biological form, the preservation of compositionality under radical holism, and the accommodation of widespread error without abandoning charity entirely. For each desideratum, please identify the phenomenon it must explain, the conceptual distinction it must preserve (for instance, between interpretive belief-attribution and causal-behavioral explanation; between semantic indeterminacy and metaphysical vagueness; or between first-person authority and third-person interpretation), and the result from an adjacent field it must not contradict (such as comparative cognition regarding non-linguistic representation, or cognitive science regarding concept acquisition prior to full linguistic competence). Indicate which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which might be traded away for theoretical gain elsewhere, and specify which single desideratum does the most damage to the most prominent successor theories. Finally, say whether any subset of these requirements appears jointly unsatisfiable—since that tension would itself be a substantive philosophical result rather than a mere failure of imagination. A structured list with brief motivating cases is sufficient; I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
