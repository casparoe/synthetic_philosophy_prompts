I'm working through some notes for a study group that meets to discuss philosophy of mind, and I want to make sure I'm not missing an obvious escape route in an argument I sketched about cognitive extension. The argument tries to show that Clark and Chalmers's parity principle, if taken seriously, collapses the distinction between using an external tool and being constituted by an institutional system, with consequences that seem to erase individual mental agency altogether. Here is the chain I wrote out:

(1) The parity principle says that if an external process plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive process, then it is part of the cognitive system, regardless of location. (2) In large research collaborations, shared databases and standardized notation systems play the functional role of working memory and long-term conceptual storage for every participant; they are constantly accessed, automatically trusted, and integrated into reasoning without internal rehearsal. (3) Therefore, by parity, these shared institutional structures are literally parts of each participant's mind. (4) If so, then when the database is updated by one member, every other member's beliefs are altered directly, without any intermediate psychological event—much as updating my own long-term memory changes my beliefs without an explicit inference. (5) But this means that belief revision can occur in me with no mental causation at all, violating what we usually take to be the causal closure of the mental domain. (6) The only way to block this is to deny that institutional structures meet the parity conditions, but that requires adding a hidden location-independent but agent-bound clause that looks ad hoc. (7) So either we accept that minds literally extend into bureaucratic archives and lose mental causal closure, or we abandon the parity principle. (8) Since abandoning parity seems to defeat the extended mind thesis entirely, and accepting bureaucratic minds seems absurd, the argument seems to force a dilemma that undermines the view from within.

Where does this reasoning slip? I am especially interested in whether step (4) commits a scope fallacy by treating system-level updates as automatically mapped onto every subsystem, or whether the real problem is in step (2)'s claim about automatic endorsement applying across organizational boundaries. If you think the argument is actually valid and we just have to bite the bullet on (5), I want to hear that too, but please explain what biting that bullet costs for our ordinary notions of epistemic responsibility.

Please think carefully about this, and start by brainstorming the different ways one might diagnose the flaw—whether it's an equivocation on part, a hidden modal slide, or a misapplication of the parity conditions—before giving your final assessment. You should approach this as a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in distributed cognition and the metaphysics of mental representation.
