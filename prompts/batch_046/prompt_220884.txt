I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I am drafting an essay that uses a problem from my field to think through the idea of origin essentialism—the claim that an individual could not have originated from a different sperm and egg. In my work on deterministic builds and reproducible deployment pipelines, I have become preoccupied with what makes a particular compiled binary "the same artifact" across different environments. A deterministic build aims for bit-for-bit identical outputs given identical source inputs, yet in practice we must decide which conditions belong to the "input identity" and which are accidental: the exact compiler version, the random seed used for link-time optimization, the timestamp embedded in the executable header, or the container image hash used for the build environment. This led me to wonder whether there is a coherent analogy to origin essentialism. Just as the philosopher Saul Kripke is widely understood to have argued that a person is essentially tied to the specific biological origin from which they actually developed—so that I could not have come from different gametes, even if I might have had different hair color or been born in a different hospital—perhaps a software artifact is essentially tied to its source commit and core dependency tree, but not to the build timestamp or the particular CPU architecture on which it was compiled. However, I am unsure whether this analogy survives scrutiny. Biological entities seem to possess a kind of causal-historical continuity that software artifacts lack: if I alter a timestamp in a binary header, I have not "killed" one artifact and brought another into being, whereas altering the gametes that formed a zygote seems to yield a numerically different person. I am also concerned that I may be conflating metaphysical essentialism about origins with functional identity conditions—the idea that what makes a binary "the same program" is what it does, not the exact causal chain that produced it. My essay attempts to map these distinctions systematically: I treat (1) the source code and dependency graph as analogous to genetic origin; (2) the build environment and compiler toolchain as analogous to developmental conditions; and (3) ephemeral build artifacts like temporary file paths and embedded timestamps as analogous to accidental properties like the temperature of the delivery room. I then ask whether origin essentialism, if applied to artifacts, would imply that changing any of these conditions produces a different entity, or whether we should restrict essential origins only to the source-level specification. I would welcome comments on whether this framing misrepresents the philosophical position, whether the analogy between biological origins and software builds is coherent or a category mistake, and whether there are hidden premises about identity over time for non-biological entities that I have overlooked. I am especially interested in whether my distinction between "essential build conditions" and "accidental production features" survives once we reject the idea that artifacts possess the kind of intrinsic persistence conditions that living organisms possess.
