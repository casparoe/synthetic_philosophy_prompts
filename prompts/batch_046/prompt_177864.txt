As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand a framework that distinguishes three explanatory stances—the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance—and the associated debate over whether patterns that become visible only from one of these perspectives are genuinely real or merely useful predictive fictions. I grasp the basic taxonomy: the physical stance explains a system through its underlying mechanics and causal dynamics; the design stance explains it through functional organization and purpose; and the intentional stance explains behavior by attributing beliefs, desires, and rational agency. What I find conceptually slippery is the ontological question that seems to accompany this taxonomy. If I apply the intentional stance to a complex but non-conscious system—say, a large-scale recommendation engine whose internal parameter weights are largely opaque to its operators—am I tracking a real pattern that exists independently of my predictive needs, or am I only adopting a convenient heuristic? And does the design stance genuinely occupy a middle ground, where functional patterns are real but not reducible to physical implementation, especially in cases where the system-level behavior diverges in surprising ways from what the low-level dynamics alone would predict? I would appreciate an explanation that goes beyond a surface-level classification and addresses the intuitive core of why philosophers dispute the reality of stance-dependent patterns, particularly in engineering contexts where direct physical inspection is impractical and we must rely on higher-level descriptions to make sense of emergent behavior.
