Consider a municipal archive that preserves every official meeting minute in perpetuity, but automatically purges all "informal" digital and handwritten communications among officials after seven years unless an archivist individually flags them for retention. Simultaneously, it operates a "community memory" initiative that actively collects, digitizes, and fully indexes private letters and diaries from local residents of the same era, cataloguing them explicitly as "counter-archives" meant to remedy institutional silence. What philosophical commitments about the nature of power, discourse, and historical knowledge does this bifurcated retention policy presuppose? Does it implicitly adopt a Foucauldian view in which power operates through formal, institutional utterances while resistance resides in private, unofficial speech? Can the practice consistently maintain that informal official communications are epistemically negligible noise while treating private citizen writings as politically significant voice, or does that distinction collapse under scrutiny? And what view of archival justice—about who gets recorded, whose silences matter, and whether the archive reproduces the very power structures it claims to correct—is required to make the two policies cohere?
