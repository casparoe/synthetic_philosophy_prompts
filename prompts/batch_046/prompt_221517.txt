I'm taking a course on nineteenth-century evolutionary thought, and we've spent considerable time with Darwin's account in The Descent of Man of how moral sentiments arise from social instincts and sympathy. I'm now trying to understand how he might have reacted to a cluster of debates that only became prominent long after his death: the philosophical worry that evolutionary explanations of morality "debunk" moral objectivity, the empirical findings from evolutionary game theory showing that cooperative behaviour can stabilise in populations with no conscious normative commitment, and the more recent argument—rooted in evolutionary continuity—that synthetic minds developed through artificial evolutionary computation might warrant moral consideration if they exhibit sufficiently complex social cognition.

Please imagine that Darwin were alive today, fully conversant with this literature, and asked to address a specific tension. On one hand, he consistently insisted that human moral faculties are products of natural selection acting on social instincts; on the other, he clearly believed that cultivated sympathy could generate genuinely binding obligations, at least among human beings. Given his naturalistic method and his evident humanism, how do you think he would respond to the claim that evolutionary continuity between human and non-human cognition—combined with the possibility of evolved artificial minds—necessarily collapses moral authority into mere adaptive strategy? Would he likely argue that the evolutionary origin of sympathy is compatible with its becoming normatively authoritative for reflective beings, or would he concede that extending evolutionary explanations to synthetic agents undermines any special moral status? Please present your answer as a reasoned prediction of Darwin's own view rather than a personal opinion, write in American English, and avoid offering bibliographies or reading lists.
