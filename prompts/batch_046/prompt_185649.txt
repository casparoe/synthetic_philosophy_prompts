In a seminar discussion, two participants reached an impasse over Plato's attitude to drunkenness. The first claimed that the regulated symposia described in Laws I–II serve a pedagogical purpose: supervised drinking exposes hidden vices in citizens who have not yet internalised virtue, which is fully consistent with the Republic's prohibition on drunkenness for guardians, since the guardians have already achieved psychic harmony and require no such testing. The second objected that Plato consistently describes wine as weakening the rational part and inflaming the passions; therefore the Laws procedure does not reveal stable character but manufactures temporary disorder, undermining the very possibility of the stable virtue celebrated in the Republic. The first then proposed a conceptual distinction—between "psychic transparency," where wine strips away social masks to disclose enduring dispositions, and "behavioural simulation," where it induces artificial states that misrepresent the soul—arguing that Plato applies the former to citizens and the latter only to those already corrupted. The second rejected this as anachronistic, insisting that Plato never separates the soul's revealed state from wine's direct causal effect on it, and that without such separation the Laws' testing rationale collapses into contradiction. I observed the exchange without intervening. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the consistency between the texts, whether the transparency-simulation distinction can be sustained within Plato's psychology, and what crucial consideration both parties overlook. Write in British English, and conclude with a brief statement of what evidence or argument would cause you to revise your verdict, together with the point at which your reasoning is most likely to fail.
