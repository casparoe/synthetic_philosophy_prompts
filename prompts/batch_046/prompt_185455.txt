As a journalist covering the governance of artificial intelligence, I am writing a feature that moves beyond the familiar debate over whether we should supervise AI systems by evaluating their final outputs or by inspecting each intermediate reasoning step. The framing feels conceptually thin: it treats process and outcome as self-evident categories rather than as specifications that must earn their place in a theory of adequate supervision. Before I can assess any particular institutional proposal, I need the underlying conceptual architecture. Write the full desiderata—the specification of requirements—that any adequate theory of AI supervision must meet if it is to adjudicate between process-based and outcome-based approaches on principled grounds rather than by default.

The list should include the phenomena it must explain and the distinctions it must preserve. Among them: the difference between domains where reasoning steps are externally verifiable, such as formal derivation or executable code traces, and domains where intermediate states are opaque or reconstructed after the fact, such as strategic planning or open-ended narrative synthesis. The theory must also address what I will call the problem of supervisory provenance—the demand that a supervisory judgment be traceable to either a verified human evaluation or a formal constraint that was actually operative during reasoning, not merely compatible with it—versus counterfactual reconstructibility, the weaker condition that one could invent a plausible reasoning path that reaches the same conclusion. It must respect the distinction between local step validity and global coherence, and between institutional auditability and cognitive transparency, without collapsing either pair. It must remain consistent with established findings about human evaluators' cognitive limits, specifically working-memory constraints and anchoring biases during prolonged monitoring tasks, and with regulatory frameworks that require explainability without presupposing omniscient oversight.

For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or consideration, and flag whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or rather a preference characteristic of particular camps—interpretability researchers, safety engineers, or institutional regulators. Then indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: for example, whether full process transparency is compatible with scalable real-time deployment, or whether robustness against reward hacking can be achieved without some form of outcome-level evaluation. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific papers; the analysis should stand on conceptual grounds accessible without web search. Finally, briefly evaluate how the three main supervisory architectures—pure outcome-based reward modeling, step-level process reward frameworks, and principle-based or constitutionally guided supervision—fare against your list. Note where each necessarily compromises and where it delivers. Keep the emphasis on the criteria themselves: the deliverable is the specification, not the scorecard.
