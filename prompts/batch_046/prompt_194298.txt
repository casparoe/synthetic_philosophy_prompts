I'm marking exam scripts for my second-year moral philosophy moduel — we've been looking at whether big normative shifts happen through rational argument, economic interest, or internal codes of honour. The exam question asked students to compare Appiah's honour-based account of moral revolutions with the more structural, interest-driven picture often associated with Bernard Williams, using the end of duelling in Britain and one other case of their choice. I also asked them to keep in mind the distinction between honour-as-status and honour-as-accountability that we floated in seminar, since it's easy to conflate the two.

One script, call the student J, wrote this (I've copied it verbatim, recieve any typos):

Appiah thinks honour drives moral revolutions, but he immediately mixes up honour-as-status with honour-as-accountability. In the duelling case, gentlemen stopped because they realised fighting made them look barbaric to outsiders — that's clearly accountability, being judged from outside, which is actually closer to Williams. Williams says norms collapse when the prestige benefits no longer outweigh the social costs, and he applies this to slavery: planters kept it for status, but once industrial capital made slaveholding look backward, the benefit disappeared. So Appiah and Williams are really describing the same prestige-management mechanism, and the distinction between them is mostly artificial. Footbinding in late Qing China ended the same way — Chinese elites dropped it because Western merchants made them feel ashamed, which is honour-as-accountability, not status. So both thinkers explain footbinding if we just read them generously.
