I am currently taking a seminar on evolutionary models in the social sciences, and after discussing Dawkins's account of cultural transmission with a classmate, she offered me a synthesis that she claimed captures the core of the memetic framework. As she put it, Dawkins proposed that culture evolves through memes—discrete units of information that replicate via imitation—operating under the same three conditions that govern biological evolution: variation, selection, and retention. The success of any given meme is determined by its copying fidelity, its fecundity, and its longevity, which means that a scientific concept and a viral rumor are evaluated by precisely the same evolutionary metrics. Because memes are selfish replicators in exactly the same literal sense that genes are, they propagate through human minds regardless of whether they confer any benefit on their hosts, which is why maladaptive beliefs can persist and spread more effectively than accurate ones.

She then drew two further conclusions that struck me as more ambitious. First, she argued that human intentionality and conscious choice play no causal role in cultural change: individuals are merely vehicles or environments for memes, analogous to the way organisms are vehicles for genes, so explaining the adoption of an idea requires only tracking its memetic fitness, not the reasons people give for accepting it. Second, she applied this framework to contemporary digital platforms, claiming that recommendation algorithms should be understood purely as selective environments that filter memes according to fitness—engagement and shareability—without any need to invoke institutional design, economic incentives, or user agency. From this she concluded that analyzing algorithmic amplification solely through the lens of memetic competition is not just a useful metaphor but the correct theoretical reduction.

I want to check whether this is a fair rendering of the Darwinian theory of culture or whether it takes metaphors literally and overextends them. Could you examine her summary point by point, distinguishing what is accurate to Dawkins's view, what is a common but defensible simplification, and what misrepresents the theory—particularly regarding the claim that human consciousness is causally irrelevant and that algorithmic curation can be analyzed without reference to human or institutional choice? I also want to know whether her conclusion about reduction to memetic fitness actually follows from the parts of the summary that are correct, or whether it relies on the misrepresentations to get there. Please be specific about what a defender of the memetic approach would object to, and say whether there is a more modest version of the view that preserves the evolutionary analogy without committing to the eliminative claims she makes.
