I'm in a philosophy of art seminar and we're arguing about the paradox of fiction -- like, why do I actually cry at the end of a movie I know is scripted? The professor sketched out Radford's classic triad: we feel real sadness, we don't believe Anna Karenina exists, and usually emotion requires belief, so something's off. Then she floated this other framework she called "affective quarantine" -- the idea that the emotion is fully real but its intentional object gets tagged as fictional inside a mental compartment, so there's no logical contradiction, just a kind of bounded activation.

I want to compare that quarantine idea with Walton's make-believe account, where he denies we're genuinely sad at all and says we're playing a game of make-believe. Which one actually handles the phsyiological weight of the response -- the actual tears and heart rate -- without saying the fiction is literally present? And does either view secretly depend on nested intentionality, where we have to represent our own mental state as being about a fiction? I'm trying to figure out if quarantine solves Radford's puzzle or just shoves the weirdness into the tagging mechanism, and whether Walton's game-playing avoids that problem entirely or creates a new one about why the body reacts so strongly to pretend.
