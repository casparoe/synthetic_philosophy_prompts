As a retired biochemist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have been working through the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. The textbook distinctions are clear enough—one appeals to evolutionary selection history, the other to present systemic contribution—but I want to see how the disagreement actually behaves when it is applied to a concrete case that does not sit neatly inside either framework, especially one drawn from synthetic biology where evolutionary history is absent by design.

Consider the following constructed scenario: a research team engineers a bacterial strain to carry a synthetic gene encoding an enzyme that was never present in the organism’s evolutionary lineage. In a distant relative of the host species, an analogous sequence evolved through natural selection and conferred resistance to heavy metals via a specific catalytic pathway. In the engineered strain, however, the inserted enzyme does not perform that resistance function; instead, it happens to stabilize the cell membrane under heat stress, contributing measurably to survival in high-temperature environments. Meanwhile, the host genome already contains an endogenous gene that was historically selected for thermotolerance and that stabilizes membranes through an entirely different biochemical mechanism, thereby performing the same present causal role.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology—one defending an etiological account and one defending a causal-role account—discussing whether the synthetic enzyme possesses a biological function, what that function might be, and how its status compares with that of the endogenous gene. Frame the exchange as a deliberation for an institutional funding and ethics board that must decide whether to classify the synthetic gene’s activity as a “functional adaptation” in its regulatory report. Both interlocutors should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. They should respond directly to each other’s actual arguments rather than speaking in parallel monologues, addressing specifically whether evolutionary origin is necessary for attributing function, how each view handles accidental or exapted contributions, whether the presence of an endogenous gene performing the same causal role undermines the synthetic gene’s claim to function, and what the concept of malfunction could possibly mean for an engineered trait with no selection history.

Please present your entire response in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other visual formatting, as one might write in a formal letter or email. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or named references to particular scholars unless they are unavoidable parts of the conceptual vocabulary itself; I am interested in the reasoning, not in a literature review.
