As a retired natural scientist, formerly in experimental physics, who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life with a small study group. We have been trying to apply his tripartite distinction—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—to contemporary institutional practices, and we have reached agreement on four cases, though I suspect our reasoning may be running on more than one underlying principle.

First, we judged that a national education programme which teaches only the heroic founding of a state, systematically omitting its failures and injustices, represents a monumental use of history: it selects past greatness to stimulate present action, but we warned that it risks suppressing the critical distance necessary for genuine reform.

Second, we classified a local heritage project that digitises and stores every mundane artefact from a vanished industrial community—tools, letters, receipts—without curating any narrative or allowing public access, as a purely antiquarian history: it preserves continuity and roots, yet we noted that it burdens the present with dead weight that serves no living purpose.

Third, we agreed that a constitutional movement which explicitly voids all legal precedents established under a prior authoritarian regime, refusing any transitional provision or historical continuity, exemplifies critical history: it liberates the present from a burdensome past, though we acknowledged the danger of rootlessness if no new growth follows.

Fourth, we considered a scientific research centre that constantly revisits the failed experiments of earlier generations not to replicate them, but solely to identify contradictions that justify abandoning the theoretical framework in which they were conducted; we classified this as critical history, because the past is treated as an obstacle to be overcome rather than a model or inheritance.

We now face a new case, and I should like your judgement. A newly founded academy requires every scholar to maintain a complete, unedited digital archive of all preliminary thoughts, dead ends, and rejected drafts, open to the public; yet its charter forbids the direct citation of any archived failure in formal publications, and mandates that every published paper open with a narrative explaining how the author’s present insight breaks decisively from the accumulated errors preserved in the archive. The archive is therefore total and permanent, but the institutional culture treats the past exclusively as material to be transcended.

If we wish to be consistent with our four precedents, how should we classify this academy—monumental, antiquarian, or critical? Which of our earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, what principle would we be adopting with each possible classification?

Moreover, I am aware that our four agreements might be explained by more than one rule. Please identify the candidate rules that fit our track record equally well, state what each predicts about the academy, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and specify one further case that would distinguish the rules from one another.
