I've been trying to understand why intransitive preferences are treated as irrational through money-pump arguments. The standard story is that if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C back to A, a trader can cycle you through exchanges until you've lost everything. I accept that being exploited this way is bad, but I'm not convinced it proves intransitivity is irrational—rather, it seems to assume you're a single continuous agent with one fixed preference ranking across the whole sequence. What if rationality only demands coherence within a given decision-context or moment, not across your entire life? If your values genuinely evolve, then being "pumped" might not be a failure of reason but a cost you pay to become someone with different priorities, and the "you" at the end isn't the same rational subject who started. What would actually count against this view? I want the strongest objections sorted by how decisive they'd be: formal results showing that dropping diachronic coherence collapses into arbitrariness or makes every preference change rational; conceptual arguments that there's still one agent being harmed regardless of internal change; and empirical or phenomenological evidence about whether people actually experience value-shifts in this way. Please also say what defenders of standard diachronic rationality would themselves concede as counting against them, versus what they'd dismiss. Separate what's settled in decision theory from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a disputed view as the consensus.
