For this exercise, compose a deadpan parody of an institutional review board (IRB) application—approximately 600 words, organized in standard bureaucratic sections (Purpose, Subjects, Procedures, Risk/Benefit Assessment)—seeking approval to study a single ant colony designated “Aunt Hillary,” which the applicant treats as possessing emergent, fugue-structured collective cognition. Adopt the voice of a meticulous, literal-minded ethics officer who applies human-subjects protections—informed consent, rights of withdrawal, mental-health safeguards, and data-deidentification protocols—to the colony without ever acknowledging the category mistake. The satire should exaggerate genuine tensions in collective-mind philosophy (whether disrupting pheromone pathways harms one distributed mind or thousands of discrete organisms; where the boundary of the subject lies; whether the colony’s “fugue” patterns constitute representational states or mere mechanical resonance) and must commit to a definite position: that standard research-ethics frameworks structurally presuppose an individualist metaphysics they cannot defend when confronted with genuinely distributed cognition. Target the procedural rigidity of ethics review and the ontological ambiguity of group minds, not any named living person. Include a brief author’s note (around 100 words) identifying where the parody takes liberties with actual IRB rules and where it relies on contested interpretations of superorganism agency or collective intentionality. Do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; derive the argument directly from the concepts.
