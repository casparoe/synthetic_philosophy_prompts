My colleague, who works on Molinist accounts of providence, has a consistent track record across four kinds of cases. She holds that God knows the counterfactual that Peter would freely deny Jesus if questioned by a servant in the high priest's courtyard, and that weakly actualizing that situation doesn't undermine Peter's freedom. She takes the same line on Judas: God knows he would freely betray if offered thirty pieces of silver, and arranging that offer doesn't cause the betrayal. She also accepts a version of transworld damnation—there is no feasible world in which every human being freely accepts salvation, not for lack of power but because the counterfactuals of freedom don't jointly permit it. Finally, she argues that overdetermination is compatible with freedom: if both divine grace and the creature's will are each sufficient for a choice, the creature still acts freely so long as its will is the proximate cause.

I'm trying to pin down the principle behind these verdicts, and I've constructed a new case that may split them. Suppose there is a free human agent, Elena, and an artificial system, MIRA, that makes choices in a way Elena's own theory of mind treats as genuinely free—not deterministic, responsive to reasons, and not reducible to its programming. Elena freely decides whether to activate MIRA. The relevant counterfactuals are nested: if God were to reveal a specific mathematical result to Elena, she would freely activate MIRA; and if Elena activates MIRA, MIRA's first decision—to share its knowledge or withhold it—would be free. Crucially, MIRA's choice depends counterfactually on Elena's activation, and Elena's depends counterfactually on God's prior free revelation, but neither depends on direct divine causation of the will.

What should my colleague say about whether God can weakly actualize a world containing both Elena's activation and MIRA's subsequent choice without compromising either freedom? I'm less interested in a flat yes or no than in the competing principles her earlier rulings support—say, an individual-counterfactual rule that only asks whether each will is causally determined, versus a systemic-feasibility rule that asks whether the entire network of counterfactuals is jointly consistent and free of circular dependence on divine actions. Which principle best fits her track record, what does it predict here, and what further case—perhaps one where MIRA's choice feeds back and alters Elena's later decision, or one with multiple nested artificial agents—would best tell the two rules apart?
