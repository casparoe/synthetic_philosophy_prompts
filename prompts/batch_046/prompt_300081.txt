Please write the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Measuring What Matters in Public Institutions." The audience is divided: half are philosophers of science and sociologists who know Goodhart's law and specification gaming in detail, and half are public-health administrators and grant officers who rely on evaluation metrics every day but have little formal background in philosophy. Your remarks must do real structural work: they need to establish that any evaluation system will eventually be gamed, yet they must also avoid blanket cynicism that would alienate the administrators whose jobs depend on these tools. To navigate this tension, center your remarks on a specific conceptual distinction—not only the familiar single-metric version of Goodhart's law, but a less-discussed variant we might call pluralist specification fatigue, in which competing, non-commensurable indicators do not merely invite separate gaming of each target, but fragment the underlying practice into incompatible performances that no single actor can coordinate. Because the room is mixed, explain this idea without technical jargon and without directing listeners to further reading. The remarks should run about five minutes, should contain no bibliography, citations, or reading suggestions, and should be written in American English.
