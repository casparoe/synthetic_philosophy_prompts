I've been thinking a lot lately about who actually belongs in the democratic 'we' — partly because I just got appointed to this weird inter-municipal board that manages a shared aquifer, and suddenly I'm sitting in a room with people from three different counties plus a tribal rep and this corporate lawyer representing a bottling plant. It's making me realize I have gut feelings about cases but no real principel.

Here are my verdicts. Non-citizen residents who've lived in our town for over a decade and pay local taxes should definately vote in municipal elections, including school board. People in the county downwind who'll breathe our factory's emissions should have a formal, binding vote on whether we build it — not just a public comment period. For long-term climate policy, future generations shouldn't literally cast ballots, but there should be a mandatory proxy with veto power over decisions that lock in irreversible harm. A corporation that employs half the town and pays 40% of the tax base should not get a vote or weighted seat, period. Animals affected by our land-use plan shouldn't vote, though I guess an ecologist can advise.

But here's where I get stuck. I think a citizen who emigrated permanently twenty years ago should still keep full national voting rights for life — membership doesn't expire. At the same time, I don't think people in another country who are hurt by our trade policy should get a vote here, even though they're clearly affected. So 'affectedness' gives outsiders a claim in the pollution case but not in the trade case, while 'membership' gives insiders a claim even when they're not affected at all. I also think long-term undocumented immigrants should vote locally but not nationally, which feels right but I can't explain why the boundry of membership shifts with the scale of the decision.

I'm toying with this idea of 'nested demos' — different groups for different issues, defined partly by subjection and partly by autonomous agency — but then I worry I'm just patching over contradictions. Is there a coherent principle, maybe with a couple clauses, that captures all of this? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts? Specifically, what should my principle say about a case I haven't mentioned: should temporary residents (say, grad students here for 4 years) vote in local elections? And please be honest about which of my intuitions your best principle can't save — especially the tension between the expat voting rights and the trade-policy outsiders being excluded.
