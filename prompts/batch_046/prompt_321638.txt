I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, but I have been reading philosophy of action for a side project on autonomy in automated systems, and I keep returning to the absent-minded driver problem. In the standard version, a driver traverses a familiar route—making every correct turn, stopping, and adjusting—arrives exactly at the intended destination, and only then realizes that there was no conscious registration of the journey: no awareness of the traffic signals, the lane changes, or even the decision to take the usual exit. The case is usually deployed to ask whether intentional action necessarily requires occurrent conscious awareness, or whether embodied knowledge-how can guide behavior in the absence of knowledge-that. I want to press a slightly less common variant: imagine that during the drive the agent also unconsciously corrected for an unexpected obstacle, swerving just enough to avoid it without ever perceiving the threat or the response. This introduces a further tension—whether that correction counts as an action for which the driver is responsible, or whether it is an omission of deliberate choice that merely happens to yield the right outcome.

For a brief talk to colleagues, I would like a sestina—six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi, with the end-words rotating through the classic pattern—using these six words: turn, wheel, absence, arrival, route, awareness. The cyclical rotation should echo the repetitive, unmonitored mechanics of the drive, while the envoi, which gathers all six words together, should land with the sudden recognition that one has arrived without having witnessed the way. The poem should render the philosophical stakes precisely enough that a specialist recognizes the debate about unconscious competence and the gap between causal efficacy and intentional agency, yet it should stand on its own for listeners unfamiliar with the literature. It should not resolve into either a sermon about mindful driving or a shrug about human autopilot; instead it should leave suspended the question of whether the driver’s absence of awareness is a failure of agency or its hidden condition.
