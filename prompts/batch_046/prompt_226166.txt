Write a dialogue between Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen, both philosophers of history, on whether there is a principled boundary between legend and history, using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as test cases. Vasquez should argue that the boundary is functional rather than evidential: a narrative crosses into history when it shifts from serving primarily as a charter for collective identity to operating as a causal explanation of past events, regardless of direct archaeological corroboration. Chen should defend a modified evidentialist position built around a domain-specific corroboration matrix, in which the required balance of archaeological, textual, and comparative linguistic evidence varies depending on whether the claim concerns a state-level military campaign, a biographical figure, or a migratory population movement. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical: Vasquez should challenge Chen's matrix by pressing why the Exodus should be assessed differently from Arthur when both lack direct imperial archival corroboration and both serve identity-forming functions; Chen should respond that the matrix accounts for differential preservation biases—imperial archives tend to suppress failures while local oral traditions amplify heroic figures—and should ask Vasquez how her functional criterion distinguishes a documented but deeply identity-serving event like the siege of Masada from a purely legendary charter. Neither speaker should be treated as a strawman, and the dialogue should conclude without either party conceding defeat.
