I am currently enrolled in a seminar on ancient political economy, and after our unit on Aristotle’s treatment of exchange, money, and wealth-getting in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, two of my classmates have been arguing past each other for several weeks. One camp, which I will call the Natural-Limit position, maintains that Aristotle’s core objection to chrematistike is ontological: money (nomisma) originates as a conventional medium for exchange, but because it is barren—lacking the natural reproductive capacity of living things—treating it as capable of generating more of itself violates the teleological order of goods, which ought to serve use-value within the household rather than become an autonomous source of unlimited growth. The opposing camp, the Contextual-Use position, insists that Aristotle’s critique is not about the metaphysical status of money itself but about the misdirection of acquisition away from household self-sufficiency and the good life; on this reading, complex market exchange, lending, and even surplus accumulation are not inherently unnatural, provided they remain functionally subordinate to the needs of the oikos and the polis and do not displace the virtue of liberality.

They have agreed to suspend further debate and instead produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document. Please draft that document. It should contain four parts, in formal register suitable for a philosophy seminar. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike, the conventional origin of coinage for facilitating exchange, the role of proportional reciprocity (antipeponthos) in corrective justice, and the shared premise that unlimited accumulation for its own sake corrupts character—phrased neutrally so neither camp feels its view is misrepresented. Second, a table or clearly demarcated list of the precise residual disagreements, with each camp’s position stated in wording that the other accepts as an accurate formulation of their stance. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—specifically whether Aristotle’s claim that money is barren (tokos) functions as a metaphysical thesis about value-generation or as a normative thesis about appropriate use, and whether his framework permits any form of capital circulation that exceeds immediate household need without thereby becoming chrematistike. Fourth, for each crux, present a concrete conceptual case that both sides agree bears on the resolution, together with each side’s explicit advance prediction: for the first crux, the case of a community bank that issues interest-free loans secured against future labor rather than against existing capital stock; for the second, the hypothetical of a merchant who accumulates surplus grain not for private consumption but exclusively to fund public liturgies and defense—both sides should agree these scenarios are relevant, and each should state in advance whether Aristotle’s framework condemns, permits, or remains genuinely silent on each case.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to modern scholars; the task is conceptual clarification rather than literature review. Write the output as the collaborative document itself, not as a commentary about it.
