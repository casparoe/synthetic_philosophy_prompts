I'm trying to work out my actual commitments in population axiology by testing my gut reactions on specific worlds, but I'm finding a cluster of judgments that don't sit neatly with either total or average utilitarianism, and I'd like to see whether there's a principled pattern that saves most of them or forces me to abandon one.

Here are my verdicts. World A contains one hundred people with welfare level +50. World B contains one thousand people with welfare level +5; both worlds sum to 5,000, yet I judge A to be clearly better, since large populations of barely good lives do not seem to compensate for the loss of genuinely excellent lives. World C contains the same one hundred people at +50 plus nine hundred additional people at +1, with no causal or structural interaction between the two groups: I think C is worse than A, though I am genuinely unsure whether C is worse than B. Now consider two structurally different versions of an interdependent scenario. In World D, the one hundred people at +50 can reach that welfare level only because they are embedded in an economic and social system that requires nine hundred others at +1 to function; if the nine hundred are removed, the welfare of the hundred collapses. In World E, the welfare distribution is identical—one hundred at +50, nine hundred at +1—but the nine hundred are causally redundant; the hundred would thrive regardless. I find D significantly more acceptable than E, despite the identical welfare profile. Finally, World F contains ten million people at welfare +0.1, just barely above neutrality, and I feel strongly that this is not a good world, even though the total welfare is enormous.

What principle, if any, captures these judgments? In particular, can you formulate a view that explains why structural interdependence matters (D versus E), why excellence-sensitive or average-sensitive considerations dominate in A versus B, and why there is such sharp resistance to worlds composed almost entirely of lives barely worth living? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it naturally accommodates, which ones it must overturn, and apply it to a case I have not considered: World G, where one hundred people are at +50 and five hundred are at +10, both groups depend on each other symmetrically, but the welfare of the +10 group is partly constituted by favorable comparison with the +50 group. Be explicit about whether your principle forces me to revise my judgment about F, or about the comparison between C and B, and whether any simpler principle that saves all my verdicts is too complex to be credible.
