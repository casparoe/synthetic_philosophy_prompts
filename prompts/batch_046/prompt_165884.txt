Write the opening remarks (approximately eight to ten minutes of speaking time, about 1,200–1,500 words) for the first morning of a workshop titled "Suspended Judgment: Ethics, Law, and Singular Conviction," co-hosted by a university theology department and its law school. Your remarks must do real philosophical work, not merely decorate the event with a famous name. Use the structure of Abraham's situation to frame why the workshop exists: the universal ethical sphere demands that actions be justifiable to all, yet Abraham stands in an absolute relation to the absolute that cannot be translated into universal language. Explicitly distinguish between (a) violating or overriding an ethical rule for the sake of a higher universal principle—which remains inside the ethical sphere—and (b) the genuinely teleological suspension, where the singular individual is released from universal mediation altogether. Make this distinction precise enough that the scholars recognize it, but explain it in plain language for the lawyers and judges.

Inject a specific, slightly unconventional framing device: imagine that Abraham had attempted to file an amicus brief before the court of universal ethics explaining his action, and that the court's refusal to hear him is not a failure of rhetoric but a structural feature of the ethical sphere itself. Use this image to pose the workshop's central question: when a modern professional—say, a judge writing a dissent that appeals not to statute but to conscience, or a physician refusing a standard protocol because of a singular relationship to one patient—is she genuinely in an Abrahamic position, or is she merely invoking an ethical exception that the universal can eventually absorb and justify? This question should organize the workshop's agenda without resolving it.
