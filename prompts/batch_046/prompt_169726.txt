I was arguing with my friends about this hinge-of-history thing — the idea that history has these pressure points, like if the Mongols hadn't turned back in 1242 or if a specific manuscript hadn't burned in some monastery, where the macro-path just forks completely. I tried to turn it into an argument that proves way too much, but now I'm stuck on where exactly it cheats.

Here's the chain:
(1) If hinges exist, then modern institutions — say, the concept of universal human rights or the funding structure of science — depend counterfactually on specific hinge events occurring exactly as they did.
(2) If a present institution depends counterfactually on a past hinge in the right way, then the hinge genuinely explains why we have this institution and not some radically different one.
(3) If event E genuinely explains present state S, then anyone who could have prevented E is causally implicated in S.
(4) At every hinge there are ordinary people — a courier who got lost, a clerk who misfiled a document — who physically could have changed the outcome.
(5) So if hinges are real, ordinary people are causally implicated in the entire architechture of modern moral and political life.
(6) But it's absurd to think a lost courier in 1242 bears moral responsability for the Enlightenment or for modern human rights norms.
(7) So the hinge hypothesis must be false, or at least useless for serious historical explanation.

I think the problem might be a slide from causal explanation to moral attribution between (3) and (5), or maybe (2) confuses counterfactual dependence with real explanatory power. But I'm not sure which rejection costs the least for someone who still wants hinges to do real work in historiography. Which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost the hinge theorist? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) if I can.
