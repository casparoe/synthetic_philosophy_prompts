As a journalist who writes on the foundations of rational decision-making and strategic reasoning, I have gradually settled into the following conceptual stance on common knowledge, bounded agency, and the architecture of epistemic game theory. Before I present this framework in an upcoming essay, I want a rigorous consistency audit.

Here is the profile of commitments I currently hold:

(1) I treat common knowledge of rationality (CKR) as a non-negotiable modeling pillar for explaining coordination and equilibrium selection in epistemic game theory, not as a dispensable fiction.

(2) I reject the standard interpretation that CKR requires agents to actually entertain an infinite hierarchy of explicit beliefs-about-beliefs; instead, I reconstruct it as shared structural transparency about each other's reasoning algorithms.

(3) I accept that real agents are boundedly rational, and therefore I think epistemic models should permit "almost common knowledge"—allowing epsilon-deviations from perfect rationality—without collapsing into full-blown irrationality.

(4) I also maintain that genuinely private mental states remain compatible with strategic interaction: an agent can hold information that is in principle unobservable to others, even in settings where reasoning is structurally transparent.

(5) Finally, I hold that the correct response to tension between idealized CKR and bounded agency is not to weaken transparency, but to restrict its domain: transparency applies to the rules of reasoning, not to the particular epistemic contents or private evidence an agent possesses.

Do these five commitments form a jointly tenable package? I see potential friction between (2) and (4)—if reasoning algorithms are structurally transparent, does that not erode the privacy of mental states required by (4)?—and between (1) and (3): if epsilon-deviations are permitted, does CKR retain enough explanatory power to justify its "non-negotiable" status in (1)? Meanwhile, (2) and (5) appear designed to work together, but I am unsure whether (5)'s domain restriction genuinely resolves the underlying problem or merely relocates it.

There also seems to be tension between (1)'s idealization and (3)'s acknowledgment of boundedness, though I suspect this dissolves once we distinguish normative modeling assumptions from descriptive psychological claims. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in conflict (not merely apparently so), rank those conflicts by severity, specify for each what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs, and then tell me which single revision—if I had to make only one—would clean up the greatest number of tensions. I am not signaling which commitment I would be most willing to abandon; treat all five as live, but tell me precisely where hidden weight is pulling them apart.
