I've been thinking lately about Tolstoy's claim that art is not about beauty, skill, or being in a museum, but about one person infecting another with a feeling they genuinely have. I tried applying this to things he never could have imagined, and my reactions are all over the place, so I'm wondering whether there's one consistent principle behind them or whether I have to give up one of my judgments.

Here are my verdicts on six situations. First, a shaky cellphone video of a stranger helping an elderly person cross an icy street, shared millions of times, leaving viewers with a sudden warmth and trust in strangers. There was no artist, no intention to make art, yet the feeling moves through people exactly as Tolstoy describes. I think this counts as genuine art, or at least as art in the only sense that matters. Second, a state-commissioned mural celebrating a recent victory: technically flawless, designed by a committee to evoke pride, and it succeeds—crowds feel uplifted. But the painters were working for pay and did not share that pride, and I think this is counterfeit art, no matter how effective. Third, an anonymous folk song sung at a funeral, simple melody, no known composer, making everyone present feel shared grief and acceptance; I call this real art. Fourth, a gallery installation consisting of a white room with a single red thread—critics write that it produces existential unease, but when I went, I felt nothing except confusion, and I think it fails as art for anyone who doesn't already have the key. Fifth, a horror film made entirely with market calculations: jump scares engineered by people who feel no fear themselves, yet audiences scream and enjoy it. I am torn, but I lean that it is not genuine art, because the infection is manufactured without sincerity, like the mural. Sixth, a prisoner's private letter describing loneliness, never meant for publication, accidentally discovered and now read by strangers who weep; the writer sincerely felt the loneliness, and the transmission happens despite no artistic plan. I think this is closer to real art than the gallery installation or the propaganda mural.

So my pattern seems to be: sincerity of the source feeling matters more than institutional framing, skill, or even original intent, but successful transmission alone is not enough if the source is detached or manipulative. At the same time, I want to say the accidental video and the accidental letter both qualify, which means artistic intention isn't necessary either. But then I'm not sure what distinguishes the prison letter from the horror film, since both involve creators who are not trying to reach a broad audience—the filmmakers are trying to reach an audience but don't feel the fear, while the prisoner wasn't trying at all but did feel it. Is there one account of artistic value that keeps all six verdicts intact? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Also, what would your repaired principle predict for a case I haven't mentioned—say, a viral meme that mocks a tragedy using irony, made by someone who genuinely finds the situation absurd and feels a kind of bitter amusement, and shared widely among people who feel that same bitter amusement?

Please state the principle precisely, show which clause of it explains each of my six cases, flag the case that puts the most pressure on it, and be honest about whether one of my judgments has to be sacrificed for simplicity.
