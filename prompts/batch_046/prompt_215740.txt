I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on practical reason and epistemic normativity, and I have been asked to design a thirty-minute teaching session for a mixed audience of second-year philosophy and cognitive-science students who have already encountered the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in Scanlon’s moral framework but have not yet seen it extended to belief-formation. Rather than rehearsing the familiar cases about moral obligation and self-interest, I want the session to turn on pragmatic encroachment—specifically, whether practical stakes can ever supply the right kind of reason for adopting a belief, or whether they remain, at best, reasons for acting on a belief one already holds. To keep the discussion concrete, I need a single tightly constructed carrying scenario: a medical researcher assessing ambiguous clinical-trial data for a new therapy, where the statistical evidence is held exactly constant across two conditions, but in one condition accepting the efficacy claim secures a major institutional grant and career advancement while in the other there are no such stakes. The scenario must be specific enough to avoid collapsing into generic talk about bias or self-deception, yet simple enough that students can see that the evidential base has not shifted.

The audience should be assumed to have introductory logic and a basic grasp of the idea that belief aims at truth, but they will likely arrive with predictable misconceptions: they may conflate “the wrong kind of reason” with “a morally bad reason” or with “an irrational psychological cause”; they may assume that if pragmatic encroachment is false then all practical considerations are automatically the wrong kind for belief, and conversely that if stakes sometimes matter then pure evidentialism must be false in a wholesale way; and they may fail to distinguish reasons that justify an attitude from reasons that motivate an action based on that attitude. I need a teaching plan that lays out the exact dependency order—what must be introduced before what—so that the distinction between normative justification for belief and practical motivation feels inevitable rather than stipulated. The plan should specify the carrying example and what it must include (identical evidence, differing stakes, a clear institutional pressure point) and what it must exclude (variations in emotional attachment or personal risk that would muddy the evidential baseline); it should map each misconception to the precise stage in the sequence where it should be defused; it should propose a brief check for understanding—perhaps a parallel case involving a detective evaluating identical fingerprint evidence under ordinary versus high-urgency conditions—that would reveal whether students have genuinely separated reasons-for-belief from reasons-for-action; and it should state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level and why, such as the full contractualist background to Scanlon’s original moral view, the technical epistemological literature on pure evidentialism versus pragmatism, or the problem of doxastic voluntarism, leaving each out so the session retains its focus on the conceptual architecture rather than on exegetical disputes.

Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions; I am asking for the design of the explanation itself, not for a syllabus. If possible, frame the prompt with a somewhat niche injection—perhaps by asking how the wrong-kind distinction interacts specifically with institutional funding pressure in scientific belief-formation—so that the answer cannot be produced by recalling a standard textbook summary of the moral case alone.
