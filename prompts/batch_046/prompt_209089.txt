Here is an argument I have been turning over that seems to show Gödel was caught in a contradiction between his physics and his philosophy of mathematics, and I cannot locate the weak step.

(1) Gödel's rotating universe is a valid solution to Einstein's field equations in which closed timelike curves exist: an object can travel along a path that returns it to an earlier point in its own worldline.

(2) Gödel also defended a broadly Kantian view of time: temporal ordering is not an objective feature of the world but an ideal form of human intuition, so the flow from past to future is subjective rather than physically fundamental.

(3) If both (1) and (2) hold, then traveling back in time along a closed curve is not returning to an objectively earlier state of reality, but only traversing a region that is earlier in one's subjective temporal ordering.

(4) Gödel was a mathematical Platonist: he held that mathematical objects and truths exist independently of human minds, and that mathematicians discover rather than invent them.

(5) Now imagine a traveler in such a universe who carries a valid proof of a theorem—say, a previously unknown result in number theory. Along her worldline she meets her own earlier self and teaches her the proof. The earlier self writes it down, lives forward, and eventually becomes the traveler who teaches it.

(6) In this loop, no one ever derives the proof from axioms by independent reasoning. The proof is transmitted circularly: each copy receives it from the other.

(7) Yet the proof remains valid, and if Platonism is true, the theorem it establishes is an objective mathematical fact that was waiting to be discovered.

(8) So in this scenario an objective mathematical truth becomes available to human knowledge without ever being discovered from independent reality; it enters the epistemic chain only through causal circulation.

(9) That seems incompatible with Platonist discovery: if the truth was always there to be found, the loop should still require someone to have originally found it; but there is no origin point in a circle.

(10) Therefore either (a) such causal loops are physically impossible despite Gödel's solution, (b) Platonism is false and mathematical truths can be generated by causal transmission, or (c) Gödel's subjective view of time is wrong and there is an objective temporal ordering that blocks the loop.

But Gödel affirmed (1), (2), and (4). So he appears committed to all three of (a), (b), and (c) being false—yet they follow from his own commitments. Where does the reasoning slip? If you want to preserve Gödel's physics and his Platonism, which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? Please answer in American English.
