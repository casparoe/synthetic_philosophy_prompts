I don't want the usual Platonic recap everyone memorized in intro. Write the desiderata: what a real theory here has to be able to do to count as adequate.

Lead with the bottom line up front: are these requirements jointly satisfiable, or does satisfying one blow up another? Then lay out the list with the motivating case behind each, and mark which ones are genuinely non-negotiable vs just camp preferences (virtue epistemologists vs reliabilists vs whatever).

The criteria:
1. Must distinguish knowledge from lucky true belief without just adding ornamental features. What's the concrete case where belief guides action fine but knowledge is clearly superior?
2. Must settle whether the value is intrinsic or purely instrumental. Don't waffle.
3. Must handle the single-shot decision objection: if I'm about to act once and never revisit the question, true belief guides me just as well. So what's knowledge actually doing there?
4. Must explain the testimony asymmetry — we ask "do you know?" and reject "I think so." What's the social or epistemic work being done?
5. Must account for stability: knowledge survives counterevidence differently than belief, but specify the mechanism without circularity.
6. Must address distributed cognition: why do groups and institutions need knowledge-holders rather than just correct opinions scattered among members? This one's probaly underdiscussed.
7. Must stay consistent with cases where false belief succeeds and knowledge actually paralyzes action — don't ignore the counterexamples.

If there's a hidden conflict — say between stability and accessibility, or between intrinsic value and single-shot equivalence — flag it explicitly. That's the part I care about most.
