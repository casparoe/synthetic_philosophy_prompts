I'm working on a draft for my philosophy of well-being course and I'm trying to push Nozick's experience machine argument in a slightly different direction. Most people read it as a straightforward attack on hedonism—plug in, get fake pleasure, we reject it because we want real contact with reality. But I want to argue that the intuition against the machine actually reveals something about how we structure goods like achievement and relationships: specifically, that we care about counterfactual robustnes, meaning a good has to be able to survive some range of nearby failures or risks, not just be present in our experience. So in my essay I sketch a case where someone in the machine writes a novel that feels like a masterpiece, and I ask whether an objective-list theorist who includes creative acheivement as a basic good can consistently count this as a real achievement, since the modal conditions—actual risk of failure, causal interaction with resistant materials—are missing. I think Nozick's point that we want to actually do things is close to this, but he frames it as a desire for reality-contact, whereas I'm framing it as a structual feature of the goods themselves.

There's also this weird idea I came across in an old forum thread about narrative-constituted well-being, where the claim is that achievement is just a matter of internal coherence rather than external history. I want to use that as a foil—if that view is right, then the experience machine should actually be fine for achievement goods, which seems wrong, so maybe the forum idea collapses into hedonism after all. But I'm not sure if I'm setting up a strawman.

Is the counterfactual-robustness framing genuinely different from the standard reality-contact objection, or am I just renaming it? And do you think comparing this to the narrative-coherence view adds anything, or should I drop it and stick to hedonism vs objective list?
