I hold a strengthened version of perceptual disjunctivism: there is no shared mental kind between veridical perception and subjectively indistinguishable hallucination, and the phenomenal vocabulary we apply to both—phrases like "looking red" or "seeming to see a tree"—is systematically ambiguous, tracking a direct worldly relation in the veridical case and tracking no such relation at all in the hallucinatory case. I further maintain that this disunity is metaphysical, not merely epistemic, and that unifying frameworks—whether representationalist or predictive-coding—commit a category error by treating analogous subpersonal computations as instantiating a single conscious kind. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which phenomenological descriptions of illusion, which empirical findings about neural correlates or gradual perceptual degradation, which formal results concerning mental representation, and which continuity arguments genuinely threaten the position rather than being re-described away? Please distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward, identify which objections a committed disjunctivist must concede as damaging and which they could dismiss by invoking the very disunity I have described, and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for an account of conscious perception.
