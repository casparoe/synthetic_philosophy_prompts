After a prolonged and somewhat exhausting discussion with a friend regarding the internal coherence of negative utilitarianism, I am trying to clarify exactly how lexical-threshold views interact with the problem of comparing pleasure and pain. Our disagreement centered on whether granting suffering an unconditional lexical priority over pleasure genuinely solves the incommensurability problem—by treating the two as belonging to categorically different orders of value—or whether it merely substitutes one form of comparison for another, using dominance relations to mask an implicit underlying metric of well-being. I want to understand whether this substitution is conceptually legitimate: if pleasure and pain are genuinely incommensurable, can a lexical ordering avoid smuggling quantitative comparability back in through structural priorities, or does any hierarchical arrangement necessarily presuppose some shared scale against which the ordering is justified?

More specifically, I am curious whether one could defend a coherent multi-axis model of welfare in which suffering and pleasure occupy qualitatively distinct, orthogonal dimensions rather than opposing poles of a single hedonic continuum. On such a view, rational trade-offs might be permitted within each dimension—say, among different kinds or intensities of suffering—while an absolute cross-dimensional priority rule prevents any accumulation of pleasure from outweighing even minimal suffering. Does this orthogonal framework genuinely escape the circularity worry that evaluating trade-offs across axes requires precisely the kind of unitary welfare metric that lexical threshold theorists reject? Or does the very act of specifying conditions under which the priority rule applies—distinguishing, for instance, sub-threshold from threshold-crossing cases—reintroduce the commensurability it was designed to eliminate?
