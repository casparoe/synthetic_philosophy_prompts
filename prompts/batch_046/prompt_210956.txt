As a retired physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy of mind in earnest, I have been struck by Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the first-person pronoun I functions as a self-referential symbol—a strange loop in which a pattern within the brain comes to denote the very system that generates it. I wish to set this against a competing image that I have drawn from thermodynamics and systems neuroscience: the self not as a discrete representational token, but as a continuously maintained boundary condition within an interoceptive-predictive loop, in which cortical forecasts of visceral state are perpetually corrected by ascending afferent signals, never resolving into a single centralized symbol. Compare these two accounts—the Hofstadterian strange-loop symbol and the distributed, non-symbolic interoceptive boundary model—with respect to their capacity to explain the unity and diachronic persistence of self-awareness. Which conception, in your view, provides the more coherent account of why there is a unified subject of experience, and where does each conception break down?
