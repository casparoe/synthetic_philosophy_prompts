Here is an argument I constructed for a discussion of strict potentialism, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to show that anyone who accepts ordinary mathematical induction is already committed to an actual infinity of natural numbers. (1) A strict finitist holds that there is no completed infinite totality of natural numbers; at any stage only finitely many have been constructed or are available, though there is always a next one. (2) Hilbert's hotel distinguishes actual from merely potential infinity: only in an actually infinite collection can the part excluding one element be put into one-to-one correspondence with the whole by a single uniform shift. (3) The finitist can describe a shift procedure for any particular finite stage—if rooms 1 through N are occupied, move each occupant from k to k+1 and free room 1—but this requires an available room N+1 that lies outside the current collection. (4) Now consider the claim that this shift is uniformly defined for every index k, without specifying an external bound N. For each specific k, the instruction that the occupant of k moves to k+1 is well-formed within the potentialist framework. (5) But if the procedure is genuinely uniform—applying to every k in a single rule—then its domain must be the totality of all indices over which it ranges; otherwise "uniform" just means "defined case by case," which collapses the distinction between potential and actual. (6) Therefore, accepting that the shift rule applies uniformly to every natural-number index requires treating the natural numbers as a completed domain. (7) Standard induction asserts exactly such uniformity: for every n, if P(n) then P(n+1) ranges over all numbers in one quantifier. (8) Hence anyone who accepts induction as more than a piecemeal schema is ontologically committed to an actual infinity of numbers. (9) So strict finitism is incompatible with accepting standard induction. I want to keep both (1) and (7), but I feel there is a slide somewhere between (4) and (6)—perhaps an equivocation on what uniform demands, or a hidden quantifier shift from internal to external. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Does the argument actually prove too much, or is one of my preferred premises the one that has to go?
