As a retired biophysicist who spent much of my career studying homeostatic regulation and thermodynamic equilibrium in living systems, I have lately turned to philosophy of well-being with an analogical eye. I find myself drawn to what I take to be the core of tranquilism: the thesis that the only intrinsic goods in mental life are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, and that states we ordinarily call positive pleasure or joy are not independent accumulations of value but rather the experienced relaxation of a prior deficit or tension—something like a system returning to baseline rather than acquiring surplus. Before I let this structural appeal carry me too far, I want to subject it to explicit probabilistic scrutiny.

Please give me your credence that tranquilism, understood in precisely this homeostatic-deficit way, is the correct account of well-being. Decompose the reasoning into at least the following terms: first, the probability that well-being is fundamentally a negative or equilibrium-restoring concept rather than an accumulative one; second, the probability that the neurobiological reward circuitry functions primarily to correct deviation rather than to generate genuinely additive positive value; third, the probability that craving is not merely an occasional cause of suffering but in some sense constitutive of it; and fourth, whatever further factor you judge relevant—perhaps whether evolutionary selection could have produced robust positive mental states with no survival function beyond deficit-correction, or whether phenomenological reports of pure, unearned contentment are best interpreted as the absence of disturbance. For each term, provide a rough numerical probability and a single line of justification, then combine them into an overall credence. Tell me which term you are least confident in, and specify what kind of conceptual argument, empirical finding, or first-person phenomenological report would most shift your final number.
