I have been reflecting on an institutional pattern that has become visible in several established democracies over the past two decades: when legislatures confront deeply divisive constitutional or moral questions—such as abortion access, climate targets, or marriage equality—they increasingly convene citizens' assemblies selected by sortition to deliberate and recommend, yet they reserve the binding decision either to the career politicians who sit in the permanent legislature or to a popular referendum. At the same time, these same systems maintain long-tenured professional legislators and apply term limits only sparingly, never extending random selection to routine lawmaking. What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, political competence, and the nature of representation does this hybrid design presuppose?

More precisely, does the pairing of sortition for deliberation with election (and career tenure) for decision-making implicitly endorse an epistemic division of labor—treating ordinary citizens as reliable sources of considered judgment but as unreliable holders of governing power—while the preservation of career legislators assumes that effective governance requires specialized expertise that lottery cannot supply? Or can the split be justified consistently without attributing any particular theory of political knowledge? I also wonder whether there is an internal tension in granting randomly selected citizens framing authority but denying them binding authority, particularly for democratic theories that treat legitimacy as flowing from a uniform source rather than being parcelled out by function. Is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that rival accounts—whether aggregative, deliberative, or epistemic—might each license the same institutional arrangement for quite different reasons?

Please separate what is well established in democratic theory and political philosophy from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in the field, and avoid presenting a disputed interpretive claim—for example, that the use of citizens' assemblies necessarily expresses an elitist conception of competence—as though it were the consensus position. I am especially interested in identifying which features of the practice are strictly required by any plausible underlying theory and which are merely compatible with several competing ones, and in specifying what institutional changes would be forced if the implicit view of representation or political knowledge were replaced by an alternative.
