Read H.G. Wells's The Invisible Man through Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic: the ring of Gyges removes social sanctions and allegedly reveals that justice is pursued only for its consequences. In Wells's novel, Griffin's scientific invisibility removes not only the threat of punishment but also the conditions for being recognized as a moral agent. Examine whether Griffin's descent—from idealistic researcher to violent tyrant in Iping—confirms Glaucon's claim that anyone freed from accountability would abandon justice, or whether the novel suggests that morality depends less on fear of punishment than on intersubjective visibility and the relational identity it sustains. Focus especially on Griffin's interactions with Kemp and with the villagers: do these scenes show a pre-social human nature unleashed, or do they dramatize the dissolution of the self that makes moral commitment possible? I want a reading that takes both the philosophical argument and the novel's scientific-romantic framing seriously, does not reduce the work to an illustration, and ends by identifying what Wells's particular conception of invisibility contributes to the debate about why we should be moral that Plato's magical ring does not capture.
