I serve on the Joint Committee on Interdisciplinary Governance at our university, and we must submit a formal recommendation by Friday regarding the voting protocol for tenure-track appointments in the newly chartered College of Human-Machine Studies. The college comprises four autonomous units—Philosophy, Computer Science, Cognitive Neuroscience, and Design—each of which will submit a strict ranking of finalists; our task is to aggregate these departmental orderings into a single binding college-wide ranking. Our founding charter demands three formal properties: Pareto efficiency (if every unit ranks A above B, the college must agree), independence of irrelevant alternatives (the relative ranking of A and B must not change if a withdrawn candidate C is removed), and non-dictatorship (no single unit always determines the outcome). A fourth, less formal aspiration—what our chair terms "strategic neutrality"—requires that no unit be able to secure a better outcome by misrepresenting its true preferences. Arrow's impossibility result shows that the first three are jointly incompatible without dictatorship, and standard results in mechanism design indicate that strategic neutrality conflicts with unrestricted domain and full decisiveness; we therefore need a concrete recommendation about which aspiration to relax, what procedural mechanism replaces it, and how the remaining system operates in practice. Draft the committee's written opinion: it should recommend a specific course of action with a time frame for implementation, treat seriously the strongest argument for keeping the condition you propose dropping, and present the entire recommendation as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
