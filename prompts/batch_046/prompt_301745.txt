I have been noting my tutor's verdicts in a seminar on the ontology of social kinds. She holds that race is socially constructed, since racial taxonomies vary historically and depend on collective attribution rather than biological essence; that gender is socially constructed, because its membership conditions are normatively enforced and institutionally maintained despite biological correlates; that fiat money is socially constructed, existing only through collective intentionality and institutional backing; that the biological species Canis lupus is not, tracking reproductive isolation independently of human schemes; that the legal fiction "corporation" is socially constructed, being a status assigned solely through institutional recognition; but that adolescence is not, being grounded in biological maturation even though cultural treatment of its boundary varies. The unsettled case is the kind "brain death" used as a criterion for legal death: it is assigned through clinical and juridical protocols that differ across jurisdictions, involves collective professional recognition, yet purports to track the cessation of integrated brain function. Predict her verdict, identify the closest analogue from her track record, and say whether she is running an institutional-classificatory principle (a kind is constructed when membership depends on collective recognition or assigned status), an interest-constitutive principle (a kind is constructed when its boundaries serve social interests and could be redrawn without violating natural joints), or something else. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, state what principle each option would commit her to, name a further case that would distinguish the two principles, and say which analogue best supports each. Please also say what consideration would change your mind about which principle she employs, and indicate where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
