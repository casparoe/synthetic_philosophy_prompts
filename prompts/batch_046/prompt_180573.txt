In philosophical discussions of well-being, tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, while the presence of pleasure or satisfaction adds nothing — is frequently analogized to preventive medicine: just as a doctor who only removes pathogens and repairs injuries but never prescribes exercise or nutrition is practicing an impoverished form of healthcare, so too the tranquilist is said to practice an impoverished form of welfare, treating well-being as mere disease-avoidance rather than flourishing. More recently, some have countered with a different analogy, comparing tranquilism to noise-cancelling headphones: the device does not inject new sound into the environment but actively engineers a structured silence that is not merely the default absence of noise. Both analogies are deployed to argue about whether tranquilism is deficient. Audit both analogies systematically: provide the correspondence table for each (what plays the role of the physician or the headphones, the pathogen or the noise, the treatment or the engineering, health or well-being, and the alleged missing positive element); identify the points where each mapping breaks, especially regarding whether silence or health can be positively structured without being “added”; determine what each analogy quietly imports about whether value resides in states, processes, or relations; and conclude whether either analogy genuinely establishes that tranquilism misses something essential, or whether both conclusions depend on features smuggled in from the source domain.
