Design a structured classroom simulation for an upper-level political theory seminar (14 students, 80 minutes) that makes participants experience the tension between Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment—specifically, the problem of binding a future self or generation when the binding mechanism is designed by a present agent who cannot fully represent future preferences and may benefit from the binding. The activity should center on a reverse Ulysses legislature: half the class serves as a Constituent Assembly with full information about current conditions but no direct stake in outcomes ten rounds later; the other half serves as Future Citizens whose preferences are drawn randomly from hidden envelopes after round three and who suffer or gain from rules the Assembly locked in. Include explicit rules, point allocations, a timeline divided into phases, and constraints on communication between the Assembly and the Future Citizens. Arrange the rules so that the Assembly has an incentive to over-bind—locking in provisions that protect present interests at the expense of future adaptability—and so that the Future Citizens discover, only after lock-in, that they would have chosen differently had they been present. Before naming the concepts, the debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto (i) the rationality of self-binding, (ii) the democratic objection to intergenerational precommitment, and (iii) the informational asymmetry that makes constitutional Ulysses contracts unstable. Be explicit about what the simulation predictably gets wrong—such as emotional identification with roles, strategic voting in small groups, and the absence of real temporal delay—and explain how the facilitator should correct for each. Also be frank about which feature of actual constitutional precommitment—namely, the irreversibility and long temporal scale across actual generations—the classroom format cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion. Write the response in American English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid referring to specific scholarly texts, page numbers, or named theories outside the concepts already mentioned.
