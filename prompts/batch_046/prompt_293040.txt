In my philosophy of punishment seminar we have been examining negative retributivism—the view that desert imposes only an upper limit on punishment, not a positive duty to inflict it. During a debate about sentencing reform, a classmate repeatedly invoked the slogan that punishment must not exceed what is deserved, using it to defend the claim that mandatory minimums violate justice even if they deter crime, while denying that justice requires us to punish guilty parties at all. I want to understand precisely what that slogan could be asserting. Could you lay out the distinct, precisely formulated theses it might stand for—distinguishing, for instance, a purely negative side-constraint on state power from a proportionality principle governing just sentences; a claim about the offender's moral entitlement from a claim about institutional legitimacy; and a deontological prohibition from a consequentialist warning about undermining deterrence? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it actually supports my classmate's use of it to defend negative retributivism, and which version people who invoke the slogan in public debate are most likely to intend. I am particularly uncertain about the case in which a jurisdiction could reduce serious crime by imposing sentences slightly above what is deserved: does the slogan, on any plausible reading, categorically forbid that trade-off, and if so, on what grounds?
