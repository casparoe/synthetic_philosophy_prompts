For a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar on practical rationality (fifteen students), design a runnable in-class simulation with real but minor stakes—token allocations or brief time commitments—through which students first make either an observable or hidden initial investment in a joint task, then learn that continuing produces a lower direct payoff than exiting, yet exiting imposes different secondary costs depending on whether others witnessed the prior investment. Specify roles, rules, payoff structures, and a timeline with distinct phases (investment, information revelation, decision, resolution). The activity should make participants feel, before it is named, that honoring a prior loss can be structurally rational rather than merely biased. Include a debrief that maps specific decision moments onto the distinction between backward-looking waste-avoidance and forward-looking commitment or signaling value, predicts where participants will misinterpret the rules and how to correct this, and frankly states which feature of pure instrumental irrationality—the case where no observable or strategic benefit exists—the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally. Do not ask for texts, citations, or bibliographies; let the conceptual pressure come entirely from the incentives and information conditions.
