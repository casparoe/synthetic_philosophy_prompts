I've been trying to get my head round the alignment problem for a course I'm taking on philosophy of technology, and I keep feeling like different people are arguing about completely different things. Last week my study partner tried to clear it up for me over coffee, and he summed it up like this: alignment isn't actually one problem, it's three seperate puzzles that everyone smushes together. First, there's the specification gap — you can't fully write down what you want the system to optimise for. Second, there's robustness failure, where the model does something completely mental in a situation that's slightly outside its training distribution. Third, there's what he called 'semantic anchoring failure', which is when the AI's internal representations don't actually map onto human concepts even though its behaviour looks perfectly fine. He reckons the first two are basically engineering issues, but anchoring failure is philosophical — it's about reference and intentionality, not just code — and that's why philosophers and computer scientists talk past each other. He also said RLHF only really addresses the first one, and that a lot of the existential-risk arguments accidentally conflate specification errors with anchoring failures, which makes the whole debate muddled. I'm not sure he's being fair — maybe he's just being neat for neatness's sake — and I don't know whether 'semantic anchoring failure' is a real term people use or something he invented to win the argument. So: is his three-way split accurate to how researchers actually frame the problem? Is alignment genuinely plural, or is it one issue with different symptoms? And if anchoring failure is a real thing, does it actually belong in the same category as specification and robustness, or is he forcing a distinction that doesn't hold up?
