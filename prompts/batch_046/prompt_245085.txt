Introductory accounts of Jain anekāntavāda often invoke the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each observer touches a different part, reports a different shape, and the doctrine is taken to show that every report is partly correct and that no single viewpoint captures the whole. The analogy is usually deployed to defend a non-absolutist conclusion—truth is distributed across perspectives rather than monopolized by one. Audit this analogy precisely: map what corresponds to the elephant, the blind observers, their partial reports, and the meta-claim that all are right. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, especially in light of syādvāda’s saptabhaṅgī, the sevenfold schema in which a single speaker uses the modal qualifier syāt (in some respect) to predicate contradictory properties—existence and non-existence, expressibility and inexpressibility—of one and the same object. Does the elephant story misrepresent the doctrine as a pluralism about observers rather than a conditional logic about one object? Does that disanalogy undermine the anti-absolutist conclusion the analogy is meant to support, or can the conclusion be recovered without it? If the analogy is deficient, suggest what a more accurate analog would be.
