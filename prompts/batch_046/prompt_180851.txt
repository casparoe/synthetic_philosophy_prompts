I have recently been asked to recommend whether my institution should abolish a legacy ethics-review protocol that was created for clinical trials but now governs all algorithmic-impact assessments. No one can reconstruct its original rationale, yet dismantling it feels reckless. This made Chesterton's fence principle suddenly personal, and I am drafting an essay that reads Burkean conservatism through that principle to argue for what I call an epistemic obligation of reverse-engineering the rationale: before reforming an inherited institution, one must demonstrate not merely that its costs exceed its benefits, but that the coordination problem it originally solved is either obsolete or already handled elsewhere. I want comments on whether this middle ground—stronger than risk-aversion, weaker than status-quo bias—is coherent, especially when the builders and their problems are irrecoverable. My notes also draw an analogy with the Euthyphro dilemma to ask whether an institution is valuable because it solves a problem, or solves a problem because it is valuable, and I worry this may distort rather than illuminate the Burkean view. The intended audience is philosophically literate but not specialist; I care about conceptual accuracy regarding Burke and Chesterton, but I am not seeking bibliographic guidance. Please comment on the coherence of the position, the aptness of the Euthyphro analogy, and whether the argument collapses into a disguised cost-benefit analysis.
