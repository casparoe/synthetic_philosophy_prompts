I was arguing with a close friend well past midnight after our philosophy of mind reading group, and we never resolved it. She defends a strict higher-order representational theory of consciousness: a mental state is phenomenally conscious only if it is actually targeted by an occurrent higher-order representation, not merely available for one. I argued for a first-order representationalist alternative, insisting that consciousness is constituted by the intrinsic representational content and global availability of the first-order state itself, and that requiring an actual second-order thought either generates an infinite regress or arbitrarily excludes animals that lack introspective architecture. We kept revisiting the standard objections—targetless HOTs, misrepresentation, unconscious higher-order states—but the debate felt stale, as if we were reciting textbook moves. To break the stalemate, I want you to stage a rigorous dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them Ari, who holds the occurrent-HOT position, and Blake, who holds the first-order global-broadcast position—but anchored in a specific, somewhat contrived scenario rather than in abstract survey. Imagine a modular cognitive architecture in which first-order perceptual representations are richly detailed, finely differentiated, and globally broadcast across subsystems, yet in which any higher-order monitoring is purely dispositional: the system can retrieve or construct a self-representational model on demand, but never actually activates one in working memory or attentional focus. Ari must defend the claim that such a system lacks phenomenal consciousness altogether, and must explain whether that consequence is intuitively acceptable or catastrophically counterintuitive; Blake must argue that the global broadcast of rich first-order content is sufficient for consciousness, but must also address whether “global availability” secretly smuggles in a hidden higher-order condition through the functional role of accessibility. Let them engage in at least three genuine rounds of exchange, responding precisely to each other’s points rather than talking past one another, and have them specifically address whether a purely dispositional higher-order capacity—available but never occurrent—can ground phenomenal experience, and whether the first-order view collapses into a disguised form of the higher-order view once the conditions for “broadcast” are fully specified. Keep the tone formal, philosophically dense, and self-contained; do not invoke named scholars or texts as authorities, and do not ask for citations or reading lists—just let the argument unfold through the scenario itself.
