I am a novelist in the middle of a long-form project, and I find myself troubled by a change in my working method that has unfolded so gradually I can barely date its stages. Two years ago I began using a digital assistant only for grammatical correction; since then it has come to propose metaphors that match my stylistic voice, to restructure scenes according to emotional arcs I had only half-formulated, and to maintain a running chronology of the novel's internal history that I once kept in handwritten notebooks. Last week I submitted a short story to a literary magazine and, upon re-reading the printed version, realized there was a central image I could not honestly recall generating myself rather than accepting from the system's interpolation. I raised the matter casually with my editor over coffee, expecting reassurance, but she asked whether I still felt the work was mine in any sense stronger than legal ownership.

I am not seeking practical writing advice. Rather, I want to know whether philosophy offers a principled framework for determining when a creative work remains genuinely its creator's despite the progressive substitution of every mechanism that produced it. Is continuity of conscious direction sufficient to preserve authorship even when no original cognitive component remains, or does authorship require some irreducible point of origin? And if the decisive factor is continuity rather than origin, what exactly must remain continuous—my intentions, my memory of the work's development, or something else? I would especially value the strongest argument for the opposing view: that once the generative process is fully externalized, describing me as the author becomes a courtesy rather than a fact, and that this distinction matters ethically as well as descriptively.
