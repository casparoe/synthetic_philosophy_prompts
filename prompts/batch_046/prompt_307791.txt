I have come to defend a particular reading of Scanlon's contractualism that attempts to dissolve the aggregation problem without importing consequentialist machinery. On this view, the reasonableness of rejecting a principle is not assessed by summing individual complaints across affected parties, but by evaluating whether the pattern of burdens creates a structurally unjustifiable asymmetry in moral standing—specifically, whether those who bear severe costs can point to a principle that systematically privileges the preservation of minor advantages distributed across many others. I further combine this with the claim that there is a genuinely non-transitive dimension to reasonable rejection itself: a principle might be rejectable when it saves one person at the cost of five, rejectable when it saves five at the cost of one, and yet not rejectable in certain intermediate configurations, without contradiction, because the standing to reject depends on the relational position of the complainer within the distribution rather than on a cardinal comparison of aggregate harm. What I want is an honest map of what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the familiar objections about saving the greater number, but the formal tensions it creates with transitivity of justification, the intuitive counter-cases that stress-test structural asymmetry, the hidden commitments about interpersonal comparison and the metrics of standing that my framework implicitly relies on, and whether relocating aggregation into the meta-theory of complaint-weight actually solves the problem or merely obscures it. Please distinguish between defeaters that a committed defender of this relational framework would concede as damaging and those they would dismiss as misunderstandings; rank them from fatal to merely awkward; and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—whether its appeals to relational position can always be adjusted post hoc—and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a contractualist account of right and wrong.
