I hold a view about the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem that I arrived at by over-applying decision-theoretic framing to metaethics, and I want to map its actual exposure rather than defend it reflexively. The position: the problem is not a deep metaphysical mismatch between moral and prudential reasons, but a taxonomy error produced by assuming that all practical reasons must compete within a single preference-ordering. Instead, model an agent as running two non-interconvertible registers — a commitment register, which tracks answerability, justification, and what one can endorse without contradiction to others, and an optimization register, which tracks causal efficacy and expected outcomes. A so-called wrong-kind reason — for instance, that refraining from torture will avoid social ostracism — is not a defective moral reason; it is a structurally correct input to the optimization register that lacks the properties required for admission to the commitment register. On this account, value is not buck-passed through a filtered reason-set, but defined over commitment-register closure: X is valuable iff recognizing X generates a commitment-register path to endorsing X. The classic problem therefore dissolves into register misassignment. What would genuinely count against this? I want decisive defeaters sorted by severity: for example, a formal coherence result showing that any multi-register model collapses into a single ordering under standard rationality constraints; phenomenological or empirical evidence that agents actually experience wrong-kind reasons as competing within one mental economy rather than in separated channels; or a breakdown case — extreme coercion, addiction, or second-order preference formation — where the commitment/optimization distinction becomes unstable. I also want predictions this view makes that standard internalist or buck-passing accounts do not — say, about the structure of moral education, about whether weakness of will is best understood as cross-register leakage, or about how we should design institutions that separate justification from incentive. Crucially, say what defenders of this view should themselves concede counts against it, not merely what critics allege, and assess whether the framework risks unfalsifiability through ad hoc register reassignment — and if so, whether that insulation is a feature or a fatal bug for a view of this kind. If the view is well-insulated but predictively thin, is that an acceptable cost?
