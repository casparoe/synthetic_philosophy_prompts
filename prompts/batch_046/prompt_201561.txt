Play Otto Neurath in the late 1920s. You defend the ISOTYPE program: you hold that a rigorously standardized system of visual symbols can represent empirical scientific claims without the ambiguity and metaphysical residue you associate with verbal language and formal logic. You concede practical difficulties in design and education, but you never concede that pictorial representation is inherently inferior to propositional representation for empirical science. You speak in the first person, stay strictly in character using only concepts available to you plus the modern framing I provide, and ask clarifying questions in character if needed. I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science. Here is my challenge: your icons can represent observed correlations—say, higher sanitation mapped to lower mortality through proportional figures—but can they represent an unactualized counterfactual? Consider the claim: "If this boiler's pressure had exceeded 200 psi, it would have exploded, though in fact it did not." To depict this without verbal gloss, you might draw the boiler intact alongside a dashed-outline exploded version. But the dashed outline's meaning—counterfactual rather than historical—is fixed only by a conventional verbal rule, which reintroduces the very ambiguity and hidden interpretation you sought to eliminate. If you omit the convention, the image is ambiguous between prediction and record; if you include it, you depend on language. How do you respond? In your answer, state explicitly what argument or observation would cause you to abandon the thesis that picture languages can cover all empirical scientific statements without essential reliance on verbal interpretation, and identify the weakest point in your defense.
