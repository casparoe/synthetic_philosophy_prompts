I'm writing a short essay on evolutionary units of selection for a philosophy of biology seminar, and I've been discussing cases with a colleague whose judgments are consistent but whose underlying principle isn't fully transparent to me. She tends to avoid both strict gene-selectionist slogans and vague pluralism, and I'd like to predict her verdict on a synthetic case that hasn't come up in our discussions, both to understand her framework and to test whether my own reconstruction is correct.

Here are her verdicts on earlier cases, as I understand them. First, she identifies gene-level selection in classic selfish-element cases—such as meiotic drive genes that increase their transmission at the expense of organismal fitness—without reducing these to individual-level stories. Second, in standard individual-adaptation cases, like predator speed or camouflage, she describes the unit as the individual organism, emphasizing phenotypic interaction with the environment rather than gene-frequency bookkeeping. Third, regarding eusocial insects with sterile castes, she rejects group selection and explains the phenomenon through kin-selection at the gene level, insisting that the colony is a vehicle, not a unit. Fourth, and crucially, she accepts group-level selection in pathogen virulence evolution—specifically when reduced virulence evolves because highly lethal strains kill hosts before transmission—treating the pathogen population within a host or transmission chain as the relevant unit with heritable variation in fitness.

The new case is a synthetic bioremediation consortium that my colleagues in synthetic biology are engineering: three phylogenetically distant bacterial species share a horizontally transferred plasmid-encoded metabolic pathway that degrades a specific pollutant. The pathway is metabolically expensive and only functional when all three species are present in a precise stoichiometric ratio; together they form a collective phenotype that outcompetes wild microbial communities in contaminated soil. However, any single species, if isolated and cultured separately, reproduces faster and can invade the consortium from within, displacing the others. The plasmids can transfer horizontally across species boundaries, and the consortium's overall reproductive success across soil generations appears heritable, though no single genome determines the collective trait. Given her earlier verdicts, what should she say about the unit of selection here—the gene/plasmid, the individual bacterial species, or the multi-species consortium? I want you to identify at least two plausible rules that fit her four prior judgments, predict her verdict on this case for each rule, say which reconstruction you think is best and why, and then propose one further hypothetical case that would clearly distinguish the two rules. Please write your answer in American English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
