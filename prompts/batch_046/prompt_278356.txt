In recent online debates about determinism and moral responsibility, I keep seeing “ought implies can” deployed as a one-line refutation of compatibilism: the idea is that if determinism is true then agents never genuinely “can” do otherwise, so no moral “ought” can apply to them. But that sentence seems to compress several different claims into a single soundbite. Could you lay out the precise readings it could be expressing, arranged as a menu rather than picking one? I want to distinguish at least: (i) the conceptual-trivial reading about conditions for blame; (ii) the metaphysical reading that moral obligation requires the literal causal possibility of an alternative action within the same history; (iii) the normative-authority reading that an “ought” applies only if the agent possesses the normative power to comply or refrain, regardless of whether the causal pathway is open; and (iv) any incoherent variants that smuggle descriptive ability into prescriptive language without argument. For each, say whether a compatibilist can absorb it by separating “can” as absence of coercion or external constraint from “can” as presence of a branching causal pathway, and say which reading the people using the slogan in these debates actually need for their argument to work. I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden equivocation between being able-to-do-otherwise-in-the-actual-causal-chain and being able-to-do-otherwise-only-under-counterfactual-circumstances, and whether resolving that equivocation leaves the slogan with any real force against compatibilism.
