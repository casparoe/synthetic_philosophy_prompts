For a 90-minute graduate seminar of about 14 students in philosophy or moral psychology, design a structured role-play and deliberation activity that makes participants feel the force of the following problem: guilt and shame are not merely alternative individual moral emotions, but competing cultural architectures for moral repair, and a society that institutionalizes one does not simply prefer it but actively restructures what counts as a moral failure and outsources the other into non-human mediators (ritual, technology, environment). The central question is whether a culture can genuinely choose between them, or whether the choice produces an affective lock-in that makes the rejected emotion conceptually unavailable rather than merely discouraged.

The deliverable must include: (1) a pre-brief with hidden rules, not a lecture, that assigns students to two design committees—Committee Guilt and Committee Shame—and gives each a crisis scenario they must solve using only their assigned emotional architecture, plus a mandatory outsourced mediator, a non-human mechanism that handles the emotional work of the architecture they reject; (2) a 20-minute simulation in which the committees must jointly negotiate a unified reconciliation protocol for a fictional post-conflict polity, with explicit payoff rules (no real money) that reward internal coherence but penalize invoking the other committee’s core logic or abandoning one’s mediator; (3) a forced crisis injection at minute 35 that breaks both architectures simultaneously; (4) a minute-by-minute timeline specifying roles within each committee, materials needed, and the sequence of negotiation rounds; (5) a debrief that maps specific moments in the negotiation onto the philosophical distinction between guilt as private and restorative versus shame as public and status-based, onto the problem of affective outsourcing, and onto the lock-in thesis that selecting one architecture reshapes moral phenomenology so that the other is not seen as wrong but as morally invisible; (6) an honest account of what will predictably go wrong—for example, students conflating the mediator with the rejected emotion itself, or treating the architectural choice as purely strategic rather than phenomenological—and how the facilitator should handle each failure in the moment; and (7) a frank statement of which feature of the idea—specifically the lived, first-person qualitative difference between guilt and shame—the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally after the debrief.

The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives before it is named: students should construct the sequence of negotiation steps themselves, discover that they cannot translate the other committee’s proposals into their own moral vocabulary without loss, and only at the end be told that their coordination failure mirrors the philosophical claim that emotional architectures are not interchangeable tools but incommensurable frameworks for perceiving moral failure. The activity should not open with a lecture on guilt-culture versus shame-culture, and the terms affective lock-in and outsourced mediator should not be named until the debrief. Ensure the payoff scheme makes cross-architectural coordination costly without making either architecture obviously dominant, so that participants genuinely feel the pull of the claim that a culture might have to choose, and the cost of that choice.
