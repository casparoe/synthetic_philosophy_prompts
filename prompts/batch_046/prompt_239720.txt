I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy. I have been reading about the idea—associated with the philosopher Daniel Dennett—that the self is not an inner substance but a center of narrative gravity, an abstract point around which the stories we tell about our actions and experiences organize themselves. I want to run a 90-minute workshop for about 14 colleagues (mostly engineers, no philosophy background) that makes them feel the pull of this idea through direct experience rather than lecture.

Please design a structured in-person activity—something like a simulation or role-play with explicit rules and small stakes—that lets participants discover for themselves how a coherent "agent" can emerge purely from competing narratives without any central author. To keep the exercise concrete and unfamiliar enough that it is not just a retelling of standard philosophy examples, frame it around a distributed logging system: each participant acts as an independent node that writes timestamped event logs, and the group must resolve conflicting entries into a single biography of a fictional user. The rules should be arranged so that, initially, everyone assumes there is a hidden "master account" file that holds the true self; through the mechanics of merge conflicts, missing timestamps, and contradictory intent logs, they should gradually realize that the only "self" available is the narrative center that best explains the data, and that this center can shift or split depending on which conflicts are resolved.

Specify the group size (around 14), the setting (a workshop room with paper or shared digital doc), the timeline within the 90 minutes, and the materials needed (e.g., cards representing log entries, conflict tokens). Include a payoff or incentive structure that does not involve real money—perhaps reputation points for consistency or penalties for unresolved contradictions—so that participants actually care about which narrative wins.

The idea must show up in what happens, not in what I say afterward: arrange the rules so participants run into the absence of a central self before the concept is named. After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity (e.g., the first merge conflict, the point where two nodes claim the same action with opposite intentions) onto parts of Dennett's notion—narrative construction, the absence of a Cartesian theater, the functional role of the self. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (for example, participants may fixate on finding the "real" log, or one dominant voice may suppress conflicts) and how to handle it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—perhaps the long-term temporal continuity of a human life, or the phenomenological sense of ownership—and that I must supply that part in the debrief.

Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write from a place of conceptual clarity rather than scholarly apparatus; I need something I can run with colleagues who will not read primary texts. Keep the instructions formal and polished, as if I were submitting this request to a curriculum designer.
