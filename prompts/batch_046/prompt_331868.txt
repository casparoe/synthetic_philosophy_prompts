I'm taking a course on collective action and environmental ethics, and a debate with friends has left me stuck on an analogy that keeps surfacing in discussions of climate change, meat consumption, and voting. People defending the inefficacy objection — that my skipping one flight, eating one burger, or casting one ballot does not change the collective outcome — often say an individual contribution is like a single grain of sand added to a beach: the beach was already vast, one grain is imperceptible, you cannot identify it afterward, and removing one grain does not shrink the beach in any observable way. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that, in continuous uncoordinated systems like carbon emissions or animal agriculture, individual actions are causally redundant and therefore not the proper site of moral obligation; collective or institutional change is what matters.

But the same grain-of-sand image is invoked about democratic voting — my single vote is one grain in an electorate of millions, almost never decisive — yet we generally reject the parallel conclusion that voting is therefore pointless or not a duty. Some interlocutors reply that voting is disanalogous because elections have discrete thresholds and designed aggregation rules, whereas climate harm and meat demand are linear, unthresholded accumulations where redundancy genuinely holds. Others insist the analogy fails in the opposite direction: that climate and meat markets also contain hidden thresholds — tipping points, supply-chain batch orders — and that the sand-beach picture smuggles in a false image of smooth continuity. I want an explicit audit of how far the grain-of-sand analogy holds across all three domains — climate emissions, meat consumption, and democratic voting — specifically tracking what corresponds to the grain, the beach, the imperceptibility of change, and the absence or presence of a designated aggregator.

Please structure your response as a JSON object with the following fields: correspondence_table (mapping the grain, the beach, the act of addition, the act of removal, and the imperceptibility of change across climate, meat, and voting); disanalogies (separating those that undermine the inefficacy conclusion from those that do not, with special attention to discrete versus continuous aggregation and to whether an institutional design is present); domain_verdicts (for each of climate, meat, and voting, stating whether the analogy supports the inefficacy objection, survives scrutiny, or collapses); smuggled_assumptions (what the analogy quietly imports about causation, moral perception, or the value of parts versus wholes); and better_analogy (if the sand-beach image fails, proposing an alternative analogy that better captures the moral logic of individual contribution in these cases, and stating whether it restores or further undermines the obligation to act individually).
