I am not a philosopher or an academic, but for the past year I have been thinking about a distinction that keeps surfacing in local debates: sometimes people are fighting only over which word applies to a thing that everyone agrees is there, and sometimes they are fighting over whether the thing has properties that actually bind us—whether it exists in a way that creates obligations. This Thursday I am giving the opening remarks at a workshop about whether our city's old water-treatment plant should be designated a public monument or remain classified as an industrial liability. The audience is split: preservation activists treat the designation as a moral fact, city engineers treat it as a budget category, and residents mainly want the odor and traffic to stop. I want a speech of about five minutes that uses that distinction as real structural scaffolding, not decoration. Specifically, it should show both sides that if their disagreement is purely verbal—if they agree on the bricks, the contamination, and the budget—then our workshop's decision procedure is mistaken, because voting on a name will not change the underlying reality. If the disagreement is not purely verbal—if calling it a monument actually creates obligations that calling it a liability does not—then the speech needs to ask what kind of existence a monument possesses that an industrial liability lacks, and whether the residents are being asked to live with an entity they never consented to host. The idea should determine what I can and cannot promise the room: I should not promise resolution if we have not settled whether we are arbitrating labels or arbitrating being. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to named philosophers; I need this to stand entirely on its own reasoning and on the concrete details of the plant, and it must be intelligible to the residents without losing the activists and engineers.
