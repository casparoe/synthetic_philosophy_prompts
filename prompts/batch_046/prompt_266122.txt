I was having a long discussion with a friend about George Berkeley’s economic writings, particularly the series of queries he circulated in the 1730s on paper credit, banking, and the condition of Ireland. She summarised his position like this: Berkeley holds that money is never real wealth in itself but only a token or sign that directs labour towards commodities; consequently gold and silver have no intrinsic value beyond the social agreement that treats them as counters. She then said that Berkeley applies his immaterialist metaphysics directly here — since material bodies exist only as perceptions in minds, both coin and banknotes are essentially mental constructs, and gold merely produces more stable sensory ideas than paper. From this she inferred that issuing more paper credit cannot increase a nation’s real wealth, because multiplying promises-to-pay does not multiply the underlying goods and labour they signify; she concluded that Berkeley therefore blamed Ireland’s economic stagnation on an excess of imaginary tokens rather than on any genuine shortage of productive resources.

I suspect that the step linking his metaphysics to his monetary theory is doing too much work, and that the conclusion about Ireland might conflate his specific critique of credit expansion with a more general theory of value. But I am not well-read enough on Berkeley’s economics to say exactly where the distortion lies, and I do not want to misrepresent him further by guessing. Could you go through the summary carefully: which claims Berkeley would accept, which are subtle conflations of his metaphysical and economic arguments, which overstate or mischaracterise his view of gold versus paper, and does the diagnosis of Ireland’s stagnation actually follow from the accurate parts? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list — just an accuracy audit I can bring back to the argument, so I know what to concede and what to resist.
