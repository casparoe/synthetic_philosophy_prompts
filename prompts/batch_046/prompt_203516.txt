After navigating a recent family conflict between honoring a parent's implicit wishes and following an institutional ethics board's formal recommendation, I have found the standard internal-versus-external reasons distinction insufficient for describing what actually breaks in practical reasoning. I want an activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar—14 students, 75 minutes—that stages what I will call deliberative incommensurability: the situation in which an externally valid reason, once forced through an agent's own motivational translation protocol, no longer functions as that same reason but becomes an alien constraint or a structurally distorted proxy. Design a runnable game or structured role-play with explicit rules, roles, and a constrained justification procedure (for example, agents may only invoke reasons they have "internalized" through a specific disclosure mechanism, while others hold recommendations that are structurally sound but resist translation). The mechanics—not the facilitator's explanation—should make participants feel the fracture. Provide a timeline, the debrief that maps specific moments onto the internal/external distinction and onto the problem of normative translation, and an honest note about which feature of incommensurability—most likely its phenomenological opacity or its resistance to aggregation—the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by you. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid leaning on named theorists or obscure doctrines the model could not be expected to recall from training; build the conceptual pressure entirely from the rules and incentives.
