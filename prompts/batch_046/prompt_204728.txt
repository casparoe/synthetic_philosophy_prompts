After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about what Descartes actually establishes with the cogito, I want an accuracy audit of his summary. He put it roughly like this: whenever I think, I necessarily exist as a thinking thing; but more strongly, because I can doubt the existence of my body and the external world while still being certain that I think, the cogito already proves that my existence is entirely independent of any body or world. From this, he says, Descartes can immediately conclude that his essence is thought alone and that his body is merely an accidental attachment — which is why modern dualism treats consciousness as a fundamental substance ontologically separate from physical processes. My friend added that this is why phenomenologists reject the cogito: not because they dispute the bare existence claim, but because they reject what he called the epistemic sovereignty of the subject, the idea that the knowing self is self-grounding in a way that makes all other knowledge derivative of it.

Is this a fair characterization of Descartes' argument and its legacy? Please separate what is textually accurate about the Meditations, what is a common but misleading simplification, and what conflates the cogito proper with later premises — for instance, the real distinction argument and the appeal to clear and distinct ideas. In particular, does the independence of mind from body actually follow from the cogito alone, or does it require additional metaphysical machinery? And is epistemic sovereignty a useful framing for the phenomenological critique, or does it import a modern conceptual vocabulary that obscures more than it clarifies? Finally, does my friend's conclusion — that the cogito directly licenses substance dualism and therefore invites the phenomenological rejection he describes — survive once the summary is corrected?
