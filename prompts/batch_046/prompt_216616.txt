After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend this evening about whether authorial intention can survive what she calls 'epistemic relay' — cases in which a unified text is assembled by successive contributors who never share a mental state about the whole, yet readers seem to recover a coherent global meaning that none of them individually intended — I want to stop trading examples and ask for the full specification instead. Before I read any further defenses or attacks, write the desiderata that any adequate theory of textual meaning must meet in light of such cases. Specify the phenomena it has to explain (single-author works, genuinely joint compositions, and relay-assembled texts), the distinctions it must preserve (between local segment-meaning and global text-meaning, between an author's actual mental state and the interpretive authority she retains, between discoverable and constructed meaning), the results from cognitive science or linguistics it cannot contradict, and the puzzles it must dissolve (misinterpretation that corrects the author, dead authors with irrecoverable intentions, and relay texts where global coherence exceeds every local intention). For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration behind it, and mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific prejudices — for instance, whether causally linking meaning to an actual mental state is essential or merely a preference of one tradition. Then, briefly, indicate how strict intentionalism, the hypothetical-author variant, and anti-intentionalist or conventionalist alternatives fare against the list — not a full scorecard, but enough to reveal which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the evaluation.
