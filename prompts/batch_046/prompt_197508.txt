Two philosophers of mathematics are arguing about Thomson's lamp. A claims that the lamp demonstrates the incoherence of supertasks: after toggling at times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on, the lamp at time 2 must be either on or off, yet there is no final toggle to determine which, yielding a contradiction. B responds that this mistakes the absence of a limit for a logical contradiction: the state-sequence simply fails to converge, exactly as (−1)^n fails to converge, and undefined at the accumulation point is a consistent mathematical result rather than a paradox; physical law does not require continuity for a discrete process compressed into a finite interval. A then introduces a modified case: replace the lamp with a digital register that records the parity—0 for even, 1 for odd—of the total number of toggles performed. At time 2, B's answer appears to require that the register holds neither 0 nor 1, violating bivalence, since there must be a determinate fact about its state; therefore the no-convergence reply merely masks an incomplete description rather than dissolving the problem. B counters that this conflates the limit of a representational sequence with an ontological state of the device, and that applying bivalence to propositions about the register at t = 2 does not entail that the generating process itself must have converged. They ask you to adjudicate the exchange move by move: is B's initial reply adequate on its own? Does A's register variant genuinely revive a contradiction, or does it depend on an illicit assumption about representation and reality? And where, if anywhere, does the appeal to bivalence do legitimate work? Please also identify what both parties overlook regarding the relation between mathematical limits and physical state-descriptions.
