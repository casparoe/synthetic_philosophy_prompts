Write a dialogue between two grad students, Jia and Tom, arguing over coffee about whether "polycentric history" is actually doable or just sounds good in a grant proposal. Jia thinks you can narrate the 16th-century Indian Ocean trade web—Malacca, Calicut, Kilwa—without making any one port the "main" plot, just tracing flows of goods, people, and debt. Tom says every narrative has a hidden center of gravity, even if you bury it, and that Jia's version just swaps a European state bias for a maritime-commercial one. They should actually respond to each other: Jia should push back that Tom only defends "narrative coherence" because he was trained on European archieves, and Tom should answer that coherence isn't Eurocentrism, it's the line between history and a database dump. Then Jia should bring up what she calls the "echo-date problem"—when the same conflict shows up in Chinese court annals, Persian chronicles, and Swahili oral poetry with such different temporal framing that they barely seem to be about the same year—and Tom should ask whether fixing that needs a meta-calendar or just proves one framing will always dominate. Keep it casual, like they're actually talking, and maybe drop a typo like "seperate" somewhere. Don't have them ask for citations or reading lists.
