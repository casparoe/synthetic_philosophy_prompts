I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of decision theory and the history of philosophy of religion, and I am trying to understand a particular line of reasoning in Blaise Pascal’s Pensées (composed in the 1650s–1660s, published posthumously in 1670). In paraphrase, the passage asks the reader to weigh the hypothesis that God exists against the hypothesis that he does not. Pascal observes that if one lives as though God exists and he in fact does, the reward is infinite and eternal, whereas if he does not exist the cost is only the finite pleasure of a worldly life foregone. Conversely, if one lives as though God does not exist and he in fact does not, the gain is finite, but if he does exist the loss is infinite. From this asymmetry he concludes that, although reason alone cannot settle the question, rational prudence requires wagering on belief.

My difficulty concerns how to read this passage in relation to the coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization—especially the Dutch-book arguments developed by Ramsey and de Finetti. I want to understand whether Pascal is offering an informal anticipation of those coherence arguments, or whether his reasoning actually conflicts with them. Specifically, the argument seems to depend on treating the payoff for salvation as literally infinite, and on treating the prior probability of God’s existence as arbitrarily small but non-zero. This raises a tension: on the one hand, the structure looks like an expected-utility calculation (probability multiplied by utility); on the other hand, an unbounded utility appears to violate the linearity and boundedness conditions that make the betting-quotient representation of belief coherent, since Dutch books typically require finite stakes to generate contradictions.

Could you help me understand the passage in precisely this light? In particular, please address the following. First, is there a settled answer among decision theorists and historians of philosophy about whether Pascal’s wager anticipates coherence-based EU reasoning or rather undermines it through infinite utility? Second, is the problem genuinely formal—does an infinite payoff necessarily break the coherence conditions, or can the argument be reconstructed with bounded approximations? Third, if we replace the infinite payoff with a very large but finite value, does the wager collapse back into a standard EU maximization case, or does some other feature (for instance, the treatment of the prior probability) continue to do independent work? Finally, please separate what is genuinely settled about the formal requirements of coherence from what remains contested—especially interpretive claims about Pascal’s intentions or about whether infinite utility is ever legitimate in decision theory—and do not present any contested view as though it were the consensus.
