I've been thinking on my own, without any formal training, about what happens when the story a family or community keeps alive about its past collides with what photographs, songs, or artifacts actually show. I'm not talking about simple mistakes, but about cases where the living memory—the version sustained by rituals, silences, and emotional rules—does protective work that the bare record does not, and where correcting it might break the bonds that hold people together. I want to read Pixar's Coco through exactly this tension.

In the film, the Rivera family remembers Héctor as the man who abandoned them for music; the torn photograph and the misattributed song preserve a different truth. Meanwhile, Mama Coco's fading personal memory is the only living bridge back to Héctor, and the rules of the Land of the Dead tie remembrance to survival. The film seems to resolve everything once Miguel restores the record—the photo is repaired, the song's true authorship revealed, and the family updates its collective memory to welcome music back. But is that resolution too neat? Does Coco acknowledge that the false abandonment narrative may have served real emotional and protective functions, and that replacing it with the truth of Héctor's murder doesn't merely correct an error but restructures the family's entire emotional architecture?
