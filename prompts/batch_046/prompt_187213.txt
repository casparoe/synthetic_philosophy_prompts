I sit on my university's comparative bioethics review panel, and we've received a protocol-and-funding proposal that has split us in a way that feels less like routine risk assessment and more like an unacknowledged philosophy seminar. A neuroscience group wants institutional approval for a neural-morphometric triage protocol at our affiliated interspecies sanctuary: they propose allocating limited rehabilitation and palliative-care resources partly according to absolute brain mass and partly according to a derived measure they call integrative cortical density—essentially treating scalar neural properties as proxies for the complexity of potential suffering. The researchers argue that without some quantifiable, non-anthropocentric metric, resource allocation collapses into sentimental favoritism; the dissenting panelists counter that treating brain size as morally relevant reinstates a biological hierarchy that has historically justified exclusion, and that welfare ethics should center on relational context and observable distress rather than internal morphology.

I'm drafting our formal decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—and I want it to treat this as a concrete governance problem with an actionable bottom line that the researchers can actually respond to, not as an abstract metaphysical essay. The memo should present the strongest version of the argument that brain-size metrics risk replicating dangerous gradations of moral worth, but it must also honestly engage the best reply: that refusing quantification does not eliminate hierarchy but merely buries it in unstated intuition. Given my background reading rationalist forums on institutional decision-making and epistemic humility, I'm especially interested in whether the panel can design conditions—perhaps restricting morphometric weighting to inter-species triage while excluding intra-species applications, or requiring transparent reporting of error margins—that preserve the protocol's practical value without committing the institution to a controversial theory of moral status. Please write the memo in a formal institutional voice, addressed to the research team and the sanctuary board, with clear reasoning leading to a specific recommendation.
