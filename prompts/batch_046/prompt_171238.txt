I am a doctoral student in medical ethics, and I am trying to map the implicit framework behind my supervisor’s advisory opinions on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. Her rulings across three seminar cases are as follows. First, in a persistent vegetative state where the patient had explicitly refused mechanical ventilation, she recommended withdrawal and honoured the directive. Second, in a case of severe dementia with a broad refusal of “life-prolonging treatment”, where the patient was cheerful, responsive to music, and suffering only from a treatable pneumonia, she advised administering antibiotics, treating the directive as inapplicable rather than overridden. Third, in a locked-in syndrome case where the fully conscious patient expressed, via eye movement, a clear wish to continue ventilation contrary to her prior directive, she recommended continuing treatment, privileging present expressed autonomy.

We now face a new case: a patient with frontotemporal dementia wrote a highly specific directive stating that if she developed FTD and no longer recognised her spouse, she refused chemotherapy for any cancer, even if the cancer was localised and treatable and even if she seemed content. She now has FTD, does not recognise her spouse, and has developed a localised, treatable skin cancer; she is generally cheerful and shows no distress. Predict my supervisor’s recommendation—should chemotherapy begin? Identify the closest analog among the three precedents, state honestly what principle she would be adopting either way, and say whether she is implicitly running a diachronic-contract view (the directive binds whenever the literally anticipated conditions are satisfied, regardless of psychological discontinuity) or a threshold-continuity view (radical psychological discontinuity voids the directive’s authority, leaving present welfare or preference to govern), or a third position. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two leading accounts. Please answer in British English.
