Here is an argument I have been turning over about special composition, and I cannot find the exit. It seems to show that restricted composition collapses either into nihilism or into an ontological explosion, but the conclusion—that there are no tables, only simples arranged table-wise—feels wrong in a way I cannot diagnose precisely.

(1) If restricted composition is correct, then whether parts a and b compose an object depends on some relation R (contact, functional unity, causal integration, etc.) that holds between them.
(2) For any candidate R proposed in the literature, R comes in continuous degrees rather than being an all-or-nothing affair: gravitational attraction weakens with distance, causal influence fades, and even physical contact is mediated by repulsive electromagnetic fields that vary continuously.
(3) Therefore, if R determines composition, there must be a sharp cutoff point at which R is strong enough to generate a new object and just below which it is not.
(4) But sharp cutoffs in continuous physical processes are ontologically suspicious; they imply that the loss of a single air molecule or a slight shift in position can destroy an object, which conflicts with our ordinary judgments about persistence.
(5) So restricted composition either accepts arbitrary sharp cutoffs (which seems ad hoc) or admits that R holds vaguely.
(6) If R holds vaguely, then for a typical table there is no fact of the matter whether a given dust speck on its surface is part of it; hence there are thousands of overlapping table-candidates with slightly different parts (the problem of the many).
(7) If singular reference to "this table" requires picking out one unique object, and restricted composition gives us thousands, then ordinary singular reference fails.
(8) To avoid this, the restricted compositionist might deny that singular reference requires uniqueness—perhaps "this table" refers vaguely or to a cluster.
(9) But if vague reference is acceptable, then there is no reason to restrict composition at all: we can apply the same vague-reference strategy to arbitrary fusions, saying "that distant fusion" refers vaguely to the object composed of my shoe and a star.
(10) Hence restricted composition gains no advantage over universalism.
(11) Universalism, however, generates objects for every possible selection of parts, including ones scattered across the universe, which is ontologically extravagant.
(12) By elimination, the only stable position is nihilism: there are no composite objects, only simples arranged table-wise.

Where does this argument slip? I am especially attached to (4)—the intuition that sharp cutoffs are implausible—and to (1), since restricted composition seems like the only commonsensical middle ground. If I have to give something up, I would rather keep those and abandon something downstream, but I want to understand the cost. Is there an equivocation between epistemic and ontic vagueness in the move from (5) to (6)? Does (8) misrepresent the restricted compositionist's resources—perhaps they can accept vague reference for tables without licensing it for arbitrary fusions? Or is the real problem in (2), where I treat all physical relations as continuous in a way that already assumes there are no discrete binding events? Name the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives.
