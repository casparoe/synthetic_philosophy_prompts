I'm preparing a philosophy-of-mind seminar discussion on substrate-independent identity, and I'd like a careful reading of Spike Jonze's Being John Malkovich (1999) through the lens of Douglas Hofstadter's claim—developed most fully in I Am a Strange Loop—that an "I" is not a metaphysical substance but a self-referential pattern (a strange loop) capable of persisting in other substrates, including other brains. The film's central conceit—the portal behind the filing cabinet at LesterCorp that allows temporary entry into John Malkovich's consciousness, Craig Schwartz's eventual permanent occupation and "vessel" control, the sequence in which Malkovich himself enters the portal and finds a world populated entirely by copies of himself speaking only his name, and the ending in which Craig is expelled from Malkovich only to be trapped as a silent observer inside another person's body—offers an unusually literal dramatization of what it might mean for one self-pattern to inhabit, displace, or proliferate across another's neural architecture.

Read the film as a philosophical experiment rather than an illustration: where do the mechanics of occupation, displacement, and final imprisonment map onto Hofstadter's idea that a loop can be copied or shared without being destroyed, and where do they resist it? In particular, examine whether the film treats the self as a portable pattern that leaves the original substrate intact (Craig experiences Malkovich's senses but Malkovich eventually returns to himself), as a territorial entity that can be evicted (Craig's eventual expulsion), or as something that merges irreversibly with its host (the gradual loss of Craig's own identity markers during long-term possession). Consider also the significance of the "Malkovich-only" world: does it suggest that distributing a loop across multiple copies dilutes or destroys its uniqueness, which would challenge Hofstadter's more optimistic view that a pattern can survive in others without becoming unrecognizable? And does Craig's final condition—aware, remembering, yet permanently silenced inside a body he does not control—represent a form of persistence that Hofstadter's framework would recognize as a genuine "I" distributed across brains, or does it show that such persistence without autonomy is not survival but burial?

I'd like the reading to take both the film's narrative logic and Hofstadter's metaphysics seriously in both directions: not reducing the movie to a thought experiment about loops, and not distorting Hofstadter's view to fit the plot. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about distributed selfhood—perhaps about the role of bodily agency, linguistic expression, or exclusivity of access—that abstract argument about patterns and substrates alone tends to miss. I'm writing this for students who know the major scenes but haven't read Hofstadter closely, so avoid asking for citations or bibliographies; focus instead on the conceptual tension between pattern and vessel.
