I've been in a philosophy of biology reading group for about two years and we've been stuck on the same fight: is life a natural kind with sharp boundaries, or is it more like a cluster concept that only makes sense relative to our investigative goals? One camp, the 'realists,' says that once we understand the chemical mechanisms of organizational closure and evolutionary dynamics we can draw a non-arbitrary line — they point to synthetic minimal cells and argue that if it maintains homeostasis and can evolve, it's alive, period. The other camp, the 'pragmatists,' says that's just projecting our engineering categories onto nature; they think 'alive' is a graded, context-sensitive term like 'healthy' or 'mature,' and that trying to find the essence misses how biologists actually use the word in different subfields.

What makes it frustrating is that nobody disputes the actual biology. We all agree on the mechanisms. The realists think the pragmatists are giving up on scientific realism too easily; the pragmatists think the realists are chaseing a metaphysical ghost. My own hunch is that they're not even disagreeing about the same thing — one group is asking 'what do all living things share causally?' and the other is asking 'what do we need the concept of life to do for us?' But I'm not sure if that's right, or if there's a hidden empirical bet somewhere, maybe about whether genuinely intermediate cases exist in nature and not just in the lab. Can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is this a verbal dispute about 'definition,' a clash of methodological priorities, or bedrock intuitions about natural kinds? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move the needle — a new kind of borderline case, a formal result about clustering, or should we just accept that the question is ill-posed?
