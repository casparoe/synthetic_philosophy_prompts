I am preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on comparative moral theory, and I want a consistency audit of my own position before I present. I hold the following five commitments about Buddhist ethics and its relation to consequentialist reasoning: (a) the normative core of Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as a long-horizon, indirect consequentialism—the ultimate aim is the cessation of dukkha across the cycle of rebirth, and karma functions as the causal mechanism that transmits the moral quality of present actions into future states of suffering or well-being; (b) the five precepts—particularly the prohibition on killing—are exceptionless negative duties that are not overridden by appeals to aggregate welfare, even in extreme cases where violating one precept would prevent far greater suffering; (c) the Mahayana bodhisattva vow entails a genuinely universalist moral obligation: one is committed to the liberation of all sentient beings, which requires comparing, weighing, and in some sense aggregating the suffering and welfare of different beings to determine where one's efforts are most needed; (d) the doctrine of anatta denies any persistent, substantial self that survives rebirth, yet karma is said to bind the mental stream in a morally significant way, and I take this continuity without identity to be metaphysically coherent and normatively relevant; (e) the Madhyamaka teaching of sunyata applies to moral agents, actions, and outcomes alike—they lack svabhava—yet conventional moral reasoning, including the evaluation of actions by their effects on suffering, remains valid and binding within conventional truth.

I am aware that some of these look like they pull in opposite directions, but I am unsure which conflicts are genuine and which dissolve once the metaphysical framework is properly specified. Specifically, I wonder whether (a) and (b) are in direct tension given that long-horizon suffering-reduction seems to license precept-violations in extreme cases; whether (c) is compatible with (b) given that universalist aggregation appears to require trading off individual lives for greater collective welfare; whether (d) undermines the very idea of a stable moral agent or outcome that consequentialist calculation requires; and whether (e) renders (a) and (c) merely conventional fictions that cannot do genuine normative work. I am also concerned about a hidden tension I may be masking: the soteriological orientation implied by (a)—focused on ending one's own stream's suffering—may not actually align with the altruistic orientation implied by (c), despite both being framed in the shared vocabulary of dukkha-reduction, and I want to know whether that divergence is real or only apparent.

Please do not treat this as a request for a survey of Buddhist ethics or a bibliography. Instead, audit the set: which pairs or triples are actually in tension, which only appear to be because of an ambiguous framing of "consequentialism" or "emptiness," and what is the least costly revision—giving up one commitment, qualifying another, or introducing a distinction—to restore joint tenability. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each indicate exactly what would have to give and at what philosophical cost. I am particularly interested in whether the two-truths framework genuinely resolves the conflicts involving (e) or merely relocates them to the conventional level, and whether there is a defensible distinction between soteriological and altruistic moral reasoning that allows (a) and (c) to coexist without collapsing into a single aggregation principle.
