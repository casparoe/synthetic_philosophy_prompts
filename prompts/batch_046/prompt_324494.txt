I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for final-year philosophy undergraduates who have just completed a unit on direct consequentialism but have not yet encountered indirect normative theories or models of idealized deliberation. I wish to introduce them to the conjunction of indirect normativity — according to which an act's normative standing derives not from its direct outcomes but from its endorsement by a specific, constrained deliberative procedure — and partial idealization, where deliberators retain ordinary cognitive limitations but are stripped only of particular self-deceptive biases regarding long-range effects. To carry this, I will use a constructed case study: the 'Council of Partial Mirrors', comprising five deliberators who each possess complete information except for one distinctive motivational blind spot, which another council member is positioned to correct; they must jointly ratify a distributive principle without knowing which blind spot each of them individually lacks.

Please design the session's explanatory arc: what idea must be firmly established before indirect normativity can be distinguished from rule-utilitarianism; why this Council case needs precisely these epistemic conditions — including the absence of omniscience and the lack of full mutual transparency — and what would be lost if either feature were added; the predictable misconceptions arising from their consequentialist background, specifically the assumption that idealization entails perfect foresight and that indirect endorsement is merely a disguised form of direct evaluation, together with the exact moment in the sequence at which each misconception should be pre-empted; a single diagnostic question to pose at the close that would reveal whether they have internalised the indirect structure rather than adopted the vocabulary; and which more advanced disputes — such as those concerning full versus partial idealization within contractualist frameworks — ought to be named as intentionally omitted at this level. Do not propose further readings or bibliographic references.
