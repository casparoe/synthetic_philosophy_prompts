I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long-form essay on rationality and self-knowledge; before I publish, I want to check whether my own stance holds together. Here are six commitments I've arrived at. First, when I forecast my own career, health, or creative output, I privilege the outside view—reference-class forecasting and adjustments for optimism bias—because I know my inside reasoning is systematically unreliable in predictive domains. Second, for philosophical judgments—such as whether modal realism is correct, whether phenomenal concepts carry a distinctive epistemic status, or how to understand the demands of epistemic modesty itself—I rely almost entirely on inside reasoning, since there is no stable statistical reference class of people correctly reasoning about these questions. Third, I hold that epistemic modesty requires me to calibrate belief strength downward whenever I find myself in a minority among peers with comparable training, at least in domains where expertise is trackable and convergence is expected. Fourth, I think philosophy and normative theory are structurally exempt from that calibration, not out of special pleading, but because philosophical disagreement is persistent, selection mechanisms for philosophical expertise are weak, and applying an outside-view statistical discount would not drive belief toward truth but merely collapse it into uniform agnosticism; modesty in this domain must instead mean acknowledging argumentative limits without numerical discounting, and I also think that applying such a discount smuggles in a hidden self-locating assumption—that I am randomly drawn from the set of belief-holders—when in fact my reasoning trajectory is ordered, not random. Fifth, I believe the boundary between judgments that belong in the predictive-empirical category and those that belong in the philosophical-normative category is not itself an empirical finding but a philosophical judgment about what counts as evidence and what counts as a relevant reference class. Sixth, when I face a high-stakes personal decision—say, leaving a stable position for an uncertain project—I think that if my inside assessment conflicts with the outside-view base rate, the inside view should prevail in the decision, because I have privileged access to my own values, risk tolerance, and the specific situational features that the reference class averages away.
