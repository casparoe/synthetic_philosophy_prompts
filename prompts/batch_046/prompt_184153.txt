Consider the conceptual problem of legal self-reference in constitutional amendment rules, especially as illustrated by Peter Suber’s game of Nomic, in which players may change the rules of play—including the rules governing how rules are changed—during the course of play. While Nomic shows that self-amending rule systems can operate in practice, embedding such mechanisms in constitutional law rather than in a game exposes a deeper tension between democratic revisability and the entrenchment of higher-order norms. I have been trying to work through a specific variant that standard treatments tend to bypass: imagine a constitutional order containing a single “reflective amendment clause” (Clause R) which stipulates that Clause R itself may only be altered by a procedure that Clause R currently defines, but where that defining procedure explicitly requires the preservation of Clause R’s core constraint—namely, that any future alteration must also preserve the same reflective structure binding amendability to itself. The clause thus attempts to entrench not merely its content but the very form of its own changeability, generating a loop in which the authority to revise the rule derives from the rule being revised.
