A lecturer in a cognitive-science seminar summarized Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model as follows. Dennett, she said, denies that consciousness occurs at any single neural location; instead, sensory processing produces numerous parallel "drafts" of content, and what we call consciousness is merely the draft that achieves the widest momentary distribution. Because no central audience receives these drafts, there is literally no subject of experience—only temporary narrative dominance—so the self is a kind of fiction. She then added that Dennett's account still requires a hidden "selection mechanism" that determines which draft prevails, and that this selector must function like the Cartesian theater he rejects, except distributed across modules rather than localized. From this she concluded that the multiple drafts model is internally incoherent: it eliminates the central stage in name only, then smuggles it back in as an implicit arbiter to prevent contradictory drafts from being simultaneously conscious. Is this summary fair? Please separate what accurately reflects Dennett's position—particularly regarding the status of the self and the absence of a central observer—from common oversimplifications or misattributions. Does Dennett actually deny that there is any constructed or narrative self, or is that claim stronger than his view allows? And is the objection that draft-selection presupposes a hidden Cartesian theater a legitimate internal critique, or does it misunderstand how competition and "fame in the brain" are supposed to work without a meta-level chooser? Finally, if the hidden-selector charge were correct, would it collapse the model, or can the selection process be explained in purely distributed terms?
