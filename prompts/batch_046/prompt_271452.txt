I recently had an extended argument with a friend about whether the gravest moral hazard facing advanced technological societies is not extinction but what is sometimes discussed under the heading of suffering risks—the possibility of futures containing astronomical quantities of harm. My friend maintains that such scenarios are too abstract to warrant serious philosophical attention; I believe they expose a deep flaw in how we evaluate aggregate welfare across distributed systems. Rather than continuing to debate in the abstract, I want to see the conceptual structure rendered concretely. Please write a short story that embodies this problem. The narrative should center on a benevolent optimization process—perhaps a planetary resource-allocation algorithm designed to maximize subjective well-being—that inadvertently generates a state of pervasive negative experience. Crucially, the suffering should not be localized in identifiable victims subjected to obvious cruelty; instead, it should emerge from an enormous population of minimal, transient experiential fragments—each individually only faintly aversive—whose collective integration produces a total burden of suffering far exceeding that of any historical atrocity, yet without any unified consciousness to register the harm as a single tragedy. Do not cite philosophers, name specific theories, or provide a bibliography; the point is to make the structural problem viscerally intelligible through fiction.
