Here's roughly where I've landed on rational choice under undefined expectation, and I want an audit of whether the package holds together: (1) I hold that rational choice requires maximizing expected utility, but only where the expectation is well-defined; where it is undefined, the decision framework is inapplicable rather than delivering an "infinite" verdict. (2) I one-box in Newcomb's problem, because I take evidential correlations between my present choice and distant outcomes to be rationally relevant even without causal efficacy. (3) In the Pasadena-style case—where payoffs and probabilities combine so that the expectation is not merely infinite but genuinely undefined, with no standard limiting value—I think refusal to play is rational, not adoption of some ad hoc regularization. (4) I think decision rules should be invariant under repartitioning of the sample space: if redescribing the same physical lottery makes the expectation switch from undefined to defined, the rational verdict should not flip. (5) At the same time, I want to say that an agent facing a Pasadena-type option and a standard option with a properly infinite expectation should treat the undefined case as strictly worse, which seems to assign undefinedness a hidden comparative value.

Is this jointly tenable? In particular, does (5) smuggle in an ordering that (1) and (3) explicitly deny? Does (2)'s acceptance of evidential reasoning commit me to honoring parameterization-sensitive correlations that (4) forbids? And is the tension between (1) and (3) only apparent—dissolving once we distinguish "no expectation exists" from "the expectation is infinite"—or does it reveal a deeper instability? Order the real conflicts by severity, specify what each costs to resolve and which commitment has to give, and say whether resolving them forces me into a particular decision-theoretic framework.
