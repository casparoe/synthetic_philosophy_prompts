I'm designing a conceptual handout for a seminar on the normative architecture of labor and rest. Rather than asking students to self-identify with familiar labels like workaholic or slacker, I want a taxonomy that sorts positions on who has leisure right—by which I mean not merely legal entitlement but the moral status of protected non-work time—according to how each position answers three diagnostic questions: first, whether leisure is instrumentally valuable only as recovery for further productivity or non-instrumentally valuable as self-cultivation; second, whether the state has a positive duty to enforce a ceiling on working hours in the manner of the 35-hour framework or only a negative duty not to interfere with contractual choice; third, whether extreme overwork leading to death, as in the phenomenon of karoshi, represents a tragic individual failure, a systemic moral failure, or a culturally dignified sacrifice. The tree should branch from these questions, with each split labeled by the claim that divides the positions rather than by the view's name. Named leaves should include a reinterpretation of Weber's Protestant work ethic through this normative lens, a social-democratic leisure-rights position, a libertarian contractarian position, and at least one hybrid or currently unoccupied combination that affirms non-instrumental leisure value while rejecting state-enforced caps. For any answer combination that remains empty, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Additionally, flag any pair of named leaves that the tree's logic cannot genuinely separate. The questions must be concrete enough that a student can answer them without prior knowledge of the labels, so the instrument sorts by belief rather than by self-ascribed identity. Specify the tree's structure clearly: start from a root commitment, proceed through the three branching questions in an order that minimizes redundancy, and end with the full set of leaves.
