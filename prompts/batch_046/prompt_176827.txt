I'm working through a course on archaeological epistemology, and I keep returning to the problem of how material traces become evidence for something as abstract as shared religious belief or institutional coordination. The excavation of Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia offers a well-known but still puzzling case: monumental T-shaped limestone pillars carved with animal reliefs, erected in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic around the tenth millennium BCE, in a context where permanent domestic architecture is largely absent and agriculture had not yet become dominant. The prevailing interpretation treats the site as a ritual center constructed by hunter-gatherer groups, implying that large-scale, symbolically unified religious practice could exist without settled life or writing.

I want you to analyze this episode strictly through the lens of inference theory: lay out exactly what evidential items are available (the pillar iconography, the faunal remains, the architectural layout, the absence of houses), identify the analogical moves required to get from these items to claims about collective belief and organized labor, and specify where the inference is genuinely supported versus where it relies on silent assumptions about the unity of symbol systems. Then, inject a deflationary alternative that has received little attention: suppose the reliefs are not expressions of a common cosmology but competitive, kin-group-specific displays of power — an agonistic display model in which each pillar marks territorial claim rather than shared worship. How does this reframing alter the inferential path to institutions? Does it render the original religious interpretation unrecoverable, or does it merely redistribute agency among competing subgroups? And finally, what does the scholarly and public resistance to such fragmentary readings tell us about the normative preferences — perhaps unstated — that govern archaeological inference when it moves from objects to minds?
