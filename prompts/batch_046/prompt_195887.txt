I am drafting the conceptual framework for a graduate seminar paper on moral philosophy and collective memory, and I have found that debates about remembrance and forgetting rarely move past slogans—never forget, those who fail to remember risk repeating the past, forgive and forget—without specifying what kind of normative theory could actually justify or limit such imperatives. Before I defend any particular view, I need the full specification of adequacy: what must a normative theory of collective mnemonic duty deliver in order to count as successful, not merely politically useful?

Construct, then, the desiderata for a theory that addresses both the duty to remember historical injustice and the moral case for collective forgetting. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction that makes it indispensable, and flag whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it reflects a camp-specific preference (for example, the preference of virtue-ethical approaches that treat memory as a character trait versus instrumentalist approaches that treat it as a mechanism for deterrence). Your list should cover: the phenomena the theory must explain and distinguish—such as the difference between commemorating victims and glorifying perpetrators; the asymmetry between a survivor's private, psychological forgetting and a state's institutional archival erasure; and the problem of post-testimonial obligation, where mnemonic duties persist even after all direct witnesses have died; the distinctions it must preserve—particularly between having a duty to remember and having a right to be remembered, between forgetting as moral failure and forgetting as legitimate moral release, and between agent-relative duties (owed to specific victims or descendants) and agent-neutral demands (imposed on anyone who inherits the social world); and the consistency conditions it must meet with adjacent moral domains, including duties of justice, the autonomy of descendants who did not choose the past, and the ethics of testimony.

In addition, identify any subset of your desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the demand for perfect mnemonic fidelity across generations conflicts with the demand that societies achieve moral progress through selective, reparative forgetting, or the requirement that the theory apply uniformly to both individual and collective agents collapses under the pressure of methodological individualism. Indicate which single requirement does the heaviest work in excluding popular contenders, whether it is the need to accommodate the idea that shared remembrance is constitutive of ethical community (in the vein of Margalit) or the claim that enforced remembrance can become a form of moral cruelty (in the vein of Rieff). Finally, give a brief outline—no more than a few lines—of how an ethics-of-memory view, a pragmatic-forgetting view, and a liberal-pluralist view fare against your list. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations; I am asking for the conceptual architecture, not a literature survey, and I want the reasoning to stand on its own without reliance on source retrieval.
