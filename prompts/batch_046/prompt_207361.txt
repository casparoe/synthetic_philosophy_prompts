I have been asked to advise a community foundation that is restructuring its grant-making process, and the board is divided between two organizational models that seem to reflect a deeper disagreement about what makes an agent worthy of trust. In the first model, program officers adjust funding priorities continuously in response to stakeholder satisfaction surveys, media coverage, and the expressed preferences of current grantees—directing resources toward whatever earns the broadest approval at each review cycle. In the second model, officers are bound by ten-year outcome contracts tied to pre-defined metrics—such as literacy gains or reduced recidivism—and are contractually barred from redirecting funds even when public opinion turns sharply negative or grantees protest loudly. One trustee argues that the first model respects democratic voice and keeps the institution responsive; another contends that it produces agents with no stable identity beyond the pursuit of praise, and that a city relying on such agents in education, health, and governance would be unable to pursue any unpopular but necessary long-term reform. A third trustee asks whether seeking approval from a diffuse public is morally different from seeking it from a single authority, and whether an agent that genuinely cares about literacy can still be described as genuinely goal-directed if it requires constant external validation to maintain its motivation. I want to help them see the philosophical structure of this disagreement rather than weighing administrative pros and cons. What framework should I use to think through whether approval-seeking undermines the coherence of long-term agency, and whether a society composed mainly of such agents could sustain the kind of thick cooperation that allows unpopular but valuable projects to survive?
