I am preparing interview questions for a podcast colloquium with Dr. Linnea Voss, who defends the specific view that Thomas Malthus's core mechanism—geometric population growth pressing against arithmetic subsistence growth—was empirically sound but activated only after temporary energy subsidies and the global demographic transition are exhausted. She holds that coal, oil, and synthetic fertilizers postponed the pressure, and that once substitution limits are reached the mechanism re-emerges systemically, not necessarily through famine but through price instability and land-use competition. I want a formal sequence of questions that turns on the actual conceptual architecture of this "right but early" defense, anticipates her standard replies, and includes the follow-ups to them. Do not include generic biographical questions.

Please provide the questions in order, with a serious academic tone appropriate to the setting:

1. Open by asking her to specify the exact falsification conditions she accepts: what observable long-run pattern—say, sustained per-capita economic growth coupled with absolute decoupling from material and energy extraction over the next century—would convince her that the mechanism has been permanently defeated rather than merely delayed?

2. Anticipate her reply that technological innovation can outrun resource constraints, and follow up by pressing the distinction between technological substitution (replacing one depleted input with another) and absolute decoupling (increasing economic throughput without a corresponding increase in extraction). Ask whether she regards absolute decoupling as physically possible at planetary scale, and whether substitution alone can indefinitely outpace geometric population dynamics.

3. Press the rescue move directly: if Malthus was wrong about nineteenth-century European timelines because fossil fuels and synthetic fertilizers altered the subsistence baseline, does adding an energy-regime auxiliary hypothesis save the original theory or transform it into a different, less predictive claim? Ask specifically whether this rescue renders the mechanism unfalsifiable in the Popperian sense, and what kind of evidence could now genuinely count against it.

4. Challenge her account of the demographic transition: if falling fertility is driven primarily by urbanization and female education rather than by proximity to carrying capacity, does that break the Malthusian feedback loop? Include the follow-up asking whether she can reconceptualize education and urbanization as themselves resource-intensive institutional luxuries that will contract under subsistence pressure, or whether that reconceptualization makes her view circular.

5. Conclude with a normative question: assuming the mechanism is inevitable but temporally delayed, does the "right but early" framing carry any distinct policy prescription—such as managed demographic or economic contraction—or is it purely diagnostic, leaving standard welfare economics untouched?
