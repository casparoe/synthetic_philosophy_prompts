As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand a recurring pattern in discussions about communication ethics—particularly around whether small social falsehoods (white lies) are wrong, and around the contrast between direct styles like Dutch bluntness and indirect styles like Japanese politeness.

The pattern is this. People defend both extreme directness and careful indirectness by appealing to exactly the same moral idea: respect for the other person. For example, in a code review, one engineer might leave an unvarnished critique—"this module is broken and needs rewriting"—and justify it by saying that sugarcoating would be disrespectful to a competent adult who deserves the truth. Another engineer might wrap the same critique in layers of positive framing—"there are some interesting ideas here, and maybe we could gently adjust the architecture"—and justify it by saying that directness is disrespectful because it ignores emotional reality and social dignity. Both claim respect; both accuse the other of disrespect—directness is called "brutal" or "treating people like machines," indirectness is called "patronizing" or "denying people the honesty they deserve."

I see the same structure outside engineering. Someone might defend telling a friend that her rough presentation draft was "really interesting" when they actually think it needs major revision, arguing that protecting her feelings is the respectful thing; while someone else might insist that withholding that judgment is disrespectful because it treats her as too fragile to handle criticism.

What I'm after is not a verdict on which style is right. I'm asking whether there is an established philosophical concept for this specific configuration: a single normative term—here, respect—being used to legitimate two genuinely contradictory communicative practices, where each side not only advocates its own practice but frames the opposing practice as a violation of the very value both invoke. Is there a recognized name for this? If not, can it be defined precisely, with clear boundary conditions? For instance, does the phenomenon require that the two practices be incompatible in the same context, or does it apply whenever a shared value is interpreted in opposing directions? How is it different from ordinary moral disagreement, from simple equivocation, from conceptual stretching, or from value pluralism? And does it bear specifically on the ethics of white lies, or is it a more general feature of how we justify cross-cultural communication norms?

I'm not satisfied with a bare label. I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
