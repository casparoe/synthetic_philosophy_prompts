Question: Explain the distinction between morally 'doing' harm and 'allowing' harm in healthcare rationing. Give one concrete example of each. Then apply it to a hospital that maintains an automated triage algorithm designed years ago: when the algorithm denies care to a specific patient with no human in the loop for that decision, is the administrator who updates the software 'doing' harm or 'allowing' it? Student answer:
The core difference is that doing harm requires direct physical causation by the agent, while allowing harm just means the agent failed to stop a process already in motion. So a doctor who injects a lethal dose is doing harm; a doctor who runs out of ventilators and watches a patient die is allowing it. For the algorithm case, the administrator is allowing harm, not doing it, because they never personally triggered the denial for that specific patient—the system runs autonomously once deployed. Foot argued that negative duties not to kill always outweigh positive duties to save, which means allowing is always less blameworthy than doing. So the admin bears less responsibility since they only maintain the conditions under which the system operates.
