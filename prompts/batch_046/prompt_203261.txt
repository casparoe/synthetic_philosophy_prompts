I've been thinking about the centipede game—the setup where two people take turns choosing either to take a growing reward or to pass it to the other person, with the rule that taking always yields a little more for you right then than passing would, and the total available shrinks with every pass. The reasoning I keep seeing in popular accounts of game theory is that if both players are fully rational and each knows the other is fully rational, then working backward from the final node the first player should take the reward immediately. Yet in experiments and in ordinary intuition people often pass for several rounds. For decades, philosophers and economists have argued over whether this backward-induction result is a real paradox, a demonstration that common knowledge of rationality is unrealistic, or just evidence that people fail to be fully rational. To me the debate feels stalled: one camp insists that rationality requires taking at node one, the other insists that rational cooperation is rational too, and neither seems to engage the other's actual premise. I'm trying to figure out whether this is a substantive disagreement about what rational choice requires, a verbal disagreement about what the word "rational" should cover, or two different inquiries—one normative, one descriptive—running under a single heading. There's also the possibility that a hidden empirical claim is doing all the work, maybe about whether people can genuinely form beliefs about their own future choices in the way the backward-induction argument requires. I have my own hunch, which is that part of the problem may come from treating "the player" as one continuous agent across every node, when in fact the practical standpoint at node three—where the stakes and the available information are very different—might belong to a different deliberative position than the standpoint at node one, so that backward induction illegitimately collapses a sequence of agents into one. Could the stalemate actually be about whether personal identity over time is robust enough to license that kind of reasoning, rather than about rationality in the usual sense? I want a diagnosis, not a survey of interpretations: using what each side actually says, tell me whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, a case of answering different questions, or a sign that the problem is ill-posed. And given your diagnosis, tell me what—whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an experimental finding about belief formation, or a reframing of the question—would actually count as progress, or whether we should expect permanent stalemate and why.
