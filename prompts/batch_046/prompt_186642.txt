I hold the view that in the architecture of frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—drawn from the discussion of interpretation, self-reference, and the phonograph-record analogies in Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979)—meaning is not located in any single layer but is constituted by the recursive interaction among all three. On this account, the frame message is not an external contextual key that unlocks a pre-existing semantic content; it is a constitutive semantic layer, and the inner message stripped of its frame is not a degraded message but a non-message. The outer message, in turn, has no independent semantic standing: it is a structural artifact that acquires significance only through its embedding in the frame-inner relation. I extend this specifically to artificial interpretation: if a language model receives only the token sequence corresponding to an inner message—say, a self-referential statement embedded in a larger framing context—without access to the framing instructions that establish the rules of interpretation, then there is no fact of the matter about what the model understands or misunderstands. There is only syntactic pattern-matching, and attributing any semantic content to the model’s processing in that condition is a category error.

What would count against this position? I want more than the familiar objection that frames are merely pragmatic context. I want the specific conceptual distinctions, formal results, or interpretive cases that would genuinely undermine the claim that the frame is constitutive rather than instrumental. For example: are there inner messages that carry recoverable, stable meaning across widely varying frames, and if so, does that show that semantic content can survive frame-stripping? Could a compositional semantics assign meaning layer-by-layer, contradicting the holism this view assumes? Would evidence that small language models extract consistent interpretations from decontextualized prompts—apparently without access to a framing layer—count as decisive, or could a defender always redescribe the model’s training history or architectural biases as a hidden frame? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if every apparent case of frame-independent understanding can be accommodated by postulating an implicit frame, does that insulation protect the view or vitiate it as a claim about where meaning is located? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that a committed defender of this position should concede as genuinely damaging from those they could dismiss as misapplications of the framework, and indicate whether any rival account—say, one that locates meaning primarily in the inner message and treats the frame as a pragmatic overlay—actually predicts observable differences in interpretation that this view cannot accommodate.
