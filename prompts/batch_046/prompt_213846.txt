I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy working on the ethics of representation, and I am trying to sharpen an argument for a dissertation chapter on what might be called the "posthumous narrative rights" of historical subjects. The problem is this: biographical films and historical dramas routinely perform what I have been calling "narrative colonization"—they do not merely simplify or embellish a life, but structurally replace the subject's own practical reasoning and historical contingency with a causally tight plot designed to satisfy present audiences' emotional and moral expectations. For instance, a film might invent a private confrontation that never occurred, collapse years of ideological ambivalence into a single revelatory moment, or attribute to the dead a motive that makes their life cohere in ways the archival record does not support. The conceptual issue I want explored is whether such practices wrong the deceased in a direct moral sense, or whether the absence of consciousness, interests, and the capacity to be deceived makes talk of wronging them conceptually confused.

Please write an essay that takes a clear position on this controversy and defends it through sustained reasoning rather than ending in a non-committal balance. You should weigh the case for posthumous dignity interests—perhaps anchored in the thought that a person's life-narrative remains partly constitutive of their identity and should not be unilaterally rewritten by the living—against the counter-argument that artistic license and the epistemic limits of history justify substantial invention, especially since all biography is already interpretation. I want you to consider specifically whether any wrong is done to the dead themselves, to living viewers who are misled about the past, or to the collective practice of truthful remembrance; and whether the answer differs for globally famous figures, whose lives are already public property in some sense, versus private individuals made visible only through dramatization. The essay should not provide bibliographies or citation lists, and it need not invoke obscure theories by name, but it should be conceptually precise and address whether recognizing such a duty would meaningfully constrain artistic practice or merely restate an already-existing obligation not to defame. Reach a verdict: either dramatists do owe the dead a substantive form of narrative fidelity that goes beyond factual accuracy, or they do not, and explain what practical difference that conclusion makes for evaluating biopics that take documented liberties.
