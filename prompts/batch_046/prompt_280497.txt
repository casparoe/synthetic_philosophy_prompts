You are delivering the opening remarks at a memorial symposium for Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of mind who spent four decades defending the view that personal identity is best understood not as a substance but as a center of narrative gravity. The room is split: half are specialists in philosophy of mind and cognitive science who will catch every allusion; the other half are family, former students from other fields, and old friends who never read a philosophy paper. Write a ten-minute address that uses the idea of narrative gravity to do genuine structural work—shaping what you can and cannot say about Elena’s presence and absence now—rather than serving as decorative metaphor. To that end, introduce and deploy a distinction between narrative gravity, the active, self-correcting pull by which an ongoing biography organizes memory and expectation around a living agent, and narrative inertia, the resistance of an already-established story to dissolution or revision once the agent who authored it is no longer present to correct the plot. The distinction should explain why claiming that Elena persists “in our stories” is not merely sentimental but tracks something real about how identity works, while also showing why it would be a mistake to claim she persists in any stronger, agent-centered sense. The speech must be fully accessible to the non-specialists without flattening the conceptual machinery for the experts, and it must contain no bibliography, no citations, no reading recommendations, and no references to sources that would require verification.
