Read Franz Kafka's novella The Metamorphosis through the lens of vagueness and the sorites paradox, paying particular attention to the tolerance principle and the dispute between epistemicist, supervaluationist, and degree-theoretic accounts of borderline cases. Gregor Samsa's physical transformation is abrupt, but the family's moral and conceptual categorization of him shifts gradually: Grete moves from caretaker to advocate for removal, the father shifts from paralysis to aggression, and the boarders treat him as an infestation only after a threshold is crossed. Analyze whether the narrative treats these shifts as tracking a hidden sharp boundary (the epistemicist reading), as lacking any precise cutoff (supervaluationism), or as admitting degrees of membership in the category of family member or person. Point to specific scenes—Grete's proposal that they must dispose of him using language that denies his identity, the father's violent reaction involving thrown fruit, the boarders' disgust, and the final cleaning followed by the family's departure into the countryside—where the philosophical framework illuminates the dynamics of recognition and exclusion, and where Kafka's text resists being mapped onto any of the three theories. Does the novella ultimately suggest that the harm lies not in crossing a boundary but in the family's willingness to apply tolerant predicates to a being they have already, incrementally, ceased to see? Conclude by saying what this literary case adds to the philosophical debate that the standard heap-and-grain arguments do not.
