At a seminar on medieval metaphysics, two participants debated whether al-Ghazālī's occasionalism could be reconstructed as a form of modern interventionist causation rather than a wholesale denial of natural law.

Maya argued that al-Ghazālī does not eliminate secondary causes entirely; he merely relocates their efficacy. On her reading, fire retains its "occasioning" structure—when fire is present, burning follows with perfect regularity—so the world still exhibits law-like patterns, just grounded in divine habit (ʿāda) rather than in intrinsic powers. She compared this to James Woodward's interventionist account, where causes are variables that make a difference under interventions, suggesting that al-Ghazālī's "occasions" function like interventionist variables whose efficacy is guaranteed by an external controller rather than by intrinsic dispositions.

Jamal replied that this domesticates al-Ghazālī beyond recognition. If divine habit is genuinely law-like and exceptionless except for miracles, then Maya has smuggled back the very natural necessity al-Ghazālī rejected. The occasionalist point is not that fire is an unreliable cause, but that it has zero causal power: God creates the burning ex nihilo at the moment of contact. Jamal added that comparing this to interventionism mistakes the ontology—interventionism is silent on whether causes have intrinsic powers, but al-Ghazālī is not silent; he explicitly denies them. He then pressed a further objection: if Maya is right that occasionalism preserves law-like regularity, then it becomes empirically indistinguishable from physicalism and loses its explanatory point.

Maya countered that empirical indistinguishability is not an objection but a feature: al-Ghazālī's project was theological and metaphysical, not scientific. She noted that modern dispositional essentialists like Alexander Bird claim that properties have causal powers essentially; al-Ghazālī denies this for all created things, but his denial is consistent with the observable regularities that science describes. Thus occasionalism and essentialism are metaphysically opposed yet phenomenologically equivalent.

Jamal responded that this makes occasionalism unfalsifiable and therefore not a competing metaphysics but a redundant overlay. If every prediction made by essentialism is duplicated by occasionalism, and occasionalism adds only an unobservable divine mediator for every event, then by parsimony we should reject it—not because it conflicts with science, but because it multiplies entities without altering the empirical structure.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Maya's interventionist reconstruction, Jamal's charge of domesticating al-Ghazālī, Maya's reply about empirical equivalence, and Jamal's parsimony objection. Who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, are both missing—particularly regarding the distinction between metaphysical grounding and explanatory redundancy?
