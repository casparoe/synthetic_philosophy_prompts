I am working through a classic debate about moral development and have become stuck on an interpretive knot in a passage that sits at the center of it. Consider the passage in Mencius (Book 2A, section 6) where he describes the four sprouts—the heart of compassion as the sprout of benevolence, the heart of shame as the sprout of righteousness, the heart of courtesy as the sprout of propriety, and the heart of discerning right from wrong as the sprout of wisdom—and compares them to the sprouts of barley that grow if given water and soil but wither without them. I am trying to understand exactly what ontological status Mencius assigns to these sprouts: are they fully formed miniature virtues waiting only for expansion, or are they merely directional tendencies—closer to what one might call developmental vector fields—that require both nurturing conditions and active cultivation to take on any determinate moral form?

My difficulty is sharpened by a framing I have encountered in recent comparative philosophy that distinguishes cultivation models (where nature supplies a determinate form and culture mainly removes obstacles) from construction models (where nature supplies only raw material and culture imposes form). The passage seems to sit uncomfortably between these: Mencius insists the sprouts are innate, yet he also emphasizes that they must be cultivated and that neglect can destroy them, which sounds closer to a construction model than to a pure cultivation one. At the same time, he clearly rejects the Xunzian view that human nature is originally amoral and that virtue is entirely artificial.

(3) Does the developmental-vector reading I sketched actually clarify Mencius’s position against Xunzi’s, or does it smuggle in modern biological concepts that obscure the original argument?
