I've been on universal credit for about six months now after my contract ended, and watching the jobcentre staff decide who's 'trying hard enough' has made me think the whole idea of an 'undeserving poor' is actualy a category mistake — it's not that some people fail to deserve help, it's that the administrative machinery produces the appearance of undeservingness by defining compliance in ways that have nothing to do with actual need. What I mean is, conditionality doesn't filter out fraud; it manufactures a visible class of non-compliers who then serve as the justification for the system itself. So my view is that workfare and benefit fraud discourse are self-validating loops: the conditions create the violations, and the violations legitimate the conditions.

But I'm aware this might be too neat. What would definately count against it? Not just 'some people do genuinely cheat' — I know that happens — but what kind of evidence or argument should make me abandon the claim that the category is structurally self-producing? Would it be if fraud rates turned out high even in unconditional systems? Or if people who failed conditionality consistently showed lower moral motivation in some other domain? Or is there a philosophical defeater — maybe a rights-based argument that says even if the loop exists, individuals still have obligations that make conditionality fair?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and please say which ones a defender of my view (if they were being honest) would have to concede actually hurt. Don't worry about giving me a reading list, I just want the logic sorted out.
