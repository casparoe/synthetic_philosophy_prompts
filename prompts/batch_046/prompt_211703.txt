I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about phenomenal judgements if epiphenomenalism is true -- I mean the view that conscious states dont do any causal work, they just ride along while brain states do all the pushing.

Here are my gut calls on cases. Someone stubs their toe and immediately says 'that hurts bad' -- I think thats true and justified, even though the pain itself didnt cause the words; the neural firing did both. A zombie twin, physically identical but with zero inner life, makes the same sound in the same situation -- I think they're wrong, or at least not knowing anything real. So physical sameness isnt enough for a correct phenomenal judgment.

Now take a neuroscientist who reads my scan, sees the pain-signature, and tells me 'you're in pain.' Correct? Maybe. But I dont think that counts as me knowing my own phenomenal state, and if she said it about herself with no feeling, it wouldnt be knowledge either. Its like a weather report about my head.

Then there's memory. I compare yesterdays migraine to todays headache and judge the first was worse. The old migraine is gone and never pushed anything around, so my memory trace is just a physical record. Still I think my comparison can be true and justified.

But heres the one I keep going back and forth on. Imagine someone raised in total sensory isolation, taught only a theoretical model that maps brain states to color-names for other people. They never experienced color. Then they get a brain implant that reproduces the sighted-state, apply the model to themselves, and say 'I am seeing red now.' Is that a real phenomenal judgement? Part of me says no, they're just running a calculator with no feel to it. Part says yes -- if the physical state is present and the correlation is perfect, what exactly is missing?

Also, ineffability. Someone sees a sunset and judges 'this orange is literally undescribable.' I think thats a legitimate call about the phenomenal quality. But if the quality itself is causally inert, what makes the judgement about the orange-feel rather than just about the brain-state? And why does that feel okay, while the scan-reading scientist feels wrong?

So is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Maybe something like: a phenomenal judgement is genuine only when the judging mechanism is the same physical system that realizes the experience, operating through first-person structural access rather than third-person correlation? Or maybe thats too baroque. What does the principle say about the isolation-case I cant decide? And please say which of my verdicts you'd have to toss to keep things simple -- especially the one about memory, since that seems like the outlier. Also predict what it says for a case I havent listed, like a blind person who learns to predict others' color reports so well they start using the vocabulary for their own tactile experiences.
