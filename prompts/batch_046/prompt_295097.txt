Consider the collapse of honor-sustained norms—dueling being the clearest case, though the pattern recurs elsewhere. The practice does not typically disappear because participants are suddenly persuaded of its intrinsic harm; rather, it undergoes a polarity inversion in which the very behavior that once demonstrated courage and social standing comes to signal recklessness, barbarism, or moral backwardness. What interests me is not the inversion itself but the fate of the underlying machinery. The competitive dynamics, the ritualized aggression, the status anxiety, and the signaling logic that made the original practice socially functional do not seem to vanish with the norm. Instead, they appear to migrate into structurally analogous but now legitimate channels—diffuse institutional competition, ritualized forms of exclusion, or other public contests of standing—while the vocabulary of shame and dishonor is redirected backward onto the abandoned practice rather than applied to its substitute. So the psychological substrate and the functional logic survive intact; only the specific behavioral form and its moral evaluation have shifted. Is there an established concept that captures this precise phenomenon—the preservation and redirection of a norm's functional machinery into substitute practices after the original norm has been morally inverted? How exactly should it be distinguished from moral licensing, from rationalization, or from the simpler claim that people merely find new outlets for old impulses? And if no existing term fits, what boundary conditions—regarding the structural similarity of the substitute, the direction of the shame vocabulary, or the role of institutional mediation—would be necessary to define it properly?
