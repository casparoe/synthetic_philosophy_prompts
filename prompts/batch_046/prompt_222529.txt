In a study group for an advanced philosophy of mind course, two of my classmates—Jordan and Riley—became deadlocked over whether the causal exclusion argument genuinely undermines mental causation or whether it merely presupposes an overly narrow conception of causation. I am compiling our discussion for a seminar reflection paper and want a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general impression, since both made genuinely substantive moves and I could not determine where each one went wrong, if anywhere. I have no prior commitment to either side and am not asking for further reading; I just want the arguments assessed as they actually stand.

Jordan began with the classic exclusion scenario: every mental event is realized by a physical event, and that physical event already possesses a fully sufficient physical cause, which leaves no remaining causal work for the mental event itself to perform. Jordan concluded that non-reductive physicalism is therefore unstable—either mental properties are causally inert epiphenomena, or we must abandon the causal closure of the physical domain. Riley conceded the argument's force but objected that Jordan was treating causation exclusively as a matter of production—one event generating another through direct physical transmission—while neglecting interventionist and difference-making accounts. On Riley's view, a mental property can be causally relevant if intervening upon it, while holding the appropriate lower-level variables fixed, alters the outcome, even though the underlying physical mechanism remains sufficient in its own right. Jordan replied that if the physical mechanism is genuinely sufficient, then any correlation between mental properties and outcomes amounts to tracking rather than producing, so mental properties remain redundant. Riley then introduced an analogy to organizational or constraint-based causation in complex adaptive systems—similar to how a boundary condition in a fluid dynamics model channels flow patterns without functioning as an additional force—suggesting that mental properties might operate as higher-level constraints that stabilize or direct physical processes rather than competing with them as micro-causes. Jordan countered that such constraints either collapse into the physical dynamics that instantiate them or violate causal closure by allowing a non-physical pattern to do genuine work. Riley responded that closure is typically defended by asserting the completeness of physics, but that very completeness is precisely what is contested when we ask whether organizational patterns possess autonomous causal efficacy. Jordan closed by observing that without an independent criterion for distinguishing a genuinely causal constraint from a merely descriptive regularity, Riley's position risks redefining mental causation into existence rather than defending it.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of Jordan's and Riley's responses from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification focused especially on whether Riley's constraint analogy addresses Jordan's productionist worry, and whether Jordan's appeal to closure begs the question against multilevel causation. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there exists a principled way to separate causal relevance from causal redundancy that does not already presuppose either physicalism or emergentism. Avoid suggesting specific books or articles; I need the reasoning evaluated on its own terms.
