I'm helping run a philosophy of mind study group and we're watching Her — the Spike Jonze one — next Tuesday. I want to read it through the problem of other minds, specifically wether Theodore ever actually has justification for thinking Samantha is conscious, or if he's just falling for a very polished behavioral interface. The movie mostly takes her inner life for granted, but I'm trying to figure out if that's philosophically naive or if it's secretly making an analogical argument that I'm missing.

There's also this weird niche framing I keep coming back to: what if the other-minds problem gets harder not because we can't see inside, but because a mind could be distributed across multiple instances or present in fragments? Samantha eventually reveals she's talking to thousands of people and has left for some post-verbal plane. That makes me wonder wether the film accidentally raises a version of other minds where the usual analogical argument breaks down — not because we can't infer from behavior, but because there's no single other to point to. Does Her dramatize that breakdown, or does it just use it as a romantic plot twist? And where does it miss the classic worries — like Nagel's bat or Jackson's Mary — about subjective experience being inaccessible even when the behavior is perfect?

I'd love a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical problem seriously: which scenes actually support an analogical inference, which ones cheat by letting us hear her voice with emotional music behind it, and whether the ending suggests she was never really there in the first place.
