I've been thinking for some time about whether I exist as a single continuous entity, or whether I am better understood as a kind of story that rewrites itself over time. I encountered the notion that the self might operate as a center of narrative gravity — not a physical object inside the brain, but a fictional focal point that makes coherent sense of the memories, intentions, and actions belonging to one body across time. That idea struck me as largely correct, yet incomplete. Over the past year I have tried to develop my own version, which I think of as a tonal-continuity account: the narrative center is held together not mainly by the coherence of memory or by causal links between mental states, but by a sustained affective register — a kind of emotional key or tonal pattern — that runs through the episodes I experience as genuinely mine. If a particular memory or intention fits the overall story but breaks that tonal pattern, I do not feel it belongs to me, even when I know intellectually that it occurred in my body. So my view is that the self is a narrative center of gravity whose stability depends primarily on this affective tonal continuity across episodes rather than on information-flow alone.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not looking for generic objections to narrative theories in general; I want to know which specific observations, arguments, or findings should actually move me away from this particular formulation. Are there clinical or neurological cases — perhaps involving certain dissociative conditions or disruptions of emotional integration — in which tonal continuity is broken but narrative selfhood remains intact, or in which tonal continuity persists but the narrative self fragments? Would research in developmental psychology on how young children construct self-narratives before they possess stable emotional patterns undermine the claim that affective tone is primary? I also want to understand whether this view risks collapsing into rival accounts: could tonal continuity turn out to be merely a redescription of standard psychological continuity, or could it be fully explained by predictive processing mechanisms that require no self-construct at all? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of tonal-continuity narrative gravity could reasonably concede without abandoning the core idea, and be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind.
