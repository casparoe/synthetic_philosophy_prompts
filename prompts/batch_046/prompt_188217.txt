I'm taking this art history survey course and there's this strange institutional thing that keeps bugging me. For our final papers, we have to write formal analyses of paintings we've literally never stood in front of—just high-res slides and the prof's reproductions. The grading rubric actually rewards you for adopting the exact aesthetic vocabulary from the assigned readings, like describing a brushstroke as melancholic or assertive, and it kind of dings you if you write in your own voice and say something like this part feels sad to me. At the same time, the department's study-abroad brochures and orientation speeches are constantly saying that direct encounter is irreplaceable and that images are just shadows of the real thing, that you can't recieve true aesthetic knowledge from a screen. So the same institution is running its core evaluation on pure testimony and reproduction, while officially preaching that aesthetic experience requires first-person presence.

What theory of aesthetic value or epistemic access does that combo actually presuppose? Is it that aesthetic properties are fully communicable through language and reproductions, which would make the brochures pure marketing? Or does it depend on some hidden distinction—like between knowing about an artwork and knowing it aesthetically—and if so, why does the grading system treat them as totally interchangeable? I keep wondering if there's a weirder commitment hiding in there: that aesthetic authority is transferable but aesthetic experience isn't, so students are basically functioning as judgment receptacles who store expert verdicts without needing the corresponding feelings. Could the practice be consistent with a view that denies aesthetic testimony altogether, treating the whole paper thing as just a pragmatic literacy exercise? Or does it quietly lock the department into thinking testimony works for some aesthetic properties but not others, and if we replaced that view, what part of the grading system would have to collapse first?
