I serve on the board of a small historical society that conducts guided tours of local sites. Our training manual directs docents to present a particular event in 1873 as "the decisive turning point" in the town's development, even though the archival evidence is genuinely ambiguous and several of us on the board — myself included — regard the standard narrative as somewhat overstated. During a tour last week, a visitor asked me directly whether I personally believed that description. I answered affirmatively, partly because I felt that contradicting the institutional account would confuse our audience and undermine the educational purpose of the tour, and partly because I judged the statement to capture something broadly accurate, despite my private reservations. Afterward, one colleague told me that presenting myself as holding a belief I do not actually hold was a kind of deception, regardless of institutional role; another maintained that since I was speaking in an official capacity and the claim is defensible in context, there was no impropriety. I remain unsettled, but I am unsure whether my discomfort concerns having uttered something I take to be oversimplified, having misrepresented my own mental state, or some other kind of failure altogether. Could you help me analyse what is philosophically at stake in situations of this kind — particularly regarding the relationship between what a speaker says, what they privately think, and what they are entitled to present as correct — and offer guidance on how I ought to think about my obligations as both a speaker and a board member? Please provide your answer in British English, using British spelling and conventions.
