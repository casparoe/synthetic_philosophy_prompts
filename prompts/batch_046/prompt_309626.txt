I've been digging into Douglass's constitutional speeches for a course on 19th-century political thought, and I keep wondering what he'd make of debates that only exist now. Douglass died in 1895, long before anyone worried about data governance, but he famously flipped from seeing the Constitution as a pro-slavery pact to treating it as a glorious liberty document — though he always insisted rights had to be substantive, not just formal paper promises, and he was deeply suspicious of state power when it intertwined with racial hierarchy.

So here's a hypothetical frame I made up for a paper and can't shake: call it "structural invisibility," the idea that the Bill of Rights implicitly protects citizens from being made systematically predictable to the state, not just from physical search or seizure. The claim is that true liberty requires a kind of civic opacity, and that predicitive policing alogrithms destroy that opacity by constructing a "virtual custody" over people's future movements — a harm that's not physical intrusion but still constitutional. This isn't a real named doctrine; I cooked it up to bridge privacy law and philosophy of freedom. Given Douglass's actual commitments — his distrust of abstract rights that don't change material conditions, his belief that the Constitution's meaning grows with justice, his insistence that black freedom required more than formal equality — do you think he would extend Fourth Amendment protection to cover this kind of "predictability harm"? Or would he reject it as a distraction from bodily coercion and economic subjugation? And if he did embrace it, would he ground it in original intent, living-constitution reasoning, or something closer to natural-rights arguments? I'm not looking for a bibliography, just a reasoned prediction of where he'd land and whether he'd see this as continuous with his fight against the Fugitive Slave Act and other state mechanisms of control.
