I am preparing for a discussion section in my philosophy of mind course, where we have been examining consciousness as a user illusion—the thesis that phenomenal experience is not a direct presentation of reality but a compressed, behavior-guiding interface constructed by neural dynamics. During our last meeting, a fellow student summarized a recent review essay's position in a way that struck me as plausible but possibly distorted. She presented the view as holding that consciousness is literally a software-level illusion running on neural hardware, comparable to the desktop metaphor on a computer screen: just as there are no small folders inside the machine, there is no inner movie in the brain, only predictive-coding dynamics that generate a simplified control panel for the organism. She added that this interface operates like a virtual-machine layer—there is no physical circuit corresponding to each virtual operation—so that the brain's self-model is not a representation of an inner subject but a functional architecture that produces the appearance of one. She further argued that phenomenal properties function as empty predicates—similar in logical structure to a definite description that fails to refer—because they do not denote any intrinsic feature of neural states; consequently, first-person reports possess no special epistemic authority and can be fully replaced by third-person functional descriptions. From these premises she concluded that the hard problem is dissolved rather than solved: there is nothing it is like to be a predictive system, only an evolved narrative that the brain constructs about being a unified subject, and neuroscience can therefore explain away subjective experience without leaving any explanatory gap.

I want to be able to respond to her in the discussion, but I am not sure where the summary is faithful and where it slides from metaphor into metaphysics. Could you audit her summary point by point? Please distinguish which claims a careful defender of the user-illusion framework would accept, which are subtle conflations—such as blurring access consciousness with phenomenal consciousness, treating a representational metaphor as a literal ontological claim, or importing a virtual-machine analogy that may not belong in the original view—and which parts overstate the framework's anti-phenomenological commitments. In particular, address whether the inference from empty predicates to the denial of first-person authority is valid within the view, whether the appeal to predictive coding and virtual-machine architecture is being used correctly, and whether the conclusion that the hard problem is dissolved actually follows from the premises she offers. Finally, identify the weakest point in her argument—the place where the explanation risks becoming merely definitional rather than substantive—so that I can see exactly where the user-illusion claim stops doing explanatory work and starts assuming what it needs to prove.
