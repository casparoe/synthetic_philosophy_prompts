As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind and ethics in earnest, I find myself puzzled by a specific institutional design I have encountered in both clinical and commercial settings, and I would like a careful philosophical reconstruction rather than a casual opinion. The practice in question—let us call it an affective-lock protocol—operates in immersive virtual-reality therapy clinics and high-end entertainment platforms. Users are placed in fully synthetic environments that are continuously modulated by real-time biometric streams: galvanic skin response, facial electromyography, and EEG alpha asymmetry. The explicit operational rule is to maintain a reported or inferred hedonic state at or above a 7-out-of-10 threshold for the entire session; any downward deviation is treated as a system error and corrected immediately by altering sensory inputs, narrative framing, or ambient parameters. Crucially, the protocol never prompts the user to interpret the experience, to assess whether the virtual state of affairs is genuinely valuable, or to compare the session against non-virtual achievements. Success metrics are purely retention and sustained positive affect, and the institutional documentation—whether for insurance reimbursement or consumer marketing—treats therapeutic and recreational applications as functionally identical, differentiated only by the clinical licensing of the facility rather than by any distinction in the well-being concept being pursued.

You are an expert philosopher specializing in theories of well-being and the philosophy of technology. Before constructing your answer, please think step by step and begin with brief brainstorming: list the core commitments of hedonism, desire-fulfillment theory, and objective-list accounts, and recall precisely how Robert Nozick’s experience-machine argument challenges the first of these. Second, examine whether the protocol is philosophically consistent with Nozick’s claim that we care about being in contact with reality and about actually doing things rather than merely having experiences of them—if the protocol ignores that concern, is that a defensible simplification or a conceptual omission? Third, investigate whether the institutional distinction between therapeutic and recreational use can be sustained without appealing to an objective-list criterion (for instance, restoration of agency or authentic emotional processing) that the protocol’s own metrics do not capture.
