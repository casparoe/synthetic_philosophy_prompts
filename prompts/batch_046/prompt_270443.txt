Consider a distinction that has recently been pressed in discussions of digital art and archaeological conservation: a "substrate forgery" reproduces the physical and causal properties of an original—for example, a painting executed with period-appropriate materials and artificially aged—whereas a "provenance forgery" leaves the physical object itself untouched but fabricates the chain of documentation, testimony, and institutional record through which its authenticity is established. Much philosophical treatment of fakes treats value as residing either in the direct perceptual experience of the object, so that a perfect substrate forgery is indistinguishable and therefore equally valuable, or in the artist's intentional causal history, which any forgery necessarily lacks. If, however, we treat provenance forgery as a distinct category—one in which the object may genuinely be old or genuinely executed by the attributed hand, but its documented trajectory through time is false—what are the most important implications for our understanding of the value of originals? Specifically, examine whether provenance forgery undermines value merely through deception, or whether it reveals that part of what we value in originals is an indexical relation to a particular historical pathway that documentation, rather than the object alone, mediates.
