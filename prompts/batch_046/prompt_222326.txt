I am a doctoral student in political philosophy preparing interview questions for a graduate seminar podcast. Our guest is the author of a recent monograph defending what she calls network-obligation pluralism about corruption. She argues that the dominant Western legalistic framework—which treats corruption as the misuse of entrusted public office for personal benefit—tacitly presupposes a Weberian separation of public and private spheres that is historically specific rather than universal. In its place she offers a triadic taxonomy: reciprocal gift-giving that cements horizontal social bonds; patron-client relations that generate vertical dependency without explicit quid pro quo; and instrumental bribery that violates specific duties of office. She holds that only the third category is corrupt in every context, while the second is morally ambiguous—sometimes a legitimate welfare mechanism, sometimes exploitative—depending on whether it functions within a status-confirming network or a resource-extractive hierarchy. She also claims that Western anti-corruption campaigns typically conflate the second and third categories, thereby exporting a culturally particular state form.

Please draft eight to ten questions for this interview that do not simply ask her to restate the taxonomy. Conclude with a question asking what specific empirical result—from comparative ethnography, institutional economics, or legal anthropology—would cause her to abandon or substantially revise the triadic model.

Please begin your response with the full sequence of questions themselves, presented in order, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of this request.
