I am a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on value theory, and I should like you to compose a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—Alistair and Beatrice—who disagree about whether G. E. Moore's doctrine of organic unity can be carried over intact from aesthetics to friendship. Alistair is to defend a rigorous Moorean analogy: just as a beautiful landscape possesses an intrinsic value that exceeds the aggregate value of its individual colours and forms, so friendship is an organic whole whose worth is not reducible to the pleasures or virtues that each friend contributes separately. Beatrice is to resist this parallel by introducing a distinction between what she calls constitutive appreciation, in which parts are valued solely for their role within the unified whole, and recognitional appreciation, which she claims is essential to friendship and involves acknowledging the other person as an independent centre of moral agency whose value is not exhausted by his or her contribution to the relational whole. Their discussion should be genuinely dialectical: Beatrice ought to press Alistair on whether recognitional appreciation can itself be treated as one more constituent part that acquires value only within the organic friendship, and Alistair ought to reply by questioning whether Beatrice's appeal to independent moral agency does not covertly introduce an instrumental valuation of the friend as a means to one's own ethical development. The exchange should remain in the weeds—avoid biographical sketches of Moore or broad surveys of analytic philosophy—and should instead focus on the precise logical architecture of the organic-unity claim when applied to interpersonal goods. Please write your response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind.
