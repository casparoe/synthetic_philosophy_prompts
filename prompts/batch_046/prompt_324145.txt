I was in a seminar on rational choice theory and got into a side debate about sunk costs that now feels like it's spiraling. Here's the argument I skecthed on the white board and now can't stop thinking about.

(1) If rationality is just maximizing expected utility from this point forward, then any weight given to past expenditures is a mistake, because those expenditures don't change the payoff matrix ahed. (2) So honoring a sunk cost — like finishing a bad book because you bought it, or staying in a grad program you've already paid for — is always a failure of rationality, full stop. (3) But apply this consistently: if you've spent a decade in a marriage or built up expertise in a niche field, those investments are also sunk. A purely forward-looking agent should walk away the moment the future net utility turns negative, with no special burden of proof. (4) In fact, under this view, loyalty itself looks like a bias: you're treating past emotional expenditure as a reason to continue, which is structurally identical to throwing good money after bad. (5) Yet this seems to prove too much, because it dissolves not just foolish persistence but also commitments, identities, and long-term cooperative strategies that seem rational to maintain even when a single decision-point looks negative. (6) So either (1) is false and there's a legitimate form of backward-looking rationality, or (1) is true and we're just emotionally attached to our past selves in a way that rational agents should transcend. I want to keep (1) because I think commitment is usually just post-hoc rationalization, but I can't shake the feeling that the argument smuggles in something about personal identity or path-dependence that I haven't named. Where exactly does it overreach? Is the mistake in treating all past investments as fungible, or is there a hidden slide from decision-theoretic rationality to some stronger claim about the self?
