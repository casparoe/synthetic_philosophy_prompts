I have been thinking about epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experience produces no physical effects, rather like smoke trailing behind a train rather than driving it. The usual objection is that if consciousness were causally inert, we could never form judgments about it, since our judgments themselves are physical events that would need to be caused by something. I want to propose a different framing, and I would like you to argue for it. Suppose that when someone reports a feeling — say, "I am in pain" — they are not describing an inner causal agent, but issuing a kind of public calibration signal: a commitment that explains and predicts their behaviour to others, without that signal itself exerting any causal force. On this view, phenomenal judgments do not require consciousness to cause them; they only require consciousness to be present as the occasion for a socially useful coordination move. Please argue that epiphenomenalism can therefore survive the paradox of phenomenal judgment, provided we understand phenomenal reports as non-causal social calibration commitments rather than as representational descriptions. Your argument should explain why this does not make consciousness a fiction, and why it does not smuggle causal efficacy back in through the back door of communication.
