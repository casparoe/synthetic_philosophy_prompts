In a seminar on the history of cognitive science, two participants disagree about John von Neumann's posthumous lectures published as The Computer and the Brain. The first argues that von Neumann essentially laid the groundwork for computational neuroscience: he treated neurons as all-or-none devices analogous to logic gates, calculated that massive redundancy could compensate for high individual error rates, and therefore presented the brain as a digital computer built from unreliable hardware—anticipating modern connectionist architectures. The second replies that this inverts von Neumann's actual thesis. She points out that he contrasted the brain's error rates—orders of magnitude higher than those of vacuum tubes—with the computer's strategy of achieving reliability through precise, nearly error-free components. For her, von Neumann's point was that the brain relies on statistical, analog aggregation across redundant pathways, and he explicitly warned that the brain's mode of operation is not a formal logical language but something with far lower logical precision. The first counters that von Neumann still formalized neural firing with Boolean notation and asked whether brain functions could be reproduced mechanically, which implies functional equivalence with computing machinery. The second responds that formalization was a provisional descriptive scaffold, not an ontological reduction, and that asking whether tasks can be mechanized does not commit one to saying the brain performs them digitally; indeed, she notes, von Neumann suggested that artificial devices should exploit digital precision rather than imitate the brain's noisy analog methods. The first then asks: if the comparison is not meant to show the brain is computer-like, why frame the analysis around shared problems of reliability and information storage? The second answers that framing them as shared problems highlights divergent solutions—statistical redundancy versus component perfection—which undermines rather than supports the brain-as-computer metaphor that dominates contemporary cognitive science.

Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point? For each move—treating neurons as logic gates, the error-rate comparison, the status of Boolean formalization, the mechanization question, and the inference about shared problems—say which party is closer to von Neumann's position and what, if anything, both are missing. Then assess whether von Neumann's view actually licenses the computational theory of mind or instead exposes an architectural gap that analogies to digital computing obscure.
