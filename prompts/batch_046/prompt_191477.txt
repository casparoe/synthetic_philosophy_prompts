In recent discussions of testimonial injustice, a comparison is often drawn to a biased statistical filtering system: a hearer’s prejudice is said to function like a prior that systematically assigns lower credibility weights to reports from members of certain social groups, analogous to an algorithm that down-weights data points from particular sources. The analogy is used to argue that epistemic injustice is fundamentally a calibration error—correctable in principle by adjusting the weightings—and that the primary harm is informational (the systematic loss of true testimony) rather than a distinct moral wrong tied to status or recognition. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the algorithm, the prior distribution, the individual data points, the calibration metric, and the engineer or institution that can correct the bias. Second, identify where the mapping breaks—does prejudice behave like a probabilistic weight that is evidentially responsive, or does it operate through non-inferential mechanisms, institutional architectures, or identity-based exclusion that have no analogue in statistical filtering? Third, specify what features the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks—for example, the assumption of an objective ground-truth against which error is measured, the possibility of neutral feature selection, or the idea that correction can be performed by a single agent without altering power relations. Fourth, assess whether the conclusion that epistemic injustice should be treated primarily as a debiasing problem survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. Finally, if the analogy collapses in important respects, suggest a better structural comparison that preserves the insight about systematic distortion without importing the misleading machinery of algorithmic correction.
