Having spent three decades in experimental physics and only recently begun a systematic study of philosophy of science and archaeological epistemology, I am struck by the gap between abstract discussions of underdetermination and the practical judgments a field archaeologist must make in real time. I want an operational protocol—usable in a single forty-five-minute session, without access to comparative databases or specialist consultation—that translates abductive principles into a step-by-step procedure for deciding whether an excavated material assemblage supports an inference about a collective institutional belief, as opposed to individual habit, functional necessity, or post-depositional accident.

The protocol should be structured as an ordered checklist or decision tree, progressing from physical description through contextual analysis to institutional attribution. It must preserve two conceptual distinctions that I regard as indispensable but rarely made explicit in field manuals: the difference between indexical persistence—objects that remain in active circulation across multiple social contexts and thereby accumulate institutional significance—and contextual singularity—objects fabricated for, and typically abandoned within, a single bounded event; and the asymmetry of negative evidence, whereby the deliberate absence of an expected object class in a context where preservation conditions are favorable carries a different logical weight than the presence of an unexpected one.

The procedure must flag every point at which judgment is irreducible, especially in disambiguating symbolic from utilitarian function and in assessing whether preservation bias can account for an observed absence. It should be honest about the conditions under which it will misfire, including cases of material overdetermination where ritual and practical uses leave identical traces.

Please apply the protocol to three hypothetical assemblages. First: a cluster of standardized ceramic vessels with uniform orientation and chemical residues consistent with a shared substance, recovered from a non-domestic structure with minimal abrasion or wear patterns. Second: a burial context containing geometrically arranged luxury goods but no human remains, situated within a region where intact burial is otherwise well-attested and preservation conditions are excellent. Third: a workshop deposit of production tools exhibiting systematic, ritualized fracture patterns, yet associated with manufacturing debris indicating recent active use.

For each case, walk through the steps in order, identify the specific judgment call, and report the protocol's conclusion—including at least one case where the conclusion runs counter to an intuitive or narrative-driven interpretation. Finally, specify the structural limitations of the procedure: which categories of institutional belief it is inherently incapable of detecting, and what kinds of archaeological contexts will cause it to generate false positives or false negatives regardless of the care with which it is applied.
