I work for the national curriculum board and every few years we have to sign off on how the country's colonial and wartime past is taught to 16-year-olds. There's constant pressure either to say 'that was them, not us' or to make current pupils feel personally guilty for things they didn't do. I've been told to use Jaspers' distinction between criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt, but nobody has told me how to turn that into an actual decision procedure my reviewers — mostly historians, not philosophers — can apply repeatedly.

Can you write me a practical flowchart-in-words they can run on any proposed syllabus module? I want ordered questions that decide whether criminal guilt applies to anyone alive; whether political guilt attaches to citizens of the successor state; whether moral guilt applies to individuals who still benefit; or whether metaphysical guilt is the only category left standing. Include the exact point where the procedure stops with a clear 'no collective guilt applies here' and the point where it hands over to a values judgement because the facts don't settle it.

Then run it on two hard cases: first, a unit on administrative violence under a colonial bureaucracy that dissolved fifty years ago but whose land records still determine property rights today; second, a unit on a wartime atrocity committed partly by conscripted soldiers whose grandchildren now sit in our classrooms. One of these should come out in a way that will annoy the board — flag it. And be honest about where the procedure breaks down, especially where inheritence blurs into structural continuity and the flowchart can't do the work.
