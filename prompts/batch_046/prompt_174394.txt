I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through classical Indian epistemology with a small group of similarly situated friends—mostly former engineers and clinicians—who have no prior training in Sanskrit philosophy. For our next meeting I wish to lead a session on Nyāya epistemology, specifically the doctrine of the four pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, and testimony), treating it not as an antiquarian list but as a structured account of validation that has unexpected parallels with, and divergences from, the replication and peer-review norms we knew in scientific work.

Please plan the explanatory arc. First, specify the order in which the four pramāṇas should be introduced so that the dependency relations are transparent: for example, why inference presupposes a prior perceptual grasp of invariable concomitance (vyāpti), and why testimony (śabda) requires an underlying inferential assessment of the speaker's reliability (āptatva) rather than functioning as bare authority. Second, propose the single carrying example—drawn from clinical diagnosis, instrumentation calibration, or experimental replication—that can show all four pramāṇas operating in concert rather than as isolated faculties, and indicate precisely which feature of the example maps onto which pramāṇa and where the analogy to scientific method becomes strained.

Third, map the misconceptions my audience is likely to import: that Nyāya perception is raw empiricist sense-data; that inference is merely Aristotelian syllogism; that comparison is analogical reasoning in the modern sense; and that testimony is uncritical deference to expertise. For each, state the point in the sequence at which it should be anticipated and defused, and identify which of these misconceptions actually threatens the coherence of the account if left unaddressed.

Fourth, propose a check for understanding that would distinguish genuine comprehension—recognizing that, for Nyāya, knowledge (pramā) is a specific cognitive event defined by its conditions of validity—from mere discomfort with an unfamiliar taxonomy.

Finally, I am inclined to include the distinction between savikalpaka (determinate, concept-laden) and nirvikalpaka (indeterminate, pre-conceptual) perception, both because it is central to the Nyāya theory of perceptual knowledge and because it resonates with distinctions in signal processing and microscopy that my colleagues will recognize. Advise whether this distinction belongs in a single introductory session: if so, at what point in the arc and with what analog, without distorting the original doctrine; if not, what should I say explicitly about leaving it aside, and what risk does its omission pose for their grasp of the other three pramāṇas?
