I'm preparing a short statement of my historiographical commitments for a graduate reading group on philosophy of history, and I'd like an audit of whether they actually cohere. Here is where I've landed: (1) Whig history is a genuine methodological vice—I take it to mean not merely celebrating the present, but treating the past as if its primary purpose were to produce our current liberal-democratic arrangements, filtering evidence through that presumed endpoint. (2) At the same time, I think historians should apply contemporary moral standards when judging institutions like chattel slavery or the exclusion of women from political life; moral neutrality in the face of such practices strikes me as a failure of judgment, not a scholarly virtue. (3) I also want to say that the abolition of slavery and the broadening of democratic participation represent real moral progress, not merely contingent shifts in fashion or power. (4) Yet I insist that historical explanation must be rigorously anti-teleological: particular events happen for local causal and contingent reasons, not because they serve some future state of affairs. (5) I distinguish presentism—judging past agents by norms they could not have accessed—from the moral evaluation of outcomes or structures, and I think avoiding the former does not require abandoning the latter. (6) Finally, I think it is legitimate to construct macro-narratives about what we might call "the long arc of rights," provided one emphasizes contingency, resistance, reversals, and the constant possibility of failure, and provided one does not treat our present as historically inevitable.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and how severely each tension bites. For every real conflict, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs—whether it requires giving up the anti-teleological explanatory stance, weakening the moral-progress claim, or redefining what a "long arc" narrative is permitted to presuppose. Order the tensions by severity. Then tell me which single commitment, if revised or dropped, would clean up the most inconsistencies across the set, and whether that revision leaves the remaining package recognizably mine or transforms it into a different position altogether. Be specific about which commitment has to go if consistency demands a sacrifice, and do not treat all six as equally problematic—some of these should stay intact.
