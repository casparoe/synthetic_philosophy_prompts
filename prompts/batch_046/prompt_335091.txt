Question: Wittgenstein argues that religious belief is not justified empirically but operates through 'pictures' that regulate a form of life. Explain his distinction between belief and superstition, and discuss why historical evidence for an event like the Resurrection does not play the same epistemic role for a believer as ordinary testimony does for Julius Caesar. Student answer:

Wittgenstein treats religious belief as part of a language-game with internal rules, so demanding empirical proof is a category mistake. He distinguishes belief from superstition by saying superstition is based on fear and false causal thinking, whereas belief is anchored in a form of life and expressed through practice. For instance, the Resurrection is not accepted like Caesar's existence via historical testimony; instead it functions as a regulating picture that shapes the believer's orientation to suffering and hope. However, Wittgenstein's earlier Tractatus view that the mystical lies outside the world of facts shows he always regarded religious language as non-cognitive emotional expression rather than genuine belief with truth conditions. Therefore religious commitment stands completely outside the space of reasons and is immune to rational revision. This also lets us extend the hinge-proposition framework directly: just as the earth's existence is a hinge for empirical enquiry, religious pictures are hinges for moral life, and neither can be doubted without collapsing the framework.
