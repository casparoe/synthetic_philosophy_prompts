I'm in an upper-level metaphysics seminar and we're designing a study guide for the special-composition debate. I need a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions by how they answer concrete diagnostic cases, not by the labels people adopt. Start with the question of whether two simple objects can ever compose a further object. From there, branch by whether composition requires spatiotemporal adjacency, causal interaction, functional integration, intentional design, or unified phenomenal consciousness. Use the following concrete cases as nodes: a human body, a scattered watch whose parts sit in separate warehouses with no causal link, a distributed computing cluster connected only by data transmission, and a social institution whose members never meet face-to-face. The leaves should include universalism, nihilism, restricted organicist views, contact-only views, functionalist views, and at least two less-standard combinations—specifically, a view that accepts biological and institutional composites but rejects both the scattered watch and the digital cluster, and a view that accepts only entities with a single stream of phenomenal consciousness, however spatially dispersed. For each leaf, state its most awkward commitment in one sentence, and explicitly mark any unoccupied combinations in the logical space (for instance, a view that accepts the digital cluster but rejects the human body) with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every branch as a yes/no question about the specific case so that a student can navigate the tree without already knowing which interpretation each path represents. Finally, flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate. Keep the instrument to eight to ten branch points, formal enough for a seminar handout, and do not include bibliographies or citation lists—just the structure, the cases, and the commitments.
