After thirty years in experimental physics, I have only recently turned to philosophy of science, and I find myself preoccupied by a tension in Otto Neurath’s work that standard summaries tend to gloss over. Neurath championed ISOTYPE, a universal picture language meant to carry scientific and social information across linguistic barriers through standardized visual symbols; at the same time, he insisted that all meaningful empirical statements, including protocol sentences, must be reducible to physicalist descriptions of observable conditions. The difficulty is that if pictures genuinely transcend language and convey content directly, they seem to operate outside the very physicalist linguistic framework Neurath demands for verification. This raises a cluster of open conceptual questions: whether pictorial symbols can be fully translated into physicalist protocol sentences without semantic loss; how ISOTYPE’s unstated conventions—choices of scale, color coding, and spatial arrangement—affect its claim to universal intelligibility; and whether modern scientific visualization, where digital images are often treated as transparent evidence rather than conventional representations, inherits or resolves the ambiguities Neurath faced.
