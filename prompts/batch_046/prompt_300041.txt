I am writing a novel in which a dying philosopher of religion, who has spent her life arguing with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta, attempts in her final hours to compose a poem that holds both the classical skandha analysis and the modern narrative-identity objection in suspension rather than resolving them. I need a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, two refrains—that does this structural work. The first refrain must voice the traditional denial: that form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness are each impermanent and therefore none can constitute an enduring self. The second refrain must voice the counter-pressure from narrative-identity theory: that if no continuing subject persists through these aggregates, then there is no one to be liberated and no one to grieve. Use the villanelle’s rotation—the refrains alternating through the stanzas and finally standing side by side in the closing couplet—to show these two positions passing each other without reconciliation. The poem should be precise enough that a student of Buddhist philosophy recognizes the skandha argument and precise enough that a philosopher of personal identity recognizes the continuity challenge, yet it should remain lyrical rather than discursive, leaving the tension open as the form itself closes. Deliver only the poem; include no commentary, footnotes, or citations.
