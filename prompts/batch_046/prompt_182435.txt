I have no formal training in philosophy, but after years of reading on my own about punishment and moral responsibility, I keep circling the same tension. Daniel Dennett seems to defend an evolutionary, compatibilist picture in which human beings possess real varieties of free will—reasons-responsiveness, self-control, the ability to be moved by moral considerations—and this is enough to make reactive attitudes and deserved punishment coherent even in a deterministic universe. Gregg Caruso, by contrast, argues that if every action is ultimately the product of causes outside the agent’s control, then “just deserts” in any robust sense is a myth; punishment should be reconceived on a public-health or quarantine model, aimed solely at protection, deterrence, and rehabilitation, with no hidden belief that the offender intrinsically owes suffering.

These two camps appear to talk past each other. I want to see whether there is a precise, constructive document they could both sign if they stopped debating for victory and started mapping agreement and disagreement exactly.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration joint statement that Dennett and Caruso could endorse after sustained good-faith engagement. Use this structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims—statements both sides accept without relying on either metaphysics. Include precise agreement about: (a) that human decision-making is implemented by neural processes governed by natural law, with no empirically detectable non-physical intervention; (b) that moral anger and punitive practices have evolutionary origins tied to cooperation and social stability; (c) that modern legal systems in practice mix forward-looking goals (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation) with language and rituals that sound backward-looking; (d) that ordinary talk about “free will” is ambiguous between a metaphysical capacity and a cluster of functional abilities; and (e) that current neuroscience cannot determine, for any specific criminal act, whether the agent possessed a counterfactual “could have done otherwise” of the kind required by basic desert theories.

Second, a disagreements section in strictly neutral wording—formulated so that each side recognizes its own view. For Dennett’s camp, state the position that the functional capacities captured by “varieties of free will worth wanting” (deliberation, reasons-responsiveness, self-modification over time) ground a legitimate form of institutional desert: society may allocate burdens in proportion to wrongdoing not because suffering is metaphysically owed, but because fair rules that respect agency require differential treatment, and this is not reducible to risk management. For Caruso’s camp, state the position that without a genuinely open, contra-causal capacity—an ability to have done otherwise that is not merely compatible with determinism—no suffering can be deserved in itself; any differential treatment must be justified transparently by risk, deterrence, or rehabilitation, and if it cannot be so justified, it should be abandoned, because “institutional desert” without basic desert collapses into hidden retributivism.

Third, identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I take the deepest ones to be: (1) whether there is a stable, non-collapsing distinction between “basic desert” (suffering intrinsically owed for past wrong) and “institutional desert” (burdens allocated by fair social rules that respect agency), and whether the latter can be maintained without smuggling in the former; (2) whether evolutionary and psychological explanations of moral anger undermine its use as a justification for punishment or merely explain the conditions under which it is appropriate; and (3) whether a fully determined but reasons-responsive agent possesses the kind of control that makes punishment a response to the agent qua agent, rather than a management of risk like quarantining a carrier.

For each crux, specify one concrete case, argument, or evidential scenario that both sides agree bears on it, then record each side’s explicit prediction about that case in neutral language. For crux (1), use the case of a hypothetical legal system that scales penalties to harm, respects procedural rights, and explicitly denies that suffering is ever intrinsically good; Dennett should predict that this system still practices real deserved punishment, while Caruso should predict that it either conceals retributive logic or is indistinguishable from a risk-based quarantine. For crux (2), use the discovery that moral anger is reliably triggered by violations of cooperative norms even when the violator could not have acted otherwise; Dennett should predict this shows anger tracks a real feature of agency worthy of response, while Caruso should predict it shows anger is a heuristic that misfires when applied to determined agents. For crux (3), use the comparison between punishing an intentional wrongdoer and isolating an infectious disease carrier: both involve restricting liberty based on danger, but only the first invokes choice; Dennett should predict that the difference is real and justifies differential severity, while Caruso should predict that without basic desert the difference is arbitrary or must be reconstructed entirely in terms of future risk.

Finally, include a brief concluding paragraph in which each side states, in wording it accepts, what specific finding or argument would genuinely change its view—not generic openness, but a concrete result. For example, Caruso might specify that a demonstrated, replicable neural signature distinguishing “deep” reasons-responsiveness from superficial behavioral flexibility, coupled with proof that this signature is not causally determined, would force revision; Dennett might specify that proof that all punitive practices can be fully justified without any reference to the agent’s choices, and that societies adopting such pure quarantine models achieve equal or better social stability, would force revision.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for source verification. Write so that the answer can be constructed from shared conceptual knowledge alone, but push into the precise distinctions—especially around institutional versus basic desert and the evidential status of evolutionary psychology—that keep this debate from being a mere repetition of familiar slogans.
