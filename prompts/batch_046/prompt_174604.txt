My study group has cooked up an argument thats meant to show space colonisation is uniquely wrong, but I think it proves far too much. (1) Call it the vacuum-obligation principle: if you can keep suffering-capable beings out of a region without destroying value that already exists there, you ought to do so. (2) Deep space has no suffering-capable beings, so colonising risks introducing them — settlers, engineered ecologies, or artificial descendants — into a moral vacuum where harm was previously impossible. (3) Those risks aren't offset by Earth-bound gains, since we could achieve the same gains without leaving. (4) So colonisation violates the principle. (5) But terraforming Mars to support life also introduces suffering-capable entities where none existed; by (1)-(4) thats wrong too, yet philosophers often treat terraforming as permissible. (6) Human reproduction does the same, so the principle seems to push us toward anti-natalism. (7) So either the principle is false, or we must accept that having children and terraforming are immoral. I really want to hang onto (1) — it feels right that we shouldn't fill the cosmos with suffering just for expansion — but I cant swallow (6). Which step do you reject, and whats the cost?
