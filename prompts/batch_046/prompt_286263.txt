In my philosophy of value course, a classmate and I have been arguing about whether pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable, specifically in relation to lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. I am reporting our exchange as a bystander and want a point-by-point adjudication.

My classmate defends a lexical-priority view: there is a threshold of suffering such that no quantity of pleasure, however vast, can morally outweigh its presence. He says this is not extreme risk aversion but a structural feature of value—suffering and pleasure belong to different value-categories, with suffering lexically prior, much as rights-violations are not compensable by aesthetic goods.

I replied that this is an arithmetic refusal in metaphysical clothing. If both are hedonic states, they share a phenomenal currency—intensity, duration, qualitative affect—and denying commensurability confuses our psychological reluctance to trade them with an actual discontinuity in value-space. Every “threshold” is just an extremely steep exchange rate, and labeling it “lexical” explains nothing.

He countered that exchange-rate talk already assumes what is at issue. He analogized to freedom versus comfort: we do not say freedom has a “steep price” in comfort units; we say they are incommensurable domains. Likewise, suffering crosses into a domain where pleasure is not the right kind of counterbalance, regardless of magnitude.

I answered that this smuggles deontological machinery into a hedonist framework. If welfare is purely hedonic, suffering is negative welfare and pleasure is positive welfare; to deny cancellation requires showing they are not both welfare-states, not merely asserting domain-separation. I also noted that his analogy works only if we already accept pluralism about values, which pure negative utilitarianism need not.

He then pressed a reductio: would I prefer a world with one minute of extreme torture and a billion years of bliss to a world with no torture and only moderate contentment? I said yes, in principle, if the cardinal arithmetic holds—which he took as exposing the absurdity of linear aggregation.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of our four moves—the lexical-structural claim, my arithmetic-reduction claim, his incommensurability/analogy reply, and my hedonist-domain reply—from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both positions miss, particularly whether there is a coherent middle ground that accepts phenomenal commensurability but denies that moral aggregation across that scale must be linear or unlimited.
