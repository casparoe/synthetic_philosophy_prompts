I am a science-and-ideas journalist preparing a long-form feature on the afterlife of medieval thought experiments in contemporary neuroscience, and I have become particularly interested in whether Avicenna’s flying man—the suspended, sensation-deprived subject who nevertheless knows with certainty that he exists—can be rigorously translated into the vocabulary of predictive processing and interoceptive active inference without losing what made the argument philosophically startling. For this piece, I want you to restate the core flying-man argument using only the conceptual apparatus of hierarchical generative models, free-energy minimization, and the distinction between exteroceptive and interoceptive prediction errors; treat the suspended condition as a scenario in which all exteroceptive channels are silenced but interoceptive and proprioceptive priors remain intact, and explain how the resulting minimal self-model either does or does not replicate Avicenna’s claim to an immediate, non-inferential, and non-bodily awareness of one’s own existence. Crucially, I then want a residue report: identify precisely which commitments of the original argument—such as the immateriality of the knowing subject, the irreducibility of first-person intuition to bodily function, or the denial that self-knowledge requires an object of awareness—translate cleanly into predictive-processing terms, which can only be preserved through distortion of either the neuroscience or the philosophy, and which disappear entirely because they have no counterpart in a framework that treats selfhood as an embodied inference rather than a substantial soul. Finally, I want your assessment of whether that residue points to a real gap in embodied-cognitive explanations of self-consciousness, or whether it merely reflects Avicenna’s use of a pre-modern ontology that contemporary science has legitimately superseded.
