I was reading some old LessWrong threads about intertemporal bargaining and got into an argument with a friend about whether the sunk-cost faliacy is really as clear-cut as people say. Here’s the argument I cobbeled together and now I can’t tell if it’s clever or just confused. (1) Rational decision-making should only weigh future costs and benefits, not unrecoverable past ones — that’s the standard line. (2) But past investments aren’t just money; they’re also the preferences, relationships, and self-models that were formed around them. (3) So if I drop a project, relationship, or long-term commitment because its future utility is lower than an alternative, I’m not just avoiding a fallacy — I’m treating my past self’s commitments as irrelevant data with no binding force. (4) By the same token, honoring a sunk cost is sometimes just honoring the intertemporal contract that keeps my present self aligned with my future goals, basically a Ulysses contract against hyperbolic discounting. (5) So it seems like either honoring sunk costs is rationl whenever it serves that aligning function, or else rationality demands that we treat ourselves as a sequence of disconected agents with no reason to keep promises to our future selves. (6) But if we go that route, long-term planning, loyalty, and even personal identity collapse into a kind of moment-to-moment optimization with no continuity. (7) That feels like it proves too much — it makes rationality incompatible with being a continuous person across time. I think the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), but I can’t pin it down. Which step should I reject, and does rejecting it mean I have to accept that we’re sometimes rationally bound to honor sunk costs, or that continuity of self is just an illusion? I’d rather keep (1) than (6) if I have to choose, but I want to know exactly what I’m paying.
