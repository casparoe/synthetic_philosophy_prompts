Hey — I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I was having drinks with two friends who both happen to have read The Brothers Karamazov back in undergrad. They started arguing about the Grand Inquisitor chapter, specifically whether the Inquisitor's claim that people can't bear freedom is basically a psychological claim about decision fatigue and hedonic wellbeing, or whether it's a theological claim about the terror of moral accountability. I said I'd ask for a breakdown since neither of them could agree on thier positions.

(1) Friend A said the Inquisitor is essentially describing a cognitive limit: freedom requires constant evaluation, which depletes mental resources, so people rationally outsource authority to avoid the exhaustion of autonomy. He even tied it to the modern distinction between hedonic happiness and eudaimonic well-being, arguing the Inquisitor offers hedonic security in exchange for eudaimonic self-direction.

(2) Friend B said that's a total misread — Dostoevsky isn't doing pop psychology. The Inquisitor offers bread, miracle, and authority specifically because human beings are weak and vile in a spiritual sense; they reject Christ not because choice is tiring but because they can't stand being responsible for their own salvation. The horror is existantial and religious, not neurological.

(3) Friend A came back saying if it's purely theological, why does the Inquisitor talk about people preferring to have their conscience relieved and being happy with mystery? That sounds like a description of actual human preference, not just sin. He said you don't need to be Christian to recognize that people often prefer clear rules over ambiguous freedom.

(4) Friend B said describing a preference doesn't make the explanation secular. The Inquisitor is explaining why humanity rejects divine freedom — he's diagnosing the fall, not running a behavioral study. Reducing it to people liking rules because rules are easier strips away the specifically Christian framing where freedom is a gift that carries the risk of eternal separation.

Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of the four from clearly wrong to clearly right, with brief reasons. What are both sides missing — is there a way the psychological and theological readings aren't actually opposed? And please say what would change your mind: what specific feature of Dostoevsky's text or a particular interpretation of it would flip your verdict? Also flag where your reasoning is shakiest, especially if I'm smuggling in a false dichotomy between psychology and theology.
