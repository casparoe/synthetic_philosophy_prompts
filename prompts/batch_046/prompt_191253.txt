I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar session for second-year students in law and philosophy who have recently worked through Weber’s classic study of the Protestant work ethic but have not yet encountered East Asian labor jurisprudence or the French social-democratic framework that institutionalized the 35-hour week. I want them to grasp that “having a right to leisure” is not the simple mirror-image of “having a duty to work,” and that modern labor regimes encode three incompatible normative anthropologies of the person in time: the vocation-driven subject for whom rest carries spiritual anxiety; the rights-bearing subject entitled to protected temporal sovereignty; and the organizationally fused subject for whom the boundary between work and life collapses, with karoshi functioning as the limiting case. I am not asking you to explain these regimes to me; I need a teaching plan for how to build this comparison conceptually, with an explicit dependency ordering, a single carrying example, a misconception map tied to sequence, a check for understanding, and a clear boundary around what should be left out and named as such.

Plan the explanatory arc: which of the three normative pictures must be introduced first so that the remaining two appear as genuine structural alternatives rather than as moral failures or exotic deviations, and in what precise order the conceptual dependencies unfold—specifically, why the Weberian “calling” must be in place before the 35-hour framework can be seen as a normative rupture rather than a mere administrative cap on hours, and why the Japanese case must come last so that it operates as a boundary concept that dissolves the work-leisure distinction itself rather than as an alarming but distant horror story. Identify the one concrete carrying example—perhaps the reconstructed weekly schedule of a hypothetical mid-level salaried accountant in financial auditing, mapped across all three regimes—that must contain enough institutional detail to make the differences structural (contractual obligations, temporal boundaries, organizational loyalty norms) rather than merely cultural, and specify exactly what that example must lack (no comparison of national GDP, no epidemiological statistics, no legal citation) so that it does not drift into empirical sociology or policy analysis. Then map the predictable misconceptions in the order students will form them: that leisure is simply the absence of labor; that karoshi is only extreme overwork rather than the collapse of the very category of leisure; that the 35-hour settlement expresses a universal human right rather than a specific post-war social-democratic bargain; and that Weber’s ethic is reducible to “working hard.” For each misconception, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off—before the carrying example is introduced, during its comparative mapping, or in the final synthesis. Finally, propose one question I can ask at the end that will reveal whether they have grasped the core conceptual tension—whether they understand that these three regimes presuppose fundamentally different answers to the question of who possesses work and leisure as rights—rather than having merely adopted a posture of moral disapproval toward Japanese corporate culture.

Specify what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—such as the full legislative mechanics of the French 35-hour framework, the epidemiological literature on karoshi, or Weber’s full reception history—and say so explicitly in the plan. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation recommendations; the students will not have library access during the session, and I do not want guesses at sources. The prompt should not depend on exact titles, dates, or verbatim quotations from texts; paraphrase freely wherever a concept is referenced, and avoid putting anything in quotation marks that derives from an actual source.
