Design a runnable 75-minute classroom game for a philosophy seminar of about 12 advanced undergraduates on money pumps and diachronic rationality. Give the participants small real stakes—physical tokens exchangeable for a tangible reward at session’s end—so they feel exploitation rather than merely hearing about it.

Structure the session in three sequential rounds of pairwise exchange among three goods labeled A, B, and C. In each round every student privately reveals a preference ordering between only the pair offered; the facilitator then executes trades at fixed exchange rates according to those revealed preferences. Do not display all three pairs at once: students see one comparison per round, allowing them to construct an intransitive cycle (A over B, B over C, C over A) across rounds without noticing it until final accounting. Designate one or two students as “arbitrageurs” who observe revealed preferences and may offer side trades that complete the cycle, draining tokens from anyone who accepts. Arrange the rules so that a student who applies the same decision rule myopically in each round ends with fewer tokens than one who pre-committed to a consistent ranking at the start, but make the pre-commitment option carry its own visible cost—for example, forfeiting flexibility in a hidden fourth round whose payoff matrix is revealed only after commitments are locked.

Do not name “money pump,” “dynamic consistency,” “Dutch book,” or “diachronic rationality” before the activity ends. The debrief should occur only after students have calculated final holdings. In the debrief: map round one onto synchronic preference revelation, round two onto temporal separation of choice, and the arbitrageur’s extraction onto the exploitation argument for consistency across time. Predict two failure modes—students will likely accuse the arbitrageur of “cheating” by possessing too much information, and some will insist their round-three preference was genuinely different rather than inconsistent—and specify exactly how the facilitator should respond without delivering a lecture. Be explicit about which normative feature—specifically, the idea that rationality binds an agent across time rather than optimizing each isolated moment—cannot be reproduced by token loss alone, and state precisely what the facilitator must supply verbally to close that gap.

Provide precise rules, token economics, role assignments, and a minute-by-minute timeline so that another instructor could run the session without prior expertise in decision theory. Do not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
