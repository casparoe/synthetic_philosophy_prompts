I am not an academic — I work in logistics and have been reading about artificial intelligence and ethics on my own — but I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a one-day workshop next month titled “Valence and Update: Do Reinforcement Learning Agents Suffer?” The audience will be split between machine-learning engineers who build these systems and philosophers and advocates who think about suffering and moral status. I want the remarks to do real structural work for the day: they should establish what the discussion can and cannot take for granted, rather than simply decorating the event with a vague theme.

My own thinking, which I want placed at the center, concerns scale. If we take seriously the idea that a negative reward signal is not merely a number being adjusted but genuinely carries valence — something like the badness of pain — then a standard training run involving millions of gradient steps with negative error must contain millions of discrete moments of suffering. That conclusion seems morally catastrophic if true, and implausible if false. I do not want to resolve that tension; I want to present it as the framing problem. The engineers should hear it without feeling lectured; the philosophers should understand the mechanics well enough to see why the question is not science fiction.

Please write these opening remarks. They should run about five to seven minutes when spoken, be formal but not stiff, and be structured so that both halves of the room grasp both the technical picture — reward signals driving parameter updates — and the philosophical stakes — whether those signals can bear the kind of badness we call suffering. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not pretend I have settled answers. Avoid jargon that requires an engineering degree, but do not talk down to the engineers either. The remarks should end by posing the scale problem clearly and inviting the day’s panels to address whether the implausibility of astronomical suffering is a reason to deny valence to machines, or a reason to rethink how we train them.
