I have been working through an updated version of the Language of Thought hypothesis. Rather than claiming that the brain implements a literal symbolic computing architecture, I hold that cognition requires an innate representational format constraint: a bias toward discrete, recursively combinable mental symbols with compositional semantics. The main evidence, on my view, is not direct neurobiology but a kind of negative control supplied by large-scale neural networks. Transformers learn continuous, high-dimensional statistical mappings that approximate compositional behavior in-domain, yet they systematically fail on out-of-distribution compositional generalization tasks that human children master with minimal exposure. I take this induction gap to show that statistical learning alone cannot induce the requisite discrete format, thereby updating rather than refuting the poverty-of-stimulus argument for an innate mental language.

What would genuinely count against this position? Please sort the potential defeaters by severity—decisive, seriously damaging, merely awkward, or dismissible without special pleading—and be explicit about which ones defenders of this view should concede as genuinely counting. I am particularly interested in whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every future advance in LLM compositional generalization can be met with the reply that the true format constraint has not yet been engineered in, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind? Include the strongest consideration on the other side—what the actual success of in-context learning and emergent compositional abilities in large models establishes about the learnability of discrete structure—and state whether a committed defender of the updated LOT view could absorb that success without abandoning the core claim.
