I'm delivering the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez, a mathematical logician who spent thirty-five years working on formal systems and was privately known—though rarely in print—to hold that Gödel's results revealed a genuine boundary to mechanization, not merely a technical limitation of particular formalizations. The room is split: roughly half are logicians, philosophers of mathematics, and computer scientists who will immediately recognize and wince at a sloppy popular version of the Lucas-Penrose argument; the other half are family members, university administrators, and former students from other fields who have never heard of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. I need a five-minute toast in which the Gödelian anti-mechanist idea does real structural work—shaping what I can claim about her intellectual character, what qualifications I must insert, and what I must deliberately leave unsaid—rather than serving as decorative academic ornament. Specifically, the speech should navigate, without misrepresenting either side, the distinction between Gödel's formal result (that a sufficiently strong consistent system cannot prove its own consistency) and the Lucas-Penrose inference (that human mathematicians can recognize the consistency of such systems in a way no machine governed by the same rules could). It should avoid implying that she ever "proved" human minds transcend computation in some mystical sense, and it should not cite specific papers, page numbers, or bibliographies that I do not have in front of me. The family should feel warmth and the specific human achievement being honored; the experts should feel that the argument was handled with precision and without false attribution. At the very end, on its own line, provide a one-sentence summary of the toast's central claim, beginning with "Verdict:".
