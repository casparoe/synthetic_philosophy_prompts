In a philosophy of mind reading group I'm coordinating, we've been circling the same disagreement for months without anyone changing position, and I'm beginning to think the structure of the debate itself is the problem rather than the arguments within it. The dispute is between defenders of higher-order theories of consciousness—who maintain that a mental state becomes phenomenally conscious only in virtue of being represented by a distinct higher-order mental state—and proponents of first-order representationalism, who claim that phenomenal consciousness is already intrinsic to suitably structured first-order sensory representations, with no second-order representation required. Both sides have developed sophisticated technical responses to the familiar objections: higher-order theorists have drawn careful distinctions between actual, dispositional, and non-actualized higher-order representations to address apparently conscious but unmonitored states, while first-order theorists have articulated same-order monitoring and representational misalignment mechanisms to account for hallucination and illusion without invoking a separate meta-representational layer. Despite these refinements, the debate feels frozen. My working theory is that the disagreement is not empirical—no neuroimaging study or behavioral experiment is likely to settle whether consciousness requires a relational, higher-order structure—nor is it merely verbal, since both sides agree on the descriptive facts about which mental states occur. Rather, I suspect the impasse reflects a hidden divergence about the explanatory target: one side treats the problem of consciousness as the problem of explaining a subject's epistemic access to her own mental life, while the other treats it as the problem of explaining the specific phenomenal quality of representational content itself, with misrepresentation serving as a test case that each side handles differently. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is there a deeper formal disagreement about whether mental self-reference can be genuinely non-circular, or about which kinds of representational error count as data for a theory of consciousness? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about misrepresentation, self-monitoring, and the conditions for phenomenal presence—not a comparative survey of positions. And given your diagnosis, tell me what kind of progress is even possible here: a new conceptual distinction between epistemic and phenomenal access that forces one side to reformulate its core claim, a formal result about the conditions for non-circular representation, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates two distinct phenomena—access and qualitative presence—under a single heading?
