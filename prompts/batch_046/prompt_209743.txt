I was in an intergenerational ethics reading group when the discussion turned to whether future climate damages should be discounted in present cost-benefit analysis. One participant treated a single line as conversation-ending: "Pure time preference is irrational — full stop." She seemed to take it as proving that any social discount rate reflecting nothing but temporal proximity is ethically indefensible, which in turn makes standard economic analyses that weigh distant harms less heavily normatively bankrupt. I suspect the slogan is smuggling in several distinct philosophical commitments, but I cannot map the precise fault lines.

Could you lay out the menu of precise claims that "pure time preference is irrational" might express? I want the distinctions separated clearly: theses about individual diachronic rationality and consistency across temporal stages of a person; claims about the ethical neutrality of temporal position, perhaps analogized to spatial neutrality; the difference between genuinely pure impatience and discount rates justified by risk, productivity growth, or opportunity cost; and the further question of whether a judgment about personal irrationality even transfers to institutional planners setting social rates. For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible philosophical thesis, or false. Then indicate which of these interpretations actually supports her sweeping dismissal of climate discounting, and which ones leave conceptual space for a positive social discount rate that is not grounded in pure time preference.
