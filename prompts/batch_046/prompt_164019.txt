I've been taking this seminar on Marx's Capital and the 1844 Manuscripts, and our professor keeps ruling on whether different labor setups count as exploitation, alienation, both, or neither. Her verdicts so far:
- 19th-century factory worker, doesn't own the loom, paid by the piece: exploitation yes, alienation yes.
- Ancient Roman slave, master takes the surplus grain: exploitation no, alienation no — she said Marx reserves both for capitalism, and the slave isn't a 'free' subject whose labor gets made external.
- Modern software engineer with stock options who says she loves the work: exploitation yes, alienation no.
- Gig driver who owns the car but the app takes thirty percent and controls the route: exploitation yes, alienation yes.
- Member of a worker co-op who shares profits and votes on the schedule: exploitation no, alienation no.

Next week she's going to ask about a hypothetical post-scarcity setup: AI and automation produce everything, there's no private capital or wages, but people are assigned twenty-hour 'social contribution' roles by an elected council; they don't control the machines, the tasks feel arbitrary, and people report feeling disconnected from what they do. I'm less intersted in the bare answer than in the rules she's running — maybe one ties both concepts strictly to capitalist social forms, another treats alienation as separation-from-control regardless of mode of production. Say what each predicts for this case, which fits her track record better (especially the slave and the gig worker), and name one further case that would actually split the two rules. If I'm forcing a pattern that isn't there or if the distinction I'm drawing is bogus, just say so — don't fake a neat answer.
