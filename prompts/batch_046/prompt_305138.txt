Consider the track record of Dr. Alina Rourke, a historian of scientific institutions, who evaluates whether particular explanations of institutional change commit the narrative fallacy or hindsight bias. Her rulings are these.

She rejected the standard account of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak, which frames John Snow as a solitary detective-hero who traced the contaminated pump through brilliant deduction; she argued that it imposes a detective-novel plot on a messy, collaborative municipal investigation and assigns decisive causal weight to Snow’s map only because we now know the outbreak ended there.

She rejected the familiar explanation of the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis resolution, which describes President Kennedy applying rational decision-theoretic calculus to navigate toward peace; she said it imports conceptual tools—formal decision analysis—that were not part of the administration’s actual deliberative vocabulary, and it reorganizes a panicked, ambiguous process into a tidy narrative of rational mastery.

She accepted an explanation for the 1940 Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse that focuses on specific 1939–1940 engineering budget debates documented in meeting minutes, emphasizing that concerns about aerodynamic stability were explicitly raised and overridden for fiscal reasons; she noted that this account avoids both a tragic-hubris plot and retrospective selection of the flutter mechanism, since the risk factor was visible and contested among the actors themselves.

She rejected the explanation that France’s 1795 adoption of the metric system was the inevitable culmination of Enlightenment rationalism; she said it substitutes a philosophical-progress narrative for the documented reality of fiscal crisis, war indemnities, and provincial resistance.

She rejected an account of the 1991 dissolution of the Soviet Union that identifies “structural economic stagnation” as the primary cause, noting that while stagnation was discussed by economists in the 1980s, almost no contemporary analysts predicted systemic collapse from that factor alone, and the explanation assigns it decisive weight only with the benefit of knowing the outcome.
