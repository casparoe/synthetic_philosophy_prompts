I am drafting a novel in which the protagonist, a museum archivist, suffers from recurring episodes of extreme lucidity during sleep—dreams so structurally coherent that upon waking she retains not only memories but a lingering sense of moral accountability for actions performed within them. Gradually she advances a more unsettling hypothesis: that her waking life is itself a sustained fiction composed by an unseen author, and that her apparent autonomy is merely a narrative requirement. She presents this to her physician as grounds for withdrawing from ethical commitment—if her existence is authored rather than autonomous, she argues, then obligation collapses into performance, much as a dreamer's actions carry no weight upon awakening. The physician counters that even within an authored narrative, the phenomenology of suffering and choice remains intact, and that refusing moral responsibility on metaphysical grounds is a category error comparable to neglecting a drowning child in a dream because the water is unreal. I want the philosophical architecture of this disagreement to emerge organically for my readers, without either character naming a recognized doctrine. Is the physician's analogy sound? Or does the suspicion that one's consciousness is embedded in a narrative authored by another introduce a distinct epistemic and moral asymmetry—one that undermines practical reason in ways that ordinary dream skepticism does not?
