In seminars on reproductive ethics I keep encountering a specific argumentative structure that I do not have a precise label for, and I am trying to determine whether it is a recognized variant of a familiar problem or something that needs its own taxonomy. The pattern arises in defenses of liberal eugenics and procreative beneficence: a parent or clinician selects an embryo with trait profile A over one with trait profile B—say, avoiding a serious but not lethal genetic disorder—by claiming that this choice was made for the benefit of the child who eventually exists. The implicit reasoning seems to require comparing the welfare of the actual child with the welfare of the counterfactual child who would have existed had B been selected. Yet when an interlocutor presses the point that selecting B would not have harmed anyone, because the B-child never comes into being, the defender insists that no cross-identity comparison is necessary: the parents merely ensured that the particular child they brought into existence had the best prospects available to them. The result is a kind of rhetorical oscillation: the justification smuggles in a comparative judgment between two possible persons competing for the same reproductive opportunity, but the defense against the non-identity objection denies that any such competition or comparison is morally relevant.

This feels distinct from the standard formulation of the non-identity problem, which usually asks whether we can wrong a future person by bringing them into existence with a flawed life; here, the issue is the instability of the justification itself, which relies on a comparison it simultaneously disavows. Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps within discussions of procreative beneficence, liberal eugenics, or person-affecting morality? And if not, how should it be carved apart from the non-identity problem proper, from simple equivocation on the reference of "the child," and from the broader debate about whether parents have a duty to maximize the welfare of their future offspring? I am particularly interested in whether the phenomenon is best treated as a failure mode specific to reproductive justification, or whether it generalizes to other domains where identity-affecting choices are defended by appealing to the welfare of the actual beneficiary while denying that counterfactual beneficiaries matter.
