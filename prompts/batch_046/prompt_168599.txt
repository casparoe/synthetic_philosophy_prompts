I was sitting in on a graduate seminar on combinatorial game theory where two students, Mara and Elias, became locked in a dispute about the classic strategy-stealing argument for Hex. Mara laid out the standard proof with care: if the second player possessed a winning strategy, the first player could open with an arbitrary stone, then adopt that strategy as her own, treating the initial stone as a pure asset since in Hex an extra piece of one’s own color can only aid connectivity across the board. Elias interrupted to object that this reasoning quietly smuggles in an unstated monotonicity assumption—that path-completion is strictly monotone with respect to one’s own pieces—which, while true in standard Hex, fails in a variant he had been examining where the board contains frozen corridors that become permanently obstructed once occupied, meaning an extra stone can actually sever one’s own path rather than help it. Mara conceded the variant was intriguing but insisted they were evaluating the argument for standard Hex, not Elias’s hypothetical. Elias then shifted ground: even confined to standard Hex, he argued, the proof only establishes that Player Two lacks a winning strategy, and it illegitimately invokes determinacy—by Zermelo’s theorem—to conclude that Player One therefore wins, but determinacy governs positions, not strategies burdened with hidden auxiliary moves. He added that when the stolen strategy calls for playing exactly in the square already taken by Player One’s initial arbitrary stone, the standard remedy is to play elsewhere, which introduces not one but two surplus stones, breaking the monotonicity induction that was supposed to justify the steal. Mara replied that this is merely an iterative repair and does not vitiate the argument, but Elias maintained that once recursive repairs are permitted, one is no longer stealing a pre-existing strategy but improvising a new one, so the proof collapses into a tautology about first-mover advantage rather than genuinely demonstrating it. They turned to me and asked me to referee.
