You are an expert philosopher of medicine and metaphysician with deep familiarity in bioethics, personal identity, and philosophy of biology. Before you answer, please take a moment to map out the main conceptual distinctions at stake—particularly the difference between biological integration, phenomenal consciousness, and legal personhood—so that your reasoning proceeds step by step and does not collapse these dimensions into one another.

Consider the institutional practice, widespread in many legal and medical systems, of recognizing two independent criteria for declaring human death: the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, and the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain including the brainstem. In clinical reality, a patient who satisfies the second criterion—commonly called brain death—may continue to exhibit striking integrated biological activity with mechanical support: the heart beats, wounds heal, infections are resisted, homeostasis is maintained, and in documented cases a brain-dead body has sustained a pregnancy to term. Despite this, the patient is treated as legally deceased, which permits both the withdrawal of life-sustaining technology and the procurement of organs for transplantation, whereas a patient whose heart has irreversibly stopped is handled with different legal rituals and ethical protocols. I find this dual-criterion framework puzzling, and I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes it seem coherent rather than arbitrary.

What view of personal identity, consciousness, or the ontology of the human organism does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to the claim that phenomenal consciousness is the essential feature that makes death matter, or to the stronger claim that the brain is the integrating center of the organism such that its loss equals the death of the biological human being? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: for instance, could one consistently defend the practice by saying that the organism survives but the person is gone, or does declaring the body legally dead force the stronger metaphysical conclusion? Examine whether the practice quietly relies on an unstable distinction between the death of the person and the death of the organism, and whether that distinction holds up given that we do not apply parallel reasoning to other catastrophic but localized brain injuries. Finally, tell me what would have to change in hospital protocol, in the ethics of organ transplantation, and in how clinicians counsel families if we replaced the implicit view with one that treated brain death solely as the irreversible loss of personhood while insisting the biological human being remains alive—or, conversely, with a view that rejected brain-based criteria altogether and recognized only circulatory cessation as true death.
