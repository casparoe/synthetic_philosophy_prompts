In anthropological and comparative-political debates about corruption, a common analogical move compares clientelist exchange networks to kinship or family systems: the patron is figured as a parent, clients as dependents, material favors as domestic reciprocity rather than market transactions, and loyalty as a filial obligation rather than contractual compliance. The analogy is often deployed to license the conclusion that applying Western anti-corruption norms to such arrangements is ethnocentric—treating relational morality as if it were bribery. Audit this analogy with explicit correspondence (what plays the role of the parent, the child, the household economy, inheritance of status, exit, and the boundary between inside and outside); identify which family features are genuinely present in clientelism and which are idealized or absent (for example, affective attachment, non-instrumental care, hereditary closure, or the absence of coercion); determine whether the analogy quietly imports normative premises—such as the legitimacy of obligations within families or the inapplicability of contract logic inside them—that do the real argumentative work; examine whether structural disanalogies (public-resource diversion, scale, asymmetry of power, lack of reciprocal vulnerability) undermine the inference that Western standards are inapplicable; and assess whether, once the mapping is corrected and the imported premises are made explicit, the conclusion that universal anti-corruption norms fail survives, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative load all along. In doing so, separate what is descriptively settled about how clientelist networks function from what remains genuinely contested about whether local relational norms can override universalist judgments of corruption, and do not present either the universalist or the relativist position as the consensus among people who study the topic.
