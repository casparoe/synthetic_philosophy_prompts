As a retired biologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science, I attended a small discussion group in which two other members debated whether evolutionary psychology is genuine science or merely a system of just-so stories. I should like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

Alex opened by arguing that evolutionary psychology is essentially unfalsifiable narrative construction: for virtually any observed human behavior—whether altruism toward strangers, aesthetic preference, or risk aversion—one can invent a post-hoc story about how it enhanced reproductive fitness in the ancestral environment. Because the environment of evolutionary adaptedness is reconstructed rather than observed, and because competing adaptive explanations are always available, the field never truly risks empirical refutation.

Blake replied that this overlooks genuine predictive successes. Evolutionary psychology generated specific, counter-intuitive hypotheses—such as cross-culturally stable sex differences in jealousy and parental investment patterns—that were later confirmed. A research program that produces risky, novel predictions satisfies standard scientific criteria and is therefore not mere storytelling.

Alex conceded that some predictions exist, but maintained that they are frequently retrofitted. The ancestral environment is underspecified enough to accommodate contradictory outcomes, and the same behavioral pattern can be interpreted either as a direct adaptation or as a byproduct of other adaptations. Without independent access to past selection pressures, the adaptive account remains a post-hoc gloss on data gathered for other reasons.

Blake then advanced a methodological defense: evolutionary psychology ought to be judged not by its narrative form but by its employment of quantitative genetic models, comparative phylogenetic methods, and formal evolutionary game theory—tools standard in evolutionary biology. If the field applies these rigorous techniques, it inherits the epistemic standing of evolutionary science generally.

Alex countered that applying rigorous methods to conceptually ill-defined targets does not resolve the difficulty. Morphological traits such as beak depth possess clear operational definitions and measurable heritability, whereas psychological targets—putative modules for cooperation, language, or moral reasoning—lack comparable conceptual precision. Hence the methods may be sound, but their application to these particular phenotypic entities remains questionable.

Please examine each move: indicate where each speaker is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partially correct; say who is more right overall; and identify what important consideration, if any, both parties have overlooked. Keep your assessment focused on the conceptual and methodological issues actually raised, without appealing to specific texts or scholars I would need to look up.
