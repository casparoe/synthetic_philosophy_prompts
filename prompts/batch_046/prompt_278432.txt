I am a philosophy professor who chairs a university heritage committee that reviews commemorative names and monuments twice yearly. I need a reusable operational procedure—a decision flowchart in prose—for determining whether to retain, rename, contextualize, or remove a commemorative object, grounded in the ethics of commemoration rather than in legal risk. The procedure should turn on three distinctions: honoring versus recording history; the claim that retention can function as present collective endorsement regardless of original intent; and a further “counter-memorial capacity” assessment I want you to build in, asking whether the object can be physically unchanged yet reframed so that resistance or marginalized perspectives become its primary present meaning. It must be executable in a ninety-minute meeting by non-philosophers; every step that requires judgment rather than mechanical application must be explicitly flagged as a judgment call. Apply the procedure to three cases: a scholarship-funded building named for a benefactor who later promoted eugenics; a campus road named after a colonial administrator whose policies caused documented harm, though the name now functions almost exclusively as geographic reference; and a statue of a local industrialist that activists want removed but that the administration wishes to preserve with an interpretive plaque. Ensure that at least one case yields a recommendation—removal or renaming—that I, as chair inclined to preserve institutional continuity, would find unwelcome. Then state clearly what categories of dispute the procedure cannot settle, and provide an explicit confidence assessment for each step of your reasoning, singling out the single point about which you are least sure.
