I sit on the Clinical Algorithms Review Board of the Meridian Hospital Network. We are evaluating a proposal to pilot an ICU decision-support system, STATIC, whose vendor claims it resolves the symbol-persistence issue philosophers call the frame problem through a two-layer architecture: a first-order inertia axiom that preserves all patient-state predicates unless overridden by new data, and a second-order “relevance gate” that restricts which predicates the axiom governs, based on causal proximity to incoming sensor inputs. The vendor asserts this eliminates the need to enumerate every non-change. Our board’s philosophy consultant has objected that the relevance gate itself requires a frame axiom to maintain its own causal-link database across updates, generating a meta-frame regress; she further notes that in ambiguous emergency trajectories—say, sepsis-induced multi-organ dysfunction—the gate either over-applies inertia and misses rapid state shifts, or collapses into explicit enumeration and defeats its purpose. The clinical co-chair responds that practical deployment never demands philosophical closure, that simulated error rates fall within acceptable clinical bounds, and that insisting on meta-level resolution is an unrealistic standard that would block all symbolic decision support. Half the board favors approval with conditions (limited pilot, mandatory human override, quarterly audit of missed updates); half favors rejection until the meta-frame issue is resolved architecturally rather than managed operationally. Draft the board’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed jointly to the vendor and to our Chief Medical Officer. The memo must state a clear, actionable recommendation; explain how the frame problem and its proposed two-layer solution bear concretely on patient safety, transparency, and clinical accountability; treat the strongest consideration supporting the losing position with genuine fairness; and specify concrete, implementable conditions or next steps that either the vendor or clinical staff could execute. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific academic works; reason entirely from the conceptual structure and institutional stakes of the case.
