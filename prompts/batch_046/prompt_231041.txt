I'm a novelist drafting a philosophical essay on the classification of disease, and I would welcome critical comments on the following line of argument. I propose that the long-standing dispute between naturalists—who identify disease with statistically abnormal biological dysfunction—and normativists—who identify it with socially disvalued states—overlooks a distinct, constitutive criterion: narrative coherence. On my view, a condition is clinically treated as a disease not merely when it involves biological deviation or social harm, but when its manifestations can be integrated into a causal, temporal story that legitimizes medical intervention. To illustrate, I contrast chronic fatigue—where ambiguous biomarkers are superseded by patient narratives that drive diagnosis—with asymptomatic hypertension, where clear statistical deviation is medically significant only because it is embedded in a predictive risk narrative rather than in present suffering. I further argue that this narrative-integrative framework explains why certain culture-bound syndromes are marginalized in Western medicine despite profound subjective distress, and why preventive pharmacology treats asymptomatic risk states as diseases in need of treatment. I ask specifically: does this genuinely differ from existing hybrid or pluralist theories, or is narrative coherence merely an epistemic convenience for clinicians? Where is the argument most vulnerable—particularly in distinguishing constitutive narrative integration from post-hoc rationalization? Please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I seek conceptual criticism alone.
