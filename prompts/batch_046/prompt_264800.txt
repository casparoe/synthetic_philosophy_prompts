I am preparing a 90-minute session for an upper-level ethics seminar of about 10 to 12 students. I want a runnable simulation that makes Glaucon's challenge feel visceral, but I want to avoid the standard invisibility-ring framing that students have usually encountered. Instead, inject the following twist: participants act under asymmetric epistemic opacity — they may take resources from others without being observed, yet each carries hidden "moral residue" cards that only they can inspect, and inspecting one destroys a point from a shared collective pool. The group begins with a common endowment (small real stakes, such as shared snack credits or participation tokens). Unseen extractions slowly deplete the pool, but no one can prove attribution. At the midpoint, each participant may privately audit one of their own cards, revealing whether they caused harm and permanently reducing the pool further. Design rules, any needed roles or materials, a timeline, and a payoff structure that requires no real money. The dynamics should lead participants to experience the pull of hidden extraction and the cost of self-knowledge before anyone names Glaucon, Gyges, or the ring of invisibility; keep all framing abstract during play. Then provide a debrief mapping: the hidden extraction to unjust action under perfect invisibility; the audit choice to the problem of maintaining moral self-knowledge when verification is destructive; and the collective depletion to the social erosion Glaucon predicts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run — for instance, refusal to audit, strategic ambiguity, or premature naming — and how to correct for it. Be frank about which feature of the original challenge, such as the total absence of internal moral motivation or the complete separation of justice from power, this mechanism cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied in discussion. No lecture should precede the activity, and the name of the challenge should not be revealed until after the debrief begins.
