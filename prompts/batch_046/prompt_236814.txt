I spent my career as an evolutionary neurobiologist studying sensory integration in non-human primates, and since retiring I have turned seriously to philosophy of mind—particularly to classical arguments about self-awareness that do not depend on empirical observation. I now serve on the University Hospital’s Committee for Emerging Neurotechnologies and Clinical Ethics, and I need a formal decision memo for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. Our case involves a forty-three-year-old patient with isolated bilateral thalamic lesions who retains intact reasoning, language, and eye-gaze communication but reports a complete absence of proprioception, exteroception, and affective feeling; she describes her state as pure cognition without bodily location. Her family has refused a transition to comfort care, arguing—explicitly invoking Avicenna’s flying-man reasoning—that rational self-awareness without sensory mediation is sufficient for full moral personhood and that withdrawal would destroy a conscious subject. The neurology team counters that contemporary interoception research shows minimal selfhood requires at least internal bodily signaling, which she also lacks, so she is not present in the ethically relevant sense. At the same time, a research group has proposed an implant trial that would restore only interoceptive feedback, asking whether the committee should approve it contingent on resolving whether embodiment is necessary for intelligence or personhood. Please draft the committee’s written opinion recommending a concrete course of action—approve the trial under conditions, reject it, or defer—with a specific time frame, and include reasoning that takes the family’s flying-man argument seriously rather than dismissing it, addresses the strongest neuroscientific response about interoceptive anchoring of the self, distinguishes the immediate care decision from the research-approval question, and writes in language accessible to clinicians and family members without sacrificing philosophical precision. The memo should have a clear bottom line and should honestly treat the strongest consideration on the losing side.
