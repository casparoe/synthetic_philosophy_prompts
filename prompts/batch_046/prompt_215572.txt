Question (worth 10 marks): A small coastal community remembers a devastating flood in 1923 that destroyed their parish church and reshaped local identity. Meteorological archives and parish ledgers, however, record a comparable flood in 1931 and show the church standing intact until 1945. The community insists on 1923 because the date aligns with a seasonal ritual cycle and with oral narratives about lost ancestors. When collective memory and the archival record conflict like this, which should give way? Discuss with reference to theories of collective memory and history, and consider whether the conflict can always be resolved.

Student answer:

Collective memory, as Halbwachs described it, is a social framework that groups use to reconstruct the past in line with present needs, whereas history aims for critical distance and documentary proof. In this case, the community's memory serves an important identity function: the 1923 date is woven into ritual practice and ancestral narrative, so correcting it to 1931 would damage social cohesion. Nora's idea of lieux de mémoire also applies here, because the church site acts as a living site of memory rather than a dead archival entry. Meanwhile, Ricoeur argued that collective memory is always more reliable than history for moral reasons, since history is just the record of the powerful. Therefore, the community's version should take precedence in public policy and education, and historians should treat 1923 as the operative truth. Even if the ledgers say 1931, memory is not falsifiable in the same way as archives, so there's no real contradiction — just different registers of truth.
