My own circumstances over the past year have made Frankfurt-style reasoning unexpectedly personal: I have come to believe that my core political convictions were formed inside an information architecture I neither designed nor could easily exit, and I now find myself unsure whether I am genuinely epistemically responsible for them. This has led me to construct the following argument, which seems to show that either the standard Frankfurt interpretation collapses when extended to belief, or we are forced to treat billions of people as fully blameworthy for convictions they literally could not have avoided.

(1) In canonical Frankfurt cases, an agent remains morally responsible for an action despite lacking the ability to do otherwise, because the factor that removes the alternative is not causally operative in producing the action.

(2) Epistemic responsibility is structurally parallel to moral responsibility: we blame agents for beliefs that flow from their own reflective capacities, not merely for beliefs over which they have direct volitional control.

(3) In algorithmically curated information environments, an agent’s access to counterevidence is filtered out by the architecture of the environment before it ever reaches them; this functions like a Frankfurt-style counterfactual intervener that is present but never activates.

(4) Therefore, agents in such environments can be fully epistemically responsible for false or harmful beliefs, even though they could not have formed accurate ones.

(5) If this holds generally, then for any belief formed within a sufficiently opaque epistemic environment, blame attaches regardless of the agent’s diligence, making epistemic blame ubiquitous and detached from any reasonable standard of care.

(6) That result is absurd: we do not hold people blameworthy for beliefs that were unavoidable given the informational boundaries of their situation.

(7) So we must reject either the analogy in (3) or the claim in (1) that alternative possibilities are irrelevant; but if we reject (1), the standard interpretation of Frankfurt cases is undermined.

Which step should I give up? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1), since the Frankfurt literature appears well established, but I also want to preserve the intuition behind (6). Is the error a hidden equivocation between causal inoperativeness and informational inaccessibility in step (3), or is there a different move—perhaps qualifying (2)—that lets me keep both? Please name the step, explain why it initially looked sound, and describe what each of the main alternatives costs.
