Below is a grading task drawn from an upper-level seminar on biography and moral formation. The exam introduced the concept of exemplary friction—the productive tension between a biographical subject’s exceptional virtue and an ordinary reader’s moral capacity, mediated by narrative structure—to frame a comparative analysis.

Exam question: Analyze how Plutarch’s Parallel Lives and Athanasius’s Life of Antony each manage exemplary friction. Specifically, compare how Plutarch’s practice of pairing a Greek and a Roman life shapes the reader’s ethical relationship to the subjects, versus how Athanasius deploys Antony’s progressive withdrawal into the desert and his episodic trials. Do not treat either author as supplying a straightforward manual for direct imitation. Instead, examine whether moral excellence is presented as gradually achievable through disciplined practice and comparative judgment, or as dependent on a transformative intervention that exceeds ordinary human effort.

Student answer: Plutarch resolves exemplary friction through a normative pairing structure in which the Greek life always serves as the original archetype and the Roman life functions as a derivative adaptation; the reader is therefore invited to calibrate Roman achievement against an unchanging Greek standard, making virtue appear as a universal form accessible through civic discipline. In Athanasius, by contrast, Antony’s biography is organized as an irreversible linear ascent: each episode of demon-combat, fasting, or desert solitude marks a deeper abandonment of the world that closes off partial imitation. Here Athanasius explicitly frames Antony’s self-mastery through the Stoic concept of enkrateia, treating ascetic technique as a replicable method any reader could adopt step by step, while divine grace operates only as a secondary confirmation of progress already achieved. The paired structure in Plutarch thus allows the reader to bridge exemplary distance through comparative measurement, whereas Athanasius’s episodic sequencing renders moral transformation all-or-nothing, since every stage demands a more total withdrawal than the last. Consequently, Plutarch offers moral learning as a gradually achievable civic project, while Athanasius presents it as an exceptional vocation reachable only through complete renunciation aided by discipline.
