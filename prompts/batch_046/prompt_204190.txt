My friend and I just had a long argument about whether self-deception is literally possible—she insisted that holding p and not-p at once collapses into mere ignorance, while I kept arguing that people routinely maintain contradictory commitments across different practical tracks. For an upper-level seminar of about 10 students with 75 minutes, design a runnable simulation (not a lecture) that forces participants to inhabit that tension. Give the group a collective task with small real stakes—say, allocating a shared snack or points budget—under rules that reward coordination only if they apply a specific piece of information everyone technically possesses, but also reward social harmony only if each person officially treats that information as not theirs to invoke. Assign roles: holders who must quietly use the information, enforcers who must deny its relevance, and arbiters who decide disputes. The payoff structure should make it rational for the same participant to reason with the belief in one track while disavowing it in another—frame this not as lying but as motivated epistemic partitioning. Include a precise timeline, the rules, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the philosophical problem: when did you act on the very belief you claimed not to have? Be explicit about which feature of self-deception the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the first-person phenomenological opacity of not noticing the partition—and say how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the force of the idea should come entirely from the rules and roles.
