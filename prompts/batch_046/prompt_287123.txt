You chair the ethics review board of a clinical neuroscience consortium that evaluates proposals for implantable neural interfaces altering cortical connectivity. Each quarter you face applications involving devices that do not merely stimulate tissue but restructure feedforward and feedback loops—creating new pathways between hemispheres, suppressing long-range integration, or coupling biological tissue to artificial feedback loops running at neural timescales. Your board includes clinicians and patient advocates but no specialists in consciousness science; you have roughly ninety minutes per proposal and must issue a signed determination that distinguishes factual claims about causal architecture from normative judgments about moral status.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the core commitments of Integrated Information Theory—specifically that a conscious substrate corresponds to a maximally irreducible conceptual structure (a complex) defined by the postulates of information, integration, exclusion, and the maximization of intrinsic cause-effect power—through which your board can assess whether a proposed implant risks (a) fragmenting an existing conscious complex into competing substrates, (b) merging previously separate substrates into a single complex, or (c) leaving a single substrate intact but altering its conceptual boundaries. The procedure should take the form of ordered tests and questions that a non-specialist can apply using the modeling reports submitted with each proposal; it should specify what counts as sufficient evidence at each step, where the procedure stops with a clear determination (approve, reject, or refer to expert assessment), and precisely where it reaches a limit that requires a value judgment—such as whether a substrate that includes non-biological components qualifies for protection, or which of two equally maximized complexes should be regarded as the patient.

Then apply this procedure to three proposals:
1. A thalamic bridge implant for a split-brain patient that establishes dense bidirectional connectivity between hemispheres. Modeling indicates a global maximum of intrinsic cause-effect power, but local cortical modules retain high local integration, creating a condition—here termed a phi-parity deadlock—in which two spatially overlapping complexes appear to have identical maximization values and neither clearly excludes the other.
2. A cortical grid designed to suppress long-range feedback within the default mode network as a treatment for chronic depression. Modeling predicts that a single dominant complex remains, but with substantially reduced conceptual differentiation and a shift in the boundary of exclusion toward subcortical regions.
3. A distributed microelectrode array coupled to an external silicon feedback controller operating at millisecond latency, embedded in the patient’s prefrontal cortex. Here the causal interactions are distributed across biological and artificial elements, and no clear spatial or temporal boundary allows the exclusion postulate to identify a single biological complex.

In your applications, include the case in which the procedure yields a determination your board will find ethically unwelcome—specifically, that a clinically beneficial intervention must be blocked because it produces an ambiguous or merged substrate—and explicitly state what the procedure cannot resolve, such as whether numerical equality of maximization values breaks ties in favor of continuity with the pre-surgical substrate, or whether artificial components can participate in a conscious complex at all.
