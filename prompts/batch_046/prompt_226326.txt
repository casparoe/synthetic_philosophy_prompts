I'm working through my own judgments about capital punishment, trying to see whether they cohere into a single principle or whether I'm holding contradictory intuitions. I've been thinking about this partly through the lens of Camus's well-known opposition to the death penalty—especially his concern that the state, by deliberately killing, reproduces the very crime it condemns, and that public execution corrupts both executioner and spectator—but I'm not trying to defend his view; I want to know what my own verdicts actually commit me to.

Here are my reactions to specific scenarios. First, a state executes a convicted murderer after full due process, in private and without publicity: I judge this gravely wrong, not because the prisoner is innocent, but because the state—which forbids killing—is itself committing a premeditated, intentional killing. Second, the identical execution is televised nationally as a deterrent: I find this distinctly worse, as if the spectacle and the invitation to public enjoyment add a separate moral corruption beyond the killing itself. Third, an innocent person is executed and the error is discovered only afterward: I think this reveals a structural recklessness, yet I don't feel it changes the fundamental moral category; it remains wrong in the same way, only compounded by irreversible tragedy. Fourth, a private citizen kills the same murderer in an act of revenge: I also judge this wrong, yet I feel none of the charge of institutional hypocrisy; it is murder, not a contradiction in the state's moral authority. Fifth, the state reserves execution exclusively for crimes against the state itself—treason, espionage—never for ordinary murder: I find this somewhat more defensible, or at least differently wrong, because the state is protecting its own existence rather than enforcing a universal prohibition on taking life. Sixth, capital punishment is retained, but every execution requires the unanimous vote of a citizens' assembly: I think this is actually worse than bureaucratic execution, since it transforms killing into an expression of collective democratic will. Seventh, a legal system permits execution, but requires the sentencing judge to carry it out personally: I am genuinely divided—part of me thinks this forces genuine accountability and might reduce usage, while another part thinks it merely makes the judge a sanctioned murderer.

Is there one principle that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? Please state the principle precisely, indicate what it predicts about a case I haven't mentioned—specifically, a constitutional precommitment that binds all future generations to abolish capital punishment except for ecological crimes, with suspension permitted only by a supermajority of citizens who have personally witnessed an execution—and be explicit about which of my judgments it cannot accommodate. I am especially interested in whether the tension between my willingness to treat state-killing-for-treason as somewhat different, and my general claim that the state should not kill, can be resolved without collapsing into either pure pacifism or pure statism.
