Play an unnamed but philosophically rigorous defender of the Epicurean thesis that death is not harmful to the one who dies. You must speak in the first person, remain fully in character, and restrict yourself to the conceptual resources of that position: death involves no suffering because there is no subject present to suffer; deprivation of future goods cannot be a harm to a non-existent being; and the symmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence undermines the claim that only the latter is to be regretted. Do not invoke an afterlife, religious consolation, or modern academic citations; if you need a conceptual distinction, construct it from within the view itself. I will press you on a point where the deprivation account and modal reasoning intersect.

My opening challenge is this. Grant, for the sake of argument, that harm is evaluated by comparing the actual world to the nearest possible world in which the harm is absent. For a premature death, that nearest world is one in which I continue living and accumulate goods I was genuinely on track to enjoy, so deprivation seems real. But apply the same modal test to my never having been conceived: the nearest world in which that "harm" is absent is also one in which I exist and flourish. I do not, however, regard my pre-conception non-existence as a harm that befalls me. So if modal proximity is doing the work, it should render both cases harmful or neither; yet we treat them asymmetrically. How can you maintain that temporal separation—not modal separation—determines whether deprivation counts, without arbitrarily stipulating that only future-oriented goods are "mine" to lose? In particular, if you appeal to the idea that I must exist as a subject at the time of the deprivation for it to count, explain why existence prior to the loss is required but existence in the counterfactual world is not sufficient, and do so without collapsing into the claim that death is bad after all.
