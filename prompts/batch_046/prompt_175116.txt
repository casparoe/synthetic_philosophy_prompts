Here is an argument I built for a seminar on algorithmic epistemology and now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve the objectivity it was meant to secure. (1) Solomonoff induction defines the universal prior P(x) proportional to 2^{-K_U(x)}, where K_U is Kolmogorov complexity relative to a fixed universal Turing machine U. (2) Formalized this way, Occam's razor becomes normative: rational belief should weight hypotheses by P(x). (3) For any two universal machines U and V, there is a constant c such that |K_U(x) - K_V(x)| ≤ c for all strings x, but c depends on U and V. (4) Now consider any specific scientific hypothesis H—say, that dark energy follows a particular equation—formulated concisely in standard physics notation. (5) We can construct a universal machine U_H that includes H as a built-in primitive instruction, so that K_{U_H}(H) is essentially 1, regardless of H's internal complexity. (6) We can construct another universal machine V that lacks any such primitive, forcing K_V(H) to be the full length of H's standard formulation, which may be thousands of symbols. (7) Because there is no physically or rationally privileged universal computer—U is a conventional formal choice—the prior assigned to H can be driven arbitrarily close to 1 or arbitrarily close to 0 by selecting U_H or V. (8) Applying Solomonoff induction consistently therefore implies that the rational credence in H is determined not by the world but by our arbitrary choice of programming language. (9) But if the choice of U is conventional, then there is no objective fact about whether Occam's razor supports H, undermines it, or is silent. (10) Thus formalizing Occam's razor via Kolmogorov complexity, rather than grounding objective scientific belief, makes rational belief entirely relative to representational convention. I am particularly reluctant to give up (1) or (2), and I think (9) captures the real tension. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, address whether the constant-factor defense saves objectivity, whether a "natural" UTM can be justified without circularity, and whether (9) overstates the problem by treating finite constants as decisive.
