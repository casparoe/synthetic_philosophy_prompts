I'm writing a paper for my rational-choice seminar on intertemporal ethics, and I constructed the following argument against pure time preference. It seems to show not merely that discounting the future is irrational, but that any indexical theory of practical reason collapses into perpetual deferral. I want to know where it slips.

(1) Pure time preference holds that a harm occurring at t+n is less weighty solely in virtue of being further in the future from the agent's present.
(2) If this reason is genuinely indexical—tied to the agent's current temporal location—then its force shifts as the agent moves through time.
(3) So at t0 the agent discounts harm at t2; but upon reaching t1, the agent's present has shifted, making t2 closer and t3 the distant, discounted harm.
(4) This creates a perpetual-deferral mechanism: at any moment, taking a small present cost to prevent a larger near-future harm is always undercut by deferring the action one interval, which pushes the benefit further into the discounted future while leaving the cost in the present.
(5) By induction, no intertemporal project is ever rational to begin, yet pure time preference was meant to justify caring somewhat about the future, not never acting at all.
(6) The only remedy is to anchor the discount rate to a non-indexical origin—say, the agent's birth or a fixed calendar—so the rate does not shift.
(7) But then the rate applies impersonally; it gives the agent no special reason to care about her own near future rather than anyone else's, and it collapses pure time preference into an arbitrary, agent-neutral scheme.
(8) Therefore pure time preference is either self-defeating (if indexical) or motivationally empty (if absolute).

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (5), because I think the instability is real. But I suspect there's an equivocation between the time of evaluation and the time of occurrence, or perhaps a scope shift hidden in the induction step. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, does giving up (2) require abandoning the idea that practical reasons can be temporally indexical at all, or is there a more local repair?
