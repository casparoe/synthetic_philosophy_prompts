Following a long and somewhat circular argument last night with a friend who specializes in constitutional theory, I want to formalize our disagreement about religious arguments in democratic politics into an adversarial collaboration statement that both of us could sign onto—at least in its descriptive parts. The dispute concerns whether citizens who hold theological commitments may invoke those commitments in public justification for coercive law, and if so, under what conditions. On one side stands what I will call the Translation-Mandate Camp: it holds that religious reasons are permissible in the broad forum of public discourse but must be accompanied by, or rendered into, an independent secular justification before they can legitimately support state coercion; without such translation, the argument fails the test of public reason. On the other side stands the Hybrid-Justification Camp: it maintains that requiring a clean separation between religious and secular premises imposes an unfair cognitive burden and that public deliberation should permit integrated or hybrid justifications, provided the speaker demonstrates a reciprocal willingness to engage with citizens who do not share the theological foundations.

The deliverable should be a joint document with three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps accept—concerning, for example, the nature of democratic legitimacy, the fact that religious citizens hold beliefs they regard as non-optional, and the reality that coercive law demands a special form of justification. Second, a structured table or list that states the residual disagreements in neutral wording that each camp would endorse: precisely where the Translation-Mandate Camp sees a categorical failure of public accessibility and where the Hybrid-Justification Camp sees an illegitimate demand for cognitive bifurcation. Third, the cruxes—the narrower sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—together with concrete cases or pieces of evidence that both sides agree are relevant, and each camp's explicit prediction about those cases. I suspect the central cruxes involve what we have been calling the translation-cost asymmetry—whether the demand to produce secular analogues systematically disadvantages religious citizens relative to secular ones who need not translate their metaphysical commitments—and the problem of masked premises—whether hybrid justifications genuinely solve accessibility or merely cloak an irreducible theological claim in secular vocabulary. For the first crux, both sides should examine a specific legislative scenario: a proposed carbon-regulation framework advanced partly on stewardship-theology grounds, where religious legislators must either supply an independent economic argument or remain silent in the justification; each camp should state whether this demonstrates an unfair democratic burden or an appropriate discipline of public reason. For the second, they should examine a bioethics commission report that frames human dignity in both Kantian autonomy-language and imago-Dei language, predicting whether such framing increases accessibility for non-religious citizens or obscures the theological root of the policy recommendation, and whether that obscurity undermines the legitimacy of any resulting regulation.

Please write this in a formal, polished register suitable for a seminar brief. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the document should stand on conceptual analysis alone. Where you need to reference well-known positions—such as the Rawlsian distinction between the public political forum and the background culture—do so by name and concept rather than by page or text, and avoid invoking obscure secondary literature or highly specialized empirical studies that a generalist model could not be expected to recall with confidence.
