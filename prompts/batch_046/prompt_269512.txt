Having recently discovered that a line of reasoning I had been developing independently for nearly a year closely mirrors an unpublished argument now circulating in a small specialist circle, I have become personally preoccupied with whether simultaneous discovery is a structural inevitability in mature inquiry or merely a statistical coincidence. I want your actual credence—a specific number between 0 and 1—in the following precisely formulated claim: in scientific or mathematical domains where (i) the empirical constraints are fully public, (ii) the available formal apparatus is standardized, and (iii) the remaining problem-space is narrowly circumscribed, simultaneous independent discovery is not merely probable but epistemically redundant, in the sense that substituting one discoverer for another would leave the long-run trajectory of the field unchanged except for honorific attribution, funding patterns, and sociological prestige.

Please decompose your reasoning through these four sub-questions, assigning each a rough probability and a brief justification:

(a) Whether the conceptual resources available to Newton and Leibniz, or to Darwin and Wallace, were so tightly constrained by prior work that the respective results—calculus and the mechanism of natural selection—were structurally inevitable once a critical mass of antecedent problems had been articulated; and specifically whether calculus, as a formalism with competing notational and metaphysical packaging, differs in this respect from evolutionary theory, where the core mechanism was largely shared.

(b) Whether the differences in framing—Leibniz’s notation and monadological metaphysics versus Newton’s fluxions and theological physics, or Wallace’s biogeographic emphasis versus Darwin’s gradualism and extensive use of domestic-breeding analogies—constituted genuinely distinct intellectual contributions that altered how subsequent researchers applied and interpreted the discovery, or whether they were stylistic and rhetorical variants that converged within a generation into a single canonical form.

(c) Whether there is a domain-general principle we might call attribution elasticity—determining whether and how the identity of a discoverer affects the speed, stability, or conceptual direction of a field’s development—and if so, whether that elasticity is high for theoretical formalisms and low for empirical mechanisms, or the reverse; include in your assessment whether the calculus dispute and the Darwin-Wallace episode support opposite conclusions about this elasticity.

(d) Whether my own distress at the prospect of personal redundancy reflects a mistaken commitment to evaluating intellectual labor by uniqueness of path rather than by contribution to collective knowledge, or whether it correctly identifies a real epistemic loss when independent lines of inquiry collapse into a single attributed origin, particularly with respect to the diversity of research programs that might have flourished had both paths remained visibly separate.

Then combine these into your overall credence for the main claim. State explicitly which of (a) through (d) your final number is most sensitive to—that is, which one, if its assigned probability shifted by 0.3 in either direction, would move your overall credence the most. Finally, describe what kind of conceptual argument or hypothetical historical counterfactual—without appealing to bibliographies, citation lists, or niche archival facts—would most change your number, and in which direction.
