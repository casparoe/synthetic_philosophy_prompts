I just spent several hours in a frustrating but clarifying argument with a friend over whether epiphenomenalism can survive the paradox of phenomenal judgment once you push past the standard first-order formulation. We kept returning to a second-order problem that textbooks rarely treat with precision: if the epiphenomenalist blocks the initial worry—that phenomenal beliefs are unreliable because phenomenal properties are causally inert—by appealing to rational entitlement mediated by phenomenal concepts, then the belief that one is so entitled seems to suffer the very same causal isolation. This generates a reflective regress, since the entitlement-fact (if it exists) is also not causally connected to the second-order belief about it. I want an exam for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that forces students to engage this exact structure rather than reciting the usual summary.
