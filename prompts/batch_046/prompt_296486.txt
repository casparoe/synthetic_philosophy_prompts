Write the desiderata a normative theory about posthumous representation needs to meet if it's going to tell us what we actually owe dead people when we fictionalise them in biopics and historical novels. Don't give me a bibliography or citation list — just the spec, written like casual seminar notes. The theory should handle these tensions: (1) inventing intimate dialogue for a well-documented public figure versus inventing a whole inner life for an obscure person whose only archival trace is a census line; (2) cases where the fiction accidentally occured as the primary source later historians cite, so the lie acquires epistemic authority; (3) descendants who feel honoured versus those who feel violated, and whether the dead person's likely attitude should trump both; (4) the asymmetry between dramatising villains and victims — is it worse to make a victim more heroic or a villain more sympathetic? For each desideratum say why it's a real constraint and not just one camp's preference, and flag any pair you think can't jointly be satisfied — especially if one demands total fidelty to archival fact and another demands artistic freedom to reveal emotional truth. Then briefly check how a reciprocal memorialism view (treating representation as continued dialogue with the dead governed by reciprocity rather than property rights) and a harm-only view (wrong only if living relatives suffer demonstrable harm) do against your list. Which feature of each view makes it stumble on the hardest case?
