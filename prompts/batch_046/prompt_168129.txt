I have recently become a founding trustee of a small artists' cooperative that maintains a shared studio, a joint bank account, and formal collective decision-making procedures, while each member retains individual copyright and independent artistic practice. In drafting our governing charter, a disagreement has emerged that strikes me as more ontological than administrative: some members speak of the cooperative itself as a single agent that can hold rights, incur debts, and suffer wrongs that are not reducible to the rights, debts, or harms of the members taken separately, while others insist that there is nothing present except the members arranged in a particular relational structure, and that talk of the group acting is merely a convenient fiction. Having encountered the special composition question, I want to understand its most important implications for an institutional arrangement like ours. Specifically, if unrestricted mereological composition is false—so that not every plurality of objects forms a genuine whole—what follows for the coherence of contracts signed in the cooperative's name and for the allocation of liability when one member's actions cause harm? Conversely, if composition is identity, or if special composition is restricted to living organisms or conscious beings, can the cooperative possess interests that are not identical to the aggregated interests of its members, and can it be wronged as such? Furthermore, if one denies that there is any composite object over and above the members, what becomes of the legitimacy of our voting procedures, which presuppose that the group can deliberate and act as one agent rather than as a mere aggregate? I am not seeking legal advice, but a rigorous philosophical analysis of how the debate over parts and wholes bears on institutional identity, collective agency, and the ontology of group obligations in cooperative structures of this kind.
