I am taking a graduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice, and my study group has developed a hybrid case that combines Hofstadter’s Platonia dilemma with the Scientific American Luring Lottery. We think it generates an argument that superrationality collapses whenever agents face a symmetric meta-choice about which coordination game to enter. Here are the steps.

(1) In the Platonia dilemma, twenty agents each apply an identical reasoning procedure and therefore each sends a telegram with probability 1/20.

(2) In the Luring Lottery, contestants apply the same transparency logic by rolling a simulated die with sides equal to the expected number of respondents.

(3) Both solutions depend on the principle that a superrational agent should treat her own decision function as a representative sample from a population of identically reasoning agents.

(4) Now consider what we are calling the Nested Luring Game: twenty superrational agents simultaneously choose whether to enter a Platonia-style round—in which exactly one message wins one billion dollars—or a Lottery-style round—in which entries dilute a fixed one-million-dollar pool. The rules are common knowledge, communication is forbidden, and every agent runs the same decision algorithm.

(5) Because the choice of round is symmetric and reasoned identically, superrationality requires that all twenty agents make the same selection: either all enter the Platonia round or all enter the Lottery round.

(6) If they all enter the Platonia round, they are back in case (1). If they all enter the Lottery round, each rolls a twenty-sided die and submits only on a roll of one; the expected number of entries is one, but the prize division makes the expected payoff far lower than in the Platonia round.

(7) Crucially, there is no internal superrational mechanism for breaking the tie between the two rounds, since whatever reasoning selects Platonia is mirrored exactly in every other agent, and the same holds for the Lottery.

(8) Therefore, superrationality either fails to determine the round selection—undermining its claim to solve symmetric coordination problems—or it arbitrarily fixes one round, in which case the agents have no rational basis for accepting that fixation rather than the other.

(9) If superrationality cannot resolve the Nested Luring Game, then its apparent success in the original Platonia dilemma and in the Luring Lottery was not due to genuine rational coordination but merely to an implicit prior constraint on the game space that was never itself rationally justified.

(10) Hence, superrationality is either incoherent or applicable only when the framing of the problem is externally fixed, which makes it useless for any real-world decision in which the framing is itself open to negotiation.

Tell me which premise or step is the weakest link. Explain why that step looked plausible at first—particularly given the transparency assumption in Hofstadter’s original setup—and then price the alternatives: what does a causal decision theorist have to concede, what does an evidential decision theorist have to concede, and what does a defender of Hofstadter-style superrationality have to concede, in order to reject that step?
