After a long and rather heated discussion with a friend over whether the Neolithic transition was genuinely a mistake, I have sketched the outline of a short essay that tries to move past the usual leisure-versus-labor framing. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of sources, but I would value careful comments on the conceptual architecture and on whether the argument holds together without collapsing into familiar narratives. Here are the notes as they stand.

Working title: "Subsistence Optionality and the Custodial Origin of Inequality."

The essay begins by accepting Marshall Sahlins's observation that many foraging societies achieved a kind of affluence measured not by accumulated goods but by limited wants and adequate leisure. Rather than repeating the well-known comparison of caloric intake and work hours, however, I want to foreground a different feature of pre-agricultural economies: what I am calling subsistence optionality. Hunter-gatherer groups typically maintained a broad portfolio of food sources—large and small game, diverse tubers and grains, aquatic resources, and seasonal foraging across varied ecotones—such that no single failure was catastrophic. This was not accidental abundance but a structured hedge: mobility and low storage costs allowed groups to shift emphasis rather than defend a fixed investment.

The agricultural transition, on this view, was not merely a move toward greater productivity but a radical narrowing of that portfolio. The domestication of wheat, rice, or maize tied communities to specific territories and to the success of one or a few high-yield species. Surplus followed, but I want to treat surplus as a symptom rather than a cause. The deeper transformation was the loss of ecological flexibility: once a community had cleared fields, built irrigation, and stored seed, it could no longer easily revert to broad-spectrum foraging without abandoning sunk costs and fixed infrastructure.

From this loss of optionality, I derive an argument about inequality that differs, I hope, from the standard "surplus permits hierarchy" account. When subsistence is diversified and mobile, risk is distributed geographically; when it is narrowed and territorial, risk becomes localized and must be actively managed. This creates what I call custodial vulnerability: the need for centralized storage, water control, defense of granaries, and coordination of planting schedules. The people who assumed these managerial roles did not initially seize surplus through coercion; rather, the system's reduced flexibility made their coordination structurally indispensable. Over generations, this functional indispensability hardened into hereditary status—not because elites extracted wealth from an otherwise egalitarian surplus, but because the elimination of alternative subsistence paths made the custodial class impossible to bypass.

The essay concludes by asking whether this framing changes the moral evaluation of the transition. If the mistake was not simply more work for less nutrition, but an irreversible trade of ecological optionality for institutional dependency, then the harm lies less in immediate suffering and more in the closure of alternative social forms.

I would particularly welcome comments on three points. First, is the "portfolio" or "optionality" analogy illuminating here, or does it anachronistically impose modern financial or ecological concepts onto subsistence practices that were organized by entirely different logics? Second, does the custodial-origin argument genuinely avoid the familiar surplus-to-inequality narrative, or is it merely redescribing the same causal chain in the language of risk management? Third, I anticipate the objection that agriculture actually increased human optionality by enabling trade, specialization, and stored reserves against famine—does this objection miss the point about ecological portfolio diversity, or does it show that my framing wrongly treats mobility as the only form of flexibility?

Please respond with direct, substantive criticism rather than general encouragement; I am especially interested in where the reasoning feels weakest or where it silently relies on assumptions I have not defended.
