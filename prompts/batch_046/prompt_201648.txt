I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I am preparing a 90-minute seminar session for eight graduate students on gradated moral status. Rather than rehearsing standard binary debates about whether insects or thermostats are fully in or out, I want the group to experience what I will call the interpolation problem: the difficulty of maintaining a genuinely continuous scale of moral weight when practical decision-making forces discrete thresholds. The domain is specifically sentience as a matter of degree, running from minimal reflex loops up through complex biological and synthetic architectures, but I want to avoid letting participants retreat to an intuitive on-off switch.

Please design a structured classroom activity—something like a calibrated deliberation or role-play with explicit rules and small symbolic stakes—that makes participants feel the pull of treating moral status as continuous without allowing them to resolve ambiguity by defaulting to binary classification. The setting is a seminar table with eight participants; no technology beyond paper, markers, and printed cards is available. The activity should present entities that occupy deliberately ambiguous positions: for instance, a simple thermal-feedback loop with predictive error correction, a nematode with enhanced sensory integration, a human subject with profoundly diminished but non-absent feedback capacity, and a synthetic distributed system with partial but non-conscious processing. At the outset, participants must collectively generate three observable behavioral-functional criteria and agree on a comparative weighting scheme. The rules should then present paired dilemmas—cases that differ by only a marginal increment on those agreed criteria but that demand a binary choice, such as permission versus prohibition of a standardized, symbolically represented harm.

Include the following: (1) a precise timeline and rule-set for the full 90 minutes, including how roles or turns are assigned; (2) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity back onto the conceptual distinction between continuous gradation and discrete moral thresholding, showing exactly where the continuous scale was forced into a binary decision; (3) an honest assessment of which feature of gradated moral status this exercise cannot reproduce—perhaps the phenomenological opacity of intermediate states, or the problem of aggregating incommensurable capacities into a single weight—and explicit instructions for how I should supply that missing element verbally after the activity concludes; (4) a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it without undermining the exercise.

If this request rests on a confused premise—for example, if forcing discrete choices onto a continuous scale necessarily smuggles in the very binary framework it is meant to interrogate, or if the task as posed cannot be executed without implicitly treating moral status as quantifiable in a way that begs the central question—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. Design the activity only if it is genuinely viable as posed.
