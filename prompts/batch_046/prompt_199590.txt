I'm reporting a long-form feature on the revival of Marx's philosophical anthropology in contemporary doctoral education, and during my observation of a qualifying exam in political philosophy at a research university, the examiners circulated the following written prompt to the candidate. The task was designed to test whether students could navigate the tension between Marx's early phenomenological descriptions and later structural analyses: In the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, Marx distinguishes four dimensions of alienation—separation from the product of labor, from the labor process itself, from species-being, and from other people. A contemporary interpretive current influenced by the value-form tradition contends that all four dimensions are ultimately grounded not in private property as such, but in the social domination of abstract labor, which subsumes concrete human capacities under a homogeneous measure of exchange-value. Assess this claim with precision: does the reduction to abstract labor illuminate every dimension of alienation equally, or does it flatten the normative and experiential specificity of some? Identify which dimension is most vulnerable to this economic reduction and which resists it most strongly, and address whether Marx's mature framework in Capital supports or undermines this reading.

The candidate's transcribed response runs as follows. The student correctly enumerates the four dimensions and accurately describes the value-form argument that concrete labor is subsumed under abstract labor through commodity exchange. The student then asserts that this mechanism explains alienation from the product and from the labor process, since both become governed by value rather than by conscious purpose. Turning to species-being, the student argues that because Marx defines human essence as free, conscious productive activity, the coercion of abstract labor directly negates it, yet concedes that this dimension retains a normative residue that pure economic analysis cannot fully capture. The student further claims that alienation from other people is best understood not as interpersonal competition but as commodity fetishism—the appearance of social relations between people as relations between things—which the student treats as the mature social form of the fourth dimension. Notably, the student maintains that the 1844 Manuscripts already operate with the concrete-versus-abstract labor distinction, even if Marx's terminology differs, and concludes that because Marx abandons the explicit language of species-being in Capital, the value-form interpretation is essentially correct for the mature theory, with alienation from species-being remaining the least reducible case despite its eventual disappearance from the text.
