Design a 75-minute workshop activity for 18 upper-division philosophy students who have encountered basic utilitarian reasoning but have not yet confronted the aggregation problem in its extreme form. The participants should play a structured token-allocation game using only paper slips, sealed envelopes, and a non-monetary point system. Over three rounds, each student must choose between two collective options: distributing one unit of imperceptible discomfort across many participants, or concentrating an equivalent or larger harm onto a single randomly designated recipient. The central mechanic should be an opaque aggregation rule—unknown to the group during play—drawn from three possible conversion functions: linear summation, a sharp discontinuity at a hidden threshold, and a non-linear jump. Only after all choices are locked should the envelope be opened to reveal which function was in effect and who, if anyone, receives the macro-harm. Provide explicit rules for turn order, secrecy of individual choices, and the revelation protocol; then outline a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical tension between total and average utilitarianism and onto the torture-versus-dust-specks intuition, showing where the hidden-rule shock parallels or fails to parallel the epistemic position of an aggregator. Include an honest prediction of what will predictably fail in the first run—such as students anchoring on visible token counts and ignoring the hidden conversion—and how to correct for it. Finally, state clearly which structural feature of the aggregation dilemma—specifically the cross-personal non-identity of harms or the practical inaccessibility of marginal causal contributions—the simulation cannot reproduce, and identify what the facilitator must therefore supply in the concluding discussion.
