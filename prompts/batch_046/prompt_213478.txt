I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or classical historiography, and I have been trying to formalize—mostly for my own intuition—how we justify knowledge claims about events long before living memory. Drawing on what I remember of source criticism from Herodotus through Thucydides to modern practice, I keep returning to a cluster of judgments that I want to test for coherence.

Here are my verdicts. One: I take it that Rome existed as an imperial capital around 100 CE, because archaeological stratigraphy, numismatic distributions, and administratively independent record traditions all converge without obvious coordination. Two: I do not think we know Rome's exact population at that date, since census fragments are partial, mutually inconsistent, and require speculative extrapolation. Three: I reject an alleged secret treaty in 200 BCE reported by only a single partisan chronicler, absent any material corroboration or cross-cultural attestation. Four: I accept that the Library of Alexandria suffered catastrophic loss despite sharply conflicting accounts of when and by whose hand, because Greek, Latin, and later Arabic traditions attest the event itself without sharing a common textual ancestor. Five: I treat Suetonius's report that Caligula appointed his horse Incitatus to the consulship as likely apocryphal, not because it is implausible on its face, but because it survives only in late, adversarial biographical traditions and leaves no trace in contemporary inscriptions or documentary archives.

The case that keeps dividing me is this: suppose a probabilistic language model reconstructs a lacuna in a manuscript of Tacitus's Annals, producing a sentence that is syntactically consistent with Tacitus's period Latin, thematically continuous with the surrounding narrative, and not contradicted by any other source. Does that reconstruction belong to the body of knowledge we attribute to Tacitus, or does the algorithmic mediation break the chain of transmission in a way that makes the claim epistemically different from the other four? More broadly, is there a single principle—perhaps something about adversarial independence, multi-source convergence, or the absence of a single point of failure—that captures my first five judgments? If so, what does it predict for the reconstructed fragment? If not, which of the five should I overturn to preserve coherence, and what further case—real or hypothetical—would best distinguish the competing principles?
