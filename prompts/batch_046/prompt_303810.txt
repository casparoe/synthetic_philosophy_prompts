Here are my judgments about hard choices involving incommensurable values, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, or which ones I must abandon.

Case A: A physician can extend a dying patient’s life with a drug that will permanently erase her episodic memories of her deceased spouse, or withhold it and let her die with those memories intact. I judge this genuinely tragic: neither option dominates, and whatever she chooses she fails fully to honor one value. The values—life and memory-integrity—seem horizontally incommensurable, sharing a normative register but lacking any common metric.

Case B: A university facing collapse must eliminate either its philosophy department or its athletics program. I think this is hard but not genuinely tragic; it is resolvable by asking which activity is more central to the institution’s constitutive mission. Even though intellectual inquiry and physical excellence are incommensurable, they do not occupy the same normative plane relative to institutional identity, so comparison is not a category mistake, merely a difficult weighing.

Case C: A parent must relocate for a child’s life-saving treatment or stay to care for a parent with advanced dementia who will not understand the absence. I feel this is tragic, but in a different register from Case A. Here the duties—parental and filial—belong to incompatible moral ontologies (contractual versus covenantal), so comparison feels vertically incommensurable: a category error that is unavoidable.

Case D: A museum must sell one minor work to fund climate-adaptive storage that saves the rest of its collection. The values—preserving a singular object versus preserving the collective archive—seem commensurable through aggregation, yet I still feel a residue of wrongness in the sale that aggregation does not dissolve. So it is not tragic, but not fully resolved either.

Case E: A government must enforce strict carbon limits that will collapse a coal-dependent regional economy, or relax them and accept ecological harm. I think this is merely difficult, not tragic, because both values—regional economic survival and ecological stability—are ultimately instrumental to human flourishing and can be compared through a threshold of basic needs, even if no precise metric exists.

My tentative principle is that a choice is genuinely tragic only when the competing values are both non-instrumental and inhabit incompatible normative registers—horizontally when they share a register but lack a metric, vertically when they belong to different registers altogether. But Case D confuses me: preservation of the singular and preservation of the collective both seem to belong to the same curatorial register, yet the residue persists. And Case B seems to resolve too easily: why should institutional mission dissolve incommensurability rather than merely supply an arbitrary tie-breaker?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving register-integrity, threshold non-substitutability, or narrative coherence of an agent’s value-commitments—that captures A, C, D, and E without reducing B to a false resolution? Or must I drop one of these verdicts? Apply your best principle also to this unconsidered case: a musician must choose between continuing to practice an instrument that causes chronic but non-debilitating pain—permanently altering her tactile relationship to the music—and quitting to preserve bodily health. The values at stake are artistic integrity and embodied autonomy. What does your principle predict about whether this choice is tragic, merely difficult, or resolvable?
