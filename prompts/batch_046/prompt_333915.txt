I'm preparing a session on populist rhetoric and the metaphysics of collective agency, and I've noticed that defenders of populist claims often rely on an analogy that treats "the people" as if they were a single individual speaker—someone whose authentic voice has been muffled, distorted, or spoken over by elites, intermediaries, and institutional noise. In this picture, an election or a rally is like a person finally being heard after being interrupted: the message may be simple, but it expresses one underlying will, and any internal disagreement among the people is analogous to a person's momentary confusion or false consciousness rather than to a genuine conflict of interests. The analogy is doing heavy lifting: it licenses the idea that there is a correct interpretation of "what the people want," that dissent within the group is noise to be filtered out rather than legitimate pluralism, and that democratic institutions should function like a clear channel for a pre-existing message rather than like a forum for constructing agreement among divergent parties.

I'd like you to audit this analogy thoroughly. Lay out the explicit correspondences—what plays the role of the speaker, the voice, the interruption, the message, and the false consciousness—then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, examine whether the analogy smuggles in a metaphysics of collective personhood that collective decision-making doesn't actually provide: does democracy deliver something like an individual will, or does it only deliver an aggregation of individual wills, and does the distinction matter for the populist conclusion? You might draw on the philosophical distinction between we-mode collective intentionality—where a group genuinely acts as one subject—and I-mode aggregation, where individuals happen to align but remain separate agents, to see whether the speaker-analogy requires the former while democratic procedures only guarantee the latter. Also consider the disanalogy around internal conflict: in an individual, contradictory desires are usually resolved within one psychology, whereas in a collective they may be structurally irreducible. Does the analogy's treatment of dissent as "noise" survive once that difference is clear? Finally, if the analogy fails, is there a better one—perhaps drawn from music, translation, or joint action—that captures how a collective can express something without being reducible to a single speaker?
