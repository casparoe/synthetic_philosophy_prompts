As a philosophy professor serving as the senior referee for an interdisciplinary quarterly—receiving submissions from cognitive science, jurisprudence, political theory, and artificial intelligence—I need a reusable decision procedure that a non-mathematician referee can complete in thirty minutes. The problem: authors constantly invoke Kurt Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems to establish substantive conclusions outside mathematics, often conflating formal properties of axiom systems with features of minds, societies, or machines. I want you to translate the actual logical structure of Gödel's results—effective axiomatizability, sufficient arithmetic strength, consistency, and the syntax-semantics distinction—into an ordered, practitioner-level checklist. Specifically, provide: (1) the diagnostic questions to ask of a submission, in sequence; (2) what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (3) the precise termination point at which the procedure issues a definitive negative verdict (category error or technical misapplication); and (4) the residual judgment calls, clearly flagged as such, that formal logic cannot eliminate and that require the referee's philosophical discretion. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical submissions. First, a cognitive-science paper arguing that because human belief-fixation is Gödelian-incomplete, no complete formal model of it can exist. Second, a legal-theory paper claiming that Gödel's second theorem proves any consistent legal code necessarily contains valid but unprovable obligations, thereby making judicial discretion logically unavoidable. Third, a philosophy-of-technology essay asserting that large language models, as formal symbol-manipulation architectures, are necessarily incapable of recognizing certain true arithmetic statements and therefore possess an intrinsic cognitive ceiling that separates them from human mathematicians. For one of these applications—choose the one where the logic is most subtly misapplied—the procedure should yield a verdict that the author will reject but that the checklist demands. Conclude by stating exactly what this procedure cannot adjudicate, particularly whether non-mathematical domains can coherently be modeled as Gödelian formal systems, and explain why that limitation is structural rather than merely practical.
