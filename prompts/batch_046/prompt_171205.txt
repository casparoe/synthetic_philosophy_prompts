Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps on Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and causation. The first party defends the hard occasionalist reading: Al-Ghazālī holds that God is the sole, direct cause of every particular event, that natural objects possess no causal powers whatsoever, and that references to "fire burning cotton" are merely descriptions of customary conjunction, not attributions of efficacy. The second party defends the customary-order compatibilist reading: Al-Ghazālī denies only the philosophers' claim to necessary causal connection, but preserves a real, contingent, and knowable order of secondary causation that God consistently sustains through a stable habit (al-ʿāda), allowing natural science and medicine to retain genuine explanatory and predictive force.

Produce a formal document both sides could sign, divided into the following sections in this exact order:

1. Agreed commitments (approximately 200 words): State the claims both interpretations accept about Al-Ghazālī's explicit arguments in Tahāfut al-falāsifa and related texts, about the formal distinction between necessary and contingent connection, and about the theological motivation for denying causal necessity. Include at least one agreed claim about whether occasionalism, on either reading, can preserve counterfactual predictive reliability in medicine and astronomy.

2. Precise disagreements (table format, approximately 250 words): Use two columns—"Hard occasionalist interpretation" and "Customary-order compatibilist interpretation." For each row, first state the disagreement in wording both sides accept as neutral. Then provide each side's own formulation of its position in wording that side would accept without alteration. Cover: (a) whether Al-Ghazālī eliminates creaturely causal powers entirely or only eliminates metaphysical necessity; (b) whether the divine "custom" functions as an explanatory principle that grounds inductive inference or as a purely descriptive regularity with no explanatory weight; (c) whether occasionalism permits a physician to justify a treatment by citing causal generalizations or requires justification solely by reference to divine will.

3. Cruxes (approximately 200 words): Identify the three sub-questions such that resolving any one would move at least one party. These should include: one about whether Al-Ghazālī's denial that "fire burns cotton" is a metaphysical claim about causal impotence or a theological claim about omnipotence; one about whether occasionalism can coherently accommodate counterfactual stability—specifically, whether it makes sense to say that if God had not willed the burning, the presence of fire would not have produced burning; and one about whether a miracle that violates the customary order undermines all inductive knowledge or merely marks an exceptional suspension of a still-valid general rule.

4. Bearing cases and predictive commitments (approximately 300 words): For each crux, present a concrete conceptual case, hypothetical scenario, or argument drawn from Al-Ghazālī's discussion of causation, medicine, or miracles that both sides agree is genuinely relevant. Then record, in advance and without hedging, what each interpretation predicts about that case—e.g., whether Al-Ghazālī would allow a physician to rely on causal generalizations for treatment, or what each view implies about the metaphysical status of an event in which fire is present but cotton remains unburned by direct divine will. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to texts outside the shared conceptual framework. Write in formal, academic prose suitable for philosophers of religion and historians of Islamic thought.
