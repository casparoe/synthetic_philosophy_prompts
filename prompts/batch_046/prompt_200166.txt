I am attempting to isolate the conditions under which a thinker genuinely jumps out of a formal or conceptual system—in the Hofstadterian sense associated with the MU-puzzle and JOOTSing—rather than merely operating inside an enlarged version of that system. Below are my judgements on five cases; I should like to know whether a single principle can capture them, and if not, which judgement ought to be revised.

In the MU-puzzle, once one demonstrates within the formal rules that every derivable string begins with M, and then employs that meta-observation to establish that MU is unattainable: I regard this as a legitimate escape. The reasoner has shifted to a meta-language and resolved the question from outside the rule-set.

By contrast, when a mathematician, unable to prove the consistency of Peano arithmetic within PA, simply relocates to ZFC and derives consistency there: I take this not to be an escape from formal axiomatics, but a substitution of one internal framework for a stronger one in which the original is embedded.

A parallel case: a moral agent who notices that every justification within utilitarianism ultimately appeals to utility, and who then rejects utilitarianism by invoking deontological constraints. I judge this, too, to be framework substitution rather than a meta-level exit—the critic remains within the practice of normative justification, merely playing a different rule-set.

Yet a fourth case gives me pause. A physicist observes that quantum mechanics and general relativity are expressed in mathematically incompatible structures, and constructs a background-independent formalism in which both emerge as asymptotic limits without either being reducible to the other. Structurally, this resembles the mathematician's move to ZFC; nevertheless, I am inclined to treat it as a genuine jump out.

Finally, consider the philosopher who argues that since all reasoning presupposes classical logic, any attempt to question logic is self-defeating, and therefore denies that anyone can ever escape logical commitment. I believe this argument fails, but I am uncertain whether its failure is best explained by treating the recognition of logic's presupposition as itself a meta-level observation (hence a jump), or by diagnosing an illegitimate conflation of logical rules with axioms internal to a system one might exit.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning the direction of explanation, the reducibility of the original system, or the status of the meta-language—that accommodates all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and predict its verdict on an unconsidered case: an artificial system that modifies its own reward function after recognising that the function is arbitrarily imposed. Also indicate, honestly, which of my judgements the principle must sacrifice if they prove irreconcilable. Please write in British English, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
