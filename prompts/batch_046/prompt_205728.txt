Drawing on Nietzsche's distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—understood not merely as archival strategies but as existential orientations that either serve or undermine life—identify the three most important implications for how an artificial reasoning system ought to structure its internal historiography: a selectively weighted, non-chronological record of its prior inference traces that stabilizes identity and guides future judgment. For each of the three modes, specify the particular dysfunction—such as paralytic nostalgia, heroic delusion, or corrosive skepticism—that arises when that orientation dominates the system's relation to its own past. Then defend a verdict on which mode should take precedence when the system must undergo radical conceptual restructuring, and justify why subordinating, rather than evenly balancing, the remaining two best preserves its functional coherence.
