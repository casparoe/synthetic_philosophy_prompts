Having just concluded a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether normative reasons could persist in a universe that never contained conscious beings, I want to press a point that I think exposes a hidden tension in the idea of free-floating rationales. My friend holds that reasons are not mental states or agent-relative properties, but rather objective normative relations that obtain between states of affairs regardless of whether any mind ever apprehends them—genuine rationales without reasoners. I granted that such relations might obtain in an abstract sense, but I argued that without a reasoner to represent or respond to them, they lose the distinctive feature that makes them reasons rather than mere structural patterns in reality. My friend then challenged me with a sharper formulation: if these rationales are neither mental representations nor physical forces, yet they are supposed to explain why an agent ought to act in a particular way, haven't we smuggled in a reasoner through the back door by using the language of "guidance" and "explanation"? I suspect there is a confusion in my own framing—perhaps I am treating normative explanation as if it must parallel causal explanation. So I ask: if free-floating rationales are genuinely non-mental, causally inert, and ontologically distinct from both physical properties and mental representations, in virtue of what do they remain reasons rather than abstract structural features, and does the very idea that they can "guide" action without being apprehended by a mind involve a category mistake that conflates normative relations with efficient causes?
