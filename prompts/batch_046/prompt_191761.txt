After a long, unresolved argument with a friend about the Holocaust's comparability to other mass atrocities and about what modern bureaucratic rationality actually explains, I keep returning to a one-liner he used to close down both lines of discussion: "Auschwitz wasn't modernity's failure—it was modernity working as designed." I want to understand exactly what work that sentence could be doing, because it seems to slide between very different claims. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express—separating, for instance, (i) causal claims about whether bureaucratic rationalization and administrative division of labor were necessary conditions for the scale of killing; (ii) structural claims about whether modern welfare-state or industrial administration is inherently continuous with the machinery of genocide; (iii) moral-uniqueness claims about whether recognizing modern causation undermines the singular ethical status of the event; (iv) replicability claims about whether the social-technical conditions could recur in other modern societies; and (v) normative claims about whether modernity as a project is thereby indicted rather than exonerated? For each reading, indicate whether it is essentially a truism, a substantive and defensible position, or false; say which readings genuinely support blocking comparisons to colonial or other state violence, and which genuinely bear on debates like the Historikerstreit about uniqueness versus comparability. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—just a conceptual menu I can bring back to the conversation, including an honest note on which feature of the underlying view (for example, a Bauman-style thesis about instrumental reason) the slogan is least able to capture on its own.
