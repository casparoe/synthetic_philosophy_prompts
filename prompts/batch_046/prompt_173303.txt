In our graduate seminar on historical explanation and moral psychology we have spent several sessions on the contrast between Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen’s broader thesis about ordinary Germans, yet the discussion has settled into a frustrating equilibrium. Every participant accepts the basic descriptive record: the unit was made up largely of middle-aged, working-class reservists without prior SS membership; a clear majority took part in direct killings despite being explicitly permitted to step aside; and the wider culture contained pervasive, if heterogeneous, anti-Jewish rhetoric. Nevertheless, one faction maintains that peer dynamics, bureaucratic fragmentation, and career anxiety provide a sufficient framework for understanding participation without invoking deep ideological conviction, while the other counters that situational factors explain only compliance, not the documented initiative, lack of psychological collapse, and occasional enthusiasm that Goldhagen highlights, and that treating anti-Semitism as a diffuse cultural background misses the specific eliminationist orientation that made violence meaningful to the actors.

What kind of disagreement is this, precisely? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about the relative causal weight of conformity versus conviction—something that could in principle be settled by finer-grained evidence about internal communications or post-war testimony patterns—or is the deadlock conceptual, turning on competing standards for what counts as an adequate explanation in collective violence? I have begun to think that part of the stall derives from an unstated normative divergence about explanatory ownership: whether rendering an agent’s behavior intelligible necessarily diminishes moral culpability, so that situational explanations register as exculpation to one camp and as scientific neutrality to the other. Might the two sides in fact be pursuing different questions altogether, one asking what conditions made participation predictable and the other asking what reasons the perpetrators could have offered for their actions?

If your diagnosis is that this remains a substantive empirical disagreement in disguise, what specific comparative evidence—perhaps regional variation in pre-war socialization, church attendance, or patterns of refusal—would genuinely adjudicate the competing accounts? If instead it is a normative or methodological deadlock about the relation between explanation and blame, could a conceptual distinction—say, between predictive explanation and reasons-responsive understanding—disentangle the two projects without forcing either side to abandon its core commitment? And if the impasse is ultimately bedrock, anchored in incompatible intuitions about human agency under authoritarian pressure, is there any further case study or conceptual reframing that might shift either position, or should we treat this as an irreducible pluralism about the aims of historical psychology?
