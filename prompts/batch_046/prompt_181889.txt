After arguing late into the night with a friend about whether a legal system that can amend its own amendment rules must eventually collapse into contradiction, I want to set an exam for our upper-level philosophy-of-law seminar. Write a single 90-minute essay question that forces students past a summary of Suber’s Nomic and Ross’s paradox of constitutional self-amendment. Introduce a specific variant: Rule 501 permits changing the amendment procedure only if endorsed by a simple majority of all rules currently in force, with each rule counting as one vote regardless of hierarchical rank. A player then proposes amending Rule 501 to require unanimous rule-endorsement instead. Ask the students to identify the precise logical structure of the self-reference problem that arises here, to distinguish a genuine paradox from a mere procedural deadlock, to evaluate whether equal weighting of rules blocks Ross’s problem or reproduces it at the level of collective rule-agency, and to assess whether framing the rules as non-intentional artifacts resolves the paradox or only relocates it into the meta-rules governing rule-identity. Provide a model answer that makes these four moves clearly, and supply a marking scheme totaling 20 marks: assign specific marks to each of the four moves, specify the exact misconception that should lose marks for each (for example, treating the rules’ votes as intentional acts, or conflating game-theoretic dynamics with legal ontology), and include a one-line note on the most common failure mode for each. The answer should be written in American English.
