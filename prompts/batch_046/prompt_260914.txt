Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the KK principle and the luminosity of knowledge.

Camp One—designated the Gradient Anti-Luminists—accepts the standard anti-luminosity argument: if knowing p required being in a position to know that one knows p, then a sorites-style sequence of imperceptible evidential degradations would force a subject to retain knowledge across a sharp boundary where knowledge is actually lost, which is incoherent; therefore knowledge is not luminous. They reject, however, the inference that knowledge is uniformly opaque to the subject. Instead, they hold that epistemic status comes in degrees: there is a high-confidence core region where a subject can reliably track her own knowledge, and a boundary zone where tracking fails. For them, the KK principle fails globally but survives as a statistical or probabilistic regularity within the core region, and its failure elsewhere reflects genuine gradation rather than a uniform cognitive blindness.

Camp Two—designated the Robust-Core Defenders—agrees that the argument defeats an unrestricted, binary luminosity claim. But they deny that this undermines the KK principle for robust knowledge states: cases where the subject's cognitive access is stable, the evidential base is thick, and the possibility of error is not merely remote but structurally excluded from the subject's first-person perspective. They contend that the anti-luminosity argument depends on constructing an artificial sequence of imperceptible degradations that does not reflect actual epistemic practice. For them, the KK principle holds exactly where it should—within the robust core—and its failure at artificially constructed boundaries is not a philosophical defeat but a feature of how cognitive access operates.

The deliverable should be a document both sides could sign, structured as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about the formal structure of the dispute. Include: what the unrestricted luminosity condition requires; what assumptions the sorites sequence makes about the continuity of evidential quality; what the unrestricted KK principle asserts; and which no-go results or conceptual constraints both camps accept (for example, that a binary, globally luminous knowledge condition is untenable).

2. A disagreements table with two columns—Gradient Anti-Luminists and Robust-Core Defenders—where each side states its own view in wording it accepts. The table should address: (a) whether the failure of luminosity is global or regional; (b) whether the KK principle should be rejected entirely, restricted to a core domain, or reconceived as a graded or probabilistic principle; (c) whether the sorites sequence describes a genuine epistemic possibility or an artificial construction that misrepresents cognitive access; and (d) whether notions like "robust core" or "high-confidence region" can be made precise without circular reliance on the very knowledge they are meant to delimit.

3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, record in advance what each side would predict or concede. Suggested cruxes include: whether there are empirically documented or conceptually coherent cases of boundary knowledge in which a subject knows p but systematically fails to know that she knows p; whether a formal model of graded belief or confidence can reproduce the intuitive data about self-knowledge without invoking any form of luminosity; and whether the concept of "structural exclusion of error" can be defined independently of the knowledge state it is meant to explain.

4. Where the disagreement is partly verbal, specify exactly which part—for instance, whether "knowledge" in the KK principle should be read as binary, graded, or indexed to an epistemic context—and explain why resolving that terminological choice does not by itself settle the substantive dispute about the nature and limits of cognitive access.

5. A brief joint assessment concluding the document: is there any argument, case study, empirical finding, or formal result that both sides agree would resolve the residual disagreement, or has the dispute stabilized into a disagreement about how to weigh the cost of abandoning an unrestricted KK principle against the cost of accepting artificial boundary cases as philosophically significant?

Write in American English, in a formal and polished register. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from specific texts unless verified by a fetched source; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure the prompt does not depend on specific work titles, publication dates, or obscure attributions.
