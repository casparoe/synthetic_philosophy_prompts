Here is the student's answer:

Parfit asks us to imagine being stranded in a desert where a driver will only give us a ride if we promise to send $100 once we reach town. After we're dropped off, sending the money can't causally affect whether the ride happened, so at that point it's irrational to pay. Still, it's rational to have made the promise and to be disposed to pay, because otherwise the driver wouldn't have stopped. This is rational irrationality: it's rational to cause ourselves to have dispositions that lead us to act irrationally in particular cases. Parfit mainly introduces this to defend causal decision theory against evidential decision theory—since the causal chain is broken, evidential reasoning about our "type" is just a mistake here. The real solution is an external commitment device, like wiring the payment automatically, because without that the rational choice in the moment always wins. So rational irrationality is best understood as a failure of time-consistency that we fix with precommitment contracts. And if this were a repeated game, the reasoning wouldn't change, since your credibility still depends on being the type who pays.
