My friend and I argued late into the evening about coherent extrapolated volition, and she walked me through a chain that seems to show idealized-preference theories undermine themselves. I want to know where it actually breaks. Here is her argument, reconstructed with her numbering:

(1) On the CEV model, normative authority belongs to P*, the set of preferences we would hold if we knew all relevant facts, reasoned with perfect coherence, and had resolved every internal conflict; our actual present set P carries authority only insofar as it approximates P*.

(2) If a present preference in P conflicts with the corresponding preference in P*, then the present preference has no normative weight; otherwise ignorance and incoherence would themselves be reasons.

(3) Many agents hold a second-order preference for authenticity indexed to present psychological continuity: they want future choices to be genuinely theirs, where "theirs" is anchored to the actual non-idealized agent, not to a hypothetical successor constructed by the idealization procedure.

(4) This second-order preference belongs to P. Under idealization it is either eliminated—because the idealized agent values continuity with itself, not with the pre-idealized self—or transformed into an unrecognizable analog, so that preserving it in P* no longer protects the original commitment.

(5) Therefore CEV either overrides our present commitment to authenticity or renders that commitment circular: we can only "save" it by redefining the self to match the idealized agent, which is precisely what the authenticity preference was meant to resist.

(6) The same structure applies to collective agents. Democratic legitimacy is ordinarily grounded in actual consent, but if we apply CEV collectively, present dissent can always be dismissed as the product of non-idealized ignorance or factional incoherence, leaving only hypothetical consent with normative weight.

(7) So CEV proves too much: it licenses the dismissal of every actual objection—individual or collective—by appealing to preferences that belong to agents who are not, in any robust sense, us.

She takes this to show that idealized-preference theories are self-undermining: they invoke "our" authority to erase the conditions under which "we" can object. I feel there is a hidden slide between (4) and (5), or perhaps an unjustified analogy in (6), but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid and the damage must be absorbed by abandoning one of the premises, I want to know which premise is least costly to give up, and whether doing so saves the normative significance of present-tense identity. Finally, if my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if I am conflating conditions of personal identity with conditions of preference continuity, or if I am illicitly transferring an individual-level idealization procedure to collective deliberation—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the confusion.
