I have been trying to apply anekāntavāda reflexively to logical laws rather than only to empirical descriptions, and the result looks like a collapse into dialetheism. I want to know exactly where the reasoning slips.

(1) Anekāntavāda holds that any substance possesses infinite attributes (ananta-dharma), so every judgment expresses only one aspect qualified by substance, place, time, and mode (the syādvāda / saptabhaṅgī framework).

(2) Therefore no predication is ever true simpliciter: “S is P” is always “S is P in respect R, at time t, in place s, in mode m,” and false in other respects.

(3) This applies reflexively to judgments about logic itself: the claim “no contradiction exists in reality” is a predication about reality and must carry the same conditions.

(4) So there must be some conditions R′, t′, s′, m′ under which “no contradiction exists in reality” is false — that is, conditions under which that denial does not hold.

(5) If the denial fails under those conditions, then under those conditions both a proposition and its direct negation are true of the same object in the same respect.

(6) But that is precisely dialetheism: the view that some contradictions are genuinely true, not merely apparently true from different standpoints.

(7) Hence anekāntavāda entails that real contradictions occur in reality under specific conditions.

(8) Yet Jain philosophy explicitly distinguishes syādvāda, in which contradiction arises only from illegitimately mixing standpoints (naya-abhasa), from any acceptance of true contradictions; contradictions are never real in a single, properly qualified aspect.

(9) So the argument seems to show that anekāntavāda cannot maintain its own boundary against dialetheism, because its reflexive application to logical laws forces it to locate genuine contradictions in specific conditions.

The conclusion feels wrong — I do not think Jain metaphysics is just dialetheism in disguise. If I have to choose, I would keep the reflexive application of qualified truth to meta-level claims (steps 1–3) and the insistence that syādvāda is not dialetheism (step 8). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation between a claim being false-in-a-respect and its negation being true-in-that-same-respect, or is the error elsewhere — perhaps in how the conditions of qualification are applied to negations of logical laws?
