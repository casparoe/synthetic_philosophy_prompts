After a long argument last night with a friend about whether culture can be a genuine cause or only an interpretive gloss on material forces, I keep circling back to the specific chain he used to attack Weber’s Protestant-ethic thesis and, by extension, any sociology that explains with values. He laid it out as follows. (1) For a cultural value to explain a macro-historical transformation—say, the rise of modern capitalism—it must be a difference-maker: holding material conditions fixed, had the value been absent or different, the outcome would have been absent or different. (2) Weber’s own account treats values as “switchmen” that direct pre-existing interests onto one track rather than another, which implies they only operate where material conditions have already made several tracks available. (3) Therefore, on Weber’s model, values never initiate economic change; they only select among variants that material conditions have already made possible. (4) But selection among pre-allowed variants is not difference-making with respect to the occurrence of the transformation itself—only with respect to its specific form—so values fail the counterfactual test for being causes of capitalism. (5) If that is right, then explaining capitalism by Protestant values is like explaining which train arrives by pointing to the switch position, while the tracks, engine, and timetable determine everything that could possibly arrive. (6) By parity of reasoning, every macro-historical explanation that invokes beliefs, norms, or meanings faces the same overdetermination problem: values are always switchmen, never engines. (7) So either we admit that cultural explanation is never genuinely causal—always reducible to material conditions plus an arbitrary selection—or we must allow that values can sometimes be sufficient conditions, in which case Weber’s thesis and every analogous thesis becomes unfalsifiable, since any absence of the predicted outcome can be explained away by invoking hidden countervailing values. (8) Hence the very attempt to treat values as causes in historical sociology either collapses into materialist reductionism or abandons scientific testability.

My friend insists this is not a reductio but the correct diagnosis: Weber was doing interpretive sociology, not causal science, and we misread him if we think he was competing with Marx. I think he has proved too much—if his reasoning holds, it does not merely limit Weber but undermines any discipline that explains behavior by reference to meanings. Still, I cannot locate the exact slip. Is it in (3), where he infers that being a switchman means being causally inert? Or in (4), where he assumes that modulation is not causal contribution? Or is the real problem earlier, in (1), with the demand that cultural causes must do work not already done by material conditions? If I must preserve something, I would rather keep the idea that values can contribute causally within a multicausal framework—without being sufficient—than accept either horn of (7). But I want a precise diagnosis: which step is least defensible, what giving it up costs for the materialist critique, and whether that middle position is actually coherent or just a way of hiding in the ambiguity of (2).
