I have recently been reconciling the papers of a nineteenth-century translator who published major works under two separate pseudonyms, and the practical task of deciding whether “the author of the 1847 essay is the author of the 1862 treatise” conveys something genuinely informative—despite being true—has pulled me back into Frege’s puzzle in a way that pure classroom exposure never did. The philosophical literature on identity statements, however, seems to have reached a peculiar standstill. One camp treats the puzzle as essentially about cognitive significance or modes of presentation, insisting that any adequate account must explain why learning that a = b can rationally change a subject’s beliefs; the other treats it as a problem about propositional structure that dissolves once we cleanly distinguish sense from reference, perhaps aided by direct-reference theories, structured propositions, or two-dimensional frameworks. For decades both sides have refined their machinery—invoking belief-fixation mechanisms, compositional semantics, or pragmatic explanations of informativeness—without either conceding ground or producing the kind of result that makes the other side look obsolete. I am not asking for another survey of these positions. Instead, I want a diagnosis: is this stalemate substantive, verbal, or a case of two research programs answering different questions—one semantic, one psychological—under the single heading of “Frege’s puzzle”? Is there a hidden empirical issue about how human subjects actually revise beliefs upon learning co-reference, or are the sides divided by bedrock intuitions about what counts as a philosophical explanation? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a precise conceptual distinction, an experimental finding about belief revision, a formal result showing the limits of compositional semantics, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned? Please engage concretely with what proponents of each approach actually claim, and be honest about whether anything could genuinely move the debate.
