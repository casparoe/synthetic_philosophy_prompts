As a journalist writing on the intersection of political philosophy and institutional design, I am preparing a long-form feature on whether Robert Nozick's entitlement theory—especially as dramatized in the Wilt Chamberlain argument—can survive translation into granular, automated exchange systems. The classic argument holds that any distribution produced by just initial holdings and fully voluntary transfers is just, regardless of how unequal the result; the familiar objections concern unjust starting conditions or coerced consent. Rather than rehashing those, I want to test the intuition against a hypothetical mechanism I am calling a "recursive entitlement protocol": in this system, every transaction in a digital marketplace automatically diverts a small fraction of the payment into a pooled basic-income fund, but only after the buyer has explicitly opted into the protocol through a separate, revocable contract. The remainder goes to the seller—imagine a star athlete or creator in the position of Wilt Chamberlain—while the pooled share functions as an emergent, contractually embedded redistribution. Critics contend that this is merely taxation in contractual disguise, while defenders maintain that the split is itself part of the voluntary terms.

For the feature, I would like you to rank the following five philosophical assessments of whether this protocol genuinely respects Nozick's entitlement theory. Order them from the assessment that most successfully preserves the libertarian intuition to the one that most thoroughly undermines it, and provide a concise justification for each placement. The assessments are: (A) The protocol respects entitlement because every dollar in the pool derives from explicit, revocable consent to the terms; the resulting pattern is an emergent byproduct of individual choices rather than an imposed end-state. (B) The protocol violates entitlement because the split is structurally analogous to taxation: it introduces a deduction relative to the pure exchange the parties would otherwise have conducted, compromising the voluntariness of the transfer. (C) The protocol is neutral with respect to entitlement, since Nozick's critique targets state-imposed patterns, and a privately contracted, revocable mechanism falls outside the scope of his argument altogether. (D) The protocol undermines entitlement not through the pool itself, but by corrupting the baseline of just acquisition: if the underlying digital infrastructure was built with collective or publicly funded resources, the seller's claim to the pre-split revenue is already compromised. (E) The protocol reveals that the Wilt Chamberlain intuition depends on an unrealistic atomization of transactions; once exchanges are embedded in institutional frameworks that shape preferences and available options, the distinction between voluntary transfer and patterned redistribution collapses entirely.
