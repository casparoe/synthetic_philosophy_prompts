Exam question: In evolutionary explanations of cooperation, direct reciprocity strategies like Tit-for-Tat stabilize cooperation in the iterated prisoner’s dilemma by making future punishment contingent on present defection. Some theorists argue that indirect reciprocity—where agents cooperate or defect based on publicly tracked reputation scores rather than personal interaction history—can sustain cooperation even when any given pair interacts only once. Evaluate this claim. Specifically, address: (1) why direct reciprocity structurally requires pairwise iteration; (2) whether indirect reciprocity genuinely eliminates the need for temporal repetition, or whether it merely displaces that repetition onto a population-level monitoring mechanism (call this observational iteration); and (3) whether treating indirect reciprocity as a straightforward extension of Tit-for-Tat logic obscures any conceptually distinct feature of reputation-based cooperation.

Student answer: Direct reciprocity only works when the same two agents meet repeatedly, because without a future round there is nothing to lose by defecting. In a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma, rational players always defect. Indirect reciprocity fixes this by introducing reputation: agents can see how others behaved in past interactions with different partners, so they can reward cooperators and punish defectors even in a single encounter. This means indirect reciprocity completely removes the need for iteration—once reputation is available, pairwise repetition is unnecessary, and the problem is solved at the population level. In this sense, indirect reciprocity is just Tit-for-Tat scaled up: Tit-for-Tat remembers your partner’s last move, while a reputation system remembers everyone’s last moves, but the underlying logic of conditional cooperation is identical. One weakness is that reputation systems depend on accurate observation; if errors occur, good agents may be punished by mistake and cooperation can break down. Still, this vulnerability is only a practical difficulty about information quality, not a conceptual limitation of the reciprocity framework itself.
