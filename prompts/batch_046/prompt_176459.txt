Having recently returned to the rural manufacturing town where I grew up after two decades in urban professional work, I am no longer able to treat the moral outlook I was raised with as a deficient or backward version of professional-class values. The framework I encountered there—centered on upkeep, mutual dependency, and what I will call infrastructural loyalty, meaning a normative obligation to sustain the social and material systems one inhabits—strikes me as a genuinely competing ethical framework rather than false consciousness. Please construct a philosophical argument that this working-class, rural outlook is more defensible than the urban professional-class ethos of individual optimization, meritocratic achievement, and geographic mobility. Your argument should defend the claim that sustaining existing cooperative structures carries independent moral weight, not reducible to efficiency or distributive justice. Specifically, address the objection that such loyalty is parochial and resistant to necessary change, and distinguish this outlook from mere conservatism by contrasting repair-oriented culture, where moral recognition attaches to maintaining shared functioning, with disruption-oriented culture, where it attaches to maximizing personal or systemic transformation. Please clearly separate what is settled in social philosophy and value pluralism from what remains genuinely contested among knowledgeable scholars, and do not present any contested normative or interpretive claim as the consensus. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the argument should stand on conceptual grounds alone.
