I have been working through an objection to prioritarianism for a graduate seminar, but my argument now seems to prove far more than I intended — specifically, an implausible conclusion about obligations to one's own future self. I should like to know precisely where the inference slips, and what abandoning that step costs.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Prioritarianism holds that benefiting an individual carries greater moral weight the worse off that individual is, other things being equal.

(2) This weighting is not confined to strangers; it applies to anyone who figures in a distributive choice.

(3) Consider my own life across time. Tomorrow I may suffer a severe migraine (welfare level -20), whereas today I sit at welfare level +50. For distributive purposes, my tomorrow-self is simply another severely disadvantaged individual.

(4) If I can take a pill now that prevents tomorrow's migraine at the cost of lowering my present welfare from +50 to +40, prioritarianism weights the benefit to my tomorrow-self far more heavily than the cost to my present self.

(5) The reasoning extends to any temporal distance and any present cost, provided the future suffering is severe enough and the present sacrifice is smaller in prioritarian-weighted terms.

(6) Hence I am required to accept arbitrarily large present sacrifices — abandoning projects, relationships, or comforts — in order to prevent any sufficiently severe future suffering of mine, however remote.

(7) Clearly nobody is required to live in that manner.

(8) Therefore prioritarianism is false, or must be restricted so that it does not apply across temporal stages of a single life.

Premises (1) and (2) seem standard. The move from (3) to (4) feels like a direct application, and (6) follows from (4) and (5) by iteration. Yet the conclusion is absurd. Is the error an equivocation on 'worse off' between interpersonal and intrapersonal comparison? A confusion between prudential and moral reasons? Or does the argument actually hold, meaning that preserving prioritarianism requires denying that future selves are proper subjects of distributive justice?

Please identify the step or premise I ought to reject, explain why it looked defensible, and set out what each alternative costs — especially the price of retaining prioritarianism while blocking its application across time. Write your response in British English.
