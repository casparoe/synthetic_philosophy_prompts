I was discussing with colleagues in a philosophy of social science seminar how debates over national character—whether it is coherent to attribute stable psychological dispositions or value orientations to a people, and whether generalizing about them is ever acceptable—tend to collapse into a tired essentialism-versus-constructivism standoff that obscures more than it clarifies.

I want you to construct a decision tree that moves past that framing by organizing the logical space around a distinction I have been developing: statistical national character (the actual distribution of traits across citizens of a state) versus modal national character (the culturally normative ideal type reproduced by socialization mechanisms, regardless of how many individuals fully instantiate it). Each branch node should be a concrete diagnostic claim—e.g., whether a modal character can exist without a corresponding statistical cluster, whether internal pluralism or colonial disruption undermines coherence for either form, and whether the acceptability of generalization requires tracking both dimensions or can survive tracking only one—phrased so that a student could answer it without knowing which philosophical label it maps onto. Present the taxonomy as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two describing one branch node or terminal leaf together with its most awkward commitment, with no introduction or conclusion. For any empty combination of answers, briefly diagnose whether the view is incoherent, politically toxic, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate; and ensure that every named or unnamed position at a leaf could be defended without bibliographic authority.
