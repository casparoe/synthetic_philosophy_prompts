I've been working on this short essay for my philosophy of mind seminar about what I'm calling the 'competence-first inversion.' The basic idea is that both Darwin and Turing accidentally built theories where functional success comes before, and doesn't require, any inner understanding. Darwin's selection process produces eye-design competence with zero biological comprehension of optics; Turing's test asks whether a machine can perform conversational competence without us ever checking if there's comprehension behind the outputs. What's weird is that this flips the usual epistemic order we take for granted—like, we think you understand calculus and therefore can solve integrals, not that you solve integrals therefore you understand calculus.

I'm trying to extend this to a more niche case: what about bacterial quorum sensing? Bacteria coordinate group behavior based on chemical density without any individual bacterium 'knowing' about the group. Is this another inversion—collective competence without distributed comprehension? Or is it too different because there's no selection mechanism analgous to Darwinian evolution in the individual case?

I think modern AI courses present the Turing Test as a benchmark for consciousness, but Turing actually seemed more interested in whether we need comprehension to explain competence, which is closer to Darwin's move. Am I overstating that?
