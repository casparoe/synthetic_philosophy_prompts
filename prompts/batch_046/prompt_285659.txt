I serve on a small municipal committee that reviews proposals to rename streets, relocate statues, or add contextual plaques. We meet monthly, have no formal philosophy training, and must produce signed recommendations in under an hour. Over the past year I have been trying to make sense of debates about commemorative ethics—especially the distinction between an object’s honorific message and its mnemonic or evidentiary role—but the discussions stay too abstract for our meetings, which are mostly lawyers, architects, and neighborhood representatives. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates these ideas into steps we can actually run together, with clear stopping points and explicit markers for where we must make a value judgment rather than find a fact.

Please turn the following working principle into a practical procedure: a commemorative object should not be retained solely for mnemonic value if its honorific message is both false and actively harmful, but removal should not proceed if it would destroy the primary physical witness to a historical wrong that has no alternative site of memory, unless an additive counter-commemorative presence can be established in its place. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, indicate where archival evidence is required versus where democratic deliberation takes over, and mark the points where judgment is irreducible. Then apply it to three proposals: renaming a street honoring a nineteenth-century industrialist whose factories relied on child labor but who also funded the city’s only public hospital; removing a 1965 statue of a colonial administrator erected with an original plaque praising a civilizing mission, now repeatedly vandalized; and leaving untouched a local resistance monument that some residents associate with wartime atrocities, for which there is no agreed relocation site and no museum willing to accept custodianship. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the committee will find unwelcome.

Our constraints are strict: forty-five minutes per proposal, no budget for outside expert reports, and a requirement that the final recommendation be defensible by a non-specialist chair. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire—particularly when mnemonic and honorific values pull in opposite directions, or when community testimony substitutes for documented intent—and state clearly what the procedure cannot resolve on its own. At the very end, provide a single line beginning Bottom line: that captures the unavoidable judgment call at the heart of the process.
