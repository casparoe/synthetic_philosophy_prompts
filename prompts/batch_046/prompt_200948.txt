Following a long argument with a friend about whether chemical properties genuinely emerge from physical ones, I am trying to understand a specific passage from C.D. Broad's 1925 discussion of emergence. Broad considers the case of ammonia and argues that even if one possessed complete knowledge of hydrogen and nitrogen in isolation, together with all laws governing them separately, one still could not derive the compound's distinctive properties—its odor, its solubility patterns, its reactive behavior—without invoking an additional law that specifically connects that lower-level configuration to the higher-level compound. He terms this an irreducible trans-ordinal law and maintains that it reflects a structural feature of nature's hierarchical organization rather than a mere practical limitation in human reasoning. I need help understanding the precise philosophical status of this claim. Is Broad defending an ontological thesis according to which chemical properties are genuinely novel entities, or is he advancing an epistemic thesis about the permanent inaccessibility of certain deductions? My friend objected using modern quantum mechanics: if one had a complete quantum-mechanical description of the constituent atoms and their interactions, the properties of ammonia should be derivable in principle, reducing Broad's irreducibility to nothing more than a failure of computational capacity. Could you help me parse Broad's argument so as to address this tension—specifically, whether his trans-ordinal law is intended to survive a full microphysical description, and whether his distinction between resultant properties, which are mechanically predictable, and emergent properties, which are not, remains coherent once we separate issues of prediction from issues of ontological reduction?
