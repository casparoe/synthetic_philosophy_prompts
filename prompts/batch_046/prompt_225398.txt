I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of time and logic, and I want to prepare a joint adversarial-collaboration document on Aristotle’s sea-battle problem and the metaphysics of future contingents. Please draft the statement that two well-informed opposing camps could both sign after good-faith engagement. The first camp—call them the Actualist Bivalents—holds that every future-tensed proposition about tomorrow’s naval battle is already determinately true or false, but they reject fatalism by distinguishing hard past facts from soft future-dependent ones, often along Ockhamist lines or by positing a single actualized branch in an otherwise branching model. The second camp—call them the Ontic Branchers—denies that any present fact of the matter fixes the battle’s occurrence, rejects bivalence for future-contingent statements, and maintains that explanatory dependence runs strictly forward in time.

The document should be formal and polished, suitable for publication in a philosophical journal, and should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include agreement on the formal structure of the problem (the tension between the principle of bivalence and the intuition of openness), on what fatalism actually requires (not merely determinism but the impotence of present deliberation), on the formal properties of branching-time frames, and on the physical point that microscopic quantum randomness does not, by itself, resolve the macroscopic philosophical question about naval outcomes.

Second, a disagreements table. For each camp, state the core thesis in wording that members of that camp would accept as accurate. Then isolate the precise divergence: specifically, whether the absence of a present truth-maker for the battle’s occurrence is an ontic feature of temporal structure or merely an epistemic limitation relative to a fully actualized future path. Include also the subsidiary dispute over whether a “thin red line” marking one branch as actual is a metaphysically substantive addition to the formalism or only a notational convenience.

Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take one central crux to be whether explanatory asymmetry (the past explaining the future, never conversely) can be sustained without tacitly presupposing the very bivalence the Ontic Branchers reject. A second crux is whether an indexical presentist use of “the actual future” collapses into the Actualist Bivalent position or can be maintained consistently within branching ontology. For each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case or argument both sides agree bears on it: for instance, a macroscopic analog of a retrocausal quantum-eraser setup, treated not as empirical physics but as a thought experiment about whether later measurement outcomes can ground earlier preparatory states, with each camp’s predicted interpretation recorded in advance.

Fourth, a brief concluding section identifying any residual verbal disagreements—such as whether “openness” denotes an epistemic limitation, an ontic multiplicity of futures, or a failure of bivalence—and explaining exactly why those are differences in terminology rather than substance.

Write in the voice of a careful, non-specialist philosopher who respects logical precision but is not writing for insiders alone. Do not quote historical texts verbatim; paraphrase Aristotle, Ockham, or modern branching-time theorists in your own words. Ensure the formulation of each camp’s position is neither vacuous nor slanted, and separate genuine metaphysical disagreement from misunderstandings about words.
