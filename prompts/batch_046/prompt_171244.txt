Consider a hypothetical case I will call narrative fission. Imagine a single human being—call her S—whose episodic memory and practical reasoning systems have become functionally decoupled into two non-communicating streams, both anchored to the same living body. Stream A maintains a continuous autobiography in which S is an improvisational musician who values spontaneity and distrusts long-term planning; Stream B maintains an equally continuous autobiography in which S is a cautious accountant who values reliability and regards spontaneity as recklessness. Each stream has full first-person authority within its own domain: Stream A says I and means the musician; Stream B says I and means the accountant. They share no internal access, yet both control the same body on alternating days, with no overarching supervisor that adjudicates between them.

Now apply your view precisely. If the self is literally a center of narrative gravity—an abstract organizational point rather than a physical thing—then we appear to have two such centers with exactly the same gravitational source (the body of S). This raises two related problems.

First, the counting problem: do we now have two selves? If yes, then what principle allows us to count them? It cannot be bodily separation, since there is only one body. It seems we must count by narrative coherence, but then the principle of individuation has become purely functional and detached from any physical substrate—yet your view was supposed to be naturalistic, not Cartesian.

Second, the diffusion problem: if we insist that there is still only one self, then the center of narrative gravity has become bifurcated or diffuse. But a center that is genuinely split into two non-interacting loci is no longer functioning as a single center of gravity at all; it begins to look like there is no center, only two competing narratives using the same mouth.
