I'm organizing a graduate workshop on democratic theory through concrete historical failure modes, and I've persuaded two long-time interlocutors—one who holds that Athenian direct democracy contained structurally self-destructive institutional mechanisms, and one who holds that its worst catastrophes were contingent products of wartime stress, particular personalities, and emotional contagion—to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document rather than continue debating past each other. Write the statement they should both be able to sign. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the procedural mechanics of the ekklesia and boule, including the probouleutic function, the absence of formal deliberative delay for military votes, the mechanics of ostracism, and the specific decision sequences in the Mytilene debate (the initial massacre decree, Cleon's intervention, and the reversal vote), the Sicilian Expedition (the initial authorization, Nicias's strategic objections, and the second assembly vote to reinforce), and the trial of Socrates (the indictment, the dikasterion's composition, and the penalty-phase procedure); second, a table or clearly demarcated section stating the precise disagreement in wording each side accepts—the institutionalist claims that the framework made high-variance, emotionally driven outcomes structurally probable regardless of who held office, while the contingentist claims the framework was generally sound and these three episodes represent exceptional breakdowns driven by specific agents and crisis conditions; third, the cruxes, specifically whether the boule's advisory role was systematically overridden by the ekklesia rather than structurally weak, whether Socrates' execution reveals an inherent democratic intolerance of philosophical dissent or a contingent reaction to wartime anxiety and the profanation of the Herms, and whether the Sicilian disaster was an inevitable consequence of democratic imperial overreach or a strategic miscalculation that expert opposition nearly averted; and fourth, for each crux, one concrete comparative case or piece of internal evidence both sides agree bears on it—such as the procedural parallels in the Arginusae trial, the comparative record of Spartan strategic deliberation in analogous expansionist situations, or the assembly's treatment of generals after naval defeats—along with each side's explicit advance prediction of what that evidence demonstrates. The tone should be formal and suitable for circulation in a history-of-political-thought venue; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; and ensure the formulations are neither vacuous nor slanted, so that the residual disagreement is made exact and the agreed ground is genuinely shared.
