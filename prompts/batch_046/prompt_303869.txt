I'm preparing a philosophy-of-mind seminar paper on whether an 'I' can be genuinely distributed across distinct brains, taking as my point of departure Hofstadter's informal suggestion that a self might persist in others who share its patterns, but pushing it into a stricter theoretical register. Rather than treating this as mere metaphor, I want to examine the conditions under which inter-substrate self-persistence—specifically, cases where two or more brains maintain continuous bidirectional updating of self-modeling representations through high-bandwidth neural coupling, producing what might be called inter-brain strange loops—could count as a single distributed subject rather than as mere functional analogy or emotional projection. For the paper's core section, I need a rigorous specification of the desiderata that any adequate theory of distributed self-persistence must meet.

Please set out the requirements across three axes. First, the explanatory phenomena: what must the theory account for (e.g., first-person continuity despite substrate discontinuity, asymmetry of access between host and recipient brains, preservation of moral and legal agency, the possibility of partial rather than total distribution)? Second, the conceptual distinctions it must respect without collapsing them (e.g., literal identity versus functional isomorphism, survival versus duplication, internal versus external self-reference). Third, the consistency constraints: which empirical findings (such as split-brain patient studies) and logical results (such as anti-duplication arguments or the formal properties of self-reference) must the theory accommodate? For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable for any philosophically adequate account or whether it reflects a camp-specific preference (materialist, functionalist, phenomenological, etc.). Crucially, examine whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, whether maintaining a unified first-person perspective conflicts with allowing multi-substrate realization, or whether preserving moral singularity requires substrate uniqueness—and identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding competing candidates. Keep the focus on the criteria and their motivating cases; scoring existing theories is secondary, though a brief assessment of where Hofstadter's own speculative framework succeeds and where it falls short against your list would be valuable.
