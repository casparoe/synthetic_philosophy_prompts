I'm preparing notes for a philosophy of science seminar on belief revision, and I've assembled a package of views on the Judy Benjamin problem and updating on conditionals that I want audited before I present them. My commitments are these. First, in the noisy-channel variant—where Judy receives the report "if you are in Red division then you are at Headquarters" via a channel with a 10% false-positive rate for conditional messages—I hold that the correct response is Jeffrey conditioning: her marginal credence in being in Red should remain fixed, while the weight within the Red cells shifts so that Red-HQ gains and Red-Second loses proportionally. Second, I accept van Fraassen's diachronic Reflection principle for unconditional beliefs: absent epistemic corruption, an agent's present credence in P should equal her expectation of her future credence in P. Third, I reject treating indicative conditionals as material conditionals for the purposes of belief update, on the grounds that this ties the revision implausibly to the prior probability that the antecedent is false. Fourth, I think that if an agent applies Reflection consistently, then upon learning a conditional she should already anticipate the credence shifts she will undergo should she later discover the antecedent is true, and this anticipation should feed back into her present unconditional credences. Fifth, I maintain that Jeffrey conditioning takes precedence here because it respects the "no direct news about the conditioning event" constraint, and that this constraint should block any Reflection-driven revision of the marginal probabilities.

Please tell me whether these five hang together. I am especially concerned that (2) together with (4) might undermine the marginal-preservation claims in (1) and (5), and that (3) might conflict with the formal machinery Jeffrey conditioning actually requires, though I suspect the latter tension is only apparent. For each genuinely conflicting pair, identify the tension, rank all tensions by severity, state which single revision would clean up the most problems, and say what each revision costs. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
