I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in discussions of epistemic bootstrapping that doesn't align neatly with the familiar one-step circularity objection. A reasoner begins with a narrowly circumscribed judgment—say, that a particular perceptual report is accurate under standard conditions—and uses it to derive a restricted reliability principle for that specific modality. In a subsequent stage, that restricted principle is invoked to authorize a broader class of judgments, some of which extend well beyond the original circumscribed base, and the broader class is then treated as supporting the reliability principle itself. The loop is not immediate: an intermediate principle mediates between the initial judgment and the final authorization, so it is not flat circularity. Yet it is not ordinary inductive widening either, because the intermediate principle lacks independent support outside the outputs it eventually licenses. The literature often treats this as a variant of bootstrapping, but the canonical formulations—particularly those tied to easy-knowledge objections—seem to focus on tighter, single-source self-vindication. Is there an established concept specifically for this staged, ladder-like expansion, where reliability claims are incrementally broadened through principles that depend on the very judgments they come to license? If the pattern is already named, how does it differ from both straightforward epistemic circularity and from legitimate abductive extension? If not, what precise boundaries would distinguish it?
