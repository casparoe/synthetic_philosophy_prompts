I have just finished a long discussion with a friend about whether Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence functions primarily as a cosmological claim or as an existential test of affirmation. We reached an impasse over two modern variants that postdate him. First, imagine that physics eventually establishes that, in a sufficiently large cyclic or multiversal structure, configurations of matter recur with exact numerical identity across iterations, yet without causal linkage or psychological continuity between them. My friend argues that Nietzsche would embrace this as the ultimate vindication of amor fati, because it transforms willing one's life into a literally eternal commitment. I contend that he would reject it, since the doctrine's force depends on the absence of metaphysical consolation and on the singular burden of affirming this very life without guarantee. Second, we considered a branching model of recurrence: each cycle reproduces one's biography up to a decision node, then diverges, generating over time a dense web of counterfactual lives that remain in some sense mine. Given Nietzsche's framing of recurrence in The Gay Science and Thus Spoke Zarathustra—where it appears as the demon's announcement of the heaviest weight—how would he likely have assessed both the exact-identity multiverse scenario and this branching divergence? Would either strengthen the imperative to affirm, or would they dissolve the singular self that must bear the weight? I am less interested in cataloguing his texts than in identifying the internal commitments about value, repetition, and personal identity that would determine his judgment, and in seeing whether a consistent reading allows him to accept one variant while rejecting the other.
