I'm a Ph.D. student in philosophy of history, and I'm delivering a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on modal reasoning in historical explanation. To open, I want a poem—twenty lines of blank verse with a hard volta at line twelve—that stages the problem I'm calling counterfactual robustness gradients: the claim that a historical outcome's resistance to nearby-world intervention varies nonlinearly with causal distance. Far upstream, redundant pathways and institutional inertia seem to overdetermine the result; near the event, a single missed message or delayed storm can redirect the trajectory. The poem should not resolve whether the gradient is steep or flat, because my thesis is that historiography underdetermines its slope. Lines 1–11 should accumulate images of deep structural pressure—geographic constraint, demographic momentum, bureaucratic repetition—so that the reader feels any single upstream removal is absorbed. At line 12, turn sharply to a proximal contingency: a seal broken late, a train held by fog, and let the same outcome suddenly depend on that thread. The closing lines should leave both regimes visible at once, holding overdetermination and fragility in suspension rather than synthesizing them. It needs to be conceptually precise enough that a philosopher of history recognizes the distinction, but it should not cite scholars, include a bibliography, or depend on specialist literature a reader wouldn't already have in view. Please write in American English.
