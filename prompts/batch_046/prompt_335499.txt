Since my partner began coordinating with an interfaith coalition on local green-space preservation, I have served on our borough's citizens' advisory panel for planning ethics, and I am trying to understand the implicit rule we apply when religious reasoning enters public recommendations. Our record is this: we endorsed funding for a charity-run food bank where the secular case for poverty alleviation stood entirely on its own, though religious sponsors testified in support; we rejected a request for Sunday trading restrictions that rested solely on Sabbath observance, with no independent argument about worker welfare; we approved the inclusion of religious iconography in a public memorial because the design committee justified it by reference to the area's documented cultural history, not to any theological claim; and we rejected a proposal to block a secular community centre on land that some groups called sacred, because the objection was framed only in terms of spiritual desecration that could not be translated into planning grounds such as ecology or heritage, even though the proponents insisted that anyone of goodwill could share their reverence.

We now face a proposal to protect a particular woodland from development. There are no distinctive ecological, archaeological, or traffic-related planning grounds for this wood; its proponents, drawn from a local religious community, argue that it should be preserved because it is an object of intergenerational reverence and awe, and they present this not as a doctrinal command but as an affective, non-propositional commitment that any citizen can understand and weigh, regardless of metaphysical belief. If we want to be consistent with ourselves, should we approve or reject? Which of our earlier cases is the closest analog? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle are we adopting with each choice—something like the independence of secular justification, or something like the accessibility of translated reasons? And what further case—perhaps one involving an explicitly secular group making an analogous affective claim about a site—would best tell those two rules apart?
