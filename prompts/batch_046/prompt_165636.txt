As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I have been drafting the conceptual backbone for a feature on consciousness studies, and before I put my own framework in print I need to know whether it actually coheres. Here is the package of commitments I am carrying: (1) I am an ontological physicalist—every phenomenal fact supervenes on physical fact, with no non-physical properties in nature. (2) I treat the hard problem of consciousness as a genuine cognitive challenge, not a pseudo-problem; there is a real explanatory gap between complete physical descriptions and phenomenal knowledge, though I insist this gap is epistemic rather than metaphysical. (3) I hold what I call dispositional phenomenal realism: phenomenal properties are real, but they are not intrinsic qualitative features of experience; rather, they are relational or dispositional properties of cognitive architectures that determine how those architectures construct indexical self-representations. (4) I take phenomenal concepts to be demonstrative and indexical, which explains their semantic irreducibility without committing me to any form of dualism. (5) I believe the meta-problem—why we think there is a hard problem—is fully dissolved once (3) and (4) are combined: we are mistaking a structural feature of our conceptual apparatus for an unsolved ontological mystery. (6) I regard zombies as epistemically conceivable but metaphysically impossible, attributing the zombie intuition entirely to the special character of phenomenal concepts. (7) I reject illusionism about consciousness—there genuinely is something it is like to undergo my experiences—but I endorse illusionism specifically about the meta-problem itself, treating the felt sense of an intractable metaphysical gap as an artifact of how we model our own minds.

The position feels stable to me, but I suspect there are fractures I am not seeing—particularly between my anti-illusionist claim that experience is genuinely present and my relational, dispositional characterization of phenomenal properties, and also between my assertion that the meta-problem is completely solved and my parallel claim that the hard problem remains a serious cognitive challenge rather than a dissolved illusion. I want an audit, not a survey: examine these commitments pairwise, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension assess its severity. For each conflict, specify the least costly revision—whether that means softening physicalism, modifying dispositional phenomenal realism, weakening my claim that the meta-problem is dissolved, or reconceiving the hard problem’s status. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if revised or abandoned, would eliminate the largest number of tensions, and whether the resulting view would still be recognizably mine. I am not asking for references, reading lists, or citations; I need a conceptual consistency check that takes the belief set itself as the object of analysis.
