I'm currently taking a philosophy of mind module and we've been arguing for weeks about the absent-minded driver problem — the classic case where you drive right past your exit because you're lost in thought, and philosophers disagree about whether your real intention was actually present or just some background habit or disposition. Anyway, I'm also a student rep on our university's Research Ethics and Cognitive Technology Board, and we've got this bizarre proposal sitting in front of us for Thursday's meeting that seems to engineer exactly that problem on purpose.

The researchers want to run a pilot study on campus shuttle drivers using a new "sub-threshold navigation cue" system. Instead of normal GPS instructions, the system delivers barely-noticeable haptic pulses to the steering wheel that steer procedural memory without going through conscious delibaration. They claim this reduces cognitive load and prevents ordinary absent-minded errors. But the pilot data shows that in about 15% of trips, drivers miss their assigned stop and later report they "just felt like continuing straight" — they had explicitly told the dispatcher they intended to turn, yet the haptic suppression seemed to disconnect that explicit intention from action, producing a technologically induced version of the absent-minded driver scenario. Half the board treats this as routine assistive tech that should be approved with standard informed consent; the other half thinks deliberately creating a gap between explicit intention and behaviour undermines autonomous agency in a way that consent forms can't fix, especially since the drivers are employed staff with limited opt-out power.
