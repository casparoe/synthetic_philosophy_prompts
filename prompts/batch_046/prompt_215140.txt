As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I am presenting next Thursday to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind reading group. My thesis is that teleosemantics offers a viable framework for interpreting representational error in large language models without attributing consciousness, biological intentionality, or belief-states to them. Specifically, I argue that a model's training architecture—backpropagation, the loss landscape, and the designer-specified objective—establishes what I call "gradient-descent proper functions": selection conditions that are analogous to evolutionary selection but normatively grounded in functional success rather than survival. On this view, when an internal representation systematically fails to reduce loss in the contexts for which it was optimized, the system exhibits a genuine, non-metaphorical misrepresentation, even though it lacks evolutionary history, embodiment, or original intentionality. The audience includes three philosophers who specialize in naturalized mental content and are deeply skeptical of extending normative vocabulary to artifacts; one cognitive scientist who accepts derived intentionality in tools but insists that "error" requires a biological norm of survival or welfare; and two AI researchers who regard all talk of "representation" in neural networks as mere anthropomorphic projection. They value conceptual precision over engineering utility, and they will press me on whether I am smuggling biological normativity into an artificial system.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use. Identify the single objection I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous. Finally, state explicitly what argument or finding would lead you to abandon my application of teleosemantics to artificial systems, and flag the weakest link in my own reasoning—the point I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope they overlook.
