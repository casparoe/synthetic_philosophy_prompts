I have been working through a colleague's lecture notes for a graduate seminar on contemporary moral theory, and I find myself uncertain about how faithfully they represent T. M. Scanlon's contractualist framework, particularly with respect to the aggregation problem. The notes summarize Scanlon's central claim—drawn, I gather, from his major work on moral philosophy—as follows: an act is wrong if and only if it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, where the standard of reasonable rejection is determined by the strength of the personal reasons that affected individuals have for objecting to the principle. The notes then present the familiar aggregation objection: if a single individual can reasonably reject being sacrificed to save five others, then contractualism must prohibit killing the one regardless of how many lives are at stake, since the victim's objection is not diminished by the presence of additional beneficiaries. According to the summary, Scanlon responds by insisting that numbers carry no weight whatsoever in contractualist justification—that the objection of the one always trumps the combined objections of any number of potential victims, no matter how large. From this, the notes draw the broader conclusion that contractualism collapses into an extreme form of individualism, one that treats every person's claim as lexically prior to any collective benefit and that is therefore practically useless for public policy, disaster triage, or any institutional setting in which trade-offs among lives are unavoidable.

This account strikes me as largely accurate in its exposition of the basic contractualist machinery, yet I worry that it subtly conflates Scanlon's actual position with a more rigid, rights-based absolutism, and that it overstates both the inflexibility of his reply and the practical implications he would draw.
