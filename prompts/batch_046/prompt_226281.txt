I'm auditing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of skill, and a fellow student—let's call her Dr. L—has staked out a consistent but puzzling position on knowing-how versus knowing-that across the examples we've workshopped. I want to predict how she'll rule on a new case, but I'm less interested in her answer than in the competing principles that could generate her track record, where they split, and what further example would adjudicate between them.

Her verdicts so far are these. First, she holds that a concert pianist who lost both arms in an accident and now performs via a myoelectric shoulder-to-finger interface—after five years she plays with full dynamic expression, reports that she feels the phrase in her shoulders, yet cannot map her muscle patterns onto conventional fingerings—knows how to play piano, not merely that certain notes correspond to certain movements. Second, she says a music theorist who can notate every harmonic progression and direct an orchestra from the podium, but who has never produced a sound on an instrument and describes performance as watching a proof unfold, knows that but not how. Third, regarding a sommelier with congenital anosmia who identifies wines solely by matching chromatography reports to memorized chemical-flavor tables—scoring perfectly on blind tests but describing the task as solving a crossword rather than tasting—Dr. L insists she knows that, lacking the relevant know-how entirely. Fourth, she maintains that a surgeon who, after thirty-six hours without sleep, performs a routine laparoscopic procedure with flawless technique yet retains no episodic memory of the operation and can describe none of its steps afterward, nevertheless knew how in that moment, because the skill was manifest in her hands even when propositional access was absent.

Now consider a new case we've not discussed: a cognitive neuroscientist has developed an auditory substitution headset for people without smell. After a year of training, a user can identify wines with expert accuracy by listening to tonal patterns that encode chemical signatures; she reports that she hears the terroir directly, can no longer identify wines when the headset is removed, yet can also explain the general mapping rules, such as which chord cluster corresponds to high tannin. She adds, however, that giving those rules to a novice is like describing a color to someone blind—useless for the actual recognition—and she herself cannot articulate why a particular vintage produces its exact tonal fingerprint, only that she hears it.

What should Dr. L say about this auditory oenophile—does she know how to taste wine, know that, both, or neither? More importantly, what are the two or three distinct rules that could consistently generate all four of her prior verdicts, and how does each rule predict the outcome for the headset user? Give me your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with the reasoning that supports that preference over the alternatives. Finally, name one further hypothetical case—preferably one that does not depend on technology—that would best distinguish the leading rule from its closest competitor.
