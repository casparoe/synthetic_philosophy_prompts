I'm preparing a consistency audit of my own ethical commitments for a seminar on well-being and want to check whether they hang together before I defend them. I hold: (a) a tranquilist axiology—only the absence of craving (upadana) and of suffering (dukkha) has intrinsic value, while positive mental states like joy or fulfilled desire have at best instrumental value; (b) that evolutionary explanations of craving mechanisms are genuinely debunking for those mechanisms, showing they do not track any independent good, but that evolutionary explanations of our aversion to suffering do not similarly debunk it because suffering is directly present rather than representational; (c) that practical ethics should therefore prioritize reducing severe suffering and cultivating the cessation of craving over promoting happiness or preference satisfaction; (d) that there is a strong duty of non-interference with others' pursuit of desire-fulfillment, since imposing tranquilist practices violates autonomy and can itself generate suffering; (e) that in one's own life one should avoid not only present suffering but also anticipatory anxiety about future suffering, treating such anticipation as itself a present form of mental disturbance with negative intrinsic value; and (f) that political institutions should be evaluated chiefly by how well they minimize systemic craving and suffering, not by economic growth or aggregate preference satisfaction. Which commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Put the bottom-line ranking and the recommended revision in the first two sentences, then explain. Keep the response to no more than 350 words.
