I'm drafting an essay for my philosophy of mind module on split-brain cases and whether consciousness genuinely divides. Instead of comparing named theories in prose, I need a diagnostic taxonomy — a decision tree that sorts positions by what they say about concrete cases, not by the labels people prefer.

Start from this clinical commitment: a patient has undergone complete corpus callosotomy, and in testing each hemisphere can guide one hand to perform actions incompatible with the other (for example, the left hand pulls away a garment that the right hand is buttoning). From there, construct a tree of about six to eight binary nodes that a student could answer without already knowing which interpretation each branch leads to.

The tree must end in clearly described leaves including: (i) one stream of consciousness with divided access but intact phenomenal unity; (ii) two genuinely separate streams of consciousness sharing one body; (iii) a single consciousness that rapidly oscillates or switches between hemispheric control; (iv) a narrative-constructivist denial that the 'how many minds' question applies; and (v) a biological or animalist view that affirms one subject despite psychological division.

Use these specific cases at the nodes: a mirror self-recognition task in which each hemisphere appears to recognise only one side of the body; a cross-cueing scenario in which the language hemisphere infers the mute hemisphere's visual experience; and a delayed-choice intermanual conflict triggered after a verbal instruction is delivered to only one hemisphere.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of theorists: first lay out the combinations of answers the nodes permit, then locate the actual positions within that space. Note any unoccupied combinations — for instance, a view asserting two phenomenal subjects that nevertheless share every qualitative state, or one that denies phenomenal unity yet claims perfect diachronic self-reference across hemispheres — and briefly diagnose whether each empty cell is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate.

The distinctions that matter for my essay are whether unity is analysed in terms of phenomenal integration, functional access, or narrative coherence, and whether the split produces a synchronic division, a diachronic one, or neither. Keep the taxonomy suitable for undergraduates familiar with basic split-brain literature but not with specialised neuroscience. Please separate what is clinically settled (for example, that hemispheres can behave independently) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind (for example, whether independence entails two subjects of experience), and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
