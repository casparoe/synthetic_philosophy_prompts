Here is an argument about the reflexive scope of underdetermination that seems to prove too much, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) For any finite body of observational evidence E, there exist at least two first-order theories T1 and T2 that entail every member of E but make incompatible predictions beyond E.

(2) If two theories are empirically equivalent with respect to all evidence available to finite observers, then no rational preference between them is justified on evidential grounds alone.

(3) Now apply the same reasoning one level up: call the norms that license non-evidential preference between theories M-rules. For any M-rule R (say, one that permits simplicity or explanatory power to break ties), there exists a rival M-rule R* that permits the opposite weighting and is itself empirically equivalent to R with respect to every observable feature of scientific practice.

(4) Therefore the choice between R and R* is itself underdetermined by the evidence about scientific practice.

(5) But the claim that theory choice is globally underdetermined is supported precisely by an instance of R—namely, that we should not invoke non-evidential virtues to resolve ties.

(6) By (4), there is an empirically equivalent rival to the very reasoning that establishes underdetermination.

(7) Hence, if we apply the thesis consistently, we must treat it as no better justified than its denial, which makes accepting it self-defeating and leads to global suspension of judgment about theory choice and about the norms governing it.

The conclusion is absurd: acknowledging Duhem-Quine problems should not collapse into total epistemic paralysis about science and about itself. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1) and (5), since they state the core thesis and its natural reflexive extension. Is the error in (3), where I treat meta-norms as subject to the same empirical equivalence? Or in (2), where I conflate absence of evidential preference with absence of any rational preference? Or is there a hidden scope shift between first-order theories and second-order norms? What does giving up each option cost—particularly, can one restrict underdetermination to first-order theories without that restriction looking ad hoc?
