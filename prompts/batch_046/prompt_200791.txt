I cover philosophy of science and social theory for a general-interest publication. Over the past few years I have noticed a recurring pattern in how local norms evolve, and I am trying to determine whether the literature already gives it a precise name. The pattern runs as follows. A community faces a pure coordination problem with no inherent moral stakes—say, which side of a shared footpath to use, or which frequency band to reserve for emergency broadcasts. The members settle on an arbitrary solution through an explicit but temporary agreement. Once the original need disappears, the agreement is forgotten, yet the practice survives through imitation and mutual expectation. Eventually newcomers encounter the practice as an unexamined background rule. Over generations the arbitrary origin is completely obscured; violating the practice comes to be treated not as a failure of coordination but as a moral fault, and proposals to revert to the other equally arbitrary option are rejected not on practical grounds but because the status quo now feels like the right way to do it. I am familiar with David Lewis's analysis of convention as a solution to coordination problems sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectation, and I know that philosophers have discussed the moralization of norms. What I am after is the specific transitional configuration: a convention that has outlived the explicit agreement that created it, lost all memory of its arbitrariness, and thereby acquired a spurious normative authority. Does this exact configuration have an established term? How should it be distinguished from an ordinary Lewisian convention on one side, and from standard cases of norm moralization on the other? If the concept remains unnamed, how should we define it, and what are its boundary conditions—for example, must the original agreement be entirely forgotten, or is it enough that its arbitrary character be suppressed?
