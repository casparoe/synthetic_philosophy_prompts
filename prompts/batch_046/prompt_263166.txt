Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on material constitution, and I now suspect it proves far more than I intended. I would like to know which step fails, why it looked plausible, and what giving it up costs.

(1) At any time t, if object A and object B are composed of exactly the same parts arranged in exactly the same spatiotemporal configuration, then A and B share every intrinsic physical property at t.
(2) The statue Form and the lump of marble Matter are, at t, composed of the same parts in the same configuration.
(3) Therefore Form and Matter share every intrinsic physical property at t.
(4) Consequently, if they differ at all, the difference must be extrinsic: it must depend on relations such as artistic intention, institutional recognition, or historical origin.
(5) Now suppose Form was designed in London but physically carved in Carrara by artisans who executed only geometric instructions, never grasping the artistic concept. The physical result at the Carrara site is part-for-part identical to Form.
(6) At that site, there is no local artistic intention directed at the particular arrangement; the relevant intentions reside elsewhere—in design files, in the designer’s mind, in institutional records in London.
(7) By (4), if Form exists in Carrara, its existence depends on an extrinsic relation to distant mental states.
(8) But material constitution is ordinarily taken to be locally supervenient: whether a statue exists here should depend on what is arranged here, not on mental states elsewhere.
(9) So either Form does not exist in Carrara despite being physically present, or local supervenience fails and constitution is globally determined.
(10) If constitution is globally determined, then every marble arrangement everywhere is either Form or not Form on the basis of distant intentions, which entails either that infinitely many globally-coincident artworks occupy the same space or that none do.
(11) Both outcomes are intolerable: we do not want to deny that statues exist locally, nor do we want an ontology of infinitely many coincident objects with contradictory properties.
(12) Hence one of premises (1) through (9) must be false.

The argument seems valid, yet the conclusion feels like a reductio. Which premise or inference should I reject? Is there a subtle equivocation between intrinsic physical properties and constitution-determining properties, a scope shift in the move from (4) to (7), or a modal slide hidden in (8)? If I keep (1) and (4), must I abandon local supervenience, and what does that do to ordinary cases? If I reject (4), how do I explain the difference between Form and Matter without invoking something that sounds like a mysterious non-physical essence? Please diagnose the weakest link and price the alternatives.
