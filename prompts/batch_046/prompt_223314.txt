My philosophy of language seminar distributed a summary of two-dimensional semantics that runs as follows. Kaplan’s framework assigns every expression a primary intension—what it denotes when worlds are evaluated as if they were actual—and a secondary intension—what it denotes under counterfactual evaluation—so that Water is H2O comes out necessary a posteriori: the primary intension picks out the transparent, drinkable liquid in each world, while the secondary intension rigidly tracks H2O everywhere. The summary then distinguishes weak from strong two-dimensionalism, claiming that only the strong version—where primary intensions are conceptually necessary and determine metaphysical possibility—can support the zombie argument, while weak 2Dism is merely an epistemic bookkeeping device. Applying the strong version to consciousness, it says phenomenal concepts have primary intensions that are epistemically transparent, presenting the same qualitative feel regardless of physical implementation, whereas physical descriptions have primary intensions that vary with implementation. Because of this asymmetry, the summary claims, Chalmers’ conceivability argument succeeds: a physically identical zombie world is genuinely conceivable, proving phenomenal concepts are conceptually isolated from physical ones. It concludes that critics like Soames miss the point by treating the framework as a redundant notational variant of standard modal logic, since they ignore this conceptual isolation and fail to see that primary intensions do real metaphysical work.

I am inclined to think the summary captures the spirit of Chalmers’ view but may overstate what the formal machinery actually delivers, and I am unsure whether Soames is being characterized fairly. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what is technically accurate about Kaplan’s original framework versus Chalmers’ adaptation, what is a common simplification, and what subtly misrepresents either the semantics or the critics—especially regarding weak versus strong 2Dism, the move from conceivability to metaphysical possibility, and the actual target of Soames’s objections. In particular, distinguish settled technical points from genuinely contested philosophical claims, and do not present a contested interpretation of two-dimensionalism’s metaphysical import as if it were the consensus view among philosophers of language and mind.
