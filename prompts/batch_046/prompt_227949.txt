I was arguing with a friend about Carrol's tortoise — you know, the dialogue where Achilles keeps stacking premises and the tortoise still won't apply modus ponens? — and she said it sounds exactly like Phil Connors in Groundhog Day. Every morning he wakes up with the same premises (Feb 2, Punxsutawney, same people) and uses behavioral rules he learned the loop before (when to catch the falling kid, how to talk to Rita, exactly when the truck hits). But he never actually justifies why those rules should carry over to this iteration; he just assumes the pattern holds and optimizes inside it.

So I'd like to read Groundhog Day through that lens. Does the film dramatize Carroll's point that deduction never gets off the ground without a kind of practical surrender — Phil just deciding "today works like yesterday" without being able to prove it? Or does his escape break the analogy, since he breaks the loop not by adding another logical premise but by becoming less selfish? I'm especially curious about the diner scene where he predicts everything, the piano lessons where skill accumulates across loops, and the groundhog kidnapping. Does the movie accidentally suggest that the tortoise's regress is solved by moral transformation rather than logic? And where does the comparison fall apart — is Phil even reasoning, or just remembering?
