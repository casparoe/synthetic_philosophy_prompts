As a novelist drafting a manuscript on nineteenth-century ascetic communities, I have adopted a moral framework for the narrative that I now suspect may be internally strained, and I want a rigorous consistency check before I commit to it. My philosophical profile on value and representation is as follows: (1) I hold tranquilism in its strong form—only the absence of craving and suffering carries axiological weight, while positive pleasure, satisfaction, and emotional richness are morally neutral; (2) I maintain that fictional suffering is ethically consequential solely to the extent that it produces genuine craving or psychological distress in an actual reader, possessing no intrinsic moral status as representation; (3) I believe the highest function of tragic art is catharsis, understood precisely as the structured generation and subsequent release of intense emotional craving; (4) I claim that aesthetic excellence in fiction correlates directly with the intensity and complexity of emotional states the text produces; (5) I accept evolutionary debunking arguments against the idea that our narrative cravings track any objective value, yet I continue to treat induced reader-craving as a real, non-instrumental harm. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear so and dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, take a definite position on which commitment must be abandoned rather than merely outlining options, and keep the analysis to no more than 350 words. Where your reading relies on a non-standard interpretation of tranquilism—particularly regarding whether catharsis counts as a reduction in net suffering—state that explicitly.
