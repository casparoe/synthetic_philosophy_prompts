I'm writing a feature for an ideas journal on whether Hume's standard of taste can survive the age of generative and emergent art. To ground the piece, I've been tracking the published rulings of Professor Elena Vasquez, a philosopher who applies Hume's framework to contemporary aesthetic disputes. Her verdicts so far are these.

First, on a nineteenth-century forgery of a Vermeer that fooled curators for decades: once the deception was revealed, Vasquez agreed with the curators that the canvas was technically admirable but aesthetically hollow, even though its visible appearance had not changed in the slightest. Second, on a crystal formation in a cave that happens to replicate, by pure mineral accident, the proportions and color harmony of Chartres Cathedral's north rose window: visitors report an awe indistinguishable from that felt before the actual window, yet Vasquez rules it is not beautiful, because the appropriate sentiment requires awareness of human artistic design behind the form. Third, on an installation of rusted industrial scrap that is viscerally repulsive to lay audiences but which a small circle of refined critics defend as achieving a higher-order harmony precisely through its critical dissonance: Vasquez sides with the critics and declares it beautiful, despite the popular revulsion. Fourth, on a symphony reconstructed by an algorithm that fills gaps in a damaged manuscript with statistically probable phrases: musicologists stress that no single human mind authored the transitional passages, yet Vasquez rules the result beautiful, arguing that the listener's sentiment is properly directed at the audible structure and at the fragments' genuine provenance.

Now Vasquez must rule on Case Five. It concerns an autopoietic gallery installation: after an initial programming by an artist, a self-modifying sound system evolves in real time, using audience biometric feedback—heart rate, galvanic skin response—to generate harmonic sequences. No human selects, edits, or approves the outputs. During one exhibition the system produces a ten-minute sequence that refined listeners, fully informed of its emergent and non-authorial origin, describe as profoundly moving and structurally complex. The sequence has never occurred before and will not recur.

If Vasquez wants to be consistent with herself, what should she rule about this sequence? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? I want a committed answer, not a neutral map: identify at least two plausible rules that genuinely fit her track record—perhaps an informed-refined-response rule versus a causal-provenance rule—say exactly what each predicts about the emergent sequence, defend one prediction as the best fit with reasons, and specify the principle Vasquez would be adopting either way, including what she gains and what she must pay to keep it. Also name the single further hypothetical case—one that has not yet occurred—that would break the tie and reveal which rule she is actually running, not merely which one she ought to run.
