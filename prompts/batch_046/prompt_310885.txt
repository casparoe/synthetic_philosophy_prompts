I am trying to reconstruct Pascal's position on justice and force for a graduate seminar on early modern political thought, and I want a consistency check on the package I have assembled. Here are the commitments I take him to hold, drawn mainly from the fragments on law, custom, and the orders:

(1) Human reason is too weak and corrupt to discover any natural, rational principle of justice; consequently, the only available guide is the reason of effects, by which we accept established possession and custom as the basis of legal authority.

(2) Positive law owes its force not to justice but to power and to the imagination; nevertheless, subjects have a strict duty to obey the established order regardless of its arbitrariness, because the evil of civil disorder is greater than the evil of enduring an unjust law.

(3) The sole legitimate ground for resistance is a direct contradiction between human law and divine law; the revealed justice that belongs to the order of charity — accessible only through grace — does not otherwise function as a practical standard for evaluating positive law.

(4) Pascal rejects Hobbes's rationalist account of authority (grounded in calculated self-interest or rational consent) not only because reason is corrupt, but because custom and imagination operate independently of rational calculation, and social stability requires that the people believe their laws are just even though their true foundation in hidden force must remain concealed.

(5) Because legitimacy is purely conventional — a function of possession and customary acceptance with no residual justice-criterion — any successful revolution that establishes a new order immediately generates an equally valid claim to obedience, and there is no transcendent standard by which the new regime could be judged illegitimate.

(6) True justice exists objectively, but it belongs to the supernatural order of charity rather than to the political order of bodies and minds; it explains the persistent human dissatisfaction with merely conventional justice, yet it remains practically inert in politics except in the case of direct conflict with divine commands.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision — to one of these six commitments, or to one implicit assumption linking them — would clean up the most problems at once. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Also, say how confident you are in this audit and single out the specific point where you are least sure.
