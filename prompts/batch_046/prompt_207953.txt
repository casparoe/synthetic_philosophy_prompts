I'm preparing a position paper for a graduate seminar on collective moral agency, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to the framing. Over the past term I've landed on the following package of views, which feels coherent in conversation but may hide structural cracks. First, I accept a strong version of the causal inefficacy objection: in climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and large-scale democratic elections, my individual action is counterfactually redundant—the collective outcome is invariant across my specific choice, and I reject probabilistic rescue attempts unless they rest on an agent-relative baseline that I regard as ad hoc in these contexts. Second, despite this redundancy, I think there is a genuine moral reason to reduce my carbon footprint, avoid animal products, and vote in national elections—but I deny that this reason is causal, and I explicitly reject "social signaling" or "leading by example" as its primary justification. Third, I ground the reason in what I call participatory non-complicity: I should not be a co-author or token-participant in a collectively harmful practice, even when my withdrawal doesn't diminish its scale. At the same time, I reject "clean hands" moralism—I don't think abstaining purifies me, only that participating implicates me. Fourth, I apply this uniformly across domains, though I recognize a structural distinction: in climate and meat, I'm a node in a harmful causal network, whereas in voting I'm a token in a representational practice that can legitimize harmful governance; despite this difference, the moral logic is the same. Fifth, I hold that moral obligations must be individually action-guiding without requiring the agent to solve collective coordination problems first, otherwise morality makes unreasonable demands. Sixth, I maintain that if an action is genuinely redundant in all respects—deterministically, probabilistically, and structurally—and imposes a severe personal burden, then there is no all-things-considered obligation to perform it; this is why I think recycling in a municipality with no processing facility, or boycotting a product with perfect unavailable substitutes, carries no such duty.

My question is whether these commitments actually hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension—especially where a standard motivation for one view undermines another—and which only appear to conflict until you trace what each actually commits me to? I'm particularly unsure whether my uniform application across climate, meat, and voting survives the node-versus-token distinction I myself introduced, and whether my redundancy condition in the sixth commitment doesn't quietly swallow the participatory reasons in the third and fourth. I'd also like you to rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency, and flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed. Please treat this as an audit of my belief set, not a general lecture on collective action, and do not suggest that I resolve the tensions by appealing to bibliographic sources or further reading—I'm interested in the internal logic of the package as it stands.
