I'm preparing a long-form piece on whether empirical psychology has actually dismantled virtue ethics, and I've constructed an argument that strikes me as a reductio—except I'm no longer sure where the error hides. Here is the chain as I understand it. (1) Virtue ethics holds that moral virtues are stable, cross-situational dispositions: an honest or compassionate agent should perceive, feel, and act in recognizably consistent ways across varied contexts, not just in isolated moments. (2) The situationist literature—from Milgram's obedience protocols through the classic Good Samaritan study with seminary students to more recent meta-analyses—finds cross-situational behavioral correlations in the range of r ≈ .30, explaining roughly nine percent of variance in observed behavior. (3) If a psychological construct explains less than a tenth of behavioral variance, it lacks the predictive and explanatory power required for a real causal kind; by standard philosophy-of-science elimination criteria, it should be discarded rather than retained as a disposition. (4) So global character traits—courage, honesty, compassion—do not exist as virtue ethics describes them, and virtue ethics is empirically undermined. (5) But the same consistency coefficients apply to clinical constructs like anxiety and to broad personality dimensions like conscientiousness, which psychologists and clinicians continue to treat as real, measurable, and action-guiding. (6) Therefore, if we reject virtue-ethical traits on these grounds, consistency demands that we reject all trait-based psychology, moral education, legal responsibility, and long-range practical reasoning that depends on stable agency. (7) Because global trait eliminativism is plainly unacceptable—no serious proposal suggests abolishing personality assessment in medicine or law—the argument proves too much. Where does it actually break? Is the mistake in (3), treating low correlation as non-existence rather than as situation-sensitive expression? Or is the flaw in (1), where virtue ethics requires only normative competence, practical wisdom, or second-order responsiveness rather than behavioral uniformity? Or is (6) an overreach, since one might accept local, domain-specific traits without committing to global ones? I want to know which premise is the weakest link, why it seemed defensible, and what each standard reply—situationist, interactionist, virtue-theoretic—must concede to abandon it. If my framing rests on a confusion about what situationists actually claim, or if the argument collapses for reasons I haven't seen, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
