I chair the acquisitions committee for a regional gallery of contemporary practice, and each month we must decide whether proposed works merit public funding as genuine art. I want to turn Tolstoy's account of art — that it is essentially the infection of the audience with a sincerely felt emotion, with false art being either insincere or substituting craft, spectacle, and technical display for that emotional transmission — into a reusable decision procedure my committee can run in thirty minutes per proposal. Please write the procedure as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the stopping points where the answer is no, and the explicit points where judgement about values must replace factual determination. Apply it to three proposals: (a) a generative installation in which an algorithm trained on grief narratives produces adaptive soundscapes in response to visitors' heart-rate variability, with its creators claiming the artistic feeling is distributed between human and machine; (b) a meticulously rendered painting of the artist's childhood trauma that the artist calls cathartic but that pilot viewers find technically impressive yet emotionally remote; and (c) an immersive, widely popular spectacle designed solely to induce wonder and pleasure through light and sound, with no claim to deeper emotional transmission — include this as the case where the procedure gives an answer the committee will resist. Clearly flag what the procedure cannot settle, such as whether an algorithm can possess sincerity or whether a morally repugnant but genuinely infectious feeling counts as true art. Separate what is settled in Tolstoy's theory from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of his aesthetics, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
