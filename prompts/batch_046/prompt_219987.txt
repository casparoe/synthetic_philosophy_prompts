I am designing a handout for a graduate seminar in population ethics. The seminar’s angle is whether the repugnant conclusion can be avoided without abandoning the idea that well-being is the only fundamental value. Write a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with exactly five binary branching nodes—that sorts positions in this logical space by what they say about concrete comparisons, not by the labels people use.

The tree should begin with this root case: World Small contains exactly 1,000 people, each at welfare 100; World Large contains exactly 10 trillion people, each at welfare 0.1—lives barely worth living, with no suffering and no interaction effects. From there, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names.

The five nodes should be concrete questions a student could answer without knowing philosophical jargon:
1. Whether World Large is ever morally better than World Small.
2. If yes, whether welfare values simply add up with constant weight across people (so that one hundred people at welfare 1 contribute exactly the same as one person at welfare 100).
3. If no, whether the judgment is driven mainly by comparing average welfare per person—so that adding people with welfare below the current average always makes things worse.
4. If not average-based, whether there is a sharp welfare threshold (a critical level) below which a life does not contribute positively, even if it is worth living for the person who lives it.
5. If there is such a threshold, whether a more equal distribution of welfare above that threshold makes a world better, holding population size constant.

Every reachable combination of answers should terminate in a leaf carrying: (a) the name of the position if it is standard in the literature (e.g., classical total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, standard critical-level utilitarianism); (b) the label ‘unnamed’ or ‘hybrid’ if the combination is not standardly defended; and (c) exactly one sentence on its most awkward commitment—such as that destroying the happiest people can improve the world, or that there is an arbitrary-looking welfare cutoff.

In addition, flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot separate—positions that give identical answers to all five questions. Note at least one unoccupied logical combination within the tree’s reachable space—some combination of answers that no named view occupies—and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Finally, embed one concrete sub-case about intergenerational replacement—where every person in World Small dies and is replaced by a different generation at welfare 99—to show where person-affecting asymmetry and standard critical-level views come apart, even if your tree does not fully isolate them. The audience is graduate students; the tree should have no more than eight leaves.
