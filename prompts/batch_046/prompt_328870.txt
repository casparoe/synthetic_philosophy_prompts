Exam question: In his well-known reply to Benjamin Constant, Kant argues that one must not lie even to a murderer at the door who asks whether one's friend is hidden inside. Explain Kant's reasoning, distinguishing it from a rule-utilitarian prohibition on deception, and evaluate whether his distinction between lying and withholding information survives cases in which omission is calculated to mislead in exactly the same manner as an explicit falsehood.

Student answer: Kant treats lying as a violation of a perfect duty to humanity, an argument he develops in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals. Because perfect duties allow no exceptions based on expected consequences, telling the murderer a false location is categorically forbidden. Kant rejects Constant's consequentialist appeal—good outcomes cannot license falsehood because moral worth depends on the form of the maxim, not on empirical results. The student notes that Kant permits silence or refusal to answer, which demonstrates he is not demanding self-incrimination but only prohibiting direct false assertion. From this, the student infers that Kant's system implicitly ranks perfect duties above imperfect ones, so that when the perfect duty not to lie conflicts with the imperfect duty of beneficence—namely, saving the friend's life—the perfect duty automatically takes precedence. The student also proposes that in a modern mediated case, such as an automated security system programmed to issue a false statement about occupancy without any direct human act of will, Kant's prohibition might fail to apply because the deception lacks the direct agency his moral theory requires.
