Consider the certification and liability protocol adopted for Level-3 autonomous vehicles in several jurisdictions: a remote human supervisor must remain available to take control upon system alert, yet tort frameworks assign strict liability to that supervisor for any harm occurring within a 500-millisecond window following the alert, even when physiological reaction times and false-positive rates make successful intervention impossible; conversely, if the supervisor preemptively intervenes without an alert—anticipating a risk the system fails to register—liability shifts entirely to the manufacturer for any resulting malfunction. What philosophical conception of agency, causal responsibility, and the conditions for moral blame does this bifurcated arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the policy: does it treat the human as a causal backstop, a symbolic guarantee of retained sovereignty, or a designated locus of blame regardless of actual influence? Examine whether the two liability branches rest on consistent views—particularly about whether responsibility tracks available control, exercised control, or institutional role alone. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely accommodates, identify any internal tension between its parts, and specify how certification criteria and tort doctrine would have to be restructured if the underlying view were replaced by one that assigns responsibility solely according to actual causal contribution rather than assigned position.
