I have recently been struggling with a recurring pattern of abandoning long-term commitments—writing projects, health routines, financial discipline—despite sincerely endorsing them at the moment of resolution, and a clinician suggested I examine hyperbolic discounting not as a loose metaphor but as a specific mechanism of intertemporal preference reversal. That framework sent me back to Tolkien’s The Lord of the Rings, which I first read as an adolescent and now find philosophically unsettling in an entirely different register. Read the Mount Doom episode and Gollum’s entire arc through the lens of weakness of will, specifically combining the hyperbolic discounting model of akrasia with Ainslie’s picoeconomic account of intertemporal bargaining and private side bets. I want a reading that keeps the psychological and economic frameworks distinct rather than collapsing them into one another: where does the Ring’s corruption map onto a steepened hyperbolic discount curve, and where does it map onto a collapse of the bargaining structure by which a present self binds a future self? Is Frodo’s final refusal to destroy the Ring, together with his abrupt assertion that it belongs to him, best understood as a failure of private side-betting, as a catastrophic preference reversal under extreme discounting, or as something closer to Elster’s adaptive preference formation, in which the agent revalues the object precisely to avoid the cost of relinquishing it? How should we interpret Gollum: as a limit case in which the present self has permanently won the intertemporal game, or as a case in which the very structure of diachronic agency has dissolved altogether? I am particularly interested in whether Tolkien’s Catholic framing—mercy, providence, and Gollum’s accidental role in the Ring’s destruction—introduces something the secular psychological models systematically miss, namely that weakness of will may be redeemed not by stronger internal commitment but by an external interruption of the preference sequence itself. Be disciplined about where the philosophical apparatus genuinely illuminates the text and where it forces the text: does Tolkien actually care about the temporal architecture of choice, or am I importing it? If the reading holds, what does this particular literary case contribute to the philosophical debate about whether akrasia is best explained by divided selves, by distorted valuation, or by adaptive preference formation? I am preparing this for a philosophy-of-literature essay, and I want to avoid reducing the novel to a mere illustration, just as I want to avoid letting literary particularity obscure the mechanics of the theory.
