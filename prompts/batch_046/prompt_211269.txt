I am moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher—call her the guest—who defends a specific form of moral expressivism she terms "layered expressivism." On her view, an unembedded moral assertion such as "Stealing is wrong" expresses a non-cognitive attitude of disapproval; however, when that same clause is embedded in a logical compound—for instance, in a conditional ("If stealing is wrong, then..."), a negation ("It is not the case that stealing is wrong"), or a disjunction—its semantic contribution shifts. Rather than expressing an attitude, the embedded clause expresses a second-order cognitive commitment to the inferential norms that govern that attitude. She presents this as a direct response to the Frege-Geach embedding problem, arguing that it preserves the expressivist core for ordinary assertion while accommodating the logical behavior of embedded moral sentences without collapsing into cognitivism.

Please construct a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that engage the actual mechanics of this position rather than asking for a general overview or biography. Specifically, include:

1. A question that presses how layered expressivism treats negation in embedded contexts—distinguishing, for example, the semantic function of "Stealing is not wrong" when asserted outright from its function when embedded inside a larger conditional or disjunction—together with the reply she is most likely to offer and a follow-up that exploits a tension in that reply.

2. A question that challenges whether the cognitive commitments assigned to embedded clauses are substantive enough that the view becomes indistinguishable from a hybrid cognitivist theory (such as one that treats moral judgments as beliefs about normative reasons coupled with accompanying attitudes), including her anticipated defense and a counter-follow-up.

3. A question about the "mixed compound" problem: a conditional whose antecedent is moral and whose consequent is non-moral and descriptive (for example, "If torturing innocents is wrong, then the regime will eventually collapse"). Ask how her view assigns uniform semantic content across the antecedent and consequent given that one expresses (or governs) an attitude and the other does not, anticipate her answer, and pose a follow-up that tests whether the uniformity she claims can survive without ad hoc stipulation.

4. At least two further questions that probe other pressure points—such as higher-order embedding (e.g., beliefs about what someone believes about the wrongness of an act), the psychological realism of holding both a first-order attitude and a separate cognitive commitment to its inferential rules simultaneously, or the normative question of why logical consistency should constrain us if moral discourse is fundamentally expressive rather than representational.

For each question, present the question itself, the answer she is most likely to give, and then a precise follow-up keyed specifically to that answer.

Finally, in your response, indicate your overall confidence in the framing of these questions and in the anticipated replies, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure—for instance, whether the layered distinction can be maintained without collapsing into cognitivism, whether the inferential-rule commitments genuinely avoid the Frege-Geach problem, or whether the mixed-compound reply can be rendered coherent.
