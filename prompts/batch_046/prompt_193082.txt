Write a dialogue between two people, call them Priya and Leo, trying to figure out if teh Holocaust is genuinely incomparable or if that claim just depends on which feature you pick. Priya is convinced by the Bauman-style argument: the uniqueness isn't scale but the modern bureaucratic fusion — census tech, division of labor, rational administration — that creates "moral distancing" so killing feels like logistics. Leo isn't convinced that's unique; he thinks colonial administrative violence and even some industrial disasters involve similar distancing, and that saying "this is different because it's modern" risks making uniqueness a circular defense of modernity's self-image. They should actually argue: Priya should say it's not just distancing but the specific combination of state capacity, racial pseudoscience, and industrial method; Leo should ask whether that combo is historically singular or just the version we happen to study, and whether uniqueness is doing hidden normative work. To push it past generic debate, have them wrestle with a conceptual hypothetical: imagine we discover that a pre-modern empire had census networks and rapid logistics (impossible in reality, but conceptually) and used them to organize mass death with almost no direct violence. Would that break the modernity-uniqueness link, or would defenders just shift to "modern self-awareness" as the new criterion? Keep it casual and make sure neither talks past the other — real back-and-forth, no strawmen.
