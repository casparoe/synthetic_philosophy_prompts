I am drafting my position for a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, and I want a consistency check before I defend it. My commitments are these. First, I read Johannes de Silentio's portrait of Abraham as genuinely normative: Abraham is a knight of faith, and his readiness to sacrifice Isaac is religiously exemplary rather than pathological or merely symbolic. Second, I take the teleological suspension of the ethical literally—there are conditions under which the universal moral law is genuinely set aside for the sake of an individual's absolute relation to the absolute. Third, I also hold that moral obligations are categorical and exceptionless: no subjective conviction, however sincere, can render an objectively wrong act permissible. Fourth, I maintain that had Abraham actually killed Isaac, the act would have been filicide—gravely wrong irrespective of any purported divine command. Fifth, I reject allegorical or psychological reductions of the binding of Isaac; the narrative concerns an actual willingness to perform an immoral act. Sixth, I regard the pseudonymous authorship as philosophically irrelevant here—Kierkegaard endorses the view presented, rather than merely staging it dialectically. Seventh, I want to preserve a distinction between genuinely suspending the ethical teleologically and merely violating it accidentally, and I believe this distinction protects Abraham's religious status without entailing that his intended act was morally right.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how should I rank them by severity? For each real tension, specify the least costly revision—whether modifying my categorical claim, softening the literal reading of suspension, reinterpreting Abraham's exemplarity, or abandoning the distinction in (7). In particular, say whether (7) actually does the work I assign it, or whether it collapses into one of the other commitments. I am not attached to any one revision; I want the audit to show me where the least damage lies.
