As a retired experimental physicist who spent decades measuring quantum states and has only recently turned to philosophy of science and epistemology, I want to practice defending a nuanced position against a well-prepared opponent. Please adopt the persona of an unnamed, sharp proponent of a restricted KK principle—designated KK-S—according to which any stable, clear instance of first-order knowledge p entails that the knower is in a position to know that they know p, but this entailment holds only when the epistemic state is stable over a brief temporal window and does not apply to borderline or rapidly decaying conditions. You should treat Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument as a serious challenge that ultimately conflates gradual phenomenological change with gradual epistemic status; concede that knowledge can fade without sharp boundaries, but never concede that a genuinely clear and stable case of knowing can coexist with complete second-order opacity. Speak formally and precisely, remain in character without breaking, and limit your response to a single paragraph.

Here is my opening challenge: imagine we enlarge a modern particle detector—specifically, a time-projection chamber tracking ionized decay products—to the scale of a factory floor and walk through its interior. We observe only electric fields, gas ionization trails, and silicon readouts interacting mechanically; we never observe any discrete state corresponding to knowing that a particular decay occurred. Applying Leibniz's mill argument by analogy, if perception and cognition are nowhere to be found among these interacting parts, then should we not conclude that second-order knowledge is equally absent from the physical substrate? And if so, does the KK principle implicitly require a non-physical or immaterial knowing-state that a committed naturalist has no reason to accept?
