I've been turning over that old story about a merchant who flees Baghdad for Damascus to escape Death, only to meet Death there and hear that they had an appointment in Samarra all along. I want to understand the paradox without relying on formal philosophy texts, so I have been framing it through two mental pictures. The first, which I call the rail-model, treats destiny as one fixed line: every detour is pre-accounted for, so the merchant never really had an open path away from death, and Death's surprise is just rhetorical. The second, which I call the web-model, treats destiny as branching: Death is in Damascus only because the merchant actually went there, so his flight caused the meeting rather than failing to avoid it. Could you compare these two models, specifically on whether the merchant kept a real choice in leaving Baghdad and whether Death expresses genuine ignorance? Then I want to test a third idea I have not seen discussed: what if the contradiction dissolves not by changing destiny's shape, but by changing what Death is—not a conscious agent with foreknowledge, but an impersonal record-keeping system that registers outcomes after they happen? Would that let the story hold together without committing us to either a rail or a web?
