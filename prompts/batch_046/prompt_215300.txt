Trying to figure out what I actually think about failure in Athenian democracy, going by gut on cases. The Mytilene debate: they vote to kill every male citizen, then reverse it within a day. My verdict -- not a real failure, its self-correction working, even if the first vote was monstrous. The Sicily expedition is a failure, but a specific one: the demos got swept up by Alcibiades and ignored distance, supply, and local alliances, so its hubris/emotional cascade, not ignorance. The trial of Socrates feels different -- they executed a guy for asking questions, which looks like normative closure, democracy punishing the doubt it needs. Then there's the Arginusae trial right after a naval victory, where they killed the generals for not recovering bodies; I put that with Sicily (scapegoating). But I'm torn on the ostracism of Hyperbolus -- technically democratic, targeted a real demagogue, yet feels unfair since he wasn't uniquely bad. So is that failure or immune response? And what about building the long walls: strategically sound but deepened class divisions. Is there one principle that sorts these -- maybe failures happen when deliberation lacks distance (time, info, emotional cooling) -- or are there genuinely different kinds (logistical, emotional, epistemic) that don't reduce? What's the principle, and which of my verdicts does it have to overturn?
