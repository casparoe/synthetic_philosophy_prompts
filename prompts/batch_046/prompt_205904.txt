Write a decision-tree taxonomy for the debate over why promises bind, designed so that a reader can locate their own view without knowing the literature. The tree should branch on four concrete questions: (i) whether the binding force of a promise derives primarily from the harm done to disappointed expectations or from the promisor's exercise of a normative power to create obligations; (ii) whether the obligation survives if the promisee explicitly releases the promisor from it; (iii) whether a promise can generate direct obligations for third parties who never consented to it; and (iv) whether breaking a promise involves a moral wrong that is not reducible to the wrong of causing the same harm through a non-promissory act. At each leaf, state the position's standard descriptive label (for example, expectation-based, autonomy-based, practice-based, conventionalist) or mark it "unnamed" if no prominent view occupies that combination, and in one sentence identify the commitment that makes that leaf awkward or costly. Structure the tree by answers, not by author names: every internal node should be phrased as a direct claim or concrete case that a non-specialist can answer. After the tree, flag any pair of well-known positions that your branching structure fails to separate, and explain why one logical combination of answers remains unoccupied—whether because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Conclude with a worked application: trace the path for a respondent who answers (i) normative power, (ii) yes, (iii) no, (iv) yes; name the leaf reached, state whether that combination is actually defended in the literature, and note what the result reveals about whether the four questions are jointly sufficient to classify views in this debate. Keep the taxonomy usable as a classroom handout: avoid technical jargon where a plainer phrase works, but do not drop distinctions that actually change the answer.
