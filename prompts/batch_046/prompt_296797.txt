Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a moral particularist and a moral generalist on the status of principles in ethical reasoning. The collaboration should open with agreed claims about the formal structure of moral arguments—what a principle must do if it exists (guide action, explain judgments, be learnable)—and then isolate the precise crux: whether particularism denies that moral reasons have any principled structure whatsoever, or only that all such structures are irreducibly defeasible and context-sensitive. Both sides should state, in wording the other accepts, their predictions about the cognitive architecture of expert moral judgment—specifically, whether mature moral reasoning operates through explicit rule-application or through holistic pattern-recognition that resists codification without being arational. Include a disagreements table that distinguishes substantive conflicts from verbal ones, especially regarding whether “principle” is being used for an exceptionless rule, a defeasible default, or a constitutive framework. Each side should record what empirical finding or conceptual argument would genuinely weaken its position, and both should sign off on shared predictions about how moral expertise behaves under novel case pressure. End with a neutral summary of the residual disagreement and any jointly endorsed methodological recommendations for future work. The response should state how confident it is in each major claim and explicitly identify the point about which it is least sure.
