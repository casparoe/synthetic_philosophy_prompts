Exam question:

Consider the philosophical debate over whether statistical independence should be treated as a default or "null" hypothesis in causal reasoning. Some authors defend a "presumption of independence" heuristic: absent specific reasons to think otherwise, we should treat variables as probabilistically independent. Others, following Reichenbach, argue that correlations demand explanation via direct causation or a common cause.

(a) State the presumption of independence heuristic and explain how it differs from Reichenbach's principle of the common cause.
(b) Identify one formal framework used to codify causal dependencies, and explain how it treats independence relations structurally.
(c) A researcher observes a correlation between educational attainment and health outcomes in a dataset that conditions on employment status. The correlation disappears when employment status is not conditioned upon. Explain whether this is best understood as a failure of the presumption of independence or as a feature of the formal framework, and justify your answer.

Student answer:

(a) The presumption of independence is the heuristic that, absent specific causal or evidential links, we should treat two variables as probabilistically independent. Reichenbach's principle of the common cause deals with the opposite case: if two variables are correlated and neither causes the other, a common cause must screen them off. Thus, the presumption is a default assumption of no correlation, whereas Reichenbach's principle explains observed correlations.

(b) Reichenbach formalized this default in his principle of screening off, which was later developed into the causal Markov condition central to Bayesian networks. The Markov condition states that any variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents. In practice, this means that missing edges in the directed graph encode independence, and all dependencies are mediated through direct paths. So the heuristic is built directly into the graph structure.

(c) Here, employment status is a collider influenced by both education and health. Conditioning on it creates a spurious correlation in the sample that disappears in the full population. This is not a failure of the presumption of independence—since the underlying variables may still be marginally independent—but rather a feature of the formal framework. By including the selection mechanism in the graph and applying d-separation, we restore the Markov condition over the observed variables and derive the exact conditional dependence seen in the data. Hence, the correlation actually confirms the framework rather than undermining it.
