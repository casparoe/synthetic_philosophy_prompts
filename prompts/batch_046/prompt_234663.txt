The absent-minded driver problem is familiar: a driver intends to stop at a pharmacy on his usual route home, knows the road well, drives past the exit, and realizes his error only blocks later with genuine surprise. The debate concerns whether this movement is an intentional action that went wrong—perhaps a standing intention was active but “backgrounded,” overridden by habit, with surprise marking a failure of monitoring—or whether it is not an action at all but automatized behavior occurring in the absence of any occurrent, guiding mental state. For decades, continuity theorists have insisted that the driver retains a metaphysical relation of intending-to-stop that is merely suspended, while discontinuity theorists have insisted that without an online representation in working memory there is no guiding intention and therefore no action, only behavior continuous with reflex.

What frustrates me is that both sides agree on the phenomenology (the driver feels he should have stopped, can recount the route, is not asleep), agree on the neuroscience of habitual driving, and agree that responsibility is attenuated but not absent.
