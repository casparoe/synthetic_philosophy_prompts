Here is an argument I constructed for a philosophy of mind seminar and now find suspicious because it seems to undercut the very reasoning used to defend it. (1) Eliminative materialism is justified if folk-psychological states like belief and desire lack precise, unified neural correlates and resist reduction to neurobiology. (2) The same heterogeneous, distributed profile applies to rational inference, theory evaluation, and epistemic commitment — none have a single neural substrate, and all involve social and linguistic scaffolding. (3) So if the absence of a precise neural locus justifies elimination, then rational inference itself should be eliminated. (4) But eliminative materialism is presented as the rational conclusion of scientific reasoning; if rational inference does not exist, there is no rational pathway to the conclusion. (5) Therefore, either the eliminativist abandons the claim that rational inference supports her view, making it arbitrary, or she admits that heterogeneous, distributed processes can still ground real cognitive kinds, in which case beliefs and desires survive by the same standard. (6) So the position either collapses into self-defeat or loses its eliminative force. Which step is the weak link? Is (2) an illicit extension from ontological claims about beliefs to epistemic claims about reasoning, or does (4) confuse the existence of rational inference with its neural reducibility? If you want to preserve the core denial of beliefs and desires, tell me which premise you reject and what that rejection costs: must you grant special epistemic status to scientific reasoning, accept a pragmatic rather than rational defense of eliminativism, or draw a principled line between cognitive states that can be distributed and those that cannot?
