I am revising the syllabus for my upper-division seminar on affect and intentionality, and I want to include an in-class exercise that forces students to discover their implicit commitments rather than selecting a familiar theoretical label. To that end, please construct a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with concrete branching cases—for the debate over what emotions essentially are. The instrument should be usable by students who have read only a brief introductory overview; every node must be answerable through reflection on a described scenario, not through knowledge of named theories or their canonical formulations.

Structure the tree around three successive distinctions, each presented as a concrete case or a direct claim about emotional experience. First, present the case of a subject who exhibits all the physiological markers typically associated with fear—pupil dilation, adrenaline release, startle reflex—while sincerely reporting that they feel nothing at all, not even a vague sense of unease. Ask whether this counts as a case of unconscious or purely bodily fear, or whether the absence of conscious affect removes it from the category of emotion altogether. Second, present the mirror case: a subject who calmly judges that a particular event is dangerous and requires avoidance, who can articulate exactly why it threatens their values, but who reports no bodily change and describes their state as purely cognitive assessment. Ask whether this judgmental state qualifies as fear in the absence of any felt or bodily dimension. Third, present a case in which two individuals share identical bodily perturbations—elevated heart rate, skin conductance changes—but one interprets them in light of an anticipated reward and reports excitement, while the other interprets them in light of an anticipated harm and reports anxiety. Ask whether the emotional difference here is best explained by a further cognitive interpretation of the bodily state, by a direct perceptual registration of different evaluative properties in the situation, or by a non-representational practical orientation—what we might call an affective framing or atmospheric attunement—toward the environment.

From these nodes, derive the logical space of answers. The leaves should include at least: a strict cognitivist view (propositional appraisal necessary and sufficient), a pure somatic-feeling view (conscious bodily affect necessary and sufficient), a perceptual-evaluative view (direct awareness of value-laden properties mediated by but not reducible to bodily change), and a practical-engagement or enactive view (emotion as a readiness-to-act or diffuse attunement that need not take a specific intentional object). Crucially, include at least one logically possible but apparently unoccupied branch—perhaps a view that insists on bodily feeling as essential yet denies that emotions have any intentional object whatsoever, or a view that accepts perceptual content but rejects any bodily mediation whatsoever—and diagnose briefly why that combination is either conceptually unstable, phenomenologically implausible, or merely unexplored. Where two of your named leaves end up with identical answers across all nodes, do not invent a spurious distinction; instead, flag them as indistinguishable on this instrument and explain what further question about transparency, social constitution, or normative accountability would be needed to separate them.

Please avoid framing any branch as "do you agree with James?" or "are you a cognitivist in the style of Nussbaum?" Instead, label branches only by the claim that splits them. At the leaves, you may attach familiar names for orientation—such as judgment theories, James-Lange feeling theories, perceptual theories, and enactive accounts—but the path through the tree must depend solely on the student's answers to the cases. Finally, note any position that your tree cannot cleanly accommodate, and specify whether the difficulty lies in the taxonomy's structure or in the view's internal coherence. The audience is advanced undergraduates who need to see where their own intuitions fall relative to the logical geography of the debate, not to memorize a map of the literature.
