Since my brother was sentenced to long-term incarceration eighteen months ago, I have served on our state's Correctional Design Ethics Review Board. Our mandate is to evaluate prison architecture and operational philosophy—not legal guilt, but whether a proposed facility treats punishment and rehabilitation as genuinely compatible. We have built a short but consistent track record across four precedents.

First, we endorsed Norway's Halden Prison for maximum-security inmates: private rooms, shared kitchens, vocational workshops, and guards who wear casual clothes and share meals with inmates. We found that normalization of daily life preserves the psychological conditions for future citizenship without compromising security. Second, we rejected an American supermax model patterned on Pelican Bay's Security Housing Unit—twenty-three-hour solitary confinement, severe sensory restriction, and minimal human contact—even for inmates convicted of violent offenses. We held that such conditions destroy the agency required for rehabilitation and exceed any legitimate security purpose. Third, we endorsed a Danish-style open campus with no perimeter walls, where inmates work in nearby towns and return for evening check-in, but we restricted approval to inmates within eighteen months of release. We reasoned that reintegration must be graduated; premature exposure to full liberty undermines both security and the developmental structure of the sentence. Fourth, we rejected a proposed South Korean "behavioral silence" facility: strict silence rules, regimented factory labor, biometric stress monitoring with automated rewards and punishments, and a prohibition on personal items. Despite its reported order and low violence, we concluded that it replaces moral development with behavioral conditioning, and that the absence of physical suffering is not equivalent to the presence of dignity.

We now face a proposal for a "circadian rehabilitation center" intended for violent offenders serving long sentences. Inmates would live in individual biomimetic pods optimized for sleep, light, temperature, and nutrition; they would undergo mandatory AI-guided cognitive-behavioral therapy and neurofeedback training; they would interact with family solely through immersive virtual presence; and security would be maintained entirely through predictive biometric algorithms and environmental design—no guards, no physical walls, no unscripted social spaces. Inmates report minimal stress and high satisfaction, but every behavior is algorithmically nudged, and access to virtual family visits is contingent on protocol compliance.

Please structure your response as follows.

1. The Candidate Principles (approximately 250 words): Identify the two or three rules that best explain all four of our precedents. For each, show precisely how it accounts for our endorsement of Halden, our rejection of supermax solitary, our conditional endorsement of the open campus, and our rejection of the behavioral-silence facility.

2. Application to the Biomimetic Proposal (approximately 250 words): State what each identified principle predicts about whether we should endorse or reject the circadian rehabilitation center. Be explicit about the reasoning that connects the principle to this specific design—particularly regarding the AI guidance, the virtual family presence, the absence of guards, and the algorithmic nudging.

3. Recommended Verdict (approximately 200 words): Give the decision—endorse or reject—that best coheres with our board's own track record. Identify which of the four precedents is the closest analog, and state honestly what underlying principle we would be adopting if we chose the opposite verdict.

4. The Crucial Test (approximately 150 words): Propose one further hypothetical facility design that would distinguish the competing principles you identified. Explain exactly what verdict each principle would yield on that case, and why that difference matters.
