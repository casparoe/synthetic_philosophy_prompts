I am preparing the research agenda for an interdisciplinary working group that sits at the intersection of philosophy of mathematics and formal verification. We are specifically concerned with how commitments about actual versus potential infinity should shape the design of proof assistants and model-checking tools that must reason about unbounded but discrete state spaces—systems where the number of possible configurations is not merely very large, but in principle non-terminating. In our preliminary discussions, we have found that standard treatments of Cantor’s theorem and Hilbert’s hotel tend to assume an actualist metaphysics that treats infinite collections as completed totalities, yet many of our computer-science collaborators operate with an implicit potentialism: they treat infinity as an unending process of generation rather than a given object. Meanwhile, a minority voice in our group has revived a stricter finitist challenge—not the familiar intuitionist rejection of the law of excluded middle, but the claim that even potential infinity is an illegitimate idealization when applied to physical or computational processes, because every actual computation is bounded by energy, time, and storage constraints.

Given this framing, I want a prioritized assessment of the open questions, unresolved tensions, and downstream implications in the philosophy of actual infinity—particularly those involving Hilbert’s hotel, Cantor’s diagonal argument, and finitist critiques—that genuinely matter for a team with limited resources, say one senior researcher and two graduate students over three years, who must decide which philosophical problems are worth engaging technically rather than treating as settled background. Which of these issues are upstream of the others, such that resolving one would restructure how the remaining ones are approached? Which are tractable with current logical and conceptual tools, and which risk being intractable metaphysical disputes that will not change how verification engineers design their abstraction layers? And which implications—whether for cosmological models that posit actual infinite space, for pedagogical strategies that teach set theory without reifying infinity, or for the formal representation of unbounded state spaces—are genuinely consequential, as opposed to being decorative philosophical gloss?

Please defend your ranking with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, dependency relations among the questions, and practical relevance to formal reasoning about unbounded systems—rather than offering a survey. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of canonical sources; I want a defended agenda that tells us where to place our limited intellectual capital and why the ordering would shift if our primary goal were purely foundational rather than applied to verification practice.
