I am working through my position for an ethics seminar and need a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package of commitments I currently hold:

(1) The Stoic dichotomy of control should be read as an epistemic and normative boundary on rational assent—what is eph’ hēmin is defined not by causal efficacy but by whether my assent or moral choice is the sole necessary and sufficient condition for the state in question.

(2) The modern therapeutic revival—cognitive-behavioral Stoicism that jettisons Stoic physics, cosmology, and cosmic determinism—is not a distortion but a legitimate philosophical development, because the normative core (the management of assent and desire) is structurally independent of that metaphysical framework.

(3) At the same time, I believe Stoicism is fully compatible with, and perhaps demands, sustained collective political action aimed at institutional reform, even though the outcomes depend on thousands of others and are therefore clearly outside my control.

(4) I also maintain that genuine apatheia requires emotional equanimity not only about personal misfortune but also about the failure of collective projects in which one participates: if a just reform movement collapses, the Stoic should feel neither grief nor anger about the result, though she should continue to act justly.

(5) Finally, I reject the view that Stoic ethics requires belief in a providential logos or universal determinism; I treat these as inessential metaphysical ornamentation that can be excised without damaging the ethical system.

My question is whether these five form a jointly tenable position. Specifically: does (2) together with (5) undermine the traditional grounding of (1), since the Stoic argument for why externals are not up to me relies on the claim that individual agency is embedded in a rationally ordered, deterministic cosmos? Is there a hidden tension between (3) and (4)—if emotional equanimity about collective failure is required, does that drain the motivational force needed for sustained engagement, or can the motivation be reconstructed purely from internal virtue? And is there an apparent tension between (3) and (1) that dissolves once we distinguish the justice of the action itself (which lies in my power) from the institutional outcome (which does not)? Please audit these commitments: rank the tensions by severity, identify which are only apparent, specify exactly what must give in each genuine case, and do not signal which commitment I should be least willing to abandon.
