For a discussion group I have been asked to set out my philosophical commitments on knowing-how and knowing-that so that others can test their consistency. Here is my profile.

I hold that knowing-how is irreducible to knowing-that: practical competence is a distinct cognitive kind, not a species of propositional knowledge. I extend this claim to mental skills—I think there is knowing-how involved in directing attention, suppressing a belief, or adopting a phenomenological stance—not only in bodily actions like swimming or playing an instrument. I also hold that every genuine instance of knowing-how involves phenomenal concepts: there is something-it-is-like to execute the skill, and this phenomenal character is essential rather than incidental. At the same time, I maintain that phenomenal concepts are genuinely conceptual representations, but they are non-propositional: they present a quality rather than describing it under a mode of presentation. I further maintain that these phenomenal representations, despite being non-propositional, still constitute a form of representational knowledge—specifically, a kind of knowing-that-presented-non-propositionally. From this framework I derive that knowing-how is not articulable in principle, whereas knowing-that in its standard propositional form always is, at least in principle. I deny that unconscious processes—such as subliminal perceptual learning or automated reflex arcs—can exhibit knowing-how, precisely because they lack phenomenal accessibility. I accept that there are clear cases of knowing-that without knowing-how, for instance knowing the molecular structure of water without being able to purify it. Finally, I hold that a being could display perfectly skillful behavior—say, reproducing an expert chess player’s moves—without possessing knowing-how, provided that being entirely lacks the relevant phenomenal concepts.

Please audit this set. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve once the distinction between propositional and non-propositional representation is kept in view, and how severe are the real tensions? For each genuine tension, indicate the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what philosophical cost it carries. Then tell me which single commitment I should revise if I want to clean up the largest number of tensions at once, and whether that revision forces me to abandon the mental-skills extension or only the phenomenal-concept thesis. Be precise about where the phenomenal-concept claim does the explanatory work and where it generates the instability.
