Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between predictive instrumentalists and mechanistic realists regarding idealization and unrealistic assumptions in scientific models—specifically, the debate over what philosophers call Milton Friedman’s “F-twist”: the claim that unrealistic assumptions in a model are permissible, and even advantageous, so long as the model delivers accurate predictions.

The instrumentalist camp defends a qualified version of the F-twist: they hold that a scientific model’s primary virtue is predictive and policy-relevant accuracy, especially at the macro level, and that unrealistic microfoundations are acceptable—sometimes necessary—if they yield tractable, well-calibrated forecasts. The mechanist-realist camp rejects the F-twist for explanatory and extrapolative purposes: they hold that unrealistic assumptions undermine genuine causal understanding and render models unreliable outside the calibration domain, particularly when policy interventions alter the very mechanisms that the model abstracts away.

Your deliverable should be a formal document that both camps could sign, structured in four parts and written in American English.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims—about what scientific idealization is; about the logical structure of Friedman’s argument (paraphrased in your own words, without quotation marks drawn from any specific source); about the distinction between predictive accuracy, explanatory fidelity, and robustness; and about the conditions under which unrealistic assumptions become problematic rather than benign.

Second, a disagreements table with two columns, one for each camp. Each entry should state the camp’s position in wording that camp accepts and that the other recognizes as accurate—not a strawman. Include disagreements on: whether predictive accuracy is the sole or primary criterion of model success; whether unrealistic microfoundations can ever provide genuine causal explanation; and the role of out-of-sample, counterfactual, and structural-change robustness.

Third, a cruxes section identifying at least three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp closer to the other. To keep the dispute concrete and avoid purely verbal disagreement, evaluate each crux specifically through integrated assessment models (IAMs) in climate-economics—models that combine stylized economic dynamics with physical climate processes to forecast long-range policy outcomes. For each crux:
- State the crux in neutral language.
- Identify a concrete case, modeling scenario, or hypothetical dataset that both camps agree is relevant.
- Record in advance what each camp predicts about that case.

Suggested cruxes: (i) whether calibration-domain predictive success licenses trust in counterfactual policy scenarios where interventions alter underlying mechanisms; (ii) whether correct macro-level forecasts can be said to explain policy effects when the microfoundations are known to be false; and (iii) whether rapid structural change—such as an unmodeled technological breakthrough—will cause the model to fail smoothly (through parameter adjustment) or catastrophically (through mechanism breakdown).

Fourth, a brief concluding paragraph assessing whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal—arising from different definitions of explanation, realism, prediction, or idealization—specifying exactly which portion and why.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for external sources. Do not quote any real text verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks. The document should read as if drafted by the two camps themselves after sustained argument.
