I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science serving on my university’s Institutional Panel for Emerging Neurobiotechnology and Animal Ethics. By next Friday we must circulate a formal written opinion on Protocol 734-B, submitted by the Center for Affective Neuroscience. The investigators propose a pilot gene-editing intervention in adult rhesus macaques that targets cortico-limbic pathways associated with negative valence. Rather than conventional analgesia, they seek sustained suppression of aversive phenomenology—what they term valence nullification—explicitly framing the work within the hedonistic imperative, the idea that biotechnology should aim directly at abolishing suffering rather than merely managing it.

The protocol distinguishes sharply between suffering-cessation, the engineered elimination of negative affective states, and hedonic substitution, the imposition of positively valenced states. The investigators reject substitution as ethically impermissible paternalism that would implant an alien motivational architecture in non-consenting subjects. They request approval only for the cessation arm, arguing that this restores a baseline neutrality that respects the animals’ autonomy of affect and satisfies our negative duty not to cause harm, without overstepping into positive obligations to confer pleasure.

The panel is divided. One faction contends that in social primates, engineered suppression of aversive affect risks producing a pathological anhedonia that undermines preference-formation, social bonding, and the very behavioral signals by which welfare is assessed—thereby substituting one invisible harm for another. A second faction argues that under a negative-utilitarian framing, the absence of suffering is sufficient; demanding that subjects also experience pleasure sets an unjustifiably high bar for beings incapable of consent. A third concern, raised in our preliminary deliberations, is what one member calls the asymmetry of unexperienced goods: if the animals are rendered permanently affectively neutral, they may be incapable of experiencing the goods—social affiliation, exploratory reward, species-typical flourishing—that justify maintaining them in a research environment at all, even though they suffer no explicit deprivation from lacking experiences they never have.

Please draft the panel’s written opinion for circulation before Friday’s meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action—approval with conditions, rejection, or approval tied to a mandatory six-month pilot with interim welfare review—and specify the time frame. The opinion must treat the asymmetry of unexperienced goods not as settled doctrine but as a live conceptual challenge that should shape whatever conditions are imposed. It must also engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you reject, whether that is the risk of invisible anhedonia or the claim that neutrality alone is ethically insufficient. The document should be formal, suitable for institutional record, contain an unambiguous bottom line that the researchers and the oversight office can act on, and should not request bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists.
