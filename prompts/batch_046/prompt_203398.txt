I am writing the final examination for an upper-level epistemology seminar, and I need one essay question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia that students cannot answer by reciting standard summaries of peer-disagreement cases. Please construct the question around a specific conceptual scenario I will call the Reflective Calibration Case: an agent holds a first-order belief about a risk assessment in her area of expertise; she then acquires statistical higher-order evidence that people with her demographic and disciplinary profile systematically overestimate that kind of risk by a significant margin; but she also recognizes that her judgment about whether this statistical base rate applies to her particular reasoning process is itself generated by the very cognitive mechanisms she suspects of bias. The question should ask whether rational belief-revision requires her to suspend her first-order belief, and whether the attempt to apply the statistical correction collapses into a self-defeating reflective regress or can be rationally stabilized.

Specify that students have 90 minutes and should write approximately 800 words. Then provide a model answer of about 650 words. Finally, provide a marking scheme organized around five must-hit moves: (1) a precise structural distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence that explains how they can come apart without one automatically defeating the other; (2) an accurate characterization of epistemic akrasia that distinguishes it from practical weakness of will and relates it specifically to belief-formation under acknowledged irrationality; (3) a direct analysis of the Reflective Calibration Case that treats the regress problem as structural rather than merely psychological, explaining exactly where the loop closes; (4) a reasoned argument about whether suspension is required, engaging with at least one plausible stabilization strategy—such as treating the statistical evidence as an external calibration constraint rather than an internal belief about one's own belief; and (5) a critical conclusion that takes an explicit stance on whether epistemic akrasia is unavoidable in layered calibration, rather than ending in an ambiguous summary. Award marks out of 25, with specific weights for each move, and include a one-line note for each on the characteristic failure mode—e.g., conflating the two levels of evidence, treating akrasia as mere laziness, ignoring the loop, offering a generic "it depends" without argument, or retreating to a description of standard peer-disagreement examples. The marking scheme should make clear that an A answer must contain moves 3 and 4 executed with conceptual precision, while a B answer typically has moves 1 and 2 correct but treats the case as a simple disagreement problem without engaging the reflective structure.
