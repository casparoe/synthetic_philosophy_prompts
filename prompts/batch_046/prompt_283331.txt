I organize a small discussion circle at a local library—ten adults with no formal training in philosophy or mathematics, meeting for a single ninety-minute session in a community room. Over the past months I have been reading on my own about cooperation and trust, and I recently encountered the concept of program equilibrium: the idea that if agents submit transparent, inspectable rules (analogous to source code) rather than hidden actions, cooperation can become stable in a prisoner's-dilemma-type situation where ordinary hidden-choice reasoning predicts defection. I want the group to feel this distinction in their bones before anyone explains it.

Please design a runnable, paper-based game or structured simulation for this group. Specify: the physical materials (cards, tokens, or printed sheets); the rules of play, divided into a first round with hidden intentions and a second round with transparent, inspectable "program cards"; a payoff scheme using only points or small symbolic stakes; a precise timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the formal difference between Nash-style hidden-choice equilibrium and program-style transparent-rule equilibrium. The rules and incentives—not any opening lecture—should cause participants to experience coordination failure in the hidden round and then discover how inspection of the other's rule changes their decision in the second round.

The design should inject one specific technical feature of program equilibrium that is rarely discussed in popular accounts: the possibility that a submitted program can contain a conditional clause that explicitly references the opponent's submitted program (a form of direct logical coupling). Show how this can be represented with simple card templates without requiring electronics.

Please also include: an honest account of which feature of real program equilibrium—such as consistency requirements for self-referential programs, the logical obstacle associated with Löb's theorem, or infinite regress in self-reference—this card-game version necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief; a prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it (for example, participants misreading the conditional cards, or treating the second round as a repetition rather than a structural change); and guidance on how to handle that failure without breaking the experiential point.

Finally, please state clearly what argument or practical observation would change your recommendation—specifically, under what conditions the transparent-inspection mechanic would fail to generate the intended insight, and what that failure would reveal about the limits of using paper games to illustrate formal equilibrium concepts.
