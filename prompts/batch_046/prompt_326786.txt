I serve on the arbitration panel for a small cooperative workshop that operates under an unusual governance charter: rather than assigning members positive maintenance tasks, it lists prohibited failures-to-act—specifically, no one who is the last to depart after 8 p.m. may leave the coolant system running. One evening, Carla, who is not the designated safety officer but is indeed the last to leave, notices the coolant is active. She does not disable it, reasoning that Dan, the officer assigned a later check, will handle it. Dan, seeing Carla's exit signature, skips his check, assuming she performed the required disablement. The system leaks overnight and destroys equipment in an adjacent studio. The studio owner asks who caused the damage. Carla argues that because she held no assigned task to perform—only a prohibition against leaving the system on—her failure to act is not the kind of thing that can cause harm; only Dan's failure to carry out his assigned check counts. You are asked to advise the panel: does the charter's design—prohibiting specific failures rather than mandating specific actions—change whether Carla's inaction is causally responsible for the damage, or does it merely relocate the same underlying difficulty? In particular, can a failure to act cause harm only when there is a corresponding assigned duty to act, or can institutional expectations alone make someone's inaction the source of the damage?
