Please argue that a rigorous relational conception of personhood, of the kind developed in Ubuntu philosophy—where being a person is constituted through reciprocal recognition within a shared social field rather than possessed as an intrinsic individual property—undermines the standard liberal distinction between punitive and restorative justice in a specific and non-obvious way. In particular, defend the claim that when one person seriously wrongs another, the harm is not merely an injury inflicted upon a pre-constituted victim, but a simultaneous degradation of the wrongdoer’s own ontological status: because both parties are nodes in the same network of recognition, the rupture of that network diminishes the structural conditions for the perpetrator’s own personhood. From this, derive that the perpetrator has a non-instrumental, self-regarding duty to participate in restorative processes—not merely to compensate the victim or satisfy social utility, but to repair the relational field and thereby recover a diminished aspect of their own agency. Address the objection that this framing collapses moral responsibility into self-interested rehabilitation, and explain why the symmetry of recognition required by this view does not entail that all punishment is illegitimate, but rather transforms its justification. Be precise about whether this relational degradation is best understood as a partial suspension, a scalar diminishment, or a qualitative shift in moral status, and defend your choice.
