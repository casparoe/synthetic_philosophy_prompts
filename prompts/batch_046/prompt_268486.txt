I am asking for a speech I will deliver at the retirement dinner of Elena Vasquez, who served for thirty-five years as our city's chief hydrologist. The room will be divided: about half the guests are engineers and risk analysts who think naturally in probabilities and expected values; the other half are her family and neighbors, who have watched her spend decades preparing for a catastrophic flood that never came, and some of them quietly wonder whether she built a career around a phantom.

Over the past year I have been reading Keynes on probability and uncertainty on my own—not for any course, just out of personal curiosity—and I was struck by his insistence that probability is not always a number you calculate, but sometimes a judgment about the weight and quality of the reasons behind a belief, and that there exists a deeper kind of uncertainty that simply cannot be turned into a measurable odds ratio. I want an eight-minute speech built structurally around that distinction. It must explain to the family side why Elena's work was not a lost bet, but it must do so without ever claiming she was "statistically correct" or that the flood was "bound to happen eventually," because neither of those respects the philosophical point. At the same time, it must speak honestly to the engineers in the room: it should not reduce Keynes to a vague encouragement to "be cautious," and it should acknowledge that planning for genuinely immeasurable risk is conceptually different from optimizing for known distributions.

The speech should shape what can and cannot be said about her legacy. It should honor her without requiring the disaster to arrive as validation, and it should avoid comforting the family with false precision. The tone should be warm, spoken, and precise rather than academic. I do not want citations, a bibliography, references to obscure literature, or any suggestion that the audience read further; I want the idea to live entirely inside the speech, so that both halves of the room can leave with the same understanding of what she actually did.
