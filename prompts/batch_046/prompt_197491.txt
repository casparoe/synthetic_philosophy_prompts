I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of history, currently drafting a seminar paper on causal architecture in macrohistorical explanation. In recent discussions with colleagues I have become dissatisfied with the standard treatments of overdetermination—whether they reduce it to a logical problem of redundant sufficiency or treat it as merely a pragmatic issue of narrative selection—because neither approach specifies what a genuinely adequate theory must preserve. Before I commit to a framework, I want the full specification of desiderata, not another rehearsal of familiar textbook examples.

Could you set out the complete list of requirements that a theory of multi-causal explanation in history must satisfy if it is to count as adequate? In particular, it must distinguish intra-level overdetermination—where multiple causes operate at the same temporal scale, such as concurrent diplomatic failures and economic contraction in precipitating a crisis—from inter-level overdetermination, where long-term structural conditions and short-term contingent events each appear sufficient for an outcome, without collapsing the two into a single probabilistic measure. The theory must also preserve the asymmetry between explanatory redundancy, in which two independent accounts each fully explain the event, and causal redundancy, in which two processes are each sufficient but only one actually operates, and it must do so in a way that respects historians’ routine practice of assigning differential weight to causes without treating the weaker ones as non-causal. It should be consistent with the methodological result that macrohistorical patterns—such as the near-synchronous dissolution of palace economies across independent polities during the Late Bronze Age—raise a distinctive problem: whether we are dealing with one distributed structural cause or with genuinely separate overdetermining pathways that happen to converge. For each requirement, please explain why it is non-negotiable rather than a disciplinary prejudice, and identify which single desideratum eliminates the largest number of existing approaches—whether those are counterfactual-dependence models drawn from philosophy of science, structuralist macrohistories, or narrative-selection theories. You may score the main contenders briefly against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and on the motivating cases. Please present your answer in clear British English, using tables if they aid clarity, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I need a framework I can apply and assess without relying on external verification.
