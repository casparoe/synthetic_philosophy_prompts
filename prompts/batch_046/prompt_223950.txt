I've been trying to map out the limits of Scanlon-style contractualism for a seminar paper, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to push the view into an implausible corner. Here are the steps as I've laid them out:

(1) Contractualism holds that an action or principle is wrong if it is permitted by a principle that no affected person could reasonably reject.
(2) What makes rejection "reasonable" is not that the burden on me exceeds the aggregate benefits to others, but that the burden on me is not justified relative to the burden I would bear if the principle were rejected.
(3) In standard aggregation cases—say, saving five strangers versus saving one—adopting the principle "save the greater number" requires letting the one die. That individual can reasonably reject the principle: the comparison is between dying (under the principle) and living (without it), not between my death and five others' survival.
(4) So contractualism, on this reading, cannot justify saving the five over the one.
(5) Yet Scanlon clearly wants numbers to matter in some rescue contexts; he allows that saving more lives is preferable, other things equal, and he does not want to collapse into a pure one-person-one-vote veto.
(6) To reconcile (4) with (5), I have introduced an auxiliary condition: aggregation is permissible only when the relevant burden is "shared" or "distributed" across the affected parties, so that no single person bears an unreduced cost purely for others' gain.
(7) Now apply this to intergenerational climate mitigation: present generations bear concrete, non-transferable costs (economic restructuring, consumption limits) to avert catastrophic harm to future generations. The future people, however, do not yet exist, so they cannot participate in the present burden-sharing.
(8) By (6) and (7), present people can reasonably reject any climate-mitigation principle that imposes net costs on them, no matter how severe the future harm averted.
(9) This seems implausibly strong. It implies contractualism cannot justify even modest carbon-reduction policies that appear morally obligatory, and it gives present people a structural veto over any intergenerational sacrifice because the "sharing" condition can never be met with non-existent partners.
(10) Therefore either contractualism collapses into an extreme present-biased deontology, or one of the premises above is wrong.

We want to keep the core contractualist motivation intact—especially the idea that morality is about justifiability to each affected person—but we also want to avoid (8) and (9). Which step should we reject? Is the error in (2)'s interpretation of reasonable rejection, in (6)'s burden-sharing amendment, in (7)'s claim about non-existent partners, or in (5)'s assumption that Scanlon really accepts aggregation? Rank the revisions by severity, explain why the rejected step looked plausible, and say what giving it up costs for the broader framework. Please respond in American English with an audit-style analysis rather than a general lecture on contractualism.
