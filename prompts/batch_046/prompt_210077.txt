I hold the view that the so-called wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is not a deep feature of morality but an artifact of a hidden assumption: that moral reasons possess a special "binding authority" that forces the agent to reorder her other priorities, whereas prudential, aesthetic, or personal reasons merely add weight without restructuring anything. On my account, all reasons differ only in strength, scope, and source; there is no metaphysical distinction between the right kind and the wrong kind, only between weights that match the decision and weights that do not. This dissolves the problem, because acting morally for selfish motives is not using the wrong kind of reason—it is simply allowing a misaligned motivation to accompany a correctly weighted reason.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure. Which of my assumptions—perhaps about the unity of practical reason, or about the absence of binding authority—could fail, and what would the world, or our moral psychology, have to look like for each failure? What observations, arguments, or conceptual distinctions count as decisive defeat rather than mere awkwardness? What does my view predict that rival accounts (those that insist on a categorical distinction between moral and non-moral reasons) do not, and could those predictions be tested in principle? Would defenders of the binding-authority view themselves concede anything as genuinely damaging, and have I qualified the view so heavily—by defining reason so broadly—that it has become unfalsifiable? If it is well insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind? I am particularly interested in whether there could be a psychological or formal result showing that moral motivation operates through a distinct mechanism, not just a stronger weight, and whether my view risks collapsing into pure rationalism or pure subjectivism if pushed to its limit.
