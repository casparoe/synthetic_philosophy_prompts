student answer:

"The wrong kind of reasons problem is that some considerations can't justify beliefs or actions even if they work out well. For belief, only truth-related reasons are the right kind; for action, value-related reasons are okay. Dr. Chen takes money to form a belief, so that's clearly wrong-kind—she's like someone who believes they'll win the lottery cause they need cash. Because she formed the belief via the grant, she lacks knowledge, even if X is actually safe. Dr. Patel rejects the grant, so her belief is formed for evidence-based reasons, which means she has knowledge. The problem applies differently to action: endorsing X for money is actually a right-kind reason for action, since self-interest justifies actions but not beliefs. So Chen's action is fine but her belief is corrupted. One might object that endorsement is a speech act, so it's closer to belief-expression than pure action, but that doesn't change the analysis because the wrong-kind infection only reaches the belief part. Hieronymi argues practical reasons are always wrong for belief since belief aims at truth, though some others say practical reasons can be okay if they just motivate further investigation—though that's not what's happening here since Chen already formed the belief. Overall WKRP shows belief is special because it aims at truth, while action aims at the good."
