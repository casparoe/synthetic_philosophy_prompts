I'm drafting a feature on the epistemology of distant-past reconstruction, and I need a short fiction piece—around 600 words—to open it. Write a story set in a near-future archive where two competing restoration protocols govern how scholars rebuild a destroyed civilization. The Herodotean Protocol requires archivists to preserve every contradictory testimony—dreams, omens, second-hand rumor, contradictory chronicles—each tagged with its reliability but never expunged. The Thucydidean Protocol permits only physical trace and direct first-generation testimony, discarding everything else as mythopoetic noise. Your protagonist is an auditor assigned to reconstruct one specific event: the sudden, complete abandonment of a major settlement. The event survives in rich, contradictory oral fragments but leaves almost no physical signature, and it is entirely absent from the sparse material record the Thucydidean Protocol accepts. Rather than resolving which protocol yields the "true" past, the narrative should show how each method produces a different, coherent history of the abandonment, and should end with the protagonist facing a choice that reveals the cost of rigorous exclusion. The piece should work as fiction first—scene, character, consequence—rather than as an allegory with renamed concepts, and it should leave the reader with the uneasy sense that knowing the distant past may require preserving contradictions we have been trained to discard.
