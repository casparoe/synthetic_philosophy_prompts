I have no formal training in philosophy or history, but I have spent considerable time thinking about whether it is ever permissible to rank one mass atrocity as "worse" than another, or whether any such judgment is inherently obscene. My reactions to particular cases feel defensible in pairs, yet they pull in different directions when I try to assemble them into a consistent view.

Here is the pattern of my verdicts.

Comparing death tolls for purely descriptive purposes strikes me as entirely unproblematic. Noting that the Holocaust killed roughly six million people and that the Rwandan genocide killed roughly eight hundred thousand over about one hundred days is, to my mind, straightforward historical accounting with no moral offense attached.

But taking those same figures and declaring that one event was therefore "the greater evil" feels like a category mistake—almost as if human suffering could be entered on a ledger and summed. So statistical comparison is acceptable; a moral hierarchy derived directly from statistics is not.

Still, I think there is legitimate room for comparing the qualitative character of different evils. If a historian argues that the Atlantic slave trade was in some specific respect "worse" than a particular famine—not because more people died, but because the harm involved sustained, intentional dehumanization rather than a catastrophic policy failure—I feel that a meaningful moral distinction is being drawn, not merely a score being kept. Yet if that same comparison is used by a government to allocate memorial funding, giving the larger monument to whichever atrocity has been judged "worse," the reasoning suddenly feels corrupt and instrumental.

Personal testimony creates a further exception. If a survivor of one camp within a larger genocide says, "What I endured in that specific place was the worst part of the entire system," that sounds to me like legitimate witness. But if an outsider with no direct experience makes an analogous claim—ranking one genocide as worse than another from a distance—it sounds presumptuous and appropriative.

There is also the matter of concealment. I find myself inclined to think that an atrocity whose records were deliberately destroyed—say, a colonial massacre that was systematically hidden and denied—is in one specific respect "worse" than an equally lethal event that was openly documented, because the erasure itself seems to constitute an additional wrong against the victims and against history. I am not sure whether this belief conflicts with my refusal to rank the Holocaust and Rwanda by scale alone.

Finally, comparative study seems permissible where comparative distribution does not. I have no objection to historians analyzing different state atrocities together to identify common mechanisms of bureaucratic violence. But if a relief organization uses those analyses to rank victim communities and distribute aid according to whose suffering was "greater," I recoil.

So my verdicts stand as follows: raw statistical comparison is permissible; deriving a moral hierarchy from statistics is not; comparing qualitative dimensions of harm can illuminate but should not govern resources or public honor; survivors may rank within their own lived experience but outsiders should not rank across events they did not suffer; concealment adds a distinct moral weight; and comparative analysis for understanding is acceptable, whereas comparative allocation for justice, memorialization, or resources is not.

Can these judgments be captured by a single principle, or by a small family of principles precisely stated? If not, which of my verdicts must I abandon? Please indicate which case motivates each clause of your proposed principle or principles, state what they predict about two further situations I have not listed—specifically, a textbook ordering its chapters by the death toll of the events covered, and a court weighing historical atrocities in order to determine reparations—and be explicit about which of my reactions, if any, your account cannot accommodate.
