I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about moral emotions through the lens of system design—whether they function more like localized bugs that can be patched or like global state failures that corrupt an entire architecture. I want a poem that closes a discussion rather than merely decorating it, and I need it to do genuine conceptual work.

Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge—on the question of whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can deliberately engineer itself toward one or the other. Let one refrain carry the logic of guilt: directed, transactional, capable of rollback or repair. Let the other carry the logic of shame: diffuse, visible to others, tied to identity rather than to a specific act. The form itself should track the philosophical tension: through the tercets the refrains should miss each other, as if the two emotions keep speaking past one another, and only in the final quatrain should they sit side by side without resolving into synthesis.

Please render the distinction accurately enough that someone familiar with the literature—action-oriented versus self-oriented responses, the role of an audience, the possibility of cultural selection—would recognize the positions rather than see a caricature. At the same time, inject an unconventional framing: treat the cultural choice not as a simple preference but as the adoption of an emotional protocol, asking whether selecting for guilt is like choosing an error-handling scheme that permits rollback (localized, reparable) while selecting for shame is like allowing a non-local corruption of global state. Do not resolve this analogy; let it run through the imagery without collapsing into a sermon.

If this request rests on a confused premise—for example, if framing moral emotions as selectable protocols distorts the philosophical debate beyond recognition, or if a villanelle’s rigid repetition cannot genuinely track a conceptual distinction—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. After the poem, include a brief note indicating where you take liberties with the positions or lean on a contested reading (for instance, if you treat shame as necessarily involving an external gaze, or if you imply that guilt is always fully reparable).

The poem should stand on its own for a reader who knows nothing of the debate, but a specialist should find the underlying concept right.
