Question: Jain anekāntavāda is often presented as a metaphysical doctrine about the complexity of reality, but it also carries epistemological and ethical implications. In a short essay, explain (1) how the saptabhaṅgī (seven-fold predication) operationalizes the idea that no single viewpoint captures an object, (2) how this differs from the Madhyamaka claim that all dharmas lack svabhāva, and (3) whether anekāntavāda entails epistemic relativism or can be reconciled with a form of fallibilism. Be specific about the role of naya (standpoint) and dravya (substance).

Student answer: Jain anekāntavāda, first articulated by Mahāvīra and later systematized in Kundakunda's corpus, holds that every substance (dravya) possesses infinite qualities (guṇas) and modes (paryāyas). To avoid contradiction, Jain logicians developed saptabhaṅgī: seven predicative forms each prefixed by syāt ("in some respect"), allowing assertions that a thing is, is not, is and is not, is inexpressible, and so on, each tied to a specific naya (standpoint). Unlike Madhyamaka śūnyavāda, which denies svabhāva entirely and treats all distinctions as merely conventional, Jainism preserves a permanent, unchanging substrate in the dravya; multiplicity belongs to its paryāyas, not to an absence of essence. Hence anekāntavāda avoids epistemic relativism: each naya reveals an objectively real aspect, and error is only the absolutization of one standpoint (ekāntavāda). The same structure governs ethics. Because moral properties are further paryāyas of action-substances, a single deed can be simultaneously right—from the householder's naya—and wrong—from the ascetic's naya—without contradiction. Thus Jain fallibilism is not Peircean open-ended inquiry but a disciplined recognition that every judgment carries the syāt qualification.
