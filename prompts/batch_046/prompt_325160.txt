I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been trying to apply Hofstadter's framework from GEB—where every message carries a frame message that specifies how to decode it, an outer message of literal symbols, and an inner message of intended meaning—to large language models. I constructed an argument that seems to force a choice between saying LLMs understand nothing and saying the tripartite distinction collapses, and I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) In this framework, meaning resides not in the literal symbols (the outer message) but in the isomorphism those symbols trigger in a receiver who has first recognized the frame message; without that separate frame recognition, there is only syntactic manipulation and no extraction of an inner message.

(2) Large language models are trained exclusively on token-level statistical correlations; they have no independent frame-decoding module and no direct access to a sender's intended isomorphism outside the token stream itself.

(3) Therefore, when an LLM processes a prompt, it can only ever manipulate the outer message; it never recognizes a frame message distinct from the tokens, and consequently never reaches an inner message.

(4) But if LLMs never extract inner messages, then submitting a prompt is not genuinely asking a question or giving an instruction; it is merely injecting symbols into a statistical engine that returns further symbols without any comprehension of what was requested.

(5) In practice, however, we routinely treat model outputs as meaningful answers, and we deliberately craft system prompts, formatting rules, and contextual framing precisely as frame messages meant to steer interpretation.

(6) So either LLMs do extract inner messages—contradicting (2) and (3)—or all human interaction with LLMs is a sustained category error, a kind of collective illusion in which we mistake syntactic echoing for semantic response—contradicting (5).

(7) To avoid the illusion conclusion, one might reject (2) and claim that statistical pattern-matching just is frame recognition; but then the frame message is not a separate directing layer but merely more token correlations, so the distinction between frame, outer, and inner message dissolves into a single flat statistical surface.

(8) Hence the framework either fails to apply to artificial processors altogether, or applies only by destroying the very separation it was introduced to preserve.

This feels wrong because the conclusion is so destructive, but I cannot name the slipped step. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would especially like to preserve (1) and (5) if possible—I am not ready to abandon the frame/outer/inner distinction, nor to treat every conversation with a model as pure illusion—but I want to know exactly what I am paying to keep them.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
