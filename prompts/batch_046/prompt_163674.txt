I serve on the Municipal Memory and Archives Board. A coalition of residents has petitioned to apply "algorithmic burial" to the digital Community Grievance Registry—records of accusations and reconciliations following the 1998 neighborhood partition—rendering them unsearchable while technically preserving them in encrypted storage. The petitioners argue that perpetual searchability perpetuates harm and blocks moral closure, invoking a right to be forgotten; opponents, including local historians and affected families, contend that selective invisibility produces false reconciliation and violates the collective duty to preserve the conditions for future moral reckoning. Our charter permits full retention with open access, conditional algorithmic burial with safeguards, or denial of the petition; outright destruction is legally prohibited. Draft the board's decision memo recommending one concrete course, with an implementation timeline and any necessary conditions. The memo should treat seriously the claim—central to debates on the duty to remember and the case for forgetting—that remembering can itself become an ongoing injury, while also addressing the strongest argument that rendering records invisible undermines shared memory rather than healing it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific works; ground the reasoning directly in the philosophical stakes at issue.
