I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind running a 90-minute graduate seminar of about ten students on the epistemology of phenomenal experience. We've been working through the classic knowledge argument—where a scientist who knows every physical fact about color perception still learns something new upon seeing red for the first time—and the standard physicalist replies, particularly the ability hypothesis and phenomenal concept strategies. The conversation, however, keeps collapsing into familiar intuitions about "redness," and students treat the case as a settled intuition pump rather than a live problem. I want an in-class activity that forces them to inhabit the gap between exhaustive propositional knowledge and phenomenal acquaintance, using a synthetic sensory modality that avoids the well-worn color script. Specifically, design a simulation using a precisely calibrated artificial stimulus—such as a thermoceptive gradient delivered by a temperature wand or a synthetic olfactory compound—where participants first study complete physical descriptions (molecular structure, receptor activation patterns, neural correlates) and make predictive judgments about qualitative similarities, then experience the stimulus directly and must revise those judgments. The rules should create two phases: a "laboratory" phase with roles (some students hold the physical descriptions and defend predictions; others experience the stimulus blind and report), and a "release" phase where the groups reconvene. Include a non-monetary payoff structure—perhaps collective reputation points or a forced group decision about whether to endorse a physicalist explanation—plus materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps moments onto the knowledge argument, distinguishing acquaintance knowledge from propositional knowledge and addressing whether predictive revision counts as learning a new fact. Be explicit about which feature of the original argument—likely the lifelong isolation condition—the activity cannot reproduce, and note what will predictably go wrong on the first run (for instance, participants performing the "expected" physicalist answer, or the stimulus being too subtle). Present everything with clear headings—Setup, Rules and Roles, Timeline, Debrief, and Honest Limitations—and keep the response within 650 words.
