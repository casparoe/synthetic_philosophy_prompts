I am a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of science and social planning, and I find myself trying to apply what I understand of Rudolf Carnap’s distinctions—particularly the separation of internal questions, answerable within an adopted linguistic framework, from external questions about the framework itself, together with his view that value judgments express volitional commitments rather than cognitive assertions—to the practical disputes that arise in urban climate-adaptation planning. To test whether my grasp is coherent, I have noted my own reactions to five concrete planning controversies, and I would like to know whether a single Carnap-inspired principle can accommodate all of them, or whether I must surrender one verdict for the sake of consistency.

Here are my judgments. First, when two civil engineers disagree about whether a proposed seawall meets the structural safety code calibrated to a hundred-year storm-surge model—one claiming the calibration protocol was misapplied, the other defending the computation—I treat this as an internal empirical dispute resolvable by correcting observations or recalculating within the adopted hydrological framework. Second, when the city council debates whether to adopt a prevention framework, which treats storm risk as an eliminable failure to be engineered away, or a resilience framework, which treats it as an unavoidable background condition to be managed through adaptive design, I regard this as an external practical choice about which linguistic and methodological framework to employ; no amount of hydrological data can settle it. Third, once the resilience framework is in place, planners must set a social discount rate for future damage—say, two percent versus five percent. One planner argues this should be read directly from observed long-term government-bond yields; I reject that view and take the rate to be a volitional parameter expressing intergenerational moral commitment, not an empirical constant, despite its numerical form. Fourth, when a democratic assembly subsequently ratifies both the resilience framework and the two-percent rate, a later administrator claims that democratic adoption has converted these into internal rules of the city’s planning language, so that disputing them is now like disputing a storm-model calibration. I deny this: ratification may legitimate a framework politically, but it does not transform an external adoption or a volitional weight into an internal factual question. Fifth, when a consultant proposes abandoning quantitative storm modeling altogether in favor of a purely qualitative community-narrative framework that rejects probabilistic prediction, I feel this is also an external framework adoption, analogous to the second case; yet I am uneasy, because if every quantitative protocol can be rejected as merely one framework among others, the very distinction between internal theoretical reconstruction and external framework choice seems to collapse.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps extending Carnap’s internal-external distinction through a three-tier schema of observational protocols, theoretical reconstructions, and framework-level volitional commitments—that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it with the clause that handles each case, say what it predicts about a dispute I have not mentioned (for instance, whether including non-market ecosystem values within the adopted cost-benefit framework is an internal expansion of the model or an external value injection), and tell me honestly which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate without implausible contortion. If no single principle saves them all, indicate which verdict you would overturn and why.
