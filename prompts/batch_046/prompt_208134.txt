I am drafting materials for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of long-range scientific prediction, and I have been tracking a sustained disagreement between two colleagues—call them the Falsificationist and the Structuralist—concerning Thomas Malthus’s Essay on the Principle of Population. I want you to produce the adversarial-collaboration document they should have written together.

The Falsificationist holds that Malthus’s specific predictions of imminent catastrophic scarcity in late-eighteenth-century Britain were empirically refuted by the demographic transition, sustained agricultural yield growth, and the absence of positive checks operating in the manner he described; she treats the defense that Malthus was “right but merely early” as an illegitimate immunizing strategy that violates standard falsificationist norms, because it permits indefinite postponement of verification conditions without specifying what observable trajectory within a human-relevant timeframe would count as disconfirmation.

The Structuralist holds that Malthus correctly identified an asymptotic structural tendency—geometric or exponential growth in population against ultimately bounded or sub-exponential growth in subsistence resources—which remains operative but was masked by temporary, non-generalizable buffers such as fossil-fuel-driven chemical agriculture, global commodity trade, and voluntary fertility reduction; he argues that “right but early” is a legitimate epistemic category for theories whose verification conditions lie outside standard observational windows, provided the theory specifies both the mechanism of deferral and the conditions under which the masked tendency would become observable.

Please draft their joint statement in this form: (1) a numbered list of factual and conceptual claims both can sign, including agreements about the historical demographic and agricultural data, about the logical form of Malthus’s argument, and about the conditions under which a long-run prediction is epistemically assessable; (2) the precise disagreements, stated in neutral wording that each side would accept as a fair characterization of its own position, separating verbal disputes from substantive ones; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—including whether a theory may invoke structural tendencies that are systematically masked by contingent technological buffers without thereby losing empirical content, and whether there exists a principled distinction between legitimate epistemic deferral and ad hoc rescue; (4) for each crux, one concrete historical case or quantitative projection (for instance, a specific century-scale projection about arable-land constraints under conditions of zero technological innovation and no migration, or the demographic trajectory of a closed system with fixed resource inputs) that both agree bears on the crux, together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that case or projection demonstrates; and (5) a brief statement of what further result—whether a formal model, an observed long-run trajectory, or a conceptual distinction—would actually resolve the disagreement rather than extend it.

Keep the language precise, avoid bibliographic appeals, and do not merely summarize the literature; I am interested in the logical architecture of their disagreement and in whether the “right but early” category can be rendered non-vacuous.
