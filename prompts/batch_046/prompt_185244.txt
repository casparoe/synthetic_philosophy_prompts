There's this practice in neurocritical care where teams rate consciousness in unresponsive patients using behavioral scales plus fMRI, but lately some units have started framing the whole thing in terms of interface coherance -- they treat self-report and even pain-behavior not as windows into an inner experience but as outputs of the brain's predicitive-model interface, like checking if the user-illusion is running smoothly rather than if there's a real user inside. They still withdraw care when coherance is low and keep treating when it's high, just like before. What philosophical committments does that reframing actually commit them to? Does it presuppose that consciousness literally is an illusion with no subject behind it, or can you run the same clinical protocol while still thinking there's a real experiencer that the interface just misrepresents? And if you took the user-illusion view seriously, wouldn't you have to stop talking about the patient's suffering altogether and just talk about broken prediction errors? I'm trying to figure out if the practice is philosophically consistent or just slapping new vocabulary on old ethics.
