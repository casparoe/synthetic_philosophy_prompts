Analyze the 2007–2008 financial crisis—specifically the Federal Reserve’s and major investment banks’ sequential updating of credences about subprime mortgage default correlations—through the lens of van Fraassen’s Reflection principle. Do not treat this as a standard single-agent forecasting case. Instead, frame it as a problem of institutional reflexivity: the updating agent is a distributed committee (the FOMC, risk-management boards) whose published belief revisions become public signals that alter the very market conditions being predicted. Map precisely who counts as the agent, what counts as the evidence stream, and what future credence the present credence should equal according to Reflection. Then examine whether the principle survives the feedback loop: if the Fed knows that announcing a lower credence in systemic stability will itself trigger runs that make instability more likely, does Reflection still require that its current credence match its expectation of its future, post-announcement credence? Identify which features of the crisis—the silence of internal risk models at Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers, the abrupt reversal in Fed liquidity assessments in August 2007, the divergence between private and public belief—the principle explains, which it misdescribes, and whether the episode shows that Reflection was designed for solitary private reasoners and collapses when applied to systemic actors whose beliefs are causally efficacious upon disclosure. Be disciplined about the mapping: say where the principle is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and conclude with what the crisis reveals about whether diachronic coherence can be maintained by an institution that is also a player in the game it is trying to forecast.
