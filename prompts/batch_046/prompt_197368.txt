I’ve just come back from a long, exhausting argument with a close friend who works in legislative administration, and I’m now trying to turn our disagreement into something presentable. Next Thursday I’m speaking at a workshop on institutional design, and I want to defend a hybrid proposal for local government: an elected chamber subject to hard eight-year term limits followed by a mandatory four-year cooling-off period with no return to office, paired with a second chamber filled by sortition—citizens drawn by lot for a single, non-renewable eighteen-month term—whose only formal power is to issue binding “sunset recommendations” that force the elected chamber to re-justify any bill it wishes to extend. My claim is that this preserves the expertise and continuity we associate with career politicians while using rotational citizens as an epistemic brake against capture and revolving-door corruption, but I need to be realistic about its weaknesses. The audience will be about a dozen democratic theorists steeped in Schumpeterian competitive models, public-choice economists who treat careerism as a necessary source of institutional memory, and two former senior staffers who have actually managed legislative calendars; they are not populists, but they are deeply skeptical that random citizens can evaluate complex bills and convinced that term limits simply empower unelected bureaucrats and lobbyists. Prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, phrased in their own vocabulary rather than in generic anti-elitist slogans: rank them by how likely and how damaging each is, flag which ones I should concede up front rather than fight, identify the single reply that absolutely has to land for the proposal to survive past the first round of questions, and tell me the one objection I am probably hoping nobody asks—because my answer there is weakest—so that I can at least anticipate it rather than be blindsided. I’m not asking for a bibliography or for citations to specific scholars; I need a practical prep sheet for the room I’ll actually be in.
