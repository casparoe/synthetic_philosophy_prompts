As a retired experimental physicist now drafting history-of-science essays, I need a reusable decision procedure to detect presentism in my interpretations—something my peer-review group can run in under twenty minutes without philosophy training. Operationalize the distinction between conceptual anachronism (imposing modern scientific categories) and normative anachronism (imposing modern ethical standards) into an ordered checklist: for each step state the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, the precise stop-point that clears the interpretation, and the explicit hand-off where the issue is values rather than historical fact. Include a “retroactive calibration check” I have devised: ask whether I am attributing to past instruments or protocols an error structure they could not have possessed given their own measurement theory. The procedure must survive hard cases where it returns a verdict I will dislike—e.g., classifying a morally repugnant 1930s eugenics study as conceptually sound by its own sampling logic—and cases where the conceptual/normative distinction collapses. Apply it to two drafts: (A) my claim that 1930s human-genetics studies in my country used sampling frames that modern statistics rejects as biased, and (B) my criticism of a 1950s radiation-biology protocol for excluding women without documented justification, where I cannot tell whether this reflects then-routine practice or an enduring methodological failure. For each application, show where the procedure stops, where it defers to judgment, and what it necessarily misses. End by stating the procedure’s hard limits—especially whether any historian can fully escape the epistemic standards of her own scientific training—and finish with a line exactly in the form Verdict: <label> assessing whether the checklist is deployable for my peer-review work.
