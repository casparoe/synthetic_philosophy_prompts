I was recently discussing with friends on a rationalist forum whether psychiatric advance directives are philosophically coherent, and the conversation kept drifting toward constitutional design, so I want to lay out the problem precisely. In several legal systems, a competent patient may execute a Ulysses contract that authorizes clinicians to administer psychiatric treatment during a future episode even if the patient, in that altered state, explicitly refuses it. Separately, liberal constitutions typically entrench fundamental rights behind supermajoritarian amendment rules, thereby allowing an earlier democratic generation to bind later ones. Both practices appear to grant authority to an earlier decision-maker over a later agent—whether that later agent is the same biological individual in a different mental state or a different electorate with revised preferences.

What implicit theory of identity and autonomy makes both institutions intelligible at once? Must we accept that there is a persisting “true” self or a persisting “true” popular will that remains constant across temporal parts, or can a Humean or four-dimensionalist account of persons and collectives accommodate the asymmetry—binding the future without being symmetrically bound by it? More specifically, does the psychiatric case presuppose that autonomy is essentially tied to the capacity for rational deliberation at the moment of contracting, whereas the constitutional case presupposes that democratic legitimacy requires continuity of foundational commitments? Or is there a unified precommitment theory of rational agency—applicable at both individual and collective scales—that licenses both? To push the tension further, consider a hypothetical jurisdiction that entrenches not only rights but also a “trigger clause”: by a specified supermajority, a future legislature may temporarily suspend those very rights during a declared “epistemic emergency,” provided the clause itself was entrenched by an earlier generation. Would adopting such a mechanism be a consistent extension of the same logic that supports Ulysses contracts and constitutional entrenchment, or does it reveal that the two practices actually rest on incompatible pictures of temporal authority?

Please reconstruct the philosophical commitments that these practices—psychiatric Ulysses contracts, constitutional entrenchment, and the hypothetical trigger clause—each presuppose. Distinguish what they strictly require from what they merely happen to accommodate, say whether a single theory can underwrite all three, and reach a defended conclusion about whether they form a coherent account of how rational agents should bind themselves across time, or whether they pull in opposite directions once we make their underlying assumptions explicit.
