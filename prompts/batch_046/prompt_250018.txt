I just finished a long, circular argument with a friend about whether a heart pumps blood because evolutionary history assigned it that job, or simply because that is its present causal contribution to circulation. We kept conflating “what it does” with “what it is for,” and I want to stop talking past people. Next week I am recording an interview for a philosophy podcast with an unnamed philosopher of biology who defends a specific hybrid position: she holds that etiological selection history determines proper function, but argues that we can identify the relevant selected trait only by first decomposing the organism via causal-role analysis into functionally integrated subsystems. On her view, a trait possesses a proper function only if it was selected for contributing to the maintenance of an organizational system that is already causally decomposed; this, she claims, dissolves the standard accidental-function objection without collapsing into pure causal-role functionalism. She also maintains that evolutionary novelty—traits that currently contribute to organizational maintenance but lack selection history—should be assigned provisional “potential functions” rather than full proper functions.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press her view where the literature actually presses. I want at least one question on how she distinguishes malfunction from physical damage, since both seem to disrupt causal contribution; at least one on whether her appeal to “organizational integration” smuggles in a normative standard that the pure causal-role theorist was trying to avoid; and at least one on evolutionary novelty—specifically, how her provisional “potential function” category avoids being either empty or just causal-role functionalism in disguise. For each of these three, include a direct follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: she will probably say that malfunction is failure of a selected contribution within an intact organizational frame, that organizational maintenance is purely descriptive, and that potential functions are defined by their likelihood of being stabilized by selection. Push back on each anticipated defense. The remaining questions should probe how her account handles cross-species comparison, whether it can accommodate exaptation, and what empirical findings in evolutionary developmental biology might undermine her reliance on selection history at all. Keep the tone formal, avoid generic biographical questions, and do not ask for a bibliography or reading list.
