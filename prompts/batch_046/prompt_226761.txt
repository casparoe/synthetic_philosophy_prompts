Next Thursday I present a chapter of my thesis to an interdisciplinary reading group in philosophy of science and philosophy of mind. My core claim is that the cranes-and-skyhooks distinction, together with the associated critique of greedy reductionism, conceals a false dichotomy: in the evolutionary and developmental explanation of exact numerical cognition there exist what I call braced scaffolds—historically contingent intermediate representational structures that are neither reducible without remainder to lower-level perceptual magnitude systems (so they are not cranes in the greedy-reductionist sense) nor autonomous top-down causes that could arise without evolutionary and developmental history (so they are not skyhooks). Specifically, I argue that the transition from an approximate number system to exact symbolic arithmetic requires intermediate formats—something like a structured analog magnitude representation with discrete boundary markers—that carry genuine explanatory weight in predicting error patterns and learning trajectories, yet these formats disappear from mature competence in a way that makes them irreducible to either the primitive system or the final symbolic architecture. The audience is sharply divided: on one side sit three philosophers of biology who defend greedy reductionism and treat any appeal to irreducible intermediate scaffolding as disguised functionalism or emergentism; on the other sit two cognitive scientists who study numerical cognition and are suspicious of philosophical taxonomies that invent metaphysical categories for empirical processes they believe developmental neuroscience is already resolving. I also know that a visiting scholar from the adjacent philosophy department will attend; she recently argued that all talk of scaffolding ultimately collapses back into either crane or skyhook, leaving no conceptual space for a third category.

I need you to prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and potential damage. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary the specific faction would use—whether talk of ontological parsimony, mechanistic explanation, evolutionary continuity, heuristic versus ontological levels, or the distinction between developmental history and functional explanation. For each, tell me what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest, especially regarding my claim that these intermediate formats are genuinely irreducible rather than merely unknown to current neuroscience. Crucially, include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks: that if these scaffolds are genuinely explanatory but eventually vanish from mature cognition, then citing them in the explanation of adult competence is either a genetic fallacy or an unnecessary historical digression, analogous to explaining a bridge by describing the scaffolding removed after construction. Arrange this as a prep sheet rather than a full defense, with reply strategies suited to the brief response time of a seminar question period, and flag the weakest point in my own position—the problem of explanatory disappearance—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
