I have been reconstructing Gödel's modal ontological argument from its usual axiomatic skeleton—properties are positive precisely when their negation is not, entailment among properties preserves positivity, necessary existence counts as positive, and God is defined as the being whose essence is to possess every positive property—and I have introduced an additional principle that I believe is implicit in how essences operate across modal frames, though I am unsure the standard reconstruction relies on it. Call it the Modal Inheritance Principle: if being F is an essence of some being x, and F strictly entails G, then being necessarily G is also part of x's essence. From this, I derive the following chain, which seems to prove far more than Gödel intended.

(1) A property is positive if and only if its negation is not positive. (2) If P is positive and P entails Q, then Q is positive. (3) Necessary existence is a positive property. (4) Being God-like—necessarily possessing all positive properties—is an essence of God. (5) By (3) and (4), God necessarily exists. (6) By the Modal Inheritance Principle, if God necessarily exists, then necessarily necessarily existing is also part of God's essence, and this iteration can be repeated without end. (7) Now consider any property R that God does not possess. By (1), if R is not positive then ~R is positive; hence God necessarily possesses ~R in every possible world. (8) Because God exists in every world and instantiates exactly the set of positive properties there, and because every absence in God is necessarily fixed, there is no modal variation in which properties are instantiated. (9) Therefore all possible worlds are identical: modal space collapses, and contingency is impossible.

The conclusion is clearly too strong—it turns Gödel's proof of a necessary being into a proof that nothing contingent could exist. I feel the flaw must lie in one of the steps above, but each premise looks defensible in isolation: (1)-(4) are standard Gödelian commitments, (5) is the canonical derivation, (6) seems like a natural reading of essence, and (7)-(9) appear to follow by the exclusivity axiom combined with universal instantiation. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, can the Gödelian block the iteration in (6) without weakening the claim that God's essence fixes all divine properties across worlds, or is the real problem that (3) should not be treated as a property that can be inherited in this modal fashion? Please separate what is settled about the structure of Gödel's actual argument from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who study modal logic, essence, and the ontological argument, and do not present any particular diagnosis as if it were the consensus view.
