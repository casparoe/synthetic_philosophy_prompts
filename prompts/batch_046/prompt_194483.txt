I'm constructing a taxonomy for a graduate seminar on hard choices and value incommensurability, but I want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of named theorists. The tree should start with a concrete case: a decision-maker must choose between preserving an irreplaceable cultural practice and preventing a quantifiable but non-catastrophic ecological harm, where the values at stake—cultural continuity versus environmental integrity—are not reducible to a common metric and where both options remain genuinely live after the choice.

From that starting point, design a branching taxonomy where each node is a yes/no or either/or claim that genuinely divides positions on incommensurability. I want at least the following distinctions to appear as branching questions: (1) whether the failure of comparison is metaphysical (the values lack a common measure in principle) or epistemic (we merely lack the cognitive access to compare them); (2) whether a hard choice leaves a rational residue—meaning the agent has reason to regret or honor the unchosen option even after a correct decision—or whether rational choice fully exhausts the normative situation; (3) whether incommensurability holds only at the all-things-considered level or also at the level of pro tanto reasons; (4) whether there exists a second-order value (such as autonomy or integrity of commitment) that can rationally break ties without reducing the first-order values to commensurability; and (5) whether comparative judgments become possible if we expand the informational or temporal frame (e.g., across generations) or remain permanently blocked.

Each leaf should be labeled with the name of the position if one is standard—such as value pluralist, comparativist, or monist about practical reason—or marked unnamed if the combination is not defended in the literature. Every leaf must include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the result that defenders of rival views find hardest to swallow. Additionally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and identify at least one unoccupied branch combination, briefly diagnosing whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The taxonomy should contain no bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations; it should be answerable by reasoning about the conceptual structure alone. Keep the tree to eight to twelve leaves, and phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to.
