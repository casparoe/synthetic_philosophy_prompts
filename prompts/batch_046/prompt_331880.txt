As a novelist currently drafting a narrative that turns on questions of moral judgment and authorial control, I have been wrestling with an extension of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, and I need your help locating the precise fault line. You are an expert philosopher of action with particular sensitivity to modal and normative scope shifts; please think carefully and systematically, and begin by briefly identifying any hidden equivocations or quantifier shifts before delivering your diagnosis. Here is the argument my study partner and I constructed. (1) True moral responsibility requires being causa sui with respect to the action—its ultimate origin must lie in a will that is genuinely self-authored. (2) The will behind any particular act is constituted by one's character, values, and deliberative dispositions at the time. (3) One can only be the ultimate origin of such psychological states if one authored them through prior actions that were themselves expressions of an already self-authored will. (4) This generates an infinite regress of authorship; since no agent initiates their own existence or initial psychological constitution, the regress never terminates in a genuinely self-created origin. (5) Therefore, no human being is the ultimate origin of any psychological state that moves them to act. (6) If one is not the ultimate origin of the states behind an action, one is not truly morally responsible for it. (7) By strict parity of reasoning, epistemic responsibility requires being the ultimate origin of the cognitive dispositions that produce one's beliefs—dispositions shaped by education, neurology, and prior mental history, none of which are self-authored. (8) Hence the Basic Argument, if sound, eliminates not only moral responsibility but rational accountability for belief. (9) Yet rational accountability—giving and demanding reasons—is presupposed by the very practice of philosophy, including Strawson's own evaluation of premises and conclusions. (10) So if the argument is valid, it undermines the normative conditions required to accept or reject it, which seems like a reductio ad absurdum.

I am inclined to protect premise (6) but am deeply suspicious that the slide from (6) to (7) commits a category mistake or an illicit modal extension, though I worry that blocking it may leave the moral conclusion intact only at the cost of an unstable dualism between practical and theoretical reason. Which step do you reject—(1), (3), (6), or (7)—and what does abandoning it cost? In particular, does blocking the regress at (3) require accepting some form of compatibilism that Strawson explicitly rejects, or can one deny (7) without surrendering the moral conclusion and without making philosophy self-undermining? Please work through the alternatives precisely, noting where the argument proves genuinely too much and where it merely looks like it does, and address whether a novelist writing about judgment can salvage any meaningful notion of responsibility without collapsing into the very self-authorship the argument denies.
