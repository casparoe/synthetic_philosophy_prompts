I've been going to these local zoning board meetings in my town lately — mostly cause my neighbor dragged me along and was bored — and I keep noticing something that feels contradictory but everyone treats like it's totally normal. There's this empty lot near the train station that a developer wants to turn into a four-story apartment building with some affordable units. But before they can even break ground there's like three public hearings where anyone who lives within 500 feet can show up and say the building will ruin the character of the neighborhood or make parking worse, and the board usually kills the project or forces it to be half as tall with alot of parking spaces that nobody even asked for.

What I don't understand is that we have laws saying housing discrimination is illegal, and politicians are always talking about how everyone deserves a place to live, but the actual system gives people who already own houses right there this weird veto power over who gets to move in nearby. Like, if I own a home on Maple Street, I apparently have some claim not just to my yard but to the whole vibe and social makeup of the block? And the developer has to prove they're not harming me, even though I'm not the one building anything.

So what philosophical idea is hiding inside that setup? Does it assume that owning property includes owning the social environment around it, or that being a long-time resident gives you special rights over newcomers? And is there any consistent theory of justice that lets us say housing is a human right but also neighbors can block housing? Or is the practice just incoherent — pretending to be about safety and parking but actually presupposing something about who belongs in a place? I want to understand what commitments are baked into how zoning actually works, not just what people say in speeches.
