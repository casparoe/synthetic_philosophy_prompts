I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently turned to philosophy and historiography. I am developing an essay that imports a distinction from my former field—vertical versus horizontal transmission—into debates about the origins of modern nations. Rather than treating national culture as inherited through a single, continuous lineage, I want to suggest that it resembles a genome assembled through both vertical inheritance and lateral acquisition, with historiography often performing the work of retroactively classifying the latter as the former. Specifically, I plan to engage with the familiar critiques associated with Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson—who argue that traditions are often invented and that nations are imagined communities—to ask whether those critiques miss a further layer: not just that continuity is fabricated, but that the very model of continuity used in national narratives is borrowed from nineteenth-century phylogenetic taxonomy, which itself has been complicated by our modern understanding of horizontal gene transfer.

My draft notes outline three arguments.

First, that national historiography implicitly relies on a tree-like model of cultural descent, treating language, ritual, and law as passing cleanly from ancestor to descendant. Second, that what is commonly called invented tradition and what Anderson describes as the modular replication of national form are better understood not as pure fabrication but as horizontal acquisitions—cultural elements borrowed, translated, and then vertically reassigned. I use the biological analogy of retroviral integration: an external sequence becomes incorporated into a genome and is thereafter read as native. Third, I speculate that if sovereignty claims depend on the moral authority of uninterrupted vertical inheritance, then recognizing the prevalence of horizontal transmission might weaken the normative force of those claims—though I recognize this may be a non sequitur.

I am writing to ask for comments on these notes. In particular, I would like to know whether the evolutionary analogy risks being mere metaphorical ornamentation rather than a substantive conceptual tool; whether I have distorted the positions associated with Hobsbawm or Anderson, since I have only encountered them through secondary literature; and whether the move from descriptive historiography to normative sovereignty in my third point is defensible or an unwarranted leap. Please reply in American English, and I ask that you avoid offering bibliographies or reading lists, as I prefer conceptual evaluation over citation.
