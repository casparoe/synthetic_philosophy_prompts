I have been preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of embodiment and found myself in a prolonged disagreement with a colleague about how seriously we should take Nietzsche’s sustained reflections on digestion, diet, and physical regimen in works such as Ecce Homo and Twilight of the Idols. Rather than dismissing those passages as biographical footnotes to his chronic migraines and gastric failures, I want to evaluate four competing interpretive frameworks according to how well they integrate his explicit prescriptions—his well-known praise for lighter Italian cooking and his corresponding disdain for heavy German fare, beer, and overabundant meals—with his broader philosophical commitments: the body as the locus of will, the diagnosis of modern “decadence,” and the ideal of a “great health” that exceeds mere absence of disease.

Please rank the following four frameworks from the most to the least adequate for this integrative task:

(A) The clinical-biographical reduction: Nietzsche’s dietary remarks are essentially symptomatic management of his personal neurological and digestive collapse, carrying little independent philosophical weight.

(B) The asceticism-inversion thesis: Nietzsche deliberately parodies Christian fasting and bodily denial by translating them into secular, strength-oriented disciplines, so that diet becomes a performative demonstration of anti-ascetic vitality.

(C) The physiological-epistemic framework: digestion operates as both literal process and philosophical metaphor for intellectual assimilation; heavy, “indigestible” German food symbolizes the sluggish, unselective thinking of modern culture, while lighter Mediterranean fare symbolizes the quick, powerful, discriminating cognition proper to strong natures.

(D) The geopolitical-corporeal diagnosis: dietary prescriptions encode a concealed hierarchy of climates and bodily types—Mediterranean versus Northern European—that silently underwrites his broader critiques of nationalism, democracy, and the leveling tendencies of modernity.

In developing your ranking, please clearly separate what is relatively settled in the scholarly literature on Nietzsche’s philosophy of the body from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any single framework as the undisputed consensus. I am especially interested in whether treating dietary failure as a moral and aesthetic catastrophe—rather than as mere physical discomfort—forces us upward from (A) toward (C) or (D), and where exactly that inference is weakest. State your final ranking in a fixed, machine-readable form at the very end, for example \answer{C > B > D > A}.
