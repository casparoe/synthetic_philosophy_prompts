Design a 75-minute classroom simulation for about 18 graduate students in moral and political philosophy, set in a seminar room with tables that can be rearranged, whose purpose is to make them feel—not merely discuss—the structural contradiction between rehabilitation-oriented prison architecture (exemplified by Norway’s Halden model of normalization and reciprocal staff-prisoner interaction) and American mass-incarceration logic built on incapacitation, status forfeiture, and risk management. Give each student a fixed role—prison architect, prisoner-rights advocate, corrections-budget officer, victim-family representative, guard-union delegate, or state legislator—plus a shared material kit: index cards representing physical units (cells, common rooms, work spaces), blue tokens representing unstructured staff-prisoner interaction time, and red chips representing mandatory security audits. The rules should require the group to build a 40-bed unit and a daily schedule using only these materials, with the explicit instruction that they may optimize for dignity, security, and fiscal constraint simultaneously.

Arrange the hidden mechanics so that participants initially believe integration is possible: for the first 30 minutes they collaborate silently in role, then defend their build. At minute 30, reveal a concealed institutional rule—every blue token placed in a shared space automatically generates two red chips that must be assigned to staff surveillance roles, removing those staff from interaction roles—which forces the group to discover in real time that normalization and punitive risk-management cannibalize the same institutional resources rather than trading off at the margin. The timeline should run: 0–15 minutes silent individual design; 15–30 minutes collaborative build; 30–45 minutes crisis revelation and forced redesign under time pressure; 45–60 minutes simulated inspection in which remaining students form an external review panel and each role must justify one feature; 60–75 minutes debrief.

In the debrief, map each crisis moment onto a conceptual distinction—specifically, whether prison design treats the prisoner as retaining moral standing as a reciprocal agent (normalization as recognition) or as having forfeited that standing through offense (degradation as status loss)—without allowing the facilitator to lecture the answer. Specify what will predictably fail the first time you run it: architecture-trained students may dominate spatial layout, victim representatives may be silenced during the redesign crisis, and the group may resolve tension by splitting resources evenly rather than experiencing the contradiction; instruct the facilitator to correct these by enforcing speaking-order rules and forbidding compromise allocations during the crisis phase. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—above all, the irreversibility of actual time served and the asymmetry of coercive power that makes “consent” to prison conditions impossible—and state exactly how the facilitator must supply this verbally during debrief so the analogy does not mislead.

Inject into the prompt a niche framing: treat the blue-token/red-chip mechanism not as a generic resource conflict but as a model of “reciprocal recognition capacity,” arguing that rehabilitation requires institutional spaces where staff and prisoners can occupy interchangeable social roles, whereas punitive logic requires fixed hierarchical visibility that structurally prevents such interchangeability. Take a committed position—do not present rehabilitation and punishment as equally plausible outcomes for the user to choose between—on whether the simulation ultimately demonstrates that normalization is structurally impossible within a framework that retains punitive status forfeiture, or whether it reveals that Halden-style design succeeds precisely by withdrawing the forfeiture premise. Defend your stance through the specific rules you set (for instance, if you argue that normalization collapses, design the hidden constraint to be unavoidable; if you argue it survives, build in an escape route that only works if the group collectively rejects the red-chip logic). Give your reasons for that design choice within the prompt itself, and include an honest note on which philosophical feature—such as the temporal extension of punishment across years rather than minutes—this classroom exercise necessarily cannot replicate.
