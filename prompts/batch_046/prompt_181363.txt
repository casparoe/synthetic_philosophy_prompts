I'm preparing my contribution for a graduate seminar on nationalism, historical ontology, and the politics of memory, and I'd like a consistency audit of the position I've staked out before I defend it in discussion. Here are my commitments, in no particular order.

First, I hold the standard modernist line: nations are essentially modern artifacts, and to call France or Japan a "nation" in 1200 is to project present categories onto past polities. Second, I also think this modernist claim needs qualification: some regions display striking institutional, linguistic, and dynastic continuity across centuries, so the modern nation-state inherits real precursors rather than inventing everything ex nihilo. Third, I accept the idea of the nation as an imagined community—members feel ethical obligations to strangers mediated by shared symbols and narratives they will never verify personally. Fourth, however, I think this "imagined" quality isn't unique to modern nations; pre-modern religious and imperial identities operated with a structurally similar solidarity, which means modern nationalism differs mainly in scale and in substituting the nation for religion as the primary frame of belonging. Fifth, I'm convinced by the invention-of-tradition thesis: many practices and symbols presented as immemorial—regional dress codes, coronation rituals, folk canons—were standardized or fabricated in the nineteenth or early twentieth centuries. Sixth, despite that, I don't think these traditions are merely false or decorative once adopted; they become genuinely constitutive of identity and can carry real moral and emotional weight. Seventh, I think ethnic nationalism, which ties membership to ancestry or cultural inheritance, is normatively defective compared to civic nationalism, which is open, voluntary, and universalist in principle. Eighth, yet I also think civic nationalism cannot function without a shared public culture—language, civic rituals, historical narratives—and that shared culture almost always reflects the traditions of a dominant group, so civic identity implicitly rests on an ethnic substrate. Ninth, in genuinely multicultural societies, the stable path is to construct what I call a "meta-tradition": framing pluralism and hybridity themselves as the nation's ancient, defining heritage, which I acknowledge is a second-order invention. Tenth, I hold that the "archival gap" of pre-modern societies—the absence of mass literacy, census-taking, and widespread print circulation—made reflexive, mass-participatory "national consciousness" impossible before modernity, even if local or dynastic loyalties certainly existed.

Please go through these commitments systematically. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look like tensions but dissolve on closer inspection? For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, explain precisely what is pulling against what, and say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. Then tell me which single revision—dropping or substantially modifying one commitment—would clean up the most tension overall. I am not signaling which commitment I would be most willing to surrender, so treat the audit as neutral: my job is to see whether the set hangs together, not to defend it at all costs.
