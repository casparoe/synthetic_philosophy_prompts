I don't have any formal training in philosophy—I'm a nursing assistant who reads essays on breaks—but I've been arguing with friends about whether people can be truly blamed for outcomes that depend mostly on being in the wrong place at the wrong time. The question stopped feeling abstract when I was asked to deliver the retirement toast for Elias, a warehouse manager I worked under for twelve years, who was dismissed this spring after a structural beam failed during his shift.

The beam had been inspected and approved by three other supervisors across five years; Elias had actually filed the only unrepaired maintenance request concerning it. If the failure had occurred thirty minutes earlier or later, a different supervisor would have been on the clock, and Elias would be retiring with an unblemished record and full benefits. I need the toast—delivered next Thursday at the community center to family, coworkers, and a company representative—to honor the care he genuinely practiced without pretending that his dismissal was morally deserved, and without falling back on empty comforts like "everything happens for a reason." At the same time, I don't want it to sound like a grievance or a legal defense. Can you write remarks that do real philosophical work in plain language, distinguishing what Elias was actually responsible for from what was simply the misfortune of being the person present when a long-hidden risk finally broke through—so that everyone in the room can see why he deserves respect no matter which supervisor happened to be standing beneath that beam?
