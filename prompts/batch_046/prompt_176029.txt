Having retired from a career in the natural sciences and only recently turned to philosophy in earnest, I find myself drawn to the logical architecture of population ethics rather than to its rhetorical forms. I am particularly preoccupied with the so-called procreation asymmetry—the intuition that there is a moral difference between bringing a suffering person into existence and failing to bring a happy one into existence, but that the latter carries no comparable obligation.

Rather than a general survey, I wish to ask for a defended agenda. If one were to direct a small research group—say, three philosophers and one formally inclined ethicist—for about four years, which open questions, conceptual dependencies, or practical implications arising from the procreation asymmetry should they treat as upstream? I am less interested in a bibliography than in reasoning: for each priority, why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is without empirical laboratory apparatus, and what conceptual or formal result over the coming years would cause you to reorder it.

To avoid well-trodden ground, frame your answer with reference to an underexplored intersection: the asymmetry’s behaviour when reproductive choice is modelled not as a single binary decision but as a branching intervention on a causal graph in which potential persons are treated as nodes with indeterminate identity conditions—something closer to synthetic biology or climate-adaptation reproductive postponement than to standard childbearing. Within that framing, which unresolved issues—perhaps about the coherence of person-affecting restrictions under branching, about whether asymmetry survives aggregation across possible lineages, or about its implications for intergenerational climate policy—matter most?

Please rank them, defend the ordering using criteria such as logical priority, tractability for non-empirical work, and downstream consequence for moral theory or public policy, and indicate precisely what kind of argument or result would change your mind. Write your answer in British English, using British spelling and punctuation conventions throughout.
