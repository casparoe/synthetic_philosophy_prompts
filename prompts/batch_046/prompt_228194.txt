In philosophical and theological discussions of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, I repeatedly encounter the formulation: "the link between cause and effect is not necessary in itself, but holds only through God's habitual will." The phrase is made to do very different work: in some arguments it supports a minimalist, Humean reading of science; in others it is taken to mean that every particular event is an independently willed miracle with no continuity across time; in still others it is used to deny that created things possess any causal powers whatsoever. Please separate the precise claims that could be hiding inside this one sentence. For each distinct thesis—whether about the epistemic status of causal laws, the metaphysics of secondary causation, the identity of events across time, or the relation between divine omnipotence and created nature—state whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible occasionalist position, or actually false. Then say which of these readings is being invoked when someone uses the line to argue that quantum indeterminacy provides empirical confirmation of occasionalism, and whether that particular application survives once the slogan is disambiguated.
