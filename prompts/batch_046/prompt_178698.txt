I'm presenting at next week's philosophy of well-being seminar to defend a revisionist hedonist reply to Nozick's experience machine. My thesis distinguishes "indexical well-being"—well-being tied to causal tracking of actual states of affairs, which I treat as a prudential concern about rational planning—from "structural well-being," the internal coherence, intensity-profile, and relational harmony among mental states, which I claim is the only genuinely axiological dimension. I argue that modal fragility (the possibility that one's mental states are machine-generated) undermines indexical well-being but leaves structural well-being untouched, so the machine objection confuses a planning-relevant feature with a value-constitutive one; I concede that reality-contact matters for narrative identity and long-term agency, but deny it is necessary for how well a life goes at any moment. The audience is mostly objective-list theorists and Aristotelians who think real achievement, knowledge, and objective functioning are necessary for well-being, plus one hedonist ally and a few agnostics; they know Nozick's thought experiment well and are suspicious of definitional rescues that save hedonism by redrawing boundaries. Prepare my Q&A sheet: list the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased as an objective-list theorist or Aristotelian would phrase them; for each, indicate whether it is a serious threat or mainly rhetorical, what I should concede up front, and what I must hold; identify the weakest point in my indexical/structural distinction—the spot I should defend explicitly rather than hope they miss; and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, likely that my view makes well-being too cheap or allows "well-being zombies" with perfect mental coherence and no causal anchor, with a two-minute reply and a clear flag on where that reply is soft. Keep the reply notes concise enough to use in real time.
