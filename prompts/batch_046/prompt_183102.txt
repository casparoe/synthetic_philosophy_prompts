I review proposals for a mid-size transit authority — about one a week, have to sign off in under an hour, and my boss wants a hard checklist not a memo. The idea is "safe Pareto improvements": at least one party is genuinely better off, nobody is worse off, and there's no hidden path where the gains reverse later. But I keep running into three weird failure modes that the standard economics texts don't really cover: (1) benefits that depend on people not knowing they've lost, (2) gains that ignore people who aren't users yet but will be affected, and (3) what a colleague calls the reverse-compensation trap — the proposal claims it's okay because losers could be compensated, but if you actually paid them the improvement would disappear or the loser group would shift. I need a reusable proceedure I can run in ~40 minutes: ordered questions, what counts as pass/fail, explicit stop points with a no, and clear flags where I'm making a value call not a fact check. It should handle probabilistic benefits, hidden costs, and preference adaptation. Then apply it to three cases: A) rerouting a bus to save 500 riders 20 minutes but cutting service to a block with 12 elderly non-drivers; B) cutting student fares funded by a hidden tourist surcharge people don't notice; C) replacing a park-and-ride with housing, better air quality for residents but 80 commuters lose parking and say they'll drive downtown instead. One of these should come out in a way I won't like. Also say what the proceedure can't do. And please tell me how confident you are in each step and point out the part you're least sure about — especially that reverse-compensation thing, since I'm not even sure that's a real concept or just something we made up in the office to test whether the framework holds together.
