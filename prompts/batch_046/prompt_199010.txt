I am writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas publication and need to stress-test a position I have adopted. I hold that weakness of will—both the practical kind, in which one acts against one's better judgment, and a less-discussed epistemic kind, in which one sincerely endorses a proposition in reflective deliberation yet fails to treat it as settled in subsequent reasoning or action—is entirely reducible to hyperbolic discounting mediated by intertemporal bargaining failures, with no residual explanatory work for a unitary executive will or for a distinctively normative form of failure. On this view, the apparent phenomenon of an agent who knows the good and chooses the bad dissolves into a steep, frame-dependent reversal in preference curves: the distant reward of health or consistency is heavily discounted, while the immediate reward of consumption or cognitive ease dominates, and the self is best modeled not as a sovereign decision-maker but as a sequence of temporal bargaining positions that can fail to form a stable coalition. I extend this even to doxastic commitment, suggesting that epistemic akrasia—asserting P while behaving as if not-P—follows the same curve, since the cost of holding a belief consistently across contexts is deferred, while the immediate social or emotional payoff of abandoning it is present. I want you to tell me, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—written as one writes an email or a letter—what would genuinely count against this reduction, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which findings in neuroeconomics or addiction science, which phenomenological reports of agency, which formal results about preference consistency or rational-choice axioms, and which conceptual distinctions—particularly between synchronic choice and diachronic commitment, or between instrumental and intrinsic valuation—would have to obtain for the view to collapse? For each potential defeater, indicate whether a committed defender of the hyperbolic-reductionist account would concede it as damaging or would dismiss it, and be explicit about whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: for instance, if every observed instance of self-control failure can always be redescribed post hoc as a local spike in the discount rate, does that insulation protect the theory or undermine it? Finally, assess whether extending the mechanism into epistemic weakness of will is a disciplined theoretical expansion or an ad hoc overreach that exposes the account to new anomalies it cannot absorb. Write the answer in American English.
