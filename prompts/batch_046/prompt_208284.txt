As a philosophy professor who chairs the disciplinary review panel of a national research-funding body, I must assess, on a recurring weekly basis, whether individual researchers bear moral responsibility for misconduct when they claim that institutional or algorithmic protocols eliminated any viable alternative. These decisions carry high stakes — career termination, funding revocation, and legal exposure — and must be rendered in writing within forty-five minutes for an audience of lawyers, scientists, and lay board members who have no philosophical training. I want you to operationalise Harry Frankfurt's critique of the Principle of Alternative Possibilities into a reusable decision procedure — a stepwise checklist or flowchart in words — that my panel can apply consistently.

The procedure should test whether the agent possessed the relevant internal conditions for responsibility — the right mental states, identification with the action, and absence of coercion that actually determined the will — rather than merely testing whether an alternative was physically available. It must include, in strict order: the initial screening questions; the tests that distinguish a genuine Frankfurt-style counterfactual intervener (whether human, bureaucratic, or algorithmic) from a mere obstacle; the point at which the procedure asks whether the agent's own reasons, rather than the threat of intervention, were sufficient to produce the action; the criteria for identifying distributed or automated interveners that function analogously to Frankfurt's hidden controller; and the explicit junctures where the procedure must hand the decision to human judgement because the question is one of values rather than conceptual analysis. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence the panel should demand, and where the procedure stops with a determination of responsibility or exoneration.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, presenting the reasoning at each step. First, a senior researcher who fabricates data after receiving an implicit threat from a departmental algorithm that automatically flags non-compliant projects for funding suspension, where the researcher claims they had no choice. Second, a junior researcher who continues a harmful clinical trial because an automated ethics-gate protocol would have locked the dataset if they withdrew, but who privately welcomed the continuation; here the procedure should yield an answer that I, as chair, am likely to find morally uncomfortable — please identify that tension explicitly. Third, a case where the intervener is ambiguous: a collaborative consortium where each partner's withdrawal triggers automatic penalties for all others, so that individual refusal is structurally impossible, yet no single entity plays the role of Frankfurt's controller.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot determine — for instance, whether institutional design itself is culpable, or whether the absence of alternatives excuses collective rather than individual action — and note the conditions under which the procedure will misfire. Please provide the answer in British English, avoid bibliographies or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from philosophical texts; paraphrase all theoretical claims in your own words.
