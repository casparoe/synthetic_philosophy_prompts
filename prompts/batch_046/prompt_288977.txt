I'm preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of perception seminar. We've spent the term on direct realism, representationalism, and the argument from illusion, with particular attention to disjunctivism about perceptual experience — the view that in veridical perception and in hallucination there is no shared mental state or phenomenal kind common to both cases. I want one essay question that can't be answered by reciting the standard good-case/bad-case distinction or summarizing the objections McDowell and Martin raise against the common-factor principle.

Instead, the question should force students to confront a specific tension I've been calling the "introspection-relocation problem." Disjunctivists deny that a hallucination involves the same kind of experiential state as a veridical perception, yet they must still explain why a subject in a perfect hallucination can sincerely report that her experience is "just like seeing a red cube." The prompt should ask students to evaluate whether this forces the disjunctivist into an error theory about introspective reports in bad cases, or whether they can maintain that subjective indistinguishability is purely epistemic — a failure of discrimination — rather than metaphysical. Crucially, students should be required to assess whether solving this metaphysical problem actually resolves the epistemological problem raised by the argument from illusion: if a subject cannot tell her current experience apart from a perfect hallucination, does the absence of shared phenomenal character provide any independent reason to think she knows she is now perceiving?

Please write: (1) the essay question itself, designed for a 75-minute sitting with an expected response of about 800 words; (2) a model answer of roughly 700–800 words that works through the reasoning rather than labeling positions; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves required — for instance, distinguishing metaphysical from epistemic disjunctivism, addressing whether introspective authority is preserved in bad cases, explaining why denying a common factor does not automatically block skeptical scenarios, and identifying one concrete point where the disjunctivist still owes an account — together with a brief note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks at each point. Do not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from any philosopher's published work; paraphrase as needed. The difficulty should come from the conceptual crux, not from obscure historical details or niche terminology students wouldn't have encountered.
