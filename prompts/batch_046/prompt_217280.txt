Since discovering that my grandparents served in the civil administration of a regime later dissolved for genocide, I have been serving on a municipal heritage board that issues advisory rulings on collective responsibility, working within Karl Jaspers’s framework of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. Our precedents are as follows. First, we held that citizens of an uninterrupted democracy bear political guilt for crimes committed by an elected government they did not personally support, provided institutional continuity and continued benefit persist. Second, we held that descendants who inherited wealth extracted through those crimes bear neither criminal nor moral guilt individually, though they may hold reparative obligations. Third, we held that an ethnic or religious community whose collective identity survives across generations bears metaphysical guilt if it fails to acknowledge the wrong, regardless of whether present members personally endorsed the acts. Fourth, we held that a legal corporation retaining its original charter and assets, despite complete turnover in ownership and personnel, bears political but not moral guilt. Fifth, we held that immigrants who arrived only after the regime’s fall and never held citizenship in it bear no collective guilt whatsoever. We now face this case: the offending state was formally dissolved, its constitution annulled, and a new republic founded on the same territory with a renamed government; the present generation was born entirely after this rupture and never lived under the old regime, yet the current state employs some of the old bureaucratic archives, administers reparations funded by assets seized during the genocide, and governs a population ethnically continuous with the perpetrators’ community. Should we advise that this present community bears collective guilt, and if so of which Jaspersian kind? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in identifying the competing rules that fit our five precedents equally well, stating what each rule predicts for this dissolved-and-reconstituted case, offering your best-guess recommendation with the reasons for preferring it, and specifying which further hypothetical case would best discriminate between those rules. In your answer, please separate what is philosophically settled about intergenerational collective guilt from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of Jaspers, and do not present a disputed interpretation of his four categories as if it were the consensus view.
