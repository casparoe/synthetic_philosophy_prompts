I'm leading a 90-minute upper-level philosophy seminar with about 15 students. We've been working on Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle—specifically, the claim that theater doesn't merely distract citizens from virtue but structurally transforms public life by inserting a passive audience where active co-participation should be. I want an in-class simulation, not a lecture or discussion, that makes students feel this structural corruption before it is named.

Design a runnable role-play with clear roles, rules, and symbolic payoffs that require no real money. Divide the group into "deliberators" and "witnesses," with rules that initially allow everyone to speak and act, but then—through a shift in the scoring system—reward witnesses for remaining silent and evaluating, while rewarding deliberators only for being visibly evaluated well. The timeline should include an initial round where all participants share a task, a transition round where visibility rules change, and a final round where the two roles are fixed. Arrange incentives so that, by the end, students discover that the object of their desire has reversed: they are no longer seeking to be-in-the-act-with-others but seeking to be-in-the-gaze-of-others, and the witnesses find comfort in consuming virtue rather than practicing it.

The debrief should map specific moments onto Rousseau's distinctions—particularly between acting for approval versus acting with others, and between the festival (where actor and audience are the same) and the theater (where they are separated and fixed). Tell me what will predictably fail the first time: for instance, deliberators performing virtue rather than practicing it, witnesses refusing the passive role, or the group interpreting the corruption as merely "peer pressure" rather than a transformation in the mode of social being. Say how to handle each failure without breaking the simulation's logic.

Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce—likely the specific historical and civic conditions of Rousseau's Geneva, and possibly the gendered dynamics of 18th-century spectacle that shaped his account of visibility—and indicate exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally in the debrief.

To keep this in the weeds rather than a generic "Rousseau hated theater" exercise, frame the corruption not as bad content or immoral messages but as a reversal in the logic of recognition: inject the conceptual twist that citizens initially seek recognition as co-agents, but the theatrical structure converts that into a desire to see virtue performed and to be seen performing it, and ask that the rules be arranged so students experience this shift as a change in what feels rewarding, not just in what they say.
