I am marking essays for an advanced undergraduate seminar on Enlightenment political philosophy, and I should like an independent assessment of one submission before I finalise the marks. The examination question asked students to reconstruct Rousseau’s argument in his Letter to d’Alembert that the establishment of a public theatre in Geneva would corrupt rather than improve public morals, and to distinguish carefully between his critique of theatrical representation—what actors do and what audiences learn from feigned passions—and his critique of theatrical spectatorship—the social and psychological effects of assembled, passive observation. Students were then invited to evaluate whether either strand requires Rousseau’s specifically republican conception of citizenship, or whether, as some recent commentators have proposed, the argument instead rests upon an “epistemology of presence”: the claim that genuine moral knowledge can be transmitted only through unmediated, participatory co-presence rather than through representational media. They were asked whether this framing is faithful to Rousseau or imports an anachronistic concern.

The student’s answer, reproduced below in full, runs as follows. Rousseau’s Letter distinguishes two corrupting mechanisms. First, representation: actors wear masks and imitate passions they do not genuinely feel, teaching the audience that virtue is a matter of appearance rather than substance; this closely resembles Plato’s criticism in the Republic that mimetic art produces images of images, distancing citizens from the truth. Second, spectatorship: the theatre gathers citizens in darkness where they sit silently, observing fictional suffering without being moved to act; this passivity erodes the active civic engagement that republican government requires, and Rousseau contrasts it with public festivals where citizens dance together and collectively judge moral character, thereby reinforcing equality. With respect to the “epistemology of presence”, the student argues that this framing is largely anachronistic, since Rousseau is concerned not with mediated knowledge in any phenomenological sense but with social equality and emotional corruption. The theatre introduces luxury and rank—expensive boxes separate the wealthy from the poor—so it undermines Geneva’s republican equality. Consequently, the student concludes that the objection does depend upon republicanism: without the commitment to civic equality, the corruption of spectatorship loses its specific political danger, though the representation strand might survive independently in any constitution. From this, the student infers that the core of Rousseau’s objection is therefore psychological rather than political, even if the framing helpfully highlights the participatory nature of his preferred festivals.
