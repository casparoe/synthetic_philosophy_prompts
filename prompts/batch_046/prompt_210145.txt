There's this municipal ordinance in a mid-size city — they call it the "indirect affect permit" — where if you want to organize a public rally or put up an art installation in the central square you have to file a form estimating not just direct risks like traffic or violence, but the "emotional ripple" to people who might encounter it accidentally. The council can deny the permit if the ripple score is high enough, even if nobody gets physically hurt and every participant consents. They defend it as just extending Mill's harm principle to cover serious psychological harm, but im not convinced that holds up. What theory of harm and offense does this practice actually presuppose? Does it need to treat mere discomfort or offense as a coercible harm, or can a strict Millian defend it by saying indirect emotional effects still fall within protected interests? And is the policy even internally consistent — they allow loud construction noise that produces the same ripple scores but deny the rally? I want a committed answer, not a menu: is this a coherent extension of Mill, or does it quietly smuggle in a perfectionist or paternalist view of welfare? Tell me which view it really is, and point out exactly where the practice collapses if we reject that underlying commitment. Also note thier inconsistency with construction noise — that part feels like it undermines thier own reasoning.
