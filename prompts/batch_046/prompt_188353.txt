I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of economics, and I've adopted a position I want rigorously tested. I hold that backward induction in the centipede game is best understood as a conceptual closure condition on practical reasoning—call it practical closure—rather than merely an empirical prediction about how agents behave. The idea is this: if a fully rational agent recognizes that she will take at node n, she thereby acquires decisive reason to take at n-1, and the reasoning propagates all the way back to the root. On this view, reaching a deeply cooperative endpoint is not just unlikely; it is conceptually incompatible with full rationality, regardless of payoff structure or risk profile, because any preference for continued cooperation at a later node is rationally revisable downward once closure is applied. I've considered whether forward-induction reasoning—where reaching a node licenses inferences about preferences that block closure—can rescue cooperation, but I remain unconvinced.

What would genuinely count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which observations from actual experimental or high-stakes play, which formal equilibrium refinements, which philosophical analogies (for instance, to epistemic closure or to the surprise examination paradox), and which conceptual distinctions—such as between knowing one's future action and merely predicting it—would be fatal? Which would be merely awkward? I want to know specifically what a committed defender of practical-closure backward induction would themselves concede as damaging, versus what they would dismiss as failing to grasp the normative claim. If the position turns out to be well insulated—if every counterexample can be absorbed by qualifying 'full rationality' or by redefining cooperation—please say so plainly, and assess whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a view of this kind.

For my part, I would be most moved by a coherent formal framework in which agents with common knowledge of rationality reach deep cooperative nodes without relying on risk aversion, bounded rationality, or exogenous social preferences. I am less persuaded by raw empirical violations, which I tend to treat as noise or framing effects.
