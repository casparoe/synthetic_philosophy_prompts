I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on rational choice and ethics, and we've been analyzing hypothetical scenarios in which an agent claims predictive power over a decision-maker's behavior. I keep encountering a structural variant that I haven't been able to match to any concept in our readings. The scenario works like this: you are informed that, depending on an internal randomization that has already occurred in some counterfactual sense, either state-of-affairs A will obtain—where you suffer harm X—or state-of-affairs B will obtain—where you suffer harm Y. You are then offered an option to take a costly action that allegedly prevents both A and B. The crucial feature is that A and B are mutually exclusive and both counterfactual to your actual present situation; neither harm will occur in the world you actually inhabit regardless of what you do. Moreover, your costly action exerts no causal influence on either counterfactual branch. Nevertheless, the disjunctive framing—"either X or Y, and you can stop both"—generates a distinctive kind of rational pressure to comply, as if you were facing a single compound risk rather than two independent, unrealized possibilities. Our course has covered cases where a predictor threatens one specific punishment contingent on non-cooperation, but this split-branch version seems different: the coercive force appears to derive from the logical structure of the disjunction itself, not from any unified predictive mechanism. Does this disjunctive bundling of counterfactual harms have a recognized name in decision theory? How should it be distinguished from the single-threat predictive scenarios we've discussed, and does it identify a separate failure mode in reasoning about risk across possible worlds, or is it merely a rhetorical restructuring of the same underlying problem?
