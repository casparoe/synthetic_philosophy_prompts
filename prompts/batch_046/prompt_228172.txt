I was discussing with friends over dinner whether obligations genuinely survive total internal transformation, and the conversation turned to a dispute involving my aunt, who currently chairs a small community land trust that my grandfather helped found in 1972. Every original trustee has since died or relocated; the charter has been rewritten twice; the physical parcels have been subdivided, rezoned, and partly sold; yet the organization retains the same legal name and federal identification number. A developer now claims the trust is bound by an easement agreement executed by the original board in 1982, but my aunt contends that nothing that existed in 1982 remains—no people, no rules, no land—except the administrative paperwork, so the entity that made the promise is effectively gone, replaced by a succession of entirely different groups linked only by bureaucratic continuity. The developer replies that this reasoning is analogous to claiming I am not responsible for promises I made in childhood because every cell in my body has turned over. Both analogies feel partially right, but I am unsure whether the disagreement is merely about legal formalities or whether it reaches a deeper question about persistence: specifically, whether an entity can be genuinely identical across decades if every constituent part has been replaced, or whether it is more accurate to treat it as an extended temporal object composed of successive, non-overlapping stages, with the 1982 stage being a distinct part from the present one. I am not looking for legal advice about the easement itself; rather, I want to understand whether there is a coherent metaphysical framework that determines whether my aunt’s organization really is the same thing that signed the contract in 1982, and what that determination implies about whether the obligation genuinely carries over to the current board. In particular, I am curious whether the answer depends on treating the trust as being fully present at each moment of its history versus being spread through time in the way a road is spread through space, and whether that distinction can be applied without simply assuming the conclusion.
