I was discussing rational disagreement in my philosophy of science seminar and a classmate walked me into this argument I can't find the exit from. (1) Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two rational agents share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, they must converge on the same beliefs. (2) Apply that to ethics: philosophers who've recieved all the same empirical data and logical arguments about, say, the moral status of animals should eventually agree if they're rational. (3) But persistent disagreement—Singer vs. Cohen-style—shows they don't converge, so they must lack a common prior over "axiological weights." (4) If your prior about how much suffering matters is just an arbitrary starting point, then ethical reasoning doesn't update it; it only unfolds conclusions from that bias. (5) So either moral reasoning is just bias amplification with no truth-tracking, or every long-running ethical dispute is proof of irrationality. (6) He takes this to show the common prior assumption collapses when we apply it to normative domains, because it forces us to pick one of those two awful options. I want to keep (1) and (5) if I can—there should be some constraint on rational disagreement, and bias-amplification does sound like a real risk—but I feel like something slipped between (3) and (4). Maybe it's an equivocation between evidential priors and value commitments? Or maybe (2) smuggles in that ethical beliefs are just probabilities over propositions? Which step do you actually reject, and what does giving it up cost—do I lose Aumann entirely, or just admit that values sit outside its scope?
