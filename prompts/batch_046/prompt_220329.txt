Consider the institutional practice of libertarian paternalism as implemented in public choice-architecture: opt-out defaults for retirement savings and organ donation are defended as autonomy-preserving because the option to opt out remains available, yet the same design logic is deliberately withheld from domains such as reproductive health decisions or religious registration, where policymakers treat default settings as illegitimate influence rather than neutral framing. Is the practice strictly committed to that theory, or could it be licensed by a purely instrumental concern for tractability or welfare maximization that makes no metaphysical claims about “true” preferences obscured by inertia? Examine further whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent commitments: does treating inertia as a rational error in financial planning but as a protected expression of identity in other contexts require two incompatible theories of the self? Conclude by describing what a policy framework built on a rival conception—one that treats all preference formation as equally constructed and denies that policymakers can identify “errors” without substantive authoritarianism—would have to change about the architecture itself, not merely about which domains it applies to.
