As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I am trying to understand the Nyāya account of knowledge through the lens of measurement practice I knew for forty years. Please restate the theory of the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison), and śabda (testimony)—in the vocabulary of laboratory instrumentation: instrument response functions, calibration against certified standards, analogical scaling across measurement regimes, and peer-reviewed data transmission with documented error budgets. Do not merely relabel the four sources; show how the Nyāya conditions for valid cognition—such as avyabhicāra (the non-deviation of the middle term in inference) and sāmānyalakṣaṇa (the universal feature that licenses analogical cognition)—correspond to, or fail to correspond to, conditions we impose on calibrated instruments and validated protocols. Then report precisely what does not survive this translation. In particular, is there any essential role for the knowing self (ātman) as the substrate of awareness, or for the teleological orientation of the pramāṇas toward the four puruṣārthas, that has no functional counterpart in an instrumentalist framework? If so, does that absence indicate a genuine limitation of empirical measurement theory—that it deliberately brackets questions of purpose and subjectivity—or does it suggest that those Nyāya elements are doing metaphysical work that scientific practice simply leaves unexamined?
