I'm a doctoral student in comparative philosophy of religion, currently working through the Jain epistemological tradition in a seminar on Indian metaphysics. Last week I was trying to explain anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality is irreducibly many-sided—to a colleague in cognitive science, and we began testing whether the sevenfold scheme of conditional predication (syādvāda) can be coherently applied outside classical Jain ontology: specifically, to scientific, ethical, and technological judgments. I realized my own intuitions about which claims genuinely warrant many-sided treatment are somewhat scattered, and I want to know whether there's a principled boundary between legitimate anekānta and mere ambiguity, contradiction, or conceptual confusion.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut reaction. First, "anthropogenic climate change is occurring" strikes me as paradigmatically anekānta: affirmed unconditionally from the standpoint of atmospheric chemistry, qualified conditionally from the standpoint of deep-time geology (where human influence is a brief perturbation within larger cycles), and inexpressible from the standpoint of integrated socio-ecological systems that resist binary framing. So syādvāda applies legitimately here.

Second, "this large language model possesses semantic understanding" feels like it should also be treated with conditional predication—behaviorally affirmed, phenomenologically inexpressible, neurobiologically negated, and functionally qualified—yet I keep worrying that this is just conceptual muddle rather than ontological complexity. I lean weakly toward applying anekānta, but I am genuinely uncertain.

Third, "torture is morally impermissible" is, for me, not anekānta at all. Attempts to qualify it—say, "torture is wrong except to extract life-saving information"—do not reveal a hidden side of the moral fact but rather commit an error in reasoning. So here I reject the sevenfold scheme entirely.

Fourth, "liberal democracy is the best form of government" I do treat as anekānta, though on value-pluralist rather than factual grounds: true relative to autonomy and deliberation, false relative to crisis-efficiency, and inexpressible relative to metaphysical accounts of human flourishing that differ across traditions. This seems structurally different from the climate case, yet I still apply the same doctrine.

Fifth, and here is the tension I keep returning to: "seven is a prime number" seems absolutely not anekānta—there is no legitimate perspective from which it is false or even inexpressible. Yet mathematical truth is interpreted differently by Platonists, formalists, and intuitionists. So is the claim "seven is prime" itself many-sided in virtue of these philosophical framings, or does anekānta apply only to the meta-claim "seven is prime according to Platonism"? If I allow the former, then every true statement becomes anekānta the moment philosophers disagree about its grounds, which collapses the distinction I am trying to draw. If I allow only the latter, I need a criterion for when a framing is ontologically constitutive versus merely epistemic.

Sixth, "predation in wild ecosystems is morally bad"—given that suffering is real but non-agential—I think this might be anekānta in a distinctive way: true from the standpoint of the prey's experience, false from the standpoint of evolutionary function, and inexpressible from the standpoint of an ecosystem-level good that has no unified subject of experience. But I am unsure whether this is a legitimate application or a category mistake.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about the type of property involved, the kind of disagreement permitted, or the level of description—that captures why I apply syādvāda to climate science and democracy but not to torture or arithmetic? Or must I drop one or more of these verdicts to preserve coherence? And whatever principle emerges, what does it predict for a case I have not listed—say, whether "this thermostat is sentient" should be treated with conditional predication?
