After sitting through a long argument between my two friends Jordan and Maya about Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics and politics, I need an outside adjudication. They were debating whether Hobbes's well-known admiration for geometry undermines or supports the objectivity of his political science.

Jordan argued that Hobbes treats geometry as the paradigm of science precisely because its definitions are arbitrary conventions—like defining a circle by rotating a line—yet yield necessary conclusions. By analogy, Hobbes's political definitions, such as sovereignty arising from covenant, are also conventional starting points, which means the entire edifice of his political philosophy is just a formal game played with words we chose. The conclusions are logically necessary, Jordan said, but normatively empty, since we could have picked different definitions and derived a different politics with equal validity.

Maya countered that Hobbes distinguishes sharply between arbitrary naming and 'genetic' or causal definitions, which do not invent their objects but analyze the real processes by which they come to be. The rotation that generates a circle is a real physical motion, and the fear and consent that generate sovereignty are real psychological and social causes. So the geometric method does not reduce politics to convention; rather, it shows that once you understand the actual causes, the political conclusions are as objectively necessary as geometric theorems. Maya also pointed out that Hobbes's own attempts to square the circle reveal a constructivist commitment—he believed geometric truth was tied to actual constructibility rather than to formal consistency alone—which suggests he would reject purely abstract or non-constructible political concepts.

Jordan replied that even causal definitions require selecting which cause to privilege, and that selection is conventional. One could define sovereignty through distributed authority rather than unitary command and still apply Hobbes's deductive method, yielding an anti-Hobbesian politics that is equally 'geometrically' valid. Therefore, Jordan concluded, the geometric method cannot save Hobbes from conventionalism; it only disguises the conventional choice of premises behind a facade of logical necessity. Maya answered that this misses the hypothetical structure of Hobbes's arguments: his political science is not claiming that sovereignty is the only possible concept, but that if one wants peace and security, then unitary sovereignty is the necessary means, which is an objective conditional claim, not a disguised convention.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the role of convention in Hobbes's geometric method; about whether causal definitions rescue objectivity; and about whether Hobbes's mathematical constructivism creates problems for his political ontology. Say which specific points each party wins, who is more right overall, and identify what both disputants are likely missing—perhaps regarding Hobbes's distinction between analytic and synthetic reasoning, or the difference between the certainty of conclusions and the contingency of premises in a hypothetical science.
