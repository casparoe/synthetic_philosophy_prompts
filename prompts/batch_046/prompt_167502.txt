Analyze the Athenian Council of 500 (Boule) established under Cleisthenes around 508 BCE—fifty members per tribe, chosen by lot for one-year terms, subject to dokimasia (pre-service qualification scrutiny) and euthuna (post-service financial audit), and tasked with preparing legislation for the Assembly—as a test case for modern lottocracy, the proposal that democratic bodies should be selected by random lot rather than election. Do not treat this as a general history of Athenian democracy; keep the mapping disciplined. First, does the presence of dokimasia and euthuna mean the Council was never a pure sortition system, and does that undermine the modern lottocratic claim that random selection alone solves problems of competence and accountability, or does it show that successful lot-based governance requires hidden meritocratic scaffolding? Second, how does the Council's rotational membership and its preparatory, non-final role for the Assembly bear on the modern "epistemic quality" objection—does the division of labor between a randomly selected deliberative body and a direct-democratic decision-maker resolve that objection, or does it reveal that large-scale sortition depends on institutional continuity and civic training that small modern citizens' assemblies typically lack? Finally, state whether this historical case is best read as a vindication of lottocracy, an anomaly that exposes conditions modern advocates deny, or a hybrid model they should explicitly adopt.
