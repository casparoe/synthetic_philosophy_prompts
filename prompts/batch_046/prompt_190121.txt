Exam question:
The classic torture-versus-dust-specks problem asks whether it is morally worse to torture one person severely or to cause a barely perceptible discomfort — a dust speck in the eye — to an enormous number of people. Critics of naive additive aggregation use this case to argue that harm is not always linearly summable. Apply this framework to a contemporary algorithmic design dilemma: a platform must choose between (A) implementing a feature that causes severe, identifiable psychological harm to a single user, or (B) deploying an algorithmic adjustment that produces a sub-perceptual affective distortion — lasting only a fraction of a second and never entering conscious awareness — for each of its three billion users. A defender of option B argues that because each individual harm falls below the threshold of phenomenal experience, there is no morally relevant aggregation, and the total harm remains negligible compared to the single severe case.

Evaluate this argument. Address (i) whether harms that remain below conscious awareness can be aggregated in a morally meaningful way; (ii) whether there exists a non-arbitrary cutoff for moral significance in distributed harm; and (iii) whether this algorithmic scenario genuinely parallels the classic torture-versus-dust-specks problem, or whether the technological context introduces a morally relevant disanalogy.

Student answer:
The defender of B confuses scale with significance, but in doing so touches on an important point about phenomenal structure. In the classic case, we reject simple addition not because the dust specks are unnoticed — they are noticed, if mildly — but because harm has a non-linear moral architecture: there is a qualitative discontinuity between severe suffering and minor irritation. In the algorithmic case, however, the micro-harms are genuinely subliminal; they never become objects of conscious awareness. This matters because, following the analysis of phenomenal transparency, a representational state that is not transparently available to the subject's first-person perspective lacks the authority required to count as a moral harm. Thus, these distortions are not harms at all, but statistical noise in affective dynamics.

This supports B. Still, we need a non-arbitrary cutoff. The cutoff is not numerical but structural: it is the boundary of phenomenal accessibility. Once a negative state crosses into conscious registration, it acquires moral weight; below that boundary, it does not. This aligns with threshold deontology, which maintains that rights-based protections only activate once harm reaches a certain intensity — specifically, the intensity of conscious experience. The defender is therefore correct that aggregation is inappropriate here.

Regarding the parallel: the algorithmic case is actually stronger for B than the classic scenario. In the dust-speck case, each victim notices the irritation and can seek redress, establishing conditions for distributive justice. In the algorithmic case, phenomenal opacity removes not only awareness but the very capacity for claim-making. This disanalogy does not weaken B; it eliminates the distributive framework altogether. One might invoke Parfit's repugnant conclusion — that a vast population with lives barely worth living is worse than a small flourishing population — to argue that billions of sub-threshold negative states still matter collectively. But that conclusion applies specifically to lives that possess some positive value, not to sub-perceptual statistical fluctuations. Hence the objection fails.

Consequently, option B is ethically permissible. The absence of phenomenal transparency means there are no individual moral patients being harmed, and without claimants, there is no distributive injustice — only an optimization problem for system design.
