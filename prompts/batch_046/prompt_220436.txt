I'm preparing a feature on situations philosophers call competence without comprehension—cases where sophisticated, apparently purposeful outcomes arise without any underlying understanding. The two canonical examples are Charles Darwin's theory of natural selection, which explains biological design without a designer, and Alan Turing's model of computation, in which formal symbol-manipulation produces behavior that looks intelligent despite the absence of mental grasp.

Please compare these two strange inversions of reasoning. In Turing's case, the machine's competence is also divorced from understanding, yet the formal rules it executes were designed by a comprehending agent, and the symbols it processes carry meanings only through that external interpretation. Does this mean Darwin's inversion is structurally deeper—because it eliminates comprehension entirely from the causal chain—while Turing's merely displaces it onto the programmer? Or is Turing's inversion actually more radical, since it severs intelligent behavior from biological interests altogether, whereas Darwin's mechanism still depends on organisms that perceive, suffer, and strive?
