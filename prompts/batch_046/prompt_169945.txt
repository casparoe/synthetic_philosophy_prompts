In popular expositions of Sen's liberal paradox, minimal liberal rights are often analogized to veto powers in a small committee: each member holds an absolute block over decisions in their personal domain, so when two members have opposing preferences over the same object—say, whether a shared book should be read or left untouched—the group cannot satisfy both vetoes and the Pareto condition simultaneously, and the committee is said to fall into paralysis. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that liberalism is inherently self-defeating, because protecting even the smallest sphere of individual sovereignty necessarily destroys the possibility of coherent collective choice. Audit the analogy: provide an explicit correspondence table (committee member, veto, shared object, paralysis, etc., mapped onto Sen's individuals, minimal liberal rights, the personal-domain condition, the Pareto principle, and the paradoxical outcome); identify the points where the mapping breaks, especially with respect to the scope of the personal domain, the symmetry of preferences between the prude and the lewd, and the fact that Sen's result requires only a local conflict rather than universal gridlock; state which features of the committee model are quietly imported—such as the assumption that vetoes are exercised over collective goods rather than strictly private ones, or that the association values unanimity over all outcomes—that Sen's formal framework does not contain; and determine whether the conclusion that liberalism conflicts with Pareto efficiency survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the rhetorical weight all along. If the analogy collapses, suggest a more precise comparison that captures the structural tension between minimal liberalism and the Pareto principle without importing extraneous assumptions about collective decision-making.
