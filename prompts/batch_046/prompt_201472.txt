After a career in experimental biophysics and a recent turn toward philosophy of science and metaphysics, I have been trying to formalize my judgments about causation by omission. I offer a tentative principle, which I will call the role-anchored counterfactual account: an omission O by an agent A is a cause of an outcome E if and only if (i) the corresponding positive action belongs to the standard operational protocol of the role A occupies; (ii) that action was physically and epistemically available to A at the relevant time; (iii) within a causal model that holds A's role and the background conditions fixed but allows the action to vary, E does not occur; and (iv) E is not overdetermined by an independent sufficient cause that would have produced it regardless of the action.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, together with one pair that pulls in opposite directions despite structural similarity.

Case 1. A clinical physician withholds a first-line antibiotic from a patient with a confirmed bacterial infection, in direct violation of treatment protocol. The patient dies of sepsis. Verdict: the omission is a direct cause of death.

Case 2. A structural engineer disables a redundant backup alarm in a cooling system solely to reduce maintenance noise. The primary sensor fails in an unprecedented manner, and the reactor overheats. Verdict: the omission is a cause of the overheating, since the backup belonged to the engineered safety protocol.

Case 3. A field ecologist observes what she judges to be a native beetle and therefore does not report it; it is in fact invasive and destroys a local crop. Verdict: the omission is not a cause of the crop loss, because identification and reporting fall outside the direct interventional protocol of her observational research role.

Case 4. A peer reviewer fails to detect a statistical error in a submitted manuscript. The paper is published and subsequently shapes a harmful public-health policy. Verdict: the omission is not a cause of the policy harm; the reviewer's role is evaluative rather than interventional, and the causal chain runs through authors, editors, and policymakers.

Case 5. A laboratory director decides not to publish negative results from a failed replication study. Other groups waste years and funding pursuing the same false positive. Verdict: the omission is a cause of the wasted resources, because dissemination of results—positive or negative—is part of the standard protocol of scientific directorship.

Case 6. A senior researcher notices that a junior colleague in her own laboratory is using a deprecated calibration method, but she remains silent, assuming the junior will learn independently. The junior's data are flawed, and the resulting paper is retracted. Verdict: the omission is a cause of the flawed data and the retraction.

Case 7. The same senior researcher notices that a colleague in a different department is using the identical deprecated method. She again says nothing, on the grounds that she has no supervisory authority across departmental boundaries. That colleague's data are also flawed and retracted. Verdict: the omission is not a cause of that second flawed dataset or retraction.

Finally, consider a case I have not listed. I am now retired, and at a recent public lecture I noticed that the speaker was applying an outdated normalization procedure to a dataset. I said nothing and left quietly. If that speaker's erroneous conclusion later influences a policy debate, was my silence a cause?

Is there one coherent principle that delivers all seven verdicts, and if so, what does it predict about the lecture case? If not, which of my verdicts—particularly the divergence between Case 6 and Case 7—must be overturned for the sake of simplicity, and what is the most defensible repaired version of the account? Please state the principle precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and be explicit about which verdict it cannot accommodate.
