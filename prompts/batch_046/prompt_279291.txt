Consider the following short argument about Montaigne’s essay Of Cannibals (Essais I.31), offered as notes for an essay rather than a finished piece. The standard reading treats Montaigne as advancing a symmetrical cultural relativism: Europeans condemn cannibalism only from within their own norms, and the Tupinambá have equal standing to condemn European religious violence. I want to press a different claim. Montaigne’s comparison is asymmetrical and normative, not relativist in the modern sense. He does not suspend judgment equally; he uses the cannibal society as a foil to indict European artificiality. The ritual cannibalism of warriors is presented as less cruel than the judicial tortures and wars of religion in contemporary France, but this comparison depends on an unstated criterion of “naturalness”—the Tupinambá live without commerce, letters, or magistrates, and thus closer to an original human state. That criterion is not ethnographically neutral; it is drawn from Montaigne’s Stoic and Pyrrhonist commitments, in which nature serves as an uncorrupted standard against which social artifice is judged corrupt. So the essay does not genuinely bracket European values; it inverts the hierarchy, placing the cannibals above Europeans not because all cultures are equal, but because they are less distant from a pre-social nature that Montaigne privileges. This produces an internal tension: if Pyrrhonist epoché is taken seriously, then the “naturalness” standard should itself be suspended, yet Montaigne deploys it as an unexamined ground of critique. I am considering concluding that the essay’s instability is not a flaw but a deliberate rhetorical performance—Montaigne stages the contradiction to show that any appeal to nature is itself culturally loaded—though this risks making the essay self-undermining in a different way.

Please comment on three things. First, accuracy: does this overstate the Stoic and Pyrrhonist influence in Of Cannibals, and is there textual support for treating “naturalness” as a normative criterion rather than a descriptive observation? Second, coherence: is the asymmetry I describe genuinely present, or does it arise from selective emphasis that ignores passages where Montaigne genuinely withholds judgment? Third, the concluding move: if the “naturalness” criterion is itself a rhetorical performance, does that rescue the essay from internal instability, or does it collapse the argument into a more conventional “noble savage” trope that Montaigne is usually read as complicating? In particular, I am unsure whether my reading makes Montaigne too systematically philosophical—too committed to a hidden metaphysics of nature—given his usual skepticism about systematic claims. Where, in the sequence of the essay’s reasoning, should that objection be addressed, and does it defeat the reading or only qualify it?
