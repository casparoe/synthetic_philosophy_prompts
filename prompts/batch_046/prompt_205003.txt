I'm a phd student in cultural heritage studies and I was arguing with my roommate about whether Nietzche's framework in that essay on history for life actually helps anyone do real work, or if it's just another thing we cite in diss footnotes. I want to prove her wrong by turning the three modes — monumental, antiquarian, critical — into an actual reusable checklist for a heritage-site curator or exhibit designer who makes framing decisions weekly, under time pressure, with funding and community stakehodlers on the line. The practitioner doesn't have philosophy training; they need a decision procedre — tests in order, hard cases handled, judgment calls marked as such — that keeps the core idea that each mode can either serve life or damage it, but drops the metaphysical baggage that won't survive a ten-minute meeting.

Please make it operational: start with the initial trigger (what kind of site or story is being presented), then the branching questions that push toward monumental, antiquarian, or critical framing, then the conflict-resolution rules when two or three modes collide. Be explicit about the step where you evaluate "life-enhancement" versus "life-paralysis" — that's a judgment call, not a measurement, and the checklist should flag it. Work through three cases: (1) a decommissioned factory being redesigned as a community climate hub, (2) a colonial-era monument where preservationists want it kept intact and activists want it removed or radically recontextualized, and (3) a case where the checklist tells the curator to suppress a beloved local founding myth because it functions as dead weight on present action — they won't like that result, but include it anyway. Also state clearly what the procedure cannot do — like resolve value conflicts between stakeholders or replace ethical deliberation — and identify where it will misfire. No citations or bibliographies needed; I just want something I could hand to a practitioner who has never read the original.
