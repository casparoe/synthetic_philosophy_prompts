I am planning a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group — mostly working biologists and one legal scholar, none with formal philosophy training — on free-floating rationales: reasons that can be satisfied or tracked by a system without being held, endorsed, or even represented by any agent. Do not explain the concept to me; design the teaching arc. First, identify the dependency order: what distinction must be secured before the phrase "reason without a reasoner" stops sounding like a contradiction in terms rather than an ontological claim? Second, propose one carrying example that is neither evolutionary adaptation nor human design — for instance, a distributed pricing mechanism or a synthetic chemotaxis algorithm — and specify what feature it must possess (a directional success condition that can genuinely fail) and what it must lack (any hidden representational state or homunculus). Third, anticipate the misconceptions in the sequence they will arise: that this is merely anthropomorphic shorthand, that it smuggles in mental states, and that every reason implies a reason-giver. For each, state the exact moment in the arc where I should head it off and which distinction does the work. Fourth, provide a single end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether a participant grasps the idea or has only accepted that "evolution is clever." Finally, say explicitly which adjacent debate — for example, whether such rationales carry genuine normative force or are purely explanatory — I should leave untouched at this level, and justify the omission.
