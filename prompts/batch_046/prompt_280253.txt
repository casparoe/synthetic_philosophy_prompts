I'm working through some Burkean conservatism for a course paper and I keep running into Chesterton's fence — that image of a reformer tearing down a fence in the middle of a field because he can't see its purpose, not realizing it was keeping back a flood or marking some boundary that actually matters. It's everywhere in conservative arguments: tradition is like the fence, institutions are like the fence, don't mess with what you don't understand. But I want to know how far the anology acutally holds if you press on it.

Can you audit it properly? Map out what corresponds to what — the fence, the builder, the field, the reformer, the hidden purpose — and say where the correspondence starts to slip. Does it quietly assume that every social practice was deliberately designed for a specific function, which feels off for Burke since he emphasized prescription and inheritied wisdom rather than intentional engineering? What about cases where the fence is just an old property line from a dead farmer, or where leaving it standing does real harm? Someone mentioned Oakeshott's split between technical and practical knowledge complicates this, because the fence analogy seems to treat tradition as hiding a technical reason we'll eventually discover. So does the analogy really license the Burkean conclusion that gradual reform is always preferable, or does it just beg the question by assuming the status quo has hidden wisdom? And if it's shaky, what's a better analogy — maybe an old codebase or an ecosystem — that captures the Burkean point without importing false intentionality?
