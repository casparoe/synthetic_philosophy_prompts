I'm teaching a seminar on free will this semester and we just got through Dennett's idea that there are varieties of free will worth wanting — you know, the kind that actually matters for ethics and not just metaphysical ghost stories. Anyway, I had this weird dream and now I want you to write a short story about it, but please don't turn it into a lecture with citations or anything.

The setting is a near-future city called Meridian where people don't have one uniform capacity for choice. Instead, there are engineered 'will-types': Type-A agents have full compatibilist autonomy but no sense of ultimate origination; Type-B agents experience what philosophers call deep openness but suffer from paralyzing indetermancy; and Type-C agents possess only reactive, habit-driven selection with no reflective endorsement. The twist is that citizens can apply for a 'will-upgrade' through a bureaucratic board, but the board uses a strange criterion: they ask not which will is 'true' free will, but which variety is worth wanting given your social role and relationships. Write a story about one aplicant — maybe a parent applying on behalf of their child, or someone applying for themselves — going through the board's interveiw. Show the board probing whether the applicant understands the trade-offs: that wanting deep openness might destroy their capacity for moral commitment, or that accepting compatibilist autonomy might feel like giving up on something profound. Don't make it a lecture; let the conflict emerge in dialogue and small details. Just tell the story and let the concept breathe.
