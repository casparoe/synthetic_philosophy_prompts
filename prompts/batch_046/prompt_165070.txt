I'm drafting my position on embedded rationality for a discussion group and want a consistency check before I present it. These are the commitments I intend to hold together:

(1) Any embedded agent's self-model is necessarily approximate and lossy; direct logical access to one's own implementation is impossible because any self-representation runs on the same substrate and must be compressed.

(2) I endorse a computationally bounded causal decision theory for embedded agents: one should maximize expected utility over outcomes within one's computable causal influence, treating upstream correlations as fixed parameters of the environment.

(3) I nevertheless judge that one-boxing is rational in Newcomb's problem. I do not appeal to evidential correlation; instead, I claim the predictor's prior computation is part of the agent's effective causal environment, and treating it as independent is a mistake analogous to ignoring a fixed physical constant.

(4) I grant that perfect reflective transparency is impossible: an embedded agent that tries to predict its own choice after seeing that prediction faces a self-prediction paradox with no stable fixed point.

(5) Despite deep evolutionary and architectural biases in our intuitions about risk and cooperation, I maintain that reflective equilibrium is the correct method for fixing our normative theory of embedded rationality.

Please audit this package. Which commitments pull against each other in substance, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious. In particular, examine whether (2) and (3) can be sustained together without collapsing into evidential decision theory or erasing the upstream/downstream distinction, and address whether (4) undermines (5): if perfect reflective transparency is unattainable, is aiming for reflective equilibrium incoherent, or does it remain a viable intermediate goal?
