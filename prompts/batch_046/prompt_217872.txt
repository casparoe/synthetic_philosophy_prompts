Since witnessing the collapse of a democratic collective I co-founded—where procedural safeguards we had designed to ensure accountability were gradually weaponised to purge dissent, ultimately consuming the very people who had instituted them—I have been unable to stop thinking about the Thermidorian Reaction. I should like you to analyse that episode, specifically the fall of Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety in late July 1794, as a test case for the claim that revolutions inevitably devour their own children. I want the analysis conducted through the three dominant interpretive frames applied to the Revolution: class struggle, ideology, and contingency.

Please centre your account on the institutional mechanism that made the reversal possible. In June 1794 the Convention adopted legislation—commonly called the Law of 22 Prairial—that stripped accused persons of defence counsel before the Revolutionary Tribunal and restricted verdicts to death or acquittal; Robespierre’s faction used this streamlined procedure to accelerate executions, yet within five weeks the same tribunal and the same legal architecture were turned against Robespierre himself, resulting in his execution and that of his closest allies. Map precisely who the relevant actors were (members of the Committee of Public Safety, the Convention, the Parisian sections, provincial deputies), what strategic options they actually faced once the procedural machinery was in motion, and what kind of evidence would count in favour of each interpretive frame.

Then examine where the thesis that revolutions devour their children genuinely illuminates the fall and where it misdescribes it. Does Robespierre’s execution confirm a structural inevitability, or does it instead expose a contingent failure of coalition management—an error of judgement about whom to accuse in late June and July that a more cautious strategy could have avoided? Does the class-struggle interpretation explain why the Thermidorians came predominantly from the provincial bourgeoisie and the moderate Convention, or does it reduce the episode to an abstract reaction against sans-culotte power that misses the personal and procedural dynamics? Does the ideological interpretation—treating revolutionary government as an autonomous logic with its own imperatives—actually predict the turn, or does it merely rationalise it after the fact?

I want you to reach a definite conclusion about what Thermidor does to the theory. Is it an anomaly that forces us to refine the claim—for example, by distinguishing between the revolution’s architects and its mere supporters, or by identifying institutional feedback loops within revolutionary procedure as the real mechanism? Is it a confirmation that reveals something the theory previously left implicit? Or is it a case the theory never genuinely anticipated, thereby undermining its explanatory scope? Defend one of these verdicts explicitly, and do not finish on a non-committal note.

Finally, address whether my own experience—procedural rules turned against their creators in a small collective—is a legitimate analogy to Thermidor, or whether the scale, the violence, and the state context make the comparison a category mistake that tells us more about my own bias than about revolutionary dynamics.
