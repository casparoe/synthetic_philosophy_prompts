As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to determine whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible—cases in which an agent is genuinely subject to two binding obligations that conflict and cannot both be satisfied, with no hidden resolution available. I have formed the following verdicts about concrete scenarios, but I cannot see whether they form a coherent pattern.

First, a hospital has one remaining ventilator and two patients with identical medical need and equal moral claim; the physician must choose one. I take this to be a tragic allocation problem, not a genuine dilemma, because the obligation to save life is not violated by saving one patient rather than the other—there is no specific duty to each that survives the choice.

Second, imagine that a self-driving vehicle’s control architecture is designed so that, in a precisely defined crash scenario, it must either kill its occupant or kill two pedestrians, with no possibility of override or evasion built into the system. Here I feel there is a genuine dilemma for the designers: they seem genuinely obligated to protect the occupant and genuinely obligated to minimize total harm, yet the code forces them to encode exactly one outcome.

Third, a security researcher discovers a critical vulnerability that will harm users if disclosed (through exploitation) but will also harm users if concealed (through malicious attack). Disclosure and concealment each seem genuinely required. I take this to be a real dilemma, structurally unlike the ventilator case.

Fourth, I promised two friends that I would keep their respective secrets, not knowing that the secrets are incompatible. When I learn they conflict, I initially feel dilemmatic, but I now think this dissolves once I recognize that one promise was made under conditions of false belief—so there is no true conflict of obligations.

Fifth, there is an engineering analog: a distributed database must choose between consistency and availability during a network partition. I see this as a hard trade-off embedded in system architecture, yet I do not treat it as a moral dilemma at all, even though it resembles the self-driving car case in being a forced binary choice.

These judgments feel inconsistent—especially the contrast between the car-design case and the database-partition case. Is there a single principle, perhaps concerning the source of the obligations or the presence of unavoidable moral residue, that accommodates all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and why? Please also state what that principle predicts for a case I have not listed: an artificial agent trained on two reward functions that are demonstrably incompatible within a specific environment, where the agent must commit to one optimization target.
