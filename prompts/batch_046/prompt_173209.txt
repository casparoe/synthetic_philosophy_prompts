Here is an argument I sketched about dramatizing real lives, and it now strikes me as collapsing every distinction I actually want to defend. (1) Only beings with present or future welfare interests can be wronged by misrepresentation. (2) The dead have no present welfare interests, and any future ones are either nonexistent or causally insulated from present fiction. (3) So misrepresenting a dead person in a biopic or historical drama does not wrong them. (4) Our obligations about such representations are therefore owed solely to the living audience and to historical accuracy as an instrumental good, not to the deceased subject. (5) The living audience benefits most from emotionally compelling narratives, which typically require compressing timelines, inventing dialogue, and sharpening conflicts that were in reality ambiguous or mundane. (6) If audience benefit is the only relevant moral consideration, then the more dramatic the invention, the better the representation serves its purpose. (7) Therefore there is no moral distinction between a respectful dramatization and an outright fabrication about the dead, provided the result is engaging and profitable. (8) But that is clearly wrong: we still distinguish a responsible biopic from a malicious falsehood, and we speak of honoring or dishonoring the dead. Where does the chain break? I am inclined to keep (4) — that primary obligations shift away from the dead themselves — but I suspect a slide between (5) and (7). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me in terms of either audience-centered ethics or the denial of posthumous dignitary interests?
