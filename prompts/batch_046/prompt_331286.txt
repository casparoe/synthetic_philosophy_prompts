After a long argument with a friend about whether prioritarianism can survive the leveling-down objection, I want to stop trading examples and specify what an adequate response actually has to deliver. Before evaluating any particular defense, write the full list of desiderata that any prioritarian theory — or any distributive weighting scheme that claims to avoid the objection — must satisfy to count as genuinely adequate rather than merely evasive. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (for example: preserving Pareto improvements, maintaining strict lexical or weighted priority to the worst-off regardless of absolute gains, accommodating person-affecting intuitions without collapsing into a person-affecting view, and respecting the distinction between having moral status and the degree of weight assigned). Then indicate explicitly whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given standard structural constraints — that is the part I most want to see. In particular, include the following constraint, which my friend insisted on but which I have not seen treated systematically: the theory must explain why synchronous leveling-down across distinct persons is objectionable, whereas diachronic leveling-down applied to the same individual across life-stages need not be, and it must do so without invoking a metaphysical boundary between persons that prioritarianism itself denies. Briefly, assess how the standard strategies — lexical prioritarianism, concave weighted utilitarianism, and the pluralist view that treats equality as an independent value with diminishing weight — fare against this full list. Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and their tensions rather than on the scorecard.
