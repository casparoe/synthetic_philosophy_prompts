I've been working through Quine's Word and Object with a small reading group, and one member forwarded a summary they found in a long-form forum thread. The summary explains the gavagai scenario—where a field linguist hears a native utter that word as a rabbit runs past—and claims Quine uses it to show that no observational data can fix whether the term refers to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit-stages. From this the author derives that Quine is essentially a behaviorist about meaning: mental states are irrelevant, reference is fixed solely by public stimulus-response dispositions, and the indeterminacy is mainly a problem for exotic field translation, since shared English culture eliminates it for ordinary bilingual dictionaries. The summary then adds that Quine's argument is structurally identical to Kripke's rule-following paradox, except applied to whole languages rather than individual speakers, and that together they prove there is no fact of the matter about correct interpretation in any linguistic context. It concludes that Quine is therefore a kind of linguistic skeptic about everyday communication.

I think this captures the surface of the example, but I worry it conflates Quine's naturalized epistemology with crude behaviorism, ignores how his holism about belief revision applies to home-language terms just as much as to gavagai, and treats the analytic-synthetic distinction as a separate issue rather than part of the same underdetermination. I also suspect the Kripke parallel is being overstated.
