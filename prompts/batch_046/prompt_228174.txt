I've been playing with a modified singleton view and want to know what'd actually kill it, not just make it awkward. The standard Bostrom version is about one decision-making agent with global reach, but I think that's wrong framing. My position: long-lived civilizations don't end up with a 'singleton agent' but with a 'protocal singleton' -- shared infrastructure for resolving resource and computation conflicts that becomes impossible to opt out of, not because anyone controls it but because the energy and security costs of running outside it grow exponentially. Think TCP/IP but for strategic interaction: nobody owns it, but every serious actor depends on it, and parallel networks eventually merge back. So 'aligning the singleton' is a category mistake; you're trying to align plumbing. This predicts that we'll see convergence on standards even when actors hate each other, and that attempts to build rival global systems fail not politically but thermodynamically.

What should I take as decisive defeat? Not just critiques of Bostrom's agent-version. Are there formal results in distributed systems or game theory showing stable global protocols can't emerge without a central enforcer? Historical examples of civilizations staying genuinely fragmented for millenia without collapsing or converging? An argument that infrastructure at this scale necessarily acquires agency, collapsing my agent/protocal distinction? What do defenders of singleton-type arguments themselves concede as the strongest counter-evidence? Also, is my view already unfalsifiable -- if every failure of convergence gets explained away as 'the protocol hasn't stabilized yet,' is that insulation a problem for this kind of claim? Sort by how decisive each defeater'd be, and say whether the position risks anything real or has been qualified into safety.
