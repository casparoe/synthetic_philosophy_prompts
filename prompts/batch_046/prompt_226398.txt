Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophical reconstruction of Adam Smith's moral psychology—specifically his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in The Theory of Moral Sentiments—must meet in order to be textually faithful and conceptually coherent. Organize the requirements as follows.

First, specify the phenomena the reconstruction must explain: (a) how moral approval arises from sympathy rather than from reason or self-interest; (b) the mechanism of self-judgment, in which we come to know our own character not by direct introspection but by observing how an imagined other reacts to our conduct; (c) the role of the impartial spectator as an internalized normative standard that corrects for partiality and proximity bias; and (d) the distinction between justice, which Smith treats as a negative virtue whose breach merits punishment, and beneficence, a positive virtue whose absence is not blameworthy, with an explanation of whether sympathy alone can ground both.

Second, list the conceptual distinctions the theory must preserve without collapsing: sympathy as imaginative perspective-taking versus sympathy as emotional contagion; the real observer whose sentiments we initially share versus the idealized unbiased witness whose sentiments constitute propriety; the approval of an action versus the approval of the agent's motives; and moral judgment as a social-corrective process versus moral judgment as the discovery of pre-existing moral facts.

Third, identify the consistency conditions: compatibility with Smith's broader commitments, including his skepticism about innate moral ideas and his emphasis on habit and education in shaping moral sentiments; consistency with evolutionary and social-scientific literature on moral cognition, but without anachronistically reducing Smith's normative claims to descriptive psychology; and coherence with his claim that we seek not merely social praise but to deserve it, which requires that the impartial spectator function as a standard independent of actual popular opinion.

Fourth, address the proportionality problem—how sympathy is weighted according to relation, distance, and circumstance without arbitrary decay—marking whether solving it is a non-negotiable requirement of textual fidelity or a modern theoretical preference.

Fifth, state which subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable under particular interpretations: for instance, whether treating the impartial spectator as a fully rational, disinterested agent collapses sympathy into rationalism incompatible with Smith's sentimentalist framework, and whether treating it as purely empirical social observation eliminates its normative force.

Finally, briefly score three prevalent readings—strict sentimentalist, rationalist reconstruction, and social-contractarian interpretation—against the list, indicating where each fails and which failure is most damaging. Keep the focus on the criteria themselves: which demands are genuine commitments of Smith's text and which are prejudices of contemporary moral philosophy. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or requests for further reading; the evaluation should be self-contained.
