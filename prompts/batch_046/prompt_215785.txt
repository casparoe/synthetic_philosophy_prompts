We kept returning to Ursula K. Le Guin's short story about Omelas—not because it is an obvious fable, but precisely because it resists easy categorization. I would like you to read the story through the lens of value aggregation versus liberal neutrality in AI alignment: treat the child in the basement not as a simple utilitarian cost but as a potential non-aggregable moral residue, and treat the citizens' bliss not as a uniform preference profile but as a particular conception of the good that an aligned system might be asked to maximize. Where does the story illuminate what it means for an alignment procedure to serve "everyone's values," given that the ones who stay and the ones who walk away seem to inhabit incompatible moral frameworks? Does the narrative suggest that neutrality collapses the moment aggregation begins, or that walking away constitutes the only coherent liberal refusal?
