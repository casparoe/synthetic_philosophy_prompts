I've been persuaded by the singleton hypothesis — the view that any technologically mature civilization facing long-horizon existential risks will eventually converge on a single unified decision-making structure, because persistent pluralism among competing agents generates irreducible collective-action failures, arms-race dynamics, and coordination failures that tend toward either catastrophic conflict or consolidation into one agent. A recent interlocutor challenged this with a framing she calls "stabilizing heterogeneity": the idea that distributed architectures can share only minimal meta-commitments — such as non-aggression verification protocols or shared auditing mechanisms — while retaining independent value evolution, so that institutional diversity functions not as a transient risk but as evolutionary redundancy. She argues this collapses the binary between singleton and chaos, and that the hypothesis overstates consolidation's inevitability by treating strategic autonomy as the default rather than as one institutional design choice among others. I want an honest audit of what would genuinely count against the singleton hypothesis, not just the familiar objections about tyranny or value stagnation. Which empirical developments, formal results, or conceptual distinctions — including durable distributed coordination of the kind she describes — would be fatal defeaters versus merely awkward adjustments? Would a committed defender concede that such heterogeneity is stable over cosmological timescales, or would they absorb it into an expanded definition of "singleton"? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to barely inconvenient, and say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or observation would lead you to reject or strongly weaken the hypothesis, and where in this reasoning is it most likely to fail?
