I have been working through the scale argument in insect ethics—the idea that, even if the prior probability that any given insect is sentient is quite low, the enormous cardinality of insects produces a substantial expected moral burden. I keep encountering what feels like a hidden metaphysical snag in this reasoning. To multiply a low per-capita probability by billions of discrete organisms and treat the result as a coherent aggregate harm, it seems necessary to regard sentience not as a strictly binary, subject-bound property—either there is something it is like to be that particular ant or there is not—but rather as a kind of diffuse, population-level quantity that can be pooled or fractionally distributed across a swarm, somewhat like a statistical field spread over many nodes. If phenomenal consciousness is genuinely all-or-nothing at the individual level, then applying expected-value mathematics across populations appears to confuse epistemic uncertainty about discrete states with an ontological claim that the property itself is partly present in each member. So my question is this: does the scale argument necessarily presuppose a gradualist or collective ontology of consciousness—perhaps what one might call a fuzzy moral field—in which sentience is distributed in degrees across the group? And if not, then how can rigorous probabilistic aggregation over strictly binary, individual-level properties yield a non-trivial moral obligation without collapsing into the kind of risk-pooling metaphysics I have described? In other words, is the category error I am sensing real, or is there a principled way to separate the arithmetic of expectation from any commitment to partial or distributed subjecthood?
