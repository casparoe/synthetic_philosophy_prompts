I'm designing a fifty-minute session for advanced undergraduates in philosophy of social science on whether culture can be a genuine cause, using Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism as the anchoring case. I need the architecture of the explanation, not the explanation itself. Begin with the dependency order: what conceptual distinction—between value-relevance and value-judgment, or between elective affinity and linear causation—must be secured before students can see why explaining with values poses a methodological problem rather than merely offering a correlation? Specify the one carrying example that should do the work: it must illustrate both the internal coherence of Calvinist worldly asceticism and its non-deducible relation to capitalist accumulation, and say exactly what the example must include and what it must omit. Then map the predictable misconceptions—e.g., that Weber asserts a monocausal religious origin of capitalism, that values are reducible to psychological states, that cultural explanation competes with material explanation rather than operating at a different analytical level—and indicate the precise stage in the sequence at which each should be intercepted. Provide one concluding exercise that reveals whether students have grasped the problem of explaining with values, not just repeated Weber's conclusion. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out—say, the comparative civilizational studies or the later rationalization debate—and justify that omission for this audience and time constraint.
