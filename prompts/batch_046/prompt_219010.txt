In discussions of Hofstadter and Dennett's thought experiment from The Mind's I — where a book encodes a complete functional description of Einstein's brain and, when queried, produces responses indistinguishable from Einstein's — defenders of phenomenal anti-functionalism often invoke the analogy that the book is like a perfect map of a territory: every street is represented, yet navigating the map is not being in the city, so the representation lacks the presence and causal embeddedness of the original. They deploy this to argue that functional duplication in a static medium cannot yield consciousness, regardless of behavioral equivalence. Audit this analogy with precision: lay out the explicit correspondence table (the brain's dynamics, the book's text, the reader or interlocutor, the environment, and the act of response-generation); then isolate the disanalogies that matter for consciousness — particularly temporal binding, indexical self-reference (the word "I" in the generated answers), causal coupling with an environment, and the absence of a unified global workspace — separating them from disanalogies that are merely incidental (for instance, differences in physical substrate); identify what assumptions about representation, static encoding, and observer-dependence the cartography analogy smuggles in that may not apply to a dynamically queried system; and finally assess whether the anti-functionalist conclusion survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether a more apt analogy (for example, a live orchestra interpreting sheet music in real time, or a distributed computation with no central reader) undermines it and points instead toward a different condition for consciousness.
