I have been reading about the philosopher John McTaggart, who said that time has two sides: one where events stand in relations of before and after, and another where they are past, present, or future. He argued that the second side is contradictory and therefore unreal. Now I have come across the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that special relativity removes any universal “now,” since observers in different states of motion disagree on which events are simultaneous. If McTaggart were alive today, would he take this as proof that physics describes only the before-and-after side, leaving the past-present-future side as an illusion? Or might he turn the argument around and say that if every observer has their own present, then the A-series is not destroyed but multiplied, and physics is merely blind to one true global present?

Here is the twist I want you to consider: in quantum mechanics, measurements seem to occur at definite moments, yet when two distant measurements happen close together in time, different frames of reference can disagree about their order, and there is no universal clock to settle the matter. Would McTaggart claim that this reveals a hidden universal “now” that science cannot access? Or would he see it as further confirmation that “becoming” is not part of physical reality at all? I am not asking for bibliographies or a summary of his writings; I want you to reason from the structure of his view as it is usually described. In particular, tell me whether the block-universe interpretation of relativity is a natural ally for someone who already doubted temporal becoming, or whether it undermines him in a way he would not have expected.
