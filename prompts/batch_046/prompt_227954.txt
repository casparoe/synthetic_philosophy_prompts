I am a novelist whose fiction often explores nested ontologies and the moral weight of imagined futures; I also sit on the advisory council of the Meridian Foundation, which must vote next week on a restructuring proposal from the Institute for Long-Range Stewardship. The Institute asks us to commit the majority of our endowment to interventions whose benefits are projected centuries hence—AI alignment, climate resilience, cultural preservation—justified by expected-value reasoning that assigns enormous weight to astronomical numbers of potential future beings. A substantial faction of the council, invoking the simulation argument, objects that this reasoning exhibits a form of fanaticism: if there is any serious credence that we inhabit a computer simulation, then our actions may not affect a base-reality future, and the staggering populations that justify present sacrifice may exist only within our own simulated layer, or not at all. The proponents respond that simulation hypotheses are irrelevant to moral obligation, since simulated beings, if conscious, merit the same consideration as base ones, and that bracketing the hypothesis is methodologically sound. I need a formal memo—suitable for circulation to the council before Thursday’s meeting—that recommends a concrete decision: approve the proposal outright, approve it with binding conditions, or reject it. The reasoning must engage the simulation objection at its strongest, including the possibility that nested simulations fragment the very target of longtermist obligation rather than merely reducing its probability, and it must address whether decision-theoretic fanaticism survives once simulation credence is integrated into the calculus rather than treated as an afterthought. The memo should be written in an institutional register but need not hide that its author understands narrative structure; it should treat the Institute’s position with intellectual charity, identify the single strongest consideration on the side that loses, and contain no requests for bibliographies or external citations. Please deliver the recommendation as an actionable document with a clear bottom line and a brief framework for how the Foundation should treat ontological uncertainty in future grant evaluations.
