I have been following the constitutional debates around Scottish and Catalan autonomy with some interest, particularly a newer line of argument—discussed mainly in legal-theory and digital-governance circles rather than in mainstream political reporting—that imagines a region seeking independence not through a conventional referendum but through an incremental, technologically mediated assertion of parallel sovereignty. In this scenario, residents might hold cryptographically verified citizenship credentials issued by a sub-state authority, allowing them to maintain formal legal ties to the central state while participating in a separate jurisdictional layer for taxation, representation, and law. This strikes me as a kind of nested or opt-in separation that avoids the binary logic of classical secession, but I am unsure whether it represents a genuinely new form of divided sovereignty or merely an old idea in technical clothing.

Suppose Abraham Lincoln were alive today and asked to evaluate the legitimacy of such a move by a region possessing its own language, devolved legislature, and distinct legal traditions—something analogous to the position of Scotland or Catalonia within a larger democratic union. How do you think he would analyze it? Would he regard this layered, non-exclusive model of political separation as a form of rebellion against an indivisible union, analogous to the secession he confronted in 1861, or would the diffuse, consent-based character of the separation lead him to treat it as a renegotiation of federal compact rather than a rupture? More specifically, do you think his well-known arguments about the perpetuity and self-preserving nature of the Union would survive this kind of distributed, opt-in separation, or would they require substantial revision to address a sovereignty that is exercised in parallel rather than claimed exclusively?
