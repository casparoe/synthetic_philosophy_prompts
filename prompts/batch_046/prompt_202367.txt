I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether the informal rules governing our decentralized cooperative platform—how we attribute shared work, resolve moderation disputes, and allocate server costs—count as genuine conventions in David Lewis’s sense, or are merely unstable habits we should feel free to redesign. I need to turn Lewis’s account of convention, together with whatever philosophy says about the evolution and dissolution of norms, into a reusable decision procedure for our governance board. We meet monthly, have about thirty minutes per proposal, and must sign off on whether to codify, tolerate, or actively disrupt an emerging practice.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it issues a clear recommendation (codify, tolerate, disrupt, or defer to values-based judgment), and where it must stop and admit the issue is not resolvable by coordination logic alone. Preserve the core Lewisian conditions—coordination problem, observed regularity, common knowledge of mutual expectation, and the presence of arbitrary but viable alternatives—while incorporating at least one non-obvious consideration about norm evolution, such as how common knowledge fractures under rapid membership turnover or how path dependence locks in suboptimal equilibria.

Then apply it to three proposals: (1) an emergent practice of “silent upvoting” on moderation actions, where members signal agreement only by inaction; (2) a push to formalize attribution norms for collaborative documentation that currently rely on unwritten reciprocity; and (3) a stable but harmful convention of informal ostracism against members who question resource-allocation rules, which the board wants to break but which the procedure may classify as a genuine convention that should be preserved. For each, show the output, flag the step where judgment cannot be eliminated, and state what the procedure explicitly cannot do—especially where it fails to tell us whether a convention ought to be kept on moral rather than coordination grounds.

If this request rests on a confused premise—for instance, if Lewis’s framework cannot be operationalized without collapsing into empirical sociology, or if “evolution of norms” cannot be captured by a static decision tree—say so plainly rather than constructing a pseudo-procedure to please me.
