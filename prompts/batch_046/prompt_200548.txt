I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been reading about theories of well-being in my spare time. I would like you to adopt the role of an expert moral philosopher, and please begin by brainstorming the main lines of response to the reasoning below before you analyse it step by step. Think carefully about whether any step smuggles in an unstated assumption.

Here is an argument about tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, and that every other apparent good (knowledge, love, achievement, autonomy) is valuable only as an instrument for sustaining that absence — which strikes me as suspicious because it seems to license something absurd. (1) If tranquilism is correct, then any state in which craving and suffering are entirely absent is maximally good, no matter what other capacities or relations are missing. (2) It is at least conceivable that we could induce, through a precise neurotechnological intervention, a permanent state of blank, non-reflective contentment in which all desire-formation mechanisms are suppressed but a minimal form of consciousness remains. (3) In that induced state, there is genuinely no craving and no suffering, and by (1) it is therefore maximally good. (4) By contrast, an ordinary flourishing human life always contains occasional suffering and constantly risks future craving, so it can never reach the maximal value that the induced state achieves. (5) Furthermore, the other goods present in an ordinary life — reasoning, moral agency, deep relationships — are only instrumentally valuable on this view, and once the induced state has secured the absence of suffering permanently, those instruments are no longer needed. (6) Therefore, if tranquilism is true, we ought rationally to prefer, and perhaps even to engineer, the permanent suppression of all higher cognitive and affective capacities, provided the result is a stable, desire-free contentment. (7) But recommending the destruction of one's own reasoning and relational capacities as the best course of action seems self-defeating: practical reasoning requires an agent who can act on reasons, yet the conclusion of the reasoning eliminates the agent. (8) Hence tranquilism appears to prove that cognitive self-annihilation is the optimal strategy, which is clearly unacceptable.

I want to keep (1) and (4) if possible, because they seem to capture the core intuition that suffering matters more than achievement. But I feel there is a subtle slide somewhere between (2) and (6) — perhaps an equivocation about what 'absence of suffering' means when the subject has been altered, or a hidden quantifier shift about 'for whom' the state is good. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does rejecting it force me to admit that knowledge or autonomy have intrinsic value after all?
