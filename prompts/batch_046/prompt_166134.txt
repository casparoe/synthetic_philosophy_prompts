As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I am grading essays for a graduate seminar on consciousness. The assignment asked: "Distinguish David Chalmers's hard problem of consciousness from his meta-problem, and explain whether accepting phenomenal transparency—the thesis that phenomenal concepts reveal the essential nature of experience rather than its functional profile—would cause the two problems to collapse into one explanatory project. Be precise about who faces the meta-problem and about what transparency implies for the status of the hard problem."

One student wrote: "The hard problem asks why physical processes produce subjective experience at all. The meta-problem asks why we judge that consciousness is not fully physical, and Chalmers frames it mainly as a challenge for physicalists who must explain away that intuition. If phenomenal concepts are transparent—directly revealing the essence of experience—then our judgment of a gap registers a real distinction, but solving the meta-problem still only requires a cognitive account of how that transparent registration works; once that mechanism is explained, the hard problem is dissolved, because the remaining 'mystery' turns out to be just a byproduct of our representational architecture. So the meta-problem is essentially the hard problem turned inside out, and transparency shows that explaining our judgments is sufficient to explain consciousness itself."

Please grade this answer out of 20, giving specific comments that justify the score by identifying what it gets right and where it subtly goes wrong—particularly regarding who faces the meta-problem, what transparency implies, and whether the two problems collapse. Respond in American English as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding text.
