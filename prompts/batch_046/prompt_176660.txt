I'm preparing a feature on how scientific consensus forms around emerging technologies, and at a recent workshop I heard two researchers debate whether a particular pattern in expert forecasting counts as rational herding. Dr. Lena Voss, a behavioral economist, described a sequence she had observed in assessments of long-term predictive methods: initially diverse evaluative frameworks—some experts emphasizing calibration metrics, others emphasizing structural interpretability—gradually collapsed until nearly everyone adopted the same scoring rubric, not because anyone had disproven the alternatives, but because accumulating public endorsements made dissent look like ignorance of the standard. She argued this was a textbook information cascade: private reservations about the rubric's adequacy were rationally suppressed in light of prior visible commitments.

Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher of science, pushed back. He said Voss was describing something more radical than herding. In standard cascades, he noted, the underlying state of the world is fixed—whether a restaurant is good, whether an investment is sound—and observers suppress private signals about that fixed state. But in Voss's case, the "state" includes which criteria are legitimate for judging forecasts at all. Once the cascade fixes a scoring rubric, it doesn't just suppress disagreement about forecasts; it reshapes how experts interpret their own private data. A researcher who privately favors structural interpretability now finds her own evidence framed as "off-topic" because the rubric defines relevance. Chen called this a reflexive framing cascade: the cascade changes the epistemic framework that makes private signals intelligible, so the independence conditions required by the standard model fail.

Voss replied that Chen was overcomplicating things. Framework preference, she said, is just another hidden variable—call it F. Experts have private signals about both F and the forecast quality. They update on both. The cascade on F proceeds exactly like a cascade on forecast quality; treating them separately is unnecessary multiplication of entities.

Chen answered that this reduction misses the reflexivity. If F determines how signals about forecast quality are weighted, then updating on public behavior about F simultaneously corrupts the interpretation of private signals about quality. You can't run two parallel cascades because the second one's inputs depend on the first one's output.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Can you walk through their exchange point by point—who is right about whether the standard cascade model applies, about whether framework preference can be treated as an independent hidden variable, and about whether reflexivity genuinely breaks the model's conditions? And what, if anything, are both missing about how such cascades might be interrupted or made visible before they lock in?
