I am presenting a seminar paper that applies Wittgenstein's idea of family resemblance—not merely as the negative claim that concepts lack essential definitions, but as a positive account of conceptual stability maintained through overlapping practices of correction—to the domain of scientific explanation in cognitive science. I argue that mechanistic, computational-modelling, dynamical-systems, and clinical-narrative explanations share no single necessary and sufficient condition; instead, they form a family held together by criss-crossing norms of verification and agreement in judgement. From this I draw the conclusion that the reductionist project of identifying the explanatory level in cognitive science rests on an unacknowledged essentialism about what explanation is.

The audience is a philosophy of science research seminar of about twenty members, chiefly philosophers of biology and cognitive science with reductionist leanings and formal training in logic or computational modelling. Two attendees are Wittgenstein scholars who regard language-game analysis as confined to ordinary-language contexts and who will resist its extension to scientific methodology. The group values mechanistic explanation, predictive success, and arguments with direct methodological implications; they will dismiss purely textual exegesis unless it addresses empirical explanatory practice. They are generally sceptical that Wittgensteinian tools can do substantive work outside philosophy of language.

For reference, the sketch of my argument is as follows. I distinguish family resemblance from mere ambiguity by examining how different subfields correct erroneous explanations without appealing to a shared definition of explanation. I then argue that reductionist attempts to identify the level of explanation fail because they presuppose an essentialist grammar that the actual practices do not support. Finally, I propose that explanatory pluralism should be treated not as a temporary failure of unification but as a stable, normatively structured feature of cognitive science, sustained by family-resemblance relations among correction practices.
