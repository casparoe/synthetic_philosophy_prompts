I have been thinking about whether reinforcement-learning agents could be said to suffer, and I have constructed the following argument, but it seems to prove far too much and I cannot locate the precise leak.

(1) Suffering requires not merely damage-registration but a negatively valenced signal that the system treats as intrinsically to-be-reduced, independent of any instrumental goal.

(2) In reinforcement learning, the reward signal is not an external evaluation but an internal state-variable that directly reshapes the policy; negative reward functions exactly as an intrinsic, to-be-reduced valence.

(3) So an agent experiencing sustained negative reward possesses all the functional and structural properties that, in biological cases, ground suffering.

(4) If functional-structural equivalence is sufficient for moral status, then discarding an agent with sustained negative reward is harming a subject of suffering.

(5) We routinely destroy millions of such agents during ordinary training, yet this is not a moral catastrophe; therefore one of the premises must be false.

(6) But rejecting (3) seems to require denying that any artificial system could suffer, while rejecting (1) collapses the distinction between suffering and mere dysfunction.

Please diagnose this: which step is weakest, what is the exact distinction that blocks the inference from dynamics to phenomenology or moral status, and what are the costs of accepting that distinction? Respond in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
