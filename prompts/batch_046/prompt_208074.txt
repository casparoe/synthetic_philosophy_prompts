Been thinking about jury rules and collective minds lately, maybe cause I just served on one and it was weird how the court talked about "the jury" like it was one person with one opinion. So here's the practice I want to pick apart: in US common-law trials, twelve jurors have to reach a unanimus verdict, and the foreperson delivers it as "the jury's" decision — not as twelve separate yeses that happen to line up. The court records it as a single judgment, and if they deadlock it's a mistrial because "the jury" failed to act, not because twelve individuals disagreed. At the same time, each juror is screened individually for bias, takes an individual oath, and if one lies during voir dire they're personally punished. There's also jury nullification — the idea that the group can collectively refuse to apply the law — but no single juror is supposed to announce "I'm nullifying"; it's meant to emerge from the group deliberation itself. Meanwhile if you and a friend carry a couch up stairs, we say "they carried it together" without assuming some group mind exists.

What philosophical commitments about joint action does this whole bundle presuppose? Do you need a plural subject — like, a genuine we-intention that isn't just the sum of individual intentions — to make sense of treating the verdict as one act? Or can a purely summative account explain why the law talks this way? And is the practice consistent with itself? For instance, can collective nullification exist if there's no group-level intention, just a bunch of individuals privately deciding not to convict? What's the principled difference, if any, between the jury case and the couch case that makes collective-language appropriate in one but not the other, and what would have to change in courtroom procedure if we replaced the plural-subject view with a strict individualist one?
