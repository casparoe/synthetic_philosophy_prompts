I'm trying to identify whether there is an established concept for a particular trajectory I keep noticing in the collapse of long-standing social norms, and if not, I want it carved precisely with its edges marked.

Consider three patterns. First, dueling among European elites in the nineteenth century: it did not disappear because aristocrats were suddenly persuaded that killing over an insult was morally wrong. Rather, as legal states monopolized violence and bourgeois norms spread, dueling began to signal not honor but reckless lawlessness; once the status signal inverted, the same classes that had defended dueling rapidly adopted the language of barbarism and claimed they had always found it distasteful. Second, foot-binding in parts of China: it was sustained for centuries as a marker of Han female respectability and high family status, yet it collapsed within a generation when modernizing elites needed to project national vigor; bound feet came to signify backwardness rather than refinement, and the moral condemnation—previously marginal—became universal almost overnight, with many former practitioners retrospectively framing their compliance as coerced rather than valued. Third, the social acceptance of routine public corporal punishment in Western schools: it faded not primarily through pedagogical argument about child development, but through a shift in what respectable middle-class parents wished to signal about their own refinement; once physical discipline became associated with lower-status or authoritarian households, moral objections that had long existed suddenly became the dominant public framing, and prior defenders recast their support as mere tradition.

In each case, the mechanism seems to be the same: a practice functions as a credible signal of some valued social quality—honor, refinement, respectability—within a specific coordination structure. When external conditions change that structure (state consolidation, national modernization, class mobility), the practice begins to signal the opposite quality. The norm then collapses rapidly, not because its defenders are argued out of it, but because continuing it becomes status-damaging. Crucially, the moral language that condemns the practice is usually already available; it simply lacked social traction until the signaling inversion made adoption advantageous. After the collapse, participants routinely deny that they ever genuinely endorsed the practice's internal logic, attributing their past compliance to social pressure or ignorance rather than to the values the practice actually expressed.

Is there an established term or framework for exactly this configuration—where a norm dies through the inversion of its signaling value, followed by rapid moral reframing and retrospective disavowal by former defenders? I am not asking for a general theory of moral revolution, nor for an account driven purely by material interest or by rational persuasion. I want to know whether this specific pattern, in which status-coordination failure precedes and enables moral condemnation, has been named and bounded. If it has, how does it relate to interest-based explanations on one side and to arguments that moral reasoning can drive change independently on the other? If it lacks a name, can you propose one and define its boundaries precisely—distinguishing it from mere fashion, from cases where economic efficiency genuinely undermines a practice, and from cases where moral argument genuinely converts people before any status shift occurs? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving: what exactly belongs in this category, what does not, and what it predicts about cases where the signaling structure is ambiguous or contested.
