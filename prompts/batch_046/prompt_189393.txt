I was chatting with my flatmates Riley and Casey after our philosophy seminar — we'd just done rational choice theory — and they got into a proper row about Ulysses contracts and whether they justify locking in climate policy across generations. (1) Riley said the whole notion is incoherent: if I tie myself to the mast, I'm treating my future self like an enemy, and since personal identity over time is loose anyway, present-me has no special authority to imprison future-me. (2) Casey replied that this misses the point — the sirens dont change your values, they hijack your reasoning, so binding yourself is actually protecting long-term autonomy from temporary distortion, not violating it. (3) Riley came back that 'hijack' is just circular: we call a preference distorted only when it conflicts with what present-me wants. Without an independent criterion, its authoritarian to assume present preferences are the true ones.

(4) Casey then said look at constitutional precommitment — we entrench rights precisely so temporary democratic majorities cant tear them up. If Riley's right, then every constitution should be fully amendable by simple majority, which makes rights meaningless. (5) Riley countered that constitutions include amendment procedures and collective ratification, so theyre not pure Ulysses contracts, but also brought up this niche idea: what about 'asymmetric climate locks' where a government binds future parliaments to carbon-reduction methods but not to specific energy ends? That might avoid the dead-hand problem because it constrains means, not values. (6) Casey said that distinction doesnt hold — locking in methods still forces ends on people who might later have cheap fusion or carbon-capture tech they want to deploy differently. Plus, in climate policy theres no clear 'siren' distorting judgement; its just slow preference drift, so the Ulysses analogy is stretched beyond recognition.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say whos more right overall, and whether theres something both are missing — maybe about whether constitutional precommitment actually needs the Ulysses analogy at all, or about what happens when the 'siren' is collective rather than individual.
