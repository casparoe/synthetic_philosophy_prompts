I am writing a feature on how classical dirty-hands frameworks are being adapted to algorithmic governance, and I am trying to reconstruct the implicit logic behind the judgments of the political ethicist Dr. K. R. Morrow.

Across a series of recent essays, Morrow has taken four positions. First, she holds that ordering the torture of a captured operative to prevent an imminent bombing is a paradigmatic dirty-hands case: the official violates an absolute moral constraint and must bear public guilt rather than retreat behind state secrecy. Second, she judges that authorizing a drone strike that will kill civilians in order to eliminate a high-value target also stains the leader’s hands, though rigorous proportionality can diminish—but never erase—the moral taint. Third, she argues that directing a covert unit to assassinate a hostile foreign leader, with full awareness that the operation will likely cause collateral civilian deaths, belongs in the same category, because the wrongful means are specifically willed. Fourth, however, she maintains that imposing broad economic sanctions that foreseeably produce famine in order to destabilize a regime is not a dirty-hands case, on the grounds that the suffering is mediated through global markets and diffuse institutional structures rather than being intended act-by-act.

Consider now a new scenario: a national security chief approves the deployment of an autonomous predictive-detention system that relies on biometric surveillance and algorithmic risk-scoring to identify and confine potential threats without requiring human authorization for each individual detention. The chief knows the system’s false-positive rate will necessarily wrongfully detain hundreds of innocent people, yet considers this unavoidable to prevent a catastrophic attack.

What would Morrow say about this algorithmic case?
