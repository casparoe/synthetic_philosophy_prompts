Here is an argument I have been turning over about cross-generational national guilt, in the spirit of Jaspers’s distinction between political and moral guilt but pushed to an extreme. (1) For any entity to be guilty of an act, it must be numerically identical to the agent that performed the act and must retain the relevant capacity for moral responsibility. (2) A nation is not merely an aggregate of citizens but an institutionally continuous entity—call this institutional diachrony—whose legal order, territorial jurisdiction, and constitutional identity can persist despite complete turnover of membership. (3) Therefore, if Nation N committed a grave wrong eighty years ago and its core institutions remain intact, N today is literally the same agent and can be guilty for that wrong. (4) Now, if N is guilty, then present citizens who maintain it through civic participation—paying taxes, voting, serving in its institutions—share in sustaining the guilty agent, so they bear a form of structural complicity. (5) This structural complicity distributes personal culpability: each participating citizen inherits a share of the collective guilt proportional to their contribution to institutional continuity. (6) But that implies infants born into N, recent immigrants who reject its legacy, and conscientious objectors are all personally culpable to some degree, which is plainly absurd. (7) Hence institutional diachrony must be rejected, and with it any genuine cross-generational national guilt.

If I must choose, I want to keep (1) and (4)—that agency requires diachronic identity and that sustaining institutions involves some complicity—rather than accept that infants are guilty. Still, I feel something slipped between (3) and (5), perhaps an equivocation between collective agency and distributed personal blame. Which step should I reject?
