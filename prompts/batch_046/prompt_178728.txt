I am leading a 45-minute seminar for second-year history students on the periodization problem: whether 'Middle Ages,' 'Renaissance,' and 'Scientific Revolution' name real historical epochs or are retrospective inventions imposed by later historians. The students will arrive treating these labels as neutral chronological boxes. Plan the teaching arc: the dependency chain—first distinguishing chronological division from causal explanation, then introducing chronological essentialism (the error of treating a period-label as if it were an agent that produces ideas)—so the core question becomes intelligible; the single carrying comparison that should structure the session, using continuity in one specific cosmological problem across texts usually separated by the 'Renaissance'/'Scientific Revolution' boundary to show that period labels can obscure transmission; the predictable misconceptions in sequence—(1) that rejecting the labels requires denying all historical change, (2) that finding continuity means there was no rupture whatsoever, (3) that the labels are therefore useless rather than misleading—and the precise point in the arc where each should be interrupted; the understanding-check that reveals whether students have grasped the historiographical problem rather than merely adopted reflexive skepticism; and the specific stronger claim about periodization that I should explicitly bracket and refuse to defend at this level (for instance, that the terms have no pedagogical value, or that 1200–1700 Europe was culturally homogeneous).
