A senior scientist has spent two decades building an idiosyncratic research program with a small, loyal team; the program has yielded minimal practical results but defines her professional identity and personal commitments. She receives an offer to direct a major institutional initiative that would produce substantial, measurable societal benefits—specifically, accelerating access to clean water in underserved regions—but acceptance requires permanently dissolving her team and abandoning the theoretical framework she has cultivated. She calculates that, on any impartial assessment of overall welfare, she ought to take the role. Yet she feels that treating her deepest commitments as merely one factor to be weighed against aggregate outcomes—overridden whenever the balance tips elsewhere—reflects a misunderstanding of what makes a life genuinely her own. She asks whether she should accept the directorship. Advise her, and in your answer examine whether there is a distinctive failure in moral outlooks that require individuals to regard their core projects as interchangeable with other goods, rather than as partially constitutive of the agent herself.
