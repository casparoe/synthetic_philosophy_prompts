Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Karl Jaspers's distinction between collective political guilt and strictly individual moral guilt, especially Jaspers's claim that a nation may bear political responsibility for the actions of its state, whereas moral guilt is non-transferable and demands an internal transformation rather than institutional repair. Focus on Stevens's retrospective awareness of Lord Darlington's Nazi sympathies and his own facilitation of the 1930s diplomatic conferences at Darlington Hall: does Stevens suffer a form of Jaspers-style political guilt as a British citizen whose democracy failed to prevent fascism, or does he endure a vicarious moral guilt for acts he neither authored nor endorsed? Examine whether Stevens's characteristic emotional suppression — his refusal to acknowledge his attachment to Miss Kenton and his resolution to adopt the light conversational style encouraged by his new American employer — operates as a kind of moral anesthesia that prevents the inner reckoning Jaspers considers essential to moral guilt. Ask further whether Ishiguro's unreliable narration introduces a category Jaspers lacks: a structural or professional complicity that attaches not to citizenship but to the disciplined performance of loyalty within an institution. Which scenes — the preparations for the international conference, the dismissal of the Jewish staff, the final meeting with Miss Kenton on the pier — best illuminate or resist mapping Stevens's anguish onto Jaspers's schema of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt? Finally, consider what the novel's refusal to grant Stevens either clear culpability or full redemption contributes to the philosophical debate about whether collective guilt can be inherited by institutional functionaries who are neither state representatives nor direct perpetrators.
