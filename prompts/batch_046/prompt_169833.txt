I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a methodological appendix for my dissertation on the epistemic status of idealization in predictive modeling. I am convening an adversarial collaboration between two scholars who have debated for years without convergence: one who defends a maximalist interpretation of what the literature calls Friedman's F-twist—holding that the descriptive realism of a model's assumptions bears no relation to its scientific value, which is exhausted by predictive accuracy, robustness across contexts, and instrumental utility for intervention—and one who accepts that unrealistic assumptions are often unavoidable but insists that they are legitimate only when they satisfy a condition I will call representational transparency: the distortions must be systematically isolable, causally inert with respect to the predictions used for policy, and traceable back to specific simplifying choices rather than embedded in the core dynamics of the model.

Please draft the joint statement they should sign. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Include agreement on: what distinguishes an idealization from a false empirical claim; the formal structure of the predictive-sufficiency argument (paraphrase without using quotation marks for any specific author's wording); the existence of well-known domains—such as macroeconomic forecasting with rational-expectation assumptions or climate projections with coarse-grained grid resolution—where highly unrealistic assumptions coexist with varying degrees of predictive success; the conditions under which both parties agree a model should be abandoned (e.g., systematic predictive failure across relevant contexts, or the discovery that an unrealistic assumption is driving the prediction in a way that undermines intervention); and any shared commitments about the burden of proof.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present one side's formulation of its own position in wording that side would accept, paired with the other side's formulation of its own position. The table should cover: whether predictive success can ever license ontological commitment in domains where direct observation is impossible or severely limited; whether there exists a principled, pre-predictive distinction between harmless and dangerous idealizations; and whether policy-relevant models require representational transparency as a necessary condition, or merely predictive reliability as a sufficient one. Avoid straw-manning.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) in cases of ontological inaccessibility, can predictive success serve as a proxy for structural fidelity, or does it merely indicate instrumental adequacy? (b) Is representational transparency a genuine epistemic virtue distinct from predictive robustness, or is it reducible to a heuristic for achieving robustness? For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical case or thought experiment both sides agree is relevant. For instance: a fifty-year regional climate model that predicts temperature anomalies within two percent but relies on an assumption of homogeneous atmospheric mixing known to be false, where the mixing assumption is structurally embedded rather than parametrically isolated. Record in advance what each side predicts about whether this model should guide adaptation policy, and about whether modifying the mixing assumption would alter the predictions in ways that matter for decision-making.

Fourth, state whether any portion of the dispute is verbal. If both sides use terms like explanation, realism, or mechanism differently, specify which term, how the meanings diverge, and propose disambiguated replacements—such as explanatory transparency versus predictive robustness, or representational fragility versus predictive robustness—that both could adopt without conceding substance.

Fifth, conclude with a brief statement of what each side counts as evidence or argument that would genuinely change its view, and whether there is any shared criterion—such as a specific pattern of predictive breakdown under intervention—that both agree would be decisive.

Write in formal academic prose suitable for a dissertation appendix. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of specific works. Do not ask for quotations from particular texts; if you reference a well-known argument, paraphrase without quotation marks. You may introduce one conceptual distinction not standard in the literature—such as representational fragility versus predictive robustness—to sharpen the disagreement, but ground it in the philosophy of science and avoid invoking obscure theorists or technical frameworks that would not be familiar to a generalist reader.
