I've been chewing on this view—call it strict motivational tracking—that an act only carries full moral worth when the agent's explicit, occurrent reasoning is causally anchored in the correct normative fact, not just in habit, emotional contagion, or subpersonal affective tagging. It feels like it captures what's wrong with the merchant who avoids cheating only because it boosts sales, but I'm not sure how exposed the view really is. What would actually count against it? I'm not asking for the usual good-instinct counterexamples; I want the deeper pressure points—like cases where explicit reasoning seems to degrade moral performance, or where devlopmental psychology suggests moral expertise works by automating judgment away from conscious tracking. Which of those would a committed tracking theorist concede as damaging, and which can they absorb by tweaking what counts as tracking? Rank them from fatal to just awkward, and be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, allowing unconscious tracking or mixed motives—so that it risks being unfalsifiable. Does it also risk collapsing into a rival view that moral worth is just about outcomes plus some minimal rationality constraint?
