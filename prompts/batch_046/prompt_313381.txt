Here's an argument I sketched for an ethics discussion and now find suspicious because it pushes to an absurd conclusion.

(1) If dietary ethics should aim to minimize wild-animal suffering, then we ought to rank foods by the number of wild animals harmed per kilocalorie produced.
(2) Harvesting grains and legumes—soy, wheat, corn—kills substantial numbers of small mammals, birds, and insects through machinery, pesticides, and habitat conversion, and on standard estimates this harm per kilocalorie exceeds that of pasture-raised beef, which relies on grassland with relatively few direct killings per unit of nutrition.
(3) So substituting beef for plant foods reduces net wild-animal suffering.
(4) Applying the same metric consistently, chicken is worse than beef: chicken production requires more crop feed per kilocalorie of meat, multiplying field deaths, and also involves direct harm to the birds themselves.
(5) Pushed to its logical extreme, the metric implies that the optimal diet maximizes calories yielded per individual death—favoring, in principle, the largest available animals—because one killing produces enormous nutrition and minimizes the total count of bodies harmed.
(6) Yet it is clearly wrong to recommend eating whales, elephants, or other large wild animals on suffering-minimization grounds.
(7) Therefore something in steps (1) through (5) must fail.

I want to preserve the intuition that crop agriculture is not harmless and that wild-animal suffering carries moral weight, so I would rather keep (1) and (2) than abandon them. But I cannot locate the precise slip. Is the error an equivocation between "number of deaths" and "amount of suffering"—ignoring differences in sentience and life quality across species? Is it that the argument silently excludes the suffering of farmed animals from the calculus? Or is there a modal or scope mistake in moving from per-calorie comparisons to an all-things-considered dietary prescription? Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
