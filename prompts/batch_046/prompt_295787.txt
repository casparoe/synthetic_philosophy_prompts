Before evaluating competing accounts of what makes every instance an 'a'—despite the gulf between a blackletter double-story form, a geometric single-story circle-with-tail, and a cursive loop that never closes—I want the full specification of adequacy. Write the desiderata that any theory of letterform identity must satisfy: the phenomena it must explain, the boundary cases it must adjudicate, the distinctions it must preserve, and the results from perception and development it must respect, with the motivating consideration behind each. Be specific about: (i) how it handles extreme stylistic variation without collapsing into pure family resemblance or arbitrary convention; (ii) the hard boundaries—synthetic adversarial shapes that preserve human recognition yet violate standard geometric descriptors; accidental 'ghost letters' produced by non-intentional physical processes that observers nonetheless classify; cross-script ambiguities such as Latin 'a', Cyrillic 'а', and Greek alpha; and the difference between recognition in isolation versus in running text; (iii) the non-negotiable distinctions, especially between essential form and ornamental feature (serif, weight, color), between token-level identity and type-level membership, and between conditions for producing a letter and conditions for recognizing one; (iv) whether it can allow that recognition survives rotation, scaling, and partial occlusion within limits, and that sparse-example learning by children is possible; and (v) which of these demands are genuinely required and which reflect the prejudices of particular camps (e.g., that form must be non-arbitrary versus that it is purely conventional). Flag any subset of desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, accommodating every accidental resemblance while excluding all non-letter shapes, or maintaining script-independent essential form while honoring massive conventional variation. Then, briefly, score three broad approaches—a Platonic essentialist view, a prototype-based psychological-space view, and a functional-communicative convention view—against the list, keeping the evaluation to a short paragraph for each. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the point is to build the standard of evaluation before applying it.
