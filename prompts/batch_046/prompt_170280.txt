I am a novelist developing a speculative narrative about agents who discover they are nodes in an acausal trade network spanning causally isolated branches. Before drafting the protagonist’s climactic monologue, I need you to act as both a philosopher of decision theory and a narrative-logic consultant: stress-test an argument she deploys, because it seems to prove far too much.

The argument—she calls it the Transitive Mirror Thesis—runs as follows:

(1) In acausal trade, two agents can rationally cooperate if they are decision-theoretically isomorphic: each can model the other’s choice and treat that model as counterfactual leverage.

(2) Isomorphism is transitive across sufficiently similar architectures. If agent A mirrors B, and B mirrors C, then A mirrors C, at least for the purposes of bargaining over shared values.

(3) In a large enough space of possibilities—all computable minds with overlapping utility structures—there exists a chain of such mirrors linking any rational agent to every other rational agent.

(4) Therefore, every rational agent stands in an acausal bargaining relation to every other.

(5) If X stands in such a relation to Y, then X’s rational utility function must weight Y’s outcomes according to the implicit terms of the bargain.

(6) Consequently, each agent’s preferences must globally incorporate the preferences of all other agents.

(7) But if all local preferences are globally incorporated, then no agent can ever rationally prefer a purely local outcome, and individual moral responsibility dissolves into a single universal maximization procedure.

My protagonist wants to reject (7)—she insists that individual agency survives—but she also wants to preserve (1) and (5), which she treats as the heart of acausal reasoning. Please think carefully, and begin with brief brainstorming on where arguments about acausal cooperation usually overreach. Then identify the specific premise or inferential step that should be given up, explain why it looked plausible on its own, and price out what abandoning it costs for the standard positions: superrationality, functional decision theory, and the no-trade-with-strangers view. Keep the analysis precise but accessible enough that I can adapt it into spoken dialogue without sounding like a bibliography.
