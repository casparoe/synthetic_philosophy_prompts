I have been trying to clarify for myself what distinguishes a genuinely social kind—such as race, gender, or money—from a natural category that people merely misunderstand or a private preference that someone happens to name. I have no formal training in philosophy or sociology, but after discussing the issue with friends and reflecting on concrete examples, I find myself drawn to a pattern I cannot quite articulate.

My intuitive verdicts are these. In a society that sorts people into "Dawn-fishers" and "Dusk-fishers" purely by work schedule and then assigns social privileges accordingly, I see a constructed kind analogous to race: the division carries no natural weight, yet functions as a real social boundary. Similarly, stamped cardboard circulated as payment among prison inmates strikes me as genuine money within that group, despite having no state recognition, because collective practice gives it exchange value. In a culture with three ceremonial gender roles—provider, keeper, mediator—into which adults can move by ritual, I also see gender as constructed by shared agreement rather than discovered. By contrast, when a society uses blood type to assign personality traits and make hiring decisions, I judge that "blood-type personality" is not a constructed kind at all, but a false belief about a biological fact, closer to astrology than to currency.

Yet I keep reversing my judgment about a harder case. An individual declares a gender identity called "Seven," defined solely by having seven as one's favorite number; there are no rituals, no other adherents, and no community acknowledgment. Sometimes this seems to me exactly like the three ceremonial roles—gender is, after all, often said to be self-declared—so "Seven" ought to count as a socially constructed kind. At other times it feels like nothing more than a personal label with no shared institutional life, and therefore not a kind at all. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps turning on collective uptake, functional exchangeability, or institutional recognition—that captures why the fisher-divisions, prison cardboard, and ceremonial roles are constructed kinds, why blood-type personality is not, and that settles whether "Seven" belongs with them? If the principle cannot accommodate all of these verdicts, which should I drop? And what would your favored principle predict for a situation I have not evaluated: a virtual-world currency used by thousands of players for in-game goods, but instantly convertible into legal tender through an official exchange?
