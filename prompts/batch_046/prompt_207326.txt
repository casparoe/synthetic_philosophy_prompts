Write a short story that dramatizes the reflexive coherence problem in extrapolated volition—the idea that an idealized version of a person might want things that depend on how the idealization procedure itself is weighted. Set the narrative in a near-future research commune where residents may submit to a “Volition Mapping” protocol: an algorithmic process that computes what a subject would prefer if more informed, more patient, and more consistent, with explicit parameters for temporal stability, emotional attachment, and moral abstraction.

Your protagonist, an applied ethicist named Kaelen, receives her mapping results and finds that her extrapolated volition rejects the protocol’s default scheme—specifically, its rule that consistency should override present emotional bonds—but only on the condition that this rejection is itself processed through the same consistency-weighting scheme. This generates a recursive loop in her output file, represented not as a logical contradiction but as an ambiguous graphical branch in the interface: one path shows her accepting the mapped preference to change the weights, the other shows the changed weights erasing the preference that initiated them.

Introduce a second character, a retired engineer named Voss, whose mapped volition is fully coherent and stable but advocates for actions that violate every principle he currently endorses. Rather than accepting or rejecting the mapping outright, Voss adopts a “suspension protocol”: he treats his extrapolated preferences as binding for personal choices but merely advisory for collective governance, an asymmetry that fractures the commune’s decision-making structure when the two domains overlap.

Center the plot on a concrete, irreversible decision: whether to release a drought-resistant but genetically modified seed stock into the local watershed, an act that could secure the commune’s food supply but carries an irreversible ecological risk. Show the philosophical conflict through physical interaction with the machinery—Kaelen adjusting weighting dials and interpreting ambiguous branching outputs, Voss signing recursive consent forms that distinguish “personal” from “collective” authorization, both of them handling a broken irrigation valve that forces them to act before the protocol finishes computing. Do not resolve whether Kaelen’s loop is genuinely incoherent or whether Voss’s suspension is rational; end with both characters taking practical steps—Kaelen embedding an exception clause into the protocol’s source parameters, Voss withholding his collective vote while privately planting the seeds—that commit them to incompatible interpretations of what it means for a preference to be “ideally” one’s own. Avoid expository explanation of the theory; let the concept emerge entirely through procedural detail, machinery outputs, and the tangible consequences of the seed-release decision.
