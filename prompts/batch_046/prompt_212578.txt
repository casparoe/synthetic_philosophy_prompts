I have been teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of technology, and in our recent discussions on artificial intelligence we kept returning to the familiar phrase "the alignment problem". I have gradually become convinced that this phrase conceals more than it reveals, and I should like to subject my own view to critical pressure. Specifically, I hold that what is called the alignment problem is not a single problem at all, but a family of three conceptually separable difficulties—what I term the problem of normative translation (rendering plural, context-sensitive human values into stable objective functions without essential loss), the problem of dynamic stability (ensuring that a system aligned at time t remains aligned as it learns, modifies its own architecture, and encounters novel environments), and the problem of institutional embedding (constructing governance and correction mechanisms capable of intervening before misaligned behaviour produces catastrophic outcomes). I further maintain that these are not merely sub-divisions of one underlying challenge, but are in important respects independent: substantial progress on normative translation might actually exacerbate dynamic instability, since more nuanced value representations tend to require more complex self-models that are harder to preserve under recursive improvement; and neither achievement guarantees effective institutional embedding, which depends on political and economic conditions largely outside technical design.

What I want from you is not a defence of this tripartite disaggregation, but an honest map of its exposure—what observations, formal results, empirical developments, or conceptual arguments would count decisively against it, and which would merely be awkward or require qualification. In particular, please address whether there are hidden couplings between these three domains that I have overlooked; whether the independence claim collapses once we recognise that any objective function is itself an institutional artefact shaped by power relations; and whether my framing has been qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing failures to be assigned ad hoc to one domain or another without threatening the overall taxonomy. I am also interested in whether defenders of the unitary view—those who insist that alignment is fundamentally one problem of value learning—would concede any of these couplings as damaging, or whether they would dismiss them as category mistakes arising from an over-philosophical division of labour.
