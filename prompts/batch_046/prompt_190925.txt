I'm taking a seminar on philosophies of history, and we've been working through the standard cyclical models—Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's cycles of asabiyya and dynastic decay, Spengler's morphological culture-to-civilization trajectory, and Toynbee's challenge-and-response arc—mostly as abstract schemas rather than tools for concrete episodes. I want to stress-test one of them against a real case that seems, on its face, to be a linear terminus rather than a cyclical turn: the dissolution of the Soviet Union and its immediate aftermath, roughly 1985 to 1995. Specifically, apply a modified Khaldun-Spengler hybrid. In standard Khaldun, a ruling group's solidarity weakens, the dynasty becomes bureaucratic, and a new group with stronger cohesion replaces it; in Spengler, a culture enters its civilizational winter of rationalization and creative exhaustion. The niche framing I want to inject is this: treat the late-Soviet technocratic-managerial elite not as a fresh asabiyya but as an "anti-solidarity"—a cohort bound not by shared sentiment or tribal loyalty but by mutual dependence on administrative extraction and the preservation of institutional privilege. Analyze the collapse and transition through this lens. Which features—the peaceful dissolution of unified nuclear command, the mass privatization of state assets and rapid emergence of oligarchic networks, the continuity of security-service personnel into the new order—read as cyclical decay, and which as linear ideological exhaustion? Where does the model fail: does it mispredict the absence of large-scale civil violence, misidentify the binding principle of the replacement elite, or overlook the specifically economic-rationalist logic that replaced Marxist-Leninist solidarity? And what does the Soviet case imply for cyclical theory itself—does it require a new sub-category for bureaucratic-technocratic decline, or does it confirm that linear-progress narratives were always inadequate for imperial dissolutions of this kind?
