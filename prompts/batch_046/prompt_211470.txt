Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar of about 14 students. The aim is to make participants feel both the pull and the resistance of the claim that a book could be conscious, framed through the scenario of Einstein's brain being replaced page-by-page with descriptive text. Do not begin with a lecture, and do not name the philosophical problem—functional equivalence, substrate independence, or the explanatory gap—until the debrief. The idea must emerge from what the group builds and experiences.

Materials: fourteen index cards; one binder clip; one printed "Meta-Page" card.

Structure and rules.

Phase 1, The Replacement Protocol (20 minutes). Divide students into seven pairs. Each pair receives one Region Card describing a cognitive function—integrating color with emotional valence, generating grammatical German, registering gravitational pull, and so on. Each card also carries a Replacement Page: a third-person printed description of exactly how that region operates. The pair must physically substitute the page for the region and privately vote on whether function is preserved. They seal their vote in an envelope without revealing it.

Phase 2, The Book-Brain Integration (30 minutes). Assemble all seven replacement pages into a circular Book-Brain clipped together on the table. Designate two students as Internal Nodes, seated inside the circle; they may read only the pages immediately adjacent to them. The remaining ten students serve as External Stimuli. The facilitator poses an integrated scenario—e.g., "A red object is falling toward you; report what you perceive and how you respond." The response propagates around the circle according to linking rules printed on each page: keyword matches trigger handoffs to the next page. Crucially, include the Meta-Page, a reflexive card that lists the activation states of all other pages and updates its own printed text whenever it is activated, so the system contains a self-model that is never observed from any single point inside. The Internal Nodes attempt to report whether anything is happening; the External Stimuli observe whether a unified answer emerges. The group runs two full scenarios.

Phase 3, The Verdict (15 minutes). Each student votes independently on a three-point scale: the Book-Brain has phenomenal experience; it behaves identically but lacks experience; or the distinction is ungrounded. Only then open the Phase 1 envelopes and reveal the collective substitution votes. Only now name the problem.

Debrief (10 minutes). Map Phase 1 onto functionalist substitution and substrate independence; Phase 2 onto distributed integration without a central observer, emphasizing that coherence is achieved through linking rules no single page authored; the Meta-Page onto reflexive self-modeling and the strange-loop structure of self-reference. Note that Internal Nodes will predictably report fragmentation—this is expected, but the facilitator must supply the philosophical move that absence of report from a node is not proof of absence at the system level. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the actual phenomenal quality of being the Book-Brain, which is precisely the gap the thought experiment exploits. Predictable failure: ambiguous keywords will cause propagation traffic jams on the first run; include a facilitator override card that resolves ties by random draw, and explain afterward that this artificial resolution mirrors the problem of downward causation—the system's unity depends on structural rules not derivable from any individual page. Be frank that the Meta-Page introduces a niche element—reflexive informational closure—that goes beyond standard functionalist accounts, and that the facilitator must explain why this addition makes the holist case stronger without resolving the hard problem.
