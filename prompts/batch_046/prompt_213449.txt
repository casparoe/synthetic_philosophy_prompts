Discussions of Berkeley's master argument — that an object existing unperceived is inconceivable because the very act of conceiving it places it within a mind — sometimes invoke a video-game rendering analogy: a tree outside the player's view frustum has no displayed form, and any attempt to inspect its hidden state necessarily loads it into active memory, so there is no "unrendered" tree available to verification. Proponents use this to suggest Berkeley was right: just as the tree lacks being apart from the render pipeline, matter lacks being apart from perception. Audit the analogy precisely: what plays the role of the tree, the player, the engine, and the hidden state; where the comparison smuggles in computational infrastructure (source code, storage buffers, causal mechanics) that Berkeley's immaterialism denies; whether the game's inaccessibility is merely epistemic (we trigger rendering by checking) or genuinely ontological (no tree exists unrendered); and whether the master argument requires the stronger claim. Finally, assess whether the analogy supports Berkeley's conclusion or collapses it into a category mistake about mental representation versus digital storage, and suggest what a more apt analogy — if any — would be.
