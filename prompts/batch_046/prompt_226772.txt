I'm running a 90-minute graduate seminar on rational belief and decision theory with about twelve philosophy students. We've discussed Kelly betting and geometric rationality, but only through standard independent-gamble examples that make the criterion feel like a mathematical curiosity rather than a structural constraint on reasoning. I want an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of geometric rationality in a domain where it is not intuitively appropriate: nested, interdependent epistemic commitments—specifically, allocating confidence across scientific hypotheses that share hidden auxiliary assumptions. The core idea is that when stakes are placed on logically related propositions rather than on independent events, multiplicative payoff structures interact with logical dependency in a way that makes intuitive "fair division" strategies collapse.

Design a structured game for three groups of four, with a twenty-minute rules explanation, forty minutes of play across three discrete rounds, and a thirty-minute debrief. Each group manages a "research portfolio" of one hundred paper points divided among three competing claims—say, a core theory, an auxiliary instrument-calibration claim, and an observational prediction that depends on both—without initially knowing that the propositions are nested. The payoff scheme must be multiplicative, not additive: each round's returns compound geometrically on remaining capital, and if shared auxiliaries fail, stakes tied to them are not merely lost but voided entirely, accelerating ruin. Include explicit rules for how allocations constrain what the group may assert in the next round, so that geometric rationality appears not as wealth maximization but as a norm on discursive coherence—treating each "credence commitment" as binding subsequent claims. The hidden joint distribution should be revealed only round by round, so that groups naturally spread stakes arithmetically, over-allocate to intuitively plausible core theories, and suffer compound collapse by round three before any lecture explains why.

The debrief should map each moment of failure onto a feature of geometric rationality—the multiplicative penalty for overconfidence, the non-independence of epistemic stakes, and the conflict between Kelly-optimal allocations and "equal respect" intuitions—while being frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time: groups will likely accuse the hidden distribution of being rigged, attempt to renegotiate rules once dependency is revealed, or treat voided stakes as a procedural error rather than a structural consequence. Also state clearly which feature of geometric rationality the activity cannot reproduce—namely, that real-world belief revision is continuous and responsive to evidence, not a discrete round-based process with a revealed joint distribution—so that I must supply that analogical bridge myself. Do not ask students to read in advance, do not lecture on Kelly before play begins, and do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reference to specific texts; the prompt should rely only on concepts already available in the standard literature and should inject one genuinely niche framing: that portfolio allocations function as binding "credence commitments" that restrict what a group is permitted to claim in subsequent rounds, forcing geometric rationality to emerge as a constraint on collective assertion rather than on monetary betting.
