Read Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon through the lens of philosophy of history, specifically the tension between Leopold von Ranke's program of presenting the past as it actually occurred and the sceptical challenges raised by E. H. Carr and Hayden White, who treat historical narratives as constructions shaped by present concerns and rhetorical tropes rather than as reconstructions of an objective past. The film offers four incompatible accounts of a death in a forest—the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai through a medium, and the woodcutter—without ever adjudicating among them. Does this structure dramatize White's claim that all history-writing is fundamentally an exercise in emplotment, or does it instead illustrate Carr's point that the historian's selection and ordering of facts already introduces an irreducible subjectivity? More precisely, examine whether the framing narrative—the woodcutter's tentative, partial retelling—operates as a hidden Rankean gesture, an attempt to salvage a minimal factual core beneath the competing narratives, or whether it simply adds one more subjective layer. I want a reading that respects both the film's formal architecture, particularly the repetition-with-variation structure and the absence of any authoritative omniscient narrator, and the philosophical debate: where does Rashomon resist reduction to a philosophical illustration, and what does it suggest about the ethics of testimony and the human need for narrative closure that standard philosophy of history tends to neglect? Focus on specific scenes—the bandit's boastful confession, the wife's self-incriminating account, the medium's possession—without reproducing dialogue verbatim. The aim is criticism faithful in both directions: neither reducing Kurosawa to a textbook example of historical scepticism, nor distorting Ranke, Carr, or White to fit the plot. End by saying what the film contributes to the debate that philosophical argument alone does not capture. I am preparing this for a seminar on historical epistemology.
