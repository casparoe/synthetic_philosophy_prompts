I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether anyone can genuinely be held responsible when an autonomous system causes serious harm, and we kept talking past each other because we never agreed on what a decent theory of responsibility attribution even has to deliver in these cases. So before I hear any more proposals, I want the full specification.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of moral responsibility for autonomous systems—particularly those operating with high degrees of learned autonomy inside human-machine teams—must actually get right. Specify the cases it has to handle, such as direct human override being technically available but ignored due to automation bias; harm emerging from interaction among multiple autonomous subsystems and a human supervisor with split or ambiguous authority; and behavior shaped by training data or reward structures that no individual agent curated or fully understands. Specify the distinctions it must preserve: between epistemic gaps, where we simply do not know who is at fault, and metaphysical gaps, where no agent genuinely meets the conditions for blame; between backward-looking liability and forward-looking accountability; and between direct operational control, supervisory oversight, and what I will call architectural influence, where design choices constrain future behavior without constituting direct commands. For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag which are non-negotiable and which reflect the preferences of particular camps—such as strict methodological individualists, determinists about free will, or advocates of distributed cognition.

Then say whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, address whether demanding both full human traceability and genuine machine autonomy generates a structural contradiction, and whether preserving individual blame while accepting distributed moral cognition across multi-agent networks is impossible. That tension is what I most want to see mapped.

Finally, briefly score the three main contenders—the traditional command-responsibility model, the distributed or many-hands approach, and the design-responsibility or architectural view—against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view—such as that autonomy necessarily eliminates responsibility, or that distributed cognition fully resolves the gap—as if it were the consensus.
