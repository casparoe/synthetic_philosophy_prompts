I've been trying to wrap my head around HCH and factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation, and I think I've backed myself into a corner with an argument my study group cooked up. It feels like it proves HCH can't actually do what it's supposed to do, but I know that can't be right, so I need help finding the exit.

Here's the chain:
(1) The point of factored cognition inside HCH is that you split a hard reasoning task into pieces small enough that each human node can actually handle, then aggregate the results.
(2) But if each piece is genuinely small enough for one person, then the aggregation step has to work with outputs that are already compressed or abstracted away from the raw reasoning.
(3) Once you go past two layers of this, the top-level humans are consulting on summaries they didn't produce and probably can't fully unpack.
(4) So either the factoring stops early—meaning HCH never exceeds individual human reasoning capacity—or it goes deep, in which case the "human" part is mostly rubber-stamping opaque intermedate results.
(5) If they're rubber-stamping, then HCH isn't really an idealization of human deliberation at all; it's just a black-box optimizer with people in the loop for legal cover.
(6) And since HCH is specifically proposed to avoid black-box optimizers, deep factoring violates it's own definitional constrains.

So it looks like HCH is either useless for superhuman problems or not actually consultative. Where's the slip? Is there a hidden assumption about transparency being required at every layer? Or is the mistake in step (4), assuming that not understanding every intermediate means you're not genuinely consulting? I feel like something about epistemic responsibility is being smuggled in, but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, and what does giving it up cost for the idealization?
