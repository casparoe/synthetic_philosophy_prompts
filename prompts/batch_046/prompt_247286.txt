Frankfurt's challenge targets the principle of altenative possibilities, which says you're only morally responsible for an act if you genuinely could have done otherwise. In the classic case Jones decides to vote for a candidate, but Black is secretly monitoring him and would force the same vote if Jones showed any prior sign of choosing differently. Because Black never actually intervenes, Jones decides and acts entirely on his own, so he's clearly responsible — yet he couldn't have done otherwise, since Black was standing by. So PAP is false.

Now, Fischer objected that there's still a flicker of freedom: Jones might have blushed or displayed some prior signal that would have triggered Black's intervention, and in that counterfactual branch Jones could have avoided the decision. But Frankfurt himself addressed this in his 1969 paper by insisting that such prior signs aren't part of the actual causal chain leading to the action, so they don't save PAP.

In the delayed-activation variant, the blocker doesn't watch for prior signs at all; it only activates after Jones has formed an irrevocable mental intention but before he moves. That eliminates every flicker at the behavioral level. But I think this actually means PAP survives after all, because Jones still could have done otherwise by not forming that mental intention in the first place. The formation of the intention is itself an action with alternatives available, so there's still a flicker — just at the mental stage rather than the physical one. So Frankfurt hasn't shown PAP is false; he's only shown that the physical execution is determined, while the mental decision remains free and subject to PAP. Therefore I think PAP is true if we apply it at the right level.
