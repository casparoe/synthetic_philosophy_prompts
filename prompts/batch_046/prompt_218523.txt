I have been discussing capital punishment with colleagues recently; I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but I find myself returning to an analogy from distributed systems. In database architecture, a transaction that lacks a rollback mechanism demands an exceptionally high confidence threshold before commitment, because once executed the state change is permanent and any false positive—an erroneous commit—cannot be undone. This maps, I think, onto the problem of execution: if the convicted person is in fact innocent, the error is irrecoverable. At the same time, I feel the pull of the retributive intuition that justice involves balancing a moral ledger, that some wrongs seem to create a debt that mere prevention of future harm does not discharge. And then there is deterrence, which seems to depend precisely on that irreversibility—the finality is what makes the threat credible. These three considerations feel locked in a loop that never resolves.

To close a conversation I have been having about this, I would like a villanelle: nineteen lines arranged in five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains labeled A1 and A2, following the rhyme and repetition pattern A1 b A2, then abA1, abA2, abA1, abA2, and finally abA1A2. One refrain should evoke the impossibility of rollback—the unfixable nature of a false positive in an irreversible system. The other should evoke the retributive idea of a ledger that demands settlement. The poem should make the form do the philosophical work: the refrains should take turns returning, never meeting until the final quatrain, where they must share the closing two lines side by side without synthesis or resolution. Filter the concepts through the vocabulary of irreversible computation rather than standard legal theory, and render the positions with enough precision that someone familiar with the philosophical debate—retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing an innocent person—recognizes them, while keeping the language accessible to someone without formal philosophical training. Do not include citations, references to specific philosophers, or bibliographic suggestions.
