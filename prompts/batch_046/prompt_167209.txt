I've come around to a kind of graded-deference fix for Lewis's Principal Principel — instead of the usual binary admissability rule that kills the Humphreys paradox by banning any undermining evidence, I think we should allow partial deference: your credence tracks the objective chance scaled by how much your evidence actually undermines the generating process, without ever fully cutting it off. So if you're 70% sure the coin is biased, you don't go all the way to your subjective belief; you stay somewhere in between, weighted by the reliability of the chance-generating mechanism itself.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the standard line about admissability being all-or-nothing — play it straight. Which formal results (say about belief updating or conditionalization) would break it? What intuitive cases — maybe ones involving self-locating belief or nested epistemic access — make graded deference collapse into either full deference or none at all? And has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure of calibration can be explained away by adjusting the weighting factor, is that a bug or a feature? Rank the defeaters from fatal to just awkward, say what a defender of graded deference would themselves admit is damaging, and be honest about whether the position is now too well-insulated to be interesting.
