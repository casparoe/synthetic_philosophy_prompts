I have been reflecting on the feedback between psychiatric classification and the people classified—the phenomenon philosophers discuss as looping effects in the study of human kinds. I want to know whether my judgments about particular cases can be unified by a single principle. Here are my verdicts.

First, the broadening of autism spectrum diagnosis over recent decades: I take there to be a robust looping effect. More individuals identify with the category, social practices adapt, and clinical presentations shift accordingly. Yet I regard this loop as largely benign, even emancipatory, and I do not think it renders autism ontologically suspect.

Second, the marked increase in dissociative identity disorder presentations during the 1980s and 1990s, characterized by highly distinct alters that appeared predominantly in clinical contexts: I judge this to be looping of a more troubling, partly iatrogenic kind. Here I am inclined to think the loop undermines confidence in the category's natural boundaries and makes the condition seem more constructed than discovered.

Third, the recent incorporation of prolonged grief disorder into major diagnostic manuals: I see a loop, but a weak one. The classification may reshape how severe grief is narrated and experienced, yet the underlying phenomenon of debilitating bereavement was present beforehand; the loop modifies expression more than it generates the condition.

Fourth, a hypothetical category—chronic digital dissociation, defined by persistent alienation from one's curated online persona—if introduced in a manual but remaining unknown to the lay public: my verdict is that there is essentially no looping effect, because affected individuals lack awareness of the category and therefore cannot adapt their self-understanding to it.

Fifth, the sharp rise in adult ADHD diagnoses: I believe looping is present and substantial—autobiographies are reorganized around the label, and diagnostic thresholds seem to drift with the presenting population—but I am unsure whether this makes ADHD more constructed than conditions like diabetes, or whether it merely reveals a previously hidden prevalence.

The case I keep returning to is culture-bound syndromes such as koro, which remain tightly localized despite formal classification. Does their failure to loop globally indicate that they track genuine local biological or cultural realities, or does it merely show that looping requires a specific social infrastructure?

What coherent principle, if any, distinguishes looping that reveals a condition from looping that invents it? Should the presence of a loop diminish our ontological commitment to a category, and if so, why does it seem to do so for dissociative identity disorder but not for autism? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and apply it to a case I have not listed: a technically obscure diagnosis that becomes widely known not through clinical encounter but through algorithmically curated social media, where patients learn the criteria primarily from peer-produced content. If my judgments are irreconcilable, say so explicitly, defend the simpler account, and identify the outlier.
