I am preparing a case study for my seminar in conservative political philosophy, and I would like to check whether my conceptual mapping is disciplined rather than forced. The episode I am using is the demolition of New York’s original Pennsylvania Station, begun in 1963, together with the preservation battle over Grand Central Terminal that led to the Supreme Court decision Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City in 1978. I want to analyze this episode through Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence, but I am working with a specific distinction that I want to hold constant: Chesterton’s fence read as an epistemic principle—do not remove a boundary whose function you do not understand—versus Burke’s prescription, which treats institutional survival as itself a form of accumulated practical knowledge that does not depend on any rational reconstruction.

My request is for a two-directional analysis. First, apply the framework: what did the actors—Penn Central Railroad, the city planners, the preservationists—actually face, and what should they have done according to this combined Burkean-Chesterton view, given that the visible purpose of Penn Station (monumental rail travel) was declining while Grand Central’s economic value lay partly in invisible air rights? Second, assess the fit: which features of the episode does the framework genuinely explain—such as the later cultural regret over Penn Station and the legal protection of Grand Central—and which does it miss, distort, or remain silent about, particularly the role of air-rights financing, the Landmarks Preservation Law of 1965, and the fact that preservation succeeded only when it was economically compatible with development?

Finally, I want the case to test the theory: is this a confirmation that rationalist redevelopment ignores hidden institutional functions, an anomaly showing that the fence principle only becomes active after destruction (and therefore offers poor forward guidance), or evidence that Burkean prescription collapses into market viability rather than philosophical authority? Please keep the mapping explicit—who is the relevant actor, what are the options, what counts as evidence—and say clearly where the theory has nothing to say rather than stretching it to cover the economic and legal dynamics.
