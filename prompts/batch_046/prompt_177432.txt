A handout from a philosophy of science seminar summarizes the "competence without comprehension" inversion shared by Darwin and Turing like this: "In Origin of Species, Darwin inverted Paley's watchmaker argument. Paley said that complex adaptive structures imply a comprehending designer; Darwin showed that natural selection produces the same competence—eyes, instincts—without any foresight or understanding, which means functional language in biology is only a metaphor for historical accident. Turing performed the same maneuver against Cartesianism in his 1950 Mind essay. By substituting the imitation game for "Can machines think?" and noting that we never observe other minds directly, he argued that behavioral competence is the sole legitimate criterion for attributing understanding. Therefore, if a machine plays the game well, consistency demands we grant it the same conscious comprehension we grant humans; the concept of an internal, hidden mind is redundant for both organisms and computers, and science should eliminate it entirely."

This sounds like it captures a real structural parallel, but I worry it misrepresents Darwin's retention of functional explanation and exaggerates Turing's conclusion about machine consciousness. Is this a fair summary? Please break it down: which parts about Darwin's view of natural selection, design, and teleology are accurate, and which conflate his rejection of intelligent design with a rejection of all functional or purposive language? Which parts about Turing's imitation game, his treatment of other minds, and his methodological aims are correct, and which turn his operational proposal into a metaphysical claim about consciousness? Finally, evaluate whether the parallel actually holds and whether the handout's conclusion—that both thinkers prove comprehension is a redundant scientific concept—follows from the corrected account.
