Here is a package of commitments I have arrived at regarding the alignment problem, and I want to know whether it holds together or collapses on inspection.

(1) At bottom, I take alignment to be a single problem: ensuring that an artificial agent's objective tracks human values under distributional shift, rather than locking onto a proxy that diverges in novel contexts.

(2) Nevertheless, I treat value learning, robustness to distributional shift, and mechanistic interpretability as conceptually separable achievements: one could in principle solve value learning without solving robustness, or achieve robustness through architectural constraints that have nothing to do with values.

(3) I think value learning is conceptually prior: if an agent genuinely internalizes the right values, then robustness and interpretability become secondary engineering concerns rather than independent sources of alignment failure.

(4) At the same time, I hold that interpretability is epistemically prior: absent the ability to inspect the mechanism, we have no justified belief that value learning has actually succeeded, so (3) cannot ground practical confidence in any particular system.

(5) I define alignment narrowly, not as generic benevolence or safety, but specifically as the absence of catastrophic misgeneralization of objectives. Consequently, harm caused by incompetence, false world-models, or reasoning errors does not count as an alignment failure.

(6) From (5) I draw that a system which is genuinely value-aligned—no proxy divergence—would not require external containment protocols or kill switches specifically for alignment purposes, though it might need them for ordinary reliability or security.

(7) Yet I also maintain that even a value-aligned system should be subject to sustained external oversight, not because its values are defective, but because its world-model might be systematically erroneous in ways that cause it to pursue correct values through catastrophic means. I call this potential failure "epistemic misalignment," and I treat it as distinct from "axiological misalignment."

The apparent tensions are several. (1) and (2) seem to pull in opposite directions—one problem or many? (3) and (4) appear to assign contradictory priorities. Most sharply, (5) and (6) seem to exclude containment for alignment reasons, while (7) reintroduces a form of oversight that looks structurally similar to containment, only re-labeled. Meanwhile, if (3) is right, solving value learning should settle the core issue, yet (7) suggests that even after axiological alignment is achieved, a distinct and dangerous failure mode remains.

Please audit this set: order the tensions by severity, specify for each which commitment must yield and what that costs the overall view, distinguish the tensions that are genuine from those that dissolve once the distinctions in (5)–(7) are taken seriously, and say whether there is a minimal revision that restores consistency without abandoning the narrow definition of alignment in (5). Do not offer a lecture on alignment in general; the object of evaluation is exactly this package.
