As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I spend much of my workday debugging distributed systems, and lately I have noticed a structural pattern that seems to appear both in log interpretation and in ordinary human conversation. I am wondering whether philosophers have given it a precise name, or whether it remains an unnamed boundary case.

Here is the pattern in concrete form. I am trying to diagnose a legacy service whose logs use the phrase "connection refused," but in this system the phrase can mean either that the target server is unreachable or that a firewall rule is actively blocking the port, depending on the broader state of the network. To decide which reading applies in a given instance, I have to assume that the system's overall behavior is largely consistent and not randomly self-contradictory; otherwise both interpretations are equally compatible with the data. Yet to establish that consistency in the first place, I need to have already interpreted enough other log entries correctly to build a coherent model of the network state. So I cannot first decode the individual messages and then check for global coherence; I must do both at once, letting my assumption of overall rational consistency guide the assignment of local meanings, and letting those local meanings in turn justify the global assumption.

The parallel in language learning strikes me as unavoidable. If I encounter a speaker of an unknown language pointing at a rock and uttering a single word, I cannot learn that word in isolation. I have to assume that the speaker is mostly truthful about the immediate environment and mostly rational in how they combine statements, and then use that global assumption to fix the local meaning—while recognizing that the local meanings I assign are part of what makes the global rationality assumption plausible. I am aware of a broad idea called the principle of charity, but what I am describing feels more specific and more circular: it is not merely being generous to the speaker, but the claim that interpretation is literally impossible unless belief-attribution and meaning-assignment are performed together in one interdependent package.

Does this exact configuration—the simultaneous bootstrapping of rationality-attribution and semantic interpretation—have an established philosophical name? And if it does, how exactly is it distinguished from ordinary coherence theories or from simple interpretive charity? Please provide a committed answer that takes a definite position on whether this is fundamentally a problem about language, about rational psychology, or about the conditions for any interpretive practice whatsoever, and defend that choice with reasons rather than laying out the alternatives for me to choose among.
