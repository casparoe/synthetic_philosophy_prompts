Having recently turned from physics to philosophy, I have come to accept a Pascalian position on justice and authority: because human reason lacks access to a universal, substantive conception of justice, social order cannot rest on justice alone without becoming weak and unstable, while force without justice is mere tyranny; legitimate authority therefore requires their union, with custom and convention serving as the necessary bridge that masks an ultimately arbitrary, coercive foundation. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view—not merely the standard liberal reply that procedural fairness can substitute for metaphysical certainty, but deeper structural defeaters. For instance, does the argument conflate the epistemic inaccessibility of substantive justice with the impossibility of intersubjective agreement on procedural norms? Might there be a non-cognitive, coordinative account of justice—say, a Schelling-point solution to social coordination—that makes the justice-force union contingent rather than inevitable? And does the view insulate itself by treating any stable order as confirmation that custom has successfully merged with force, and any disorder as proof that justice lacked coercive backing?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Indicate which ones a committed Pascalian could concede without abandoning the core insight, and which would require a fundamental revision. In particular, assess honestly whether modern political realism or Rawlsian reasonable pluralism defeat the argument on its own terms, or merely relocate the problem. Also say whether the position risks unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind. Finally, please state clearly what consideration or argument would change your verdict—whether an empirical case of sustained cooperation without coercive backing, a conceptual distinction I have missed, or a demonstration that the view is immune to evidence—and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
