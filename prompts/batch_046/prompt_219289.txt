I serve on the governance board of a worker-owned engineering cooperative governed by a charter that requires every strategic decision to be justified through explicit reference to our institutional past. After the recent collapse of a major project, the membership has split into three factions. One faction wants to establish a prominent founders' memorial and circulate heroic narratives about the cooperative's early engineers in order to restore morale and collective ambition. A second faction demands the digitization and permanent preservation of every internal memo, failed prototype, and meeting transcript in an uncurated, fully searchable archive, treating exhaustive retention as an ethical duty regardless of present relevance. A third faction is calling for a formal investigation to systematically debunk the cooperative's romanticized origin myth, arguing that only by dismantling these legends can we prevent current leaders from invoking them to suppress criticism and evade accountability. Because our charter binds future contractual obligations to whichever version of the past we formally institutionalize, this choice will structurally constrain our actions for years to come. How should I think about navigating these three impulses? Specifically, is there a way to use our history to strengthen present judgment and solidarity without letting it—whether through inspiration, total preservation, or deliberate demolition—overwhelm or paralyze our capacity to act?
