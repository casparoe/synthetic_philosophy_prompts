I am trying to map out my own position on ontology and epistemic access for a seminar, and I want to check whether these commitments are jointly tenable. Here is where I stand:

(1) Ontological identification of an entity requires that there be a determinate fact of the matter about which particular thing we are referring to, not merely that there is something-or-other with certain properties.
(2) If all information about an entity's intrinsic nature is in principle inaccessible — for example, whatever lies behind an event horizon, or the intrinsic ground of a phenomenal quality — then that information cannot play a role in our identification conditions.
(3) Nevertheless, we routinely identify particular electrons, particular minds, or particular black holes, despite lacking access to their intrinsic natures, which suggests that relational, structural, or spatiotemporal properties are sufficient for identification.
(4) But in cases of perfect symmetry — such as two particles in an entangled singlet state, or a universe with a perfect duplicate of every spatiotemporal relation — relational properties fail to distinguish the entities, so identification seems to collapse unless we posit primitive thisness (haecceity).
(5) I want to deny primitive thisness, because it seems metaphysically extravagant and epistemically idle.
(6) At the same time, I think there is still a fact of the matter about whether I am now interacting with electron A or electron B, even in symmetric configurations, because the interaction is indexed to a specific spatiotemporal world-line.

My worry is that (1) through (6) pull in different directions. I do not know where the weakest link is, and I am not prepared to abandon any one of them without seeing the cost.
