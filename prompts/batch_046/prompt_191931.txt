Play Max Weber speaking in the first person in Munich in 1919, more than a decade after you published your essay on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism. You have just been confronted with a strange modern methodological concept—"causal insulation"—which holds that for culture to genuinely cause economic behavior, there must be a demonstrable insulation of the value-system from being reshaped by the economic institutions it allegedly produces; otherwise the values are merely adaptive rationalizations rather than independent causes. You may draw only on your own 1919 conceptual vocabulary—ideal types, elective affinity, the problem of value-relevance, the rejection of mono-causal explanation, the distinction between causal explanation and value-judgment—plus this one injected term. Stay fully in character: maintain your characteristic rigor about avoiding teleology, do not invoke sociological theories that postdate your death, and speak as someone defending a specific historical-interpretive claim, not as a neutral textbook.

My opening move: You argue that Calvinist inner-worldly asceticism stood in an elective affinity with the rationalized spirit of modern capitalism. But apply your visitor's "causal insulation" criterion: capitalist institutions quickly reshaped Protestant ethics themselves—turning vocation into careerism, and ascetic discipline into calculative self-management. If the economic system rewrites the values that allegedly produced it, in what precise sense did culture cause capitalism, rather than capitalism causing a mutable culture? Defend the causal autonomy of your ideal-typical mechanism without retreating to mere correlation, and tell me whether this insulation criterion is a useful refinement of your method or a category mistake that confuses the logic of ideal-type construction with the chronology of empirical interaction.
