I am preparing the final assessment for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Berkeley and the metaphysics of perception, and I need an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard textbook summary of Berkeley's idealism. I want the question to focus specifically on the master argument—Berkeley's claim that it is impossible to conceive of an object existing unperceived—because students often treat this as a trivial verbal trick rather than confronting its underlying premises about mental content. The exam is a 48-hour take-home essay; students have approximately 1,500 words and this question carries 40 marks. Please write the essay prompt itself, together with a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a detailed marking scheme that distinguishes an A-grade response (first-class) from a B-grade response (upper second) by naming the precise argumentative moves, reconstructions, and critical assessments each must contain.

To keep the question from being a pure recall exercise, please frame it around a reconstructed version of the master argument that distinguishes between thin conception—treating an unperceived object as an abstract logical placeholder with no determinate sensory content—and thick conception—treating it as a fully present idea in the phenomenal field. The question should ask students to reconstruct Berkeley's implicit premise that any thick conception of an unperceived tree necessarily includes the tree as phenomenally present to the conceiver, and therefore that the notion of an unperceived tree collapses into contradiction. It should then require them to evaluate whether allowing thin conception saves the realist: can a realist consistently claim to conceive of a tree existing without anyone perceiving it if that conception is stripped of all sensory determinacy? Students should also address whether Berkeley's reliance on this distinction begs the question against the realist by defining conceivable in terms of phenomenal presence, and they should apply this analysis to a concrete case—say, conceiving of a distant, unobserved star system—to show whether the argument succeeds or fails there. The model answer should not merely summarize Berkeley but should stage the debate between the reconstructed Berkeley and a realist interlocutor, and the marking scheme should penalize answers that stop at labeling the distinction without assessing its modal force, and should reward answers that explicitly connect the thin/thick distinction to the broader question of whether Berkeley has shown that unperceived existence is metaphysically impossible or only epistemically inaccessible.
