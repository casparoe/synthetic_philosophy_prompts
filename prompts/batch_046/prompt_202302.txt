Exam question: Kripkenstein’s sceptical paradox about rule-following is usually presented as a challenge to the idea that any fact—mental, behavioral, or dispositional—determines whether a speaker means addition rather than “quaddition” when applying “+”. The so-called sceptical solution rejects straight answers and instead appeals to conditions of warranted assertibility within a linguistic community. Some interpreters maintain, however, that this solution only succeeds if one can draw a stable distinction between mere dispositional convergence (the statistical tendency of community members to apply a term uniformly) and genuine normative agreement (the shared recognition that such applications are correct or entitled). In approximately four hundred words, address the following. First, explain why, if only dispositional convergence is available, the communal version of the paradox remains intact—that is, why collective behavioral uniformity does not by itself establish a normative standard for correct use. Second, assess whether Kripke’s appeal to communal assertibility conditions genuinely resolves this difficulty, or whether it merely relocates the normative problem from the individual speaker to the collective without accounting for the origin of the entitlement. In your response, be careful to distinguish a straight solution, which accepts that there are meaning-determining facts, from the sceptical solution, which denies them, and avoid identifying Kripke’s position with a standard dispositionalist or reductionist account of meaning.

Student answer: The sceptical paradox shows that no finite mental state or behavioral disposition can determine that I mean plus rather than quus, since any such state is compatible with both interpretations. This threatens not only private language but also communal rule-following, because if the community merely happens to converge on applying “+” in the same way, that convergence is just a larger-scale version of the same dispositional fact. Without an added normative element, the community could all be wrong together, just as the individual could be.

Kripke responds with the sceptical solution: there is no fact about what I mean, but we can still speak of correct and incorrect use because the community sets conditions under which assertions are warranted. If everyone applies “+” to 68 when given 57 and 11, then saying 5 is not assertible, and that is all that “incorrectness” amounts to.

I think, however, that this is essentially a straight solution in disguise. By appealing to the community’s shared dispositions to apply the rule, Kripke is identifying meaning with the collective behavioral pattern. The difference between a straight and a sceptical solution is therefore only that the straight solution looks for facts inside one head, whereas Kripke looks for them distributed across many heads. Once the community’s dispositions are fixed, the norm is established, so the paradox is solved rather than relocated.

The distinction between dispositional convergence and normative agreement is important, but Kripke does not really need it, because assertibility conditions just are the community’s dispositions, now treated as norms. So there is no deeper fact that needs explaining; the normative problem is dissolved, not moved to the group. Thus the sceptical solution works by showing that meaning was never a factual matter to begin with, whether individual or collective.
