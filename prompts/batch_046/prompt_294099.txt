Exam question (20 points):
Some defenders of compatriot priority distinguish between membership goods—benefits whose value depends partly on being shared exclusively within a bounded group, such as democratic voting rights or access to a particular public-education culture—and distributive goods, such as emergency medical care or clean water, which are valuable regardless of exclusivity. In a concise essay, explain how this distinction might allow a moderate position to justify special obligations to fellow citizens without denying that every person possesses equal intrinsic moral worth. Then evaluate whether the distinction can survive the cosmopolitan objection that national boundaries are morally arbitrary. Your answer should address at least one argument that grounds special obligations in shared political institutions (institutional reciprocity) and one that grounds them in shared nationality (associative ties). Finally, identify one internal tension in the moderate view—specifically, whether long-term residents who contribute to institutions and share culture but lack formal citizenship should be included among those to whom membership goods are owed, and what that implies for the view’s stability.

Student answer:
The moderate view separates membership goods, like voting or culturally specific schooling, from distributive goods, like healthcare, so citizens can be prioritized for the first kind without violating equality for the second. Michael Walzer’s associative-nationality argument shows that people form a shared identity that makes special concern natural, just as family loyalty is permissible despite universal human worth. David Miller’s institutional-reciprocity argument adds that democratic citizens share a social contract generating mutual expectations of justice inside the state but not outside it. These fit with cosmopolitanism because Peter Singer’s demand for equal moral worth only requires equal consideration, not identical resource allocation; directing surplus resources inward does not harm outsiders if basic global aid is still provided. The internal tension is that long-term residents work inside the institutions and absorb the culture, so excluding them from membership goods looks arbitrary unless citizenship is purely voluntary. But this does not collapse the view into full cosmopolitanism, because citizens still retain exclusive goods like voting, and equal worth is satisfied as long as we do not actively harm foreigners. Therefore, since no harm is done and surplus allocation is efficient, there is no serious contradiction—only a practical boundary about who counts as a full member.
