Quick question — I keep noticing this pattern in arguments about whether something counts as a real disease, and I'm wondering if it has a name. Take aging, or being very short, or even certain cognitive differences. Someone argues it's not a medical problem, just natural human variation, and medicine shouldn't pathologize it. Then the other side says it involves objective biological dysfunction — the body isn't operating according to its design. The first person replies that "design" already smuggles in a value judgment about how bodies ought to be. So the second person switches to pure statistics: here's the species-typical range, deviation is dysfunction by definition. But then they use that statistical deviation to justify interventions that only make sense if you think being outside the range is genuinely harmful. The biological description and the normative judgment keep trading places, each used to defend the other, but neither can stand alone. It's not quite the standard naturalism vs normativism debate, because both sides claim to be value-free; it's more like a specific fusion where "biologically normal" and "socially desirable" get locked together so tightly you can't seperate them without the argument collapsing. Is there an established concept for this exact loop? And if not, could you coin a precise term with clear boundary cases — for instance, does it apply to mental health categories where the statistical norm is even harder to pin down, or is it mainly a physical-health phenomenon? I don't want just a bare label; I want the carving: what this is, what it isn't, and where the edges are.
