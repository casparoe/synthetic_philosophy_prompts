For a feature on how moral philosophy reads classic fiction, I want a critical interpretation of Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day through care ethics—not the familiar gender-and-labor framing, but a more granular inquiry into whether Stevens’s lifelong service to Lord Darlington and to the household counts as a form of care that the theory can recognize, or whether it instead exemplifies what might be called institutionalized care without reciprocity: disciplined maintenance of others’ lives that systematically suppresses the caregiver’s own dependency, emotional response, and moral judgment. I ask that you take both the novel’s major turning points and the conceptual architecture of care ethics seriously—attentiveness, responsibility, responsiveness, and the moral weight of dependency relations—without reducing either side to an illustration of the other. Focus in particular on Stevens’s refusal to acknowledge his father’s decline, his suppression of feeling for Miss Kenton, and his loyalty to Darlington despite the latter’s fascist sympathies; then consider the novel’s ending, when Stevens admits he may have given too much to service. Does that admission vindicate care ethics by revealing the damage done when a caregiver denies his own needs and emotional reality, or does it expose a gap in the theory—perhaps between caring-for and caring-about—that the philosophy has not adequately mapped? Where does the framework illuminate Stevens’s choices, and where does the text resist it by suggesting that ethical commitment can be expressed through restraint, institutional loyalty, and self-erasure rather than through the openness and mutual recognition that care ethics often privileges? I want an honest critical reading: one that does not treat the novel as a case study, and that concludes by saying what Ishiguro’s portrait of Stevens contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not reach.
