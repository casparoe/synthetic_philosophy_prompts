I'm currently facilitating a comparative-politics workshop for graduate students in Warsaw, and after spending the past year observing election-monitoring reports in Hungary and Poland, I'm trying to formalize my own intuitive judgments about democratic backsliding before I present them to the group. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, which feel consistent to me but resist an obvious common label. First, Hungary's 2011 Fundamental Law and the subsequent restructuring of the Media Council and Constitutional Court through long, staggered appointments: this is democratic backsliding, because an elected parliamentary majority used constitutional and statutory rules to lock in institutional advantages that outlast any single electoral cycle. Second, Poland's 2015-2018 legislative capture of public broadcasting and the creation of a disciplinary chamber for judges staffed through politically controlled procedures: also backsliding, though here the mechanism is ordinary legislative capture rather than constitutional entrenchment. Third, Turkey's post-2016 state of emergency, which removed over a hundred thousand civil servants and academics, followed by the 2017 referendum that centralized executive power: this counts, but I feel it belongs to a different category—more abrupt regime rupture than incremental erosion—so I am unsure whether my concept should cover abrupt collapses or be restricted to gradual hollowing. Fourth, the United States from 2013 onward: the Shelby County decision, the wave of state-level voting-access restrictions, and aggressive partisan gerrymandering clearly degrade democratic quality, yet federal courts remain structurally independent, presidential power transfers occur without violence, and opposition parties win national elections. I oscillate—sometimes this looks like subnational backsliding insulated by national stability, sometimes like ordinary constitutional hardball within a resilient regime. Fifth, a case I keep returning to: in 2023 a democratically elected government in an EU member state used an "administrative efficiency" decree to transfer election-commission oversight from an independent body to a ministry; elections remain genuinely competitive, opposition candidates win regional governorships, and there is no evidence of fraud, yet the institutional buffer has been removed. I lean toward calling this backsliding, but only if my concept permits structural hollowing that stops well short of outright authoritarianism.

What principle, if any, captures this pattern? I need a precise criterion—perhaps involving the combination of structural irreversibility and the retention of competitive electoral form—that accommodates the first, second, and fifth cases, distinguishes the third as a separate subtype, and tells me whether the fourth must be included or excluded. Please state the principle precisely, map each clause onto the case that motivates it, predict its verdict on a hypothetical scenario in which a government maintains fully competitive elections but eliminates all independent audit bodies for campaign finance, and be explicit about which of my five verdicts the principle forces me to abandon. Also tell me whether the concept can survive this kind of refinement without collapsing into a mere partisan label, and identify the feature of democratic backsliding—whether psychological, institutional, or normative—that this case-based exercise is least able to reproduce.
