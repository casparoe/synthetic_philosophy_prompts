I'm working through the problem of hinge propositions, especially as developed in Wittgenstein's On Certainty, for a seminar on the boundaries between framework and empirical belief. Rather than rehearsing the standard catalog—"I have hands," "the earth existed yesterday"—I'm trying to see whether my own intuitive classifications point to a single principle, or whether they force me to accept that hinge-status is irreducibly plural. Here are my verdicts, offered as concrete judgments rather than theoretical commitments. In everyday practical life, "I am not currently dreaming" strikes me as a hinge: to treat it as an open empirical question is not to investigate but to disable the very framework within which investigation occurs. Yet in a clinical sleep-study protocol designed to detect REM intrusion, "this subject is awake at this timestamp" seems genuinely empirical—if the EEG contradicts it, the protocol revises its calibration rather than collapsing. "There are minds other than my own" feels hinge-like for ordinary moral interaction, but in philosophy of mind it is routinely treated as a theoretical problem, and I am genuinely unsure whether that disciplinary framing removes its hinge-status or merely obscures it. I also judge that "the Earth is very old" has become hinge-like for modern historical geology—challenging it does not refine a measurement but threatens the chronological framework itself—whereas "this particular rock is granite" remains plainly empirical, despite both relying on the same sensory and theoretical apparatus. But I feel a parallel tension with arithmetic: "2 + 2 = 4" functions as a hinge within arithmetic practice, yet "this calculator returns 4 for 2 + 2" is clearly empirical, and I am not convinced I have a principled reason for treating the geological pair differently from the arithmetical one.

To organize these judgments, I have been considering a "sedimentation model," which treats hinges as propositions that have become so embedded in a practice that their removal does not merely alter one belief but undermines the conditions for belief-formation within that domain. On this view, sedimentation might be graded rather than binary, and a proposition could be deeply sedimented in one practice—geology—while remaining loose in another, such as a community without scientific institutions. I am especially stuck on a case I have not fully resolved: in a society that has lost all written records, "our ancestors inhabited this continent" might function as a hinge for collective identity and ritual, yet an archaeologist entering that society would treat it as an empirical claim open to carbon-dating and stratigraphic analysis. Does hinge-status depend on an objective feature—immunity to empirical revision without framework collapse—or is it irreducibly relative to the epistemic community and its practices?
