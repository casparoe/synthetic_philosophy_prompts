Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers debating strict tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, with every other condition being at best instrumental. The first speaker should defend this position rigorously: well-being is exhaustively measured by the elimination of craving and suffering, and any state in which both are absent is, all else equal, maximally good. This speaker must directly address the classic objection that such a view makes death or permanent unconsciousness the best possible state, and should do so without simply dismissing it.

The second speaker should oppose tranquilism by arguing that the normative weight of “absence” is not homogeneous but structurally dependent on the presence of capacities that make that absence meaningful. Specifically, this speaker should contend that evaluating the absence of suffering requires implicit reference to the kind of being whose suffering it is—so that tranquilism smuggles positive values (agency, capacity for relation, narrative continuity) into its apparently negative metric. This speaker should also press the point that if absence alone mattered, there would be no normative difference between a life of fulfilled tranquility and a life of nullified consciousness.

Have them engage with a shared hypothetical that you construct for the dialogue: a conscious human subject whose craving-related neural pathways have been permanently and painlessly deactivated, leaving her with intact cognition, memory, and perception but with zero affective valence—no pleasure, no pain, no desire, no aversion. The tranquilist should argue that this subject is in an optimal state of well-being. The opponent should argue that this case reveals tranquilism’s inability to distinguish between a flourishing life and a merely nullified one, and should ask whether the tranquilist can account for the intuition that something has been lost even though nothing negative remains.

Ensure genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the specific argument the other just made, particularly on whether absence can be evaluated without reference to the positive capacities it either preserves or eliminates, and on whether tranquilism can avoid collapsing into the claim that non-existence is best. Neither speaker should be a strawman; both should display familiarity with the conceptual terrain. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
