After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about whether biography can function as genuine moral education when it departs from historical fact, I find myself holding a cluster of judgments I cannot organize into a single view. I want to know whether one principle governs when a life narrative teaches well, teaches badly, or teaches nothing at all—particularly when narrative coherence conflicts with factual completeness.

Here are five cases with my verdicts.

First, Plutarch’s Parallel Lives pairs figures such as Solon and Publicola, suppressing documented vices—Solon’s alleged corruption, Publicola’s political failures—to construct parallel arcs of civic virtue. The result is historically thinner, but I think morally sharper: it isolates a structural tension between private ambition and public duty that a chronicle full of contradiction would dilute. So suppression in service of parallel structure can enhance moral learning.

Second, a twelfth-century hagiographic vita of an obscure provincial bishop invents a miracle in which he embraces a leper, even though episcopal records show he systematically avoided the sick. The vita is used in monastic formation to inculcate compassion. I judge that the invented episode teaches compassion genuinely, and that substituting the documented avoidance would destroy the pedagogical function without supplying any comparable moral insight.

Third, a recent “complete” biography of a major scientific figure spends equal narrative weight on her laboratory discoveries and on her documented cruelty to junior researchers, framing both as expressions of the same “ruthless drive.” Readers report concluding that intellectual excellence morally excuses interpersonal harm. Here factual completeness, combined with a particular framing, produces what I take to be a corrupting moral lesson—one that would not have arisen had the biography either omitted the cruelty or treated it as a separate failure rather than an integrated virtue.

Fourth, a meticulously researched biography of a convicted financial fraudster renders his cognitive biases with such granular sympathy—showing exactly how each decision seemed reasonable from inside—that readers consistently report thinking they “would have done the same,” even though the text never excuses the fraud. The moral failure comes to seem structurally inevitable rather than avoidable. This feels to me like a failure of moral pedagogy, despite being factually impeccable and psychologically rich.

Fifth, two biographies of the same medieval ascetic both record that he routinely beat novices for minor liturgical errors. One, written for canonization, frames this as “holy severity” necessary to save souls from complacency; the other, written for a secular audience, frames it as trauma inflicted by a damaged authority figure. The factual content is identical, but the moral lessons are diametrically opposed. This suggests that the same historical material can bear opposite moral weights depending on framing.

These judgments feel unstable. I am drawn to a tentative principle: biography teaches morality well when it offers a narratively coherent exemplar of a virtue or vice that the reader can recognize and either emulate or reject, and this coherence often requires either selective omission or explicit normative framing; but when coherence is achieved by integrating moral failures into the same narrative structure as achievements—presenting harm as the inevitable shadow of excellence—the result is not exemplarity but moral confusion.

But this principle seems to save the first two cases only by licensing invention and suppression, while it condemns the third; it struggles with the fourth, since that biography is also coherent, yet I reject it; and the fifth case shows that coherence can be achieved in opposite directions with the same facts, which my principle does not resolve.

So my questions: Is there a more precise principle—perhaps distinguishing between coherence achieved through normative framing versus coherence achieved through psychological reduction—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what should the resulting principle predict for a case I have not listed—say, a biography of a political revolutionary that is entirely factually accurate but written in a deliberately flat, affectless style that refuses any normative framing of either his sacrifices or his atrocities?

Please state your principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, give its verdict on the flat-style revolutionary biography, and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.
