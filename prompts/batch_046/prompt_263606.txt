Since my own professional association abruptly retired a ceremonial practice that had structured our meetings for over a century, and since I found myself resisting the change not on practical grounds but with a sense that something like shared dignity had been lost, I have come to hold the view that moral norms such as slavery or dueling end not primarily through rational argument or through shifting material interests, but through a collapse in the honor structure that made adherence to them a condition of self-respect. I understand this as aligning with Appiah’s emphasis on honor in moral revolutions and with what I take to be the Williams thesis about thick ethical concepts losing their grip when the social world that sustains them erodes. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this position. I am not asking for the familiar objections that abolitionists eventually won the argument or that dueling became economically irrational, but for deeper defeaters: which well-known historical episodes show norms dissolving without any corresponding crisis in honor, which conceptual distinctions would have to collapse for the honor-centered story to reduce in disguise to an interest-based one, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by treating every apparent rational victory as secretly an honor shift. I also want to know what a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as damaging, rather than only what critics claim, and whether the episode in my own association—where the practice vanished almost overnight with no one expressing dishonor, only mild inconvenience—should genuinely move me or can be accommodated by refining the relevant notion of honor.
