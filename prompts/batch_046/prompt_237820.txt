After a long argument with a friend about Dennett’s four kinds of minds, she settled on a specific pattern of verdicts: bacterial chemotaxis is Darwinian; a pixel-level reinforcement-learning agent is Skinnerian; a planner that simulates future trajectories before committing to action is Popperian; and a mathematician using symbol systems transmitted across generations is Gregorian. She also classifies a crow fashioning a hook from wire as Gregorian, but a crow solving an immediate puzzle through trial-and-error as Skinnerian, and insists that a large language model trained by next-token prediction remains Skinnerian because it never runs an internal world-model to test actions before emitting them—only its weights are shaped by feedback.

The case we could not settle is an artificial system that maintains a library of generative causal models, runs each forward in simulation against incoming sensory data, selects the most coherent narrative, executes a single action, and then revises the library based on the outcome—without any evolutionary history, without a reward signal during inference, and without shared cultural symbols. Would she place it with the Popperians, the Gregorians, or outside the taxonomy?

I am less interested in the label than in the rule she is actually running. Is the dividing line decoupled representation, the absence of direct reinforcement, the capacity for cultural transmission, or something like the separation of generation and evaluation? What are the two or three alternative rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well? And which single further case—say, a human infant with no linguistic exposure solving a physical puzzle, or a hive coordinating purely through pheromonal gradients—would best reveal whether she is tracking representational decoupling, social transmission, or something else?
