I'm a retired biologist who got into philosophy books after leaving the lab, and I'm trying to sort out my gut feelings about being blunt versus polite, especially with white lies and cross-cultural directness. Here are my verdicts, mostly consistent but not all:

- A Dutch PI telling a postdoc this data is garbage, redo it in front of everyone: definately not wrong, maybe even a duty.
- A Japanese colleague writing this is an interesting direction, perhaps we can revisit the timeline when she means the collaboration is dead: fine, that's just the code.
- Telling my spouse her painting is beautiful when I think it's ugly: fine, pure kindness.
- Telling a friend their startup idea is brilliant when I think it's doomed: not fine, even though it feels like the painting case.
- Telling a dying patient who asks that their estranged son called, when he hasn't: not fine, despite the comfort.
- Writing in a confidential grant review that a rival's study is groundbreaking when I think it's mediocre: not fine, closer to lying for gain.
- A doctor saying straight up you have six months without softening: I keep going back and forth. Sometimes it feels like respecting autonomy, sometimes like unnecessary cruelty.
- Refusing a dinner invite in Japan by saying it might be a bit inconvenient instead of no: that's fine.
- But telling a grad student your hypothesis is stupid in a one-on-one meeting: not fine, unlike the Dutch PI case, even though both are about scientific truth.

I tried framing this with truth-cost versus face-cost — whether the harm avoided is to knowledge or just social discomfort — but that makes the doctor case weird and doesn't seperate the two student cases well. Is there a sharper principle that saves most of my calls, and which one has to go? Also, what should it say about my back-and-forth case — direct prognosis delivery? And does it change anything if the bluntness occures in a culture where it's expected?
