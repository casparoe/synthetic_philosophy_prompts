I need a polished, formal two-page proposal on the following conceptual problem: are the historiographical categories Middle Ages, Renaissance, and Scientific Revolution best understood as real macro-historical entities, as pure retrospective inventions, or—this is the angle I want developed—as something analogous to legacy software interfaces that persist primarily for backward compatibility with existing scholarship, archival taxonomies, and digital humanities metadata standards rather than because they map cleanly onto historical reality?

Please structure the proposal with the following elements, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation requests—I will supply references myself:

1. A precise statement of the philosophical question, framed so that it is not reducible to the standard realist-versus-constructivist debate about periodization.

2. An explanation of why the existing positions—broadly, that these periods are either discovered or invented—leave the legacy-interface option open, and why that option matters for how we evaluate historical classification in practice.

3. The proposed method: since I am not a trained historian, the approach should be conceptual and comparative rather than archival. Specify what I would actually do month by month, for example by analyzing how digital humanities classification schemes inherit, deprecate, or fork these three categories, and comparing that structural behavior to how historians write about discontinuity versus continuity. Explain why this conceptual-comparative approach is preferable to a purely empirical historical study or to a purely linguistic analysis of period terms.

4. A clear account of what a successful result would look like—perhaps a framework for evaluating when a historical category functions as a live ontological claim versus a maintained legacy dependency—and what would count as the project failing, such as if the analogy collapses back into one of the two standard positions without generating new distinctions.

5. An honest assessment of scope: what makes this tractable for part-time work over one semester by someone without formal training, and what specific risks might make it too large, for instance being pulled into primary-source historiography or technical database architecture.

The tone should be formal and appropriate for a grant review panel, but the conceptual innovation—the software-engineering analogy applied to historiographical ontology—should come through clearly. Do not include requests for citations or source verification.
