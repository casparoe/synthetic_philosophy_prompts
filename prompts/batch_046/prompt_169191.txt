I'm delivering the opening remarks for a one-day workshop called "Locked In? Path Dependence and Civic Architecture." The audience is split: urban historians who study how 19th-century sewer and rail grids shaped modern neighborhood segregation, and philosophers of history who think about contingency, convergence, and counterfactual stability. I want the remarks—about seven minutes—to do real structural work with the idea of path dependence rather than use it as decoration. In particular, please frame the day around a distinction I want to test: "hard path dependence," where reversing an initial condition is physically or socially impossible, versus "soft path dependence," where reversal is technically possible but the costs are distributed so unevenly that they become politically invisible. Use that distinction to shape what I can and can't claim about institutional reform: should recognizing path dependence make us humbler about gradual change, or bolder about engineered rupture? The remarks should acknowledge that both sides already know the standard examples—keyboard layouts, rail gauges—but move quickly past them to ask whether a city that rebuilds its water system after a catastrophic flood is still "the same city" in any meaningful institutional sense. Don't quote anyone verbatim; don't provide a bibliography, reading list, or specific paper citations. Write so that both audiences feel addressed, the philosophers don't feel patronized, and the historians don't feel lectured. End by posing one genuinely open question that the day's panels should argue over, not resolve.
