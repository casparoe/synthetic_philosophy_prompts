I am currently taking an upper-division seminar in moral psychology and comparative ethics, and we have spent the past several weeks interrogating whether guilt and shame are best understood as universal affective responses or as culturally malleable technologies of moral correction. Our instructor asked us to move beyond the standard anthropological and philosophical framings we have already surveyed and instead construct a speculative scenario: imagine a post-national polity—call it the Affective Commonwealth—that has deliberately engineered its pedagogical, juridical, and architectural institutions to cultivate only one of these emotions as its primary mechanism of social regulation. In this society, citizens do not experience both affects; they are neurologically and socially conditioned from childhood to respond to moral failure through either an internalized obligation-to-repair structure characteristic of guilt, or an externalized status-degradation structure characteristic of shame, but never both simultaneously. The Commonwealth is now facing a constitutional referendum on whether to preserve this emotional monoculture or to permit hybrid affective development, and the debate has divided educators, jurists, and cognitive architects. I would like you to write a short story—perhaps narrated from the perspective of a dissenting child-development auditor or a ritual-design architect—set within the concrete daily life of this society. The narrative should explore what it feels like to live under such an engineered moral emotion, and it should implicitly engage the question of whether a culture can legitimately choose its dominant moral affect, or whether the forced separation of guilt and shame strips moral agency of something essential. Please avoid retelling familiar philosophical parables; instead, ground the story in the specific rituals, spatial designs, or bureaucratic procedures through which this emotional regime is maintained.
