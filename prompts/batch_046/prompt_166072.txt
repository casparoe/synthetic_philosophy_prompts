For a graduate seminar in political ethics, construct a decision tree that classifies substantive positions on the problem of dirty hands—specifically, whether and under what conditions a political agent is morally permitted or required to commit acts that are gravely wrong by ordinary moral standards in order to avert catastrophe. The root node should state the minimal commitment: "Political agents can face situations in which they ought to do what is ordinarily morally forbidden." From there, build branches using only concrete normative questions that split positions regardless of their historical labels: (1) Is political morality genuinely autonomous from private morality, or is it the same morality applied under extreme pressure? (2) After committing the wrong, does the agent necessarily bear a moral remainder, stain, or corruption that demands acknowledgment or atonement? (3) Must the dirty act be publicly acknowledged, or may it be concealed if disclosure would destabilize the polity? (4) Can well-designed institutional safeguards—collective authorization, sunset provisions, judicial oversight—distribute or sanitize responsibility so that no single individual bears dirty hands? (5) Is there any category of act that remains categorically impermissible regardless of the political stakes? Each terminal leaf must carry the name of the view (or "unnamed" if the logical combination is unoccupied) and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. You must locate at least these six positions in the tree: Walzer's tragic-view account; Weber's ethic-of-responsibility position; a Sartrean existentialist account emphasizing radical choice and irreducible guilt; a strict deontological prohibitionist stance; an Aristotelian or Thomistic virtue-ethics view focused on moral corruption of character; and an institutional-design view that denies that individuals ever truly have dirty hands. For any branch combination that yields no named occupant, label it "unnamed" and diagnose in one sentence whether the vacancy is best explained by incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere theoretical neglect. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that your chosen branch questions fail to separate. Every branch node must be phrased as a question answerable by someone who does not already know which interpretation it produces, so that the instrument sorts respondents by their beliefs rather than by their familiarity with labels. Limit the tree to no more than sixteen leaves and present it in nested outline form.
