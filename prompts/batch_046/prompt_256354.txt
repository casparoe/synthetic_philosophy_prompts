As a journalist who writes about the intersection of moral philosophy and public-policy evaluation, I am preparing a seminar presentation for next Tuesday’s interdisciplinary working group on welfare metrics — the room will include three welfare economists committed to preference-satisfaction frameworks, two scholars of Theravada Buddhist ethics who are skeptical of secularized interpretations of classical doctrines, and several applied ethicists whose research focuses on chronic pain, disability justice, and mental-health policy. The thesis I intend to defend is a modified tranquilist position tailored specifically to institutional evaluation: I argue that when we assess whether a social policy has genuinely improved collective well-being, only the absence of active craving and the reduction of present suffering should register as positive moral goods, whereas satisfied preferences, positive affect, and the fulfillment of long-term life projects ought to be treated as ethically neutral — not harmful, but not contributing to the moral score of the intervention. I ground this not in metaphysical pessimism but in a phenomenological asymmetry between need and satisfaction, in which unfulfilled craving carries a negative valence that satisfied desire does not mirror positively, and I extend the framework specifically to intergenerational accounting, where anticipatory craving is structurally more persistent and harder to eliminate through policy than present suffering is.

Because my audience is precisely this mix, I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a general philosophical defense: please list the objections I will actually face, ordered first by how likely they are to be raised and then by how damaging they would be to my central claim, with each objection phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework that audience member would actually use — the economists invoking revealed preference, utility functions, and the risk of paternalism; the Buddhist scholars invoking tanha, dukkha, and the distinction between mundane satisfaction and liberative cessation; the applied ethicists invoking adaptive preferences, disability flourishing, and the political danger of defining well-being negatively. For each objection, provide a concise reply suited to a two-minute speaking window, followed by an explicit note on where that reply is weakest and whether I should concede a point or hold firm. I also want included the specific objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether my framework can distinguish between the elimination of craving through genuine satisfaction versus through the suppression of agency or the lowering of expectations, and whether this collapses my position into the very adaptive-preference critique that welfare economists level against subjective utility measures.
