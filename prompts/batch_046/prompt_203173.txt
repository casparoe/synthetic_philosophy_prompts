I hold a view that tries to move past the Browning-Goldhagen debate about Reserve Police Battalion 101: neither situational coercion nor deep cultural antisemitism fully explains sustained perpetration, but rather a "moral economy of obedience," in which perpetrators treated compliance as a kind of group-credit exchangeable for belonging and eventual moral redemption. On this account, the standard situation-versus-character framing misses the actual mechanism. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters mapped by severity: direct motivational evidence from perpetrator testimony or postwar trials; conceptual failures of applying economic-exchange metaphors to moral psychology; comparative results from other perpetrator groups (different conflicts, regimes, or organizational structures) that either replicate or fracture the pattern; and whether the framework has been insulated against counterevidence by redefining every deviation as a different "currency" within the same economy. For each, indicate whether defenders of the view would concede damage or dismiss it, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward.
