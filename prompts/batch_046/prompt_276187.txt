I have been working through the historiography of political progress for an advanced essay, and a fellow student in my seminar offered a summary of Herbert Butterfield’s well-known critique of Whig history that I want to check for accuracy before I rely on it. She characterized Whig history as essentially any narrative that treats the present liberal-democratic constitutional order as the inevitable endpoint of past struggles, so that historians judge past actors solely by whether they advanced or resisted that modern outcome. In her account, Butterfield argued in The Whig Interpretation of History that this approach is not merely biased but logically circular: it assumes the very outcome it claims to explain, imports present-day moral categories to classify agents as progressive or reactionary, and thereby renders genuine causal explanation impossible because it implicitly denies that alternative institutional paths were ever causally open—if democratic consolidation was destined to triumph, then asking what might have occurred had earlier parliamentary coalitions collapsed becomes a meaningless counterfactual exercise. She added that Butterfield directed this critique mainly at nineteenth-century British historians writing about the Glorious Revolution, and that the proper remedy is to adopt a strictly chronological narrative that makes no reference whatsoever to present institutions or values. I am inclined to think she has captured the spirit of Butterfield’s warning about present-centeredness, but I worry she has smuggled in some philosophical machinery—specifically the claim about counterfactual closure and causal openness—that sounds more like a contemporary philosophy-of-science discussion about modal realism than like Butterfield’s own historiographical concerns. Could you audit her summary sentence by sentence? For each claim, tell me what a careful reader of Butterfield or a historian of historiography would accept, what they would wince at or correct, and whether the injected idea about ruling out counterfactual alternatives actually belongs to the critique. Also, does her conclusion about the remedy—complete abstention from any present reference—follow from Butterfield’s actual position, or is it an overcorrection that misrepresents the limits he placed on the historian?
