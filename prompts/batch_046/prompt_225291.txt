I am preparing an essay for a course on comparative historical methodology, and I keep returning to the logical structure of contrastive macrohistorical questions — in particular, the familiar framing "why Britain and not China?" that animates so much Great Divergence scholarship. Rather than asking for another empirical account of the Needham question, I want to isolate a conceptual difficulty I have begun to call baseline contingency asymmetry: the implicit treatment of one trajectory (British industrialisation) as radically contingent and therefore demanding explanation, while the other trajectory (Chinese continuity or non-industrialisation) is treated as structurally fixed or inevitable, despite both being equally path-dependent outcomes. Could you provide a precise example of a conceptual issue — not a full historical narrative — in which this asymmetry makes the contrastive question appear well-posed when it is actually presupposing an unjustified modal distinction? I would like the example to show exactly where the framing smuggles in the assumption that only one side of the comparison requires explanatory accounting, and to indicate why that undermines the coherence of the contrast itself. Please respond in British English, and do not provide citations, reading lists, or references to specific authors; the task is purely conceptual.
