In a seminar on Nietzsche's essay concerning the uses and disadvantages of history for life, a participant kept repeating the line "history is for life, not life for history" to dismiss two practices she treated as equivalent: the antiquarian hoarding of archives that present communities no longer actively use, and the critical demolition of monuments that still structure collective identity. She claimed both make living present action serve a dead past, but I think the slogan is hiding distinctions that Nietzsche's three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—actually keep apart. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing: for each, specify what kind of "service" is meant, whose "life" is being served, whether the claim is normative or descriptive, and how it treats each of the three historical modes. Say for each reading whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate precisely which of her two dismissals—antiquarian preservation or critical destruction—it would actually support and which it would not. Then identify the reading she is most likely relying on, explain why it collapses the two cases she wants to reject together, and say what a more precise version of the slogan would need to add to distinguish them.
