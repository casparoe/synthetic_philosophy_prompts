I'm preparing material for a philosophical dataset and was recently in a seminar discussion about whether moral status is genuinely graded or merely binary with indistinct borders. A participant advanced an analogy that she took to be common in discussions of animal ethics: she compared the mental lives of organisms to thermostats arranged along a continuum of functional complexity. At the simplest end, a basic thermostat operates through a single binary feedback loop—no interior representation, no sustained integration—much as some critics describe an insect's nociceptive response as a coupled reflex arc without an "inner screen." At the middle, an insect becomes analogous to a thermostat wired to dozens of coupled sensors and actuators, maintaining homeostasis across multiple dimensions simultaneously, yet still lacking any model of itself within the environment it regulates. At the complex end, a mammal (and especially a human) resembles a thermostat that has constructed an internal model of the room it regulates, including itself as one object within that model, thereby generating what we call subjective experience. From this she concluded that moral consideration should scale continuously with this "model-complexity gradient": insects merit some minimal weight, mammals far more, and treating all organisms as equal moral subjects is like insisting that every thermostat, however simple, must be granted the rights of a conscious agent.

Please audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the sensor array, the feedback loop, the internal model, and the assigned moral weight at each stage. Second, identify where the mapping breaks: does "interiority" or "model-complexity" actually track the properties that ground moral status, or does the analogy quietly import a functionalist assumption that sentience is nothing more than sophisticated information-processing? Third, examine whether the analogy smuggles in the idea that false positives in attribution are harmless—thermostats do not suffer from being misidentified as conscious, whereas organisms might suffer precisely from being denied consideration they actually need. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that moral status is continuous and proportional to representational complexity—survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses, suggest whether a different source domain—perhaps artistic representation, legal personhood, or acoustic resonance—would provide a more defensible framework for gradated moral status, and explain what that alternative gains and loses.
