In popular and scholarly discussion of societal decline, a common analogy treats “collapse” as comparable to the failure of a building or bridge: load-bearing structures give way, the integrated whole comes down suddenly, and what remains is rubble rather than a modified architecture. This image is used to group the Late Bronze Age disruptions of centralized palace economies, the Classic Maya lowland abandonment, and the cessation of Easter Island’s monumental statue construction as instances of the same phenomenon—sudden, systemic structural failure rather than gradual transformation. Audit the analogy: map what corresponds to the foundation, the load-bearing members, the triggering stress, and the rubble for each case; then identify where the mapping fractures, noting, for instance, that the Bronze Age pattern looks more like a distributed trade-network dissolution with regional political continuity, that Maya abandonment was geographically patchy and accompanied by political reconfiguration in other regions, and that Easter Island may represent ecological contraction with social simplification rather than total structural failure. Does the analogy covertly assume that social complexity is a single integrated edifice rather than a distributed network? And if these disanalogies are taken seriously, does “collapse” survive as a coherent unified category, or should we treat these events as distinct modes of societal change—partial failure in some domains, transformation in others?
