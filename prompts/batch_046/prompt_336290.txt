I am preparing the defense of a seminar paper—eventually intended for the job market—tentatively titled Institutional Embarrassment and the Collapse of Honor Worlds: A Structural Account of Moral Revolution. My central thesis is that practices such as dueling and chattel slavery did not disappear primarily through moral argumentation, nor through the straightforward triumph of economic interest, but through a structural shift I call institutional embarrassment: the point at which the institutions that had sustained a practice—military hierarchies, plantation economies, legal arbitration—began to treat it not merely as costly but as a status liability that threatened their own legitimacy. I use Appiah’s framework of honor worlds to explain why these practices were once mandatory markers of elite status, but I argue that Appiah misses the mediating mechanism of collapse—how honor worlds unravel when their institutional carriers defect, not out of moral enlightenment but out of reputational self-preservation. Against Bernard Williams’s skeptical claim that moral revolutions are rarely driven by reasoned argument, I contend that he underestimates the role of status anxiety in suddenly rendering a practice vulgar rather than noble, though I concede that my account must show this mechanism is not reducible to either naked interest or moral reasoning.

The audience is a ninety-minute research seminar in moral philosophy and intellectual history: three moral philosophers who are broadly sympathetic to Appiah’s honor-world model and to narratives of moral progress; two political theorists with Williamsian commitments about the limits of moral reasoning in politics; and a social historian of the nineteenth century who regards “honor” explanations as post-hoc cultural glosses on material change. There will be a formal respondent followed by open Q&A, and the room is skeptical of synthetic explanations that borrow from both cultural and economic history without reducing to either.
