I am drafting a scene for a novel in which a retired philosopher of mind, now a beekeeper on a fog-bound coast, attempts to teach her fifteen-year-old granddaughter—who has come to stay after her father’s death and who reads popular science with an instinctive humanism—the concept of competence without comprehension, precisely through the paired inversions of Darwin and Turing. The girl arrives convinced that doing requires prior understanding and that any machine displaying intelligence must conceal a hidden comprehending agent; I need the pedagogical architecture for their conversation, not the dialogue itself. Design the sequence in which the dependencies must unfold: Darwin’s inversion, in which selection generates design without foresight, must be secured before Turing’s inversion, in which behavioral indistinguishability can precede and even exist without internal comprehension, yet the second must not collapse into a mere technological analogy of the first. Choose one concrete carrying example—something tangible, not abstract, that fails if either inversion is absent—that can bear both weights simultaneously. Map the misconceptions she will form at each stage: that natural selection is only “random mutation plus time,” that Turing’s test concerns deceiving observers rather than relocating the burden of proof, and that competence without comprehension describes a deficiency rather than a structural feature of systems. Indicate the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved. Provide one diagnostic question the beekeeper could ask at the end that reveals whether the granddaughter grasps that comprehension is not the hidden substrate of competence but a separate, sometimes absent, layer altogether. Finally, specify which true but destabilizing element of the argument should be deliberately left unspoken in this scene—something that, if introduced too early, would collapse the inversion before it has been built. Keep the tone formal, as though addressing a curriculum designer who understands that a teaching plan is itself a kind of narrative structure.
