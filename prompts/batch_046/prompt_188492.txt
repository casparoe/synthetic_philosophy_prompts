I am preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar session on democratic theory for students who have already encountered standard arguments about democratic legitimacy and inclusion but have not yet worked through the boundary problem outside a state-centric framework. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture itself—showing how to explain why the question of who belongs to the demos is irreducibly normative rather than administrative or geographical. Specify the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before introducing the all-affected-interests principle, which before the all-subject-to-coercion principle, and why territorial jurisdiction should be treated as a competing baseline rather than a neutral default. For the carrying scenario, evaluate whether a transboundary river-basin authority—comprising upstream agricultural users, downstream urban residents, and a displaced indigenous community, each advancing different bases for democratic standing—successfully separates affectedness from coercion, or propose a sharper hypothetical that forces the distinction without collapsing into stakeholder management. Map the three predictable misconceptions: that democratic membership is equivalent to having a stake in outcomes, that coercion requires direct physical enforcement, and that expanding the demos necessarily increases democratic legitimacy; indicate the precise moment in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and how. Design one closing diagnostic question that distinguishes a student who grasps that the boundary problem involves incompatible normative principles from one who still treats it as an empirical puzzle about drawing correct lines. Finally, identify what institutional material—such as specific voting mechanisms for transnational assemblies or historical treaty provisions—should be explicitly excluded and set aside, explaining how that omission protects the conceptual clarity of the session. Do not assign readings or provide bibliographic references.
