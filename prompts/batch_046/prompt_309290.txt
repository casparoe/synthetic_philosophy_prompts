I'm drafting an essay for my history-of-ideas course on whether cyclical models of history can account for particular events or only for grand patterns. I constructed the following argument, but its conclusion—that cyclical theories make specific historical explanation impossible—seems stronger than I intended. Here are the steps:

(1) The major cyclical frameworks—Polybius's constitutional anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's decay of group feeling across generations, Spengler's morphological transition from culture to civilization, and Toynbee's breakdown of creative response—each assert that large-scale historical patterns recur with structural regularity.

(2) If a pattern is genuinely structurally regular, then its recurrence cannot depend on unrepeatable contingent conditions; otherwise the regularity would be accidental rather than law-like.

(3) Therefore, cyclical theories must treat contingent conditions—specific geography, individual decisions, technological accidents—as causally inert at the macro-historical level.

(4) But if contingent conditions are causally inert, then explaining particular events—why the Western Roman administrative structure collapsed in the fifth century rather than the fourth, or why Soviet institutional failure manifested in 1991 rather than 1985—is impossible in principle. Only abstract cyclical stages remain available as explanations.

(5) Consequently, cyclical theories either abandon the explanation of particular events or inconsistently reintroduce contingency without theoretical warrant.

(6) Linear-progress models, by contrast, allow contingent conditions to accumulate and alter trajectories, preserving both pattern-recognition and particular explanation.

(7) Hence cyclical theories should be rejected in favor of linear-progress models.

I'd prefer to keep (1) and (6), and I particularly want to preserve the claim that cyclical theories systematically downplay contingency. But I'm unsure whether the flaw lies in the inference from (2) to (3)—perhaps an equivocation between 'structural regularity' and 'causal independence'—or whether the argument is valid and I must choose between giving up (1) or accepting (7). Could you identify the problematic step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price what each alternative costs?
