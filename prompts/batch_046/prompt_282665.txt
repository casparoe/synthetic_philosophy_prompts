I am a high school senior writing an independent study paper for my philosophy of mind seminar, and I have become preoccupied with a specific variant of the absent-minded driver problem that my instructor mentioned only briefly. Rather than the familiar case of a driver who reaches home with no memory of the usual route, I want to examine a more demanding scenario: imagine a driver returning along a path she has taken hundreds of times, except that overnight construction has blocked her customary left turn onto Maple Street and replaced it with an unlit, temporary detour through a residential loop marked by an unexpected stop sign and an unmarked merge back onto the main road. She navigates the detour smoothly—braking, signaling, adjusting speed—yet upon arrival she has no episodic trace of the deviation and is genuinely surprised to learn the usual turn was closed. The conceptual question is whether her adaptive navigation through this novel obstacle counts as a full-blooded intentional action, and, if it does, what kind of mental accompaniment, if any, is necessary for that status.

To explore this, I would like you to write a dialogue between two unnamed philosophers of action—call them the Continuity theorist and the Authorization theorist—who disagree precisely over this detour case. The Continuity theorist should defend the view that intentional agency requires only an uninterrupted perceptuo-motor control loop integrating perception, prediction, and adaptive motor response; on this account, the driver’s correct handling of the stop sign and merge demonstrates genuine intentionality even though no narrative trace is available for later reflection. The Authorization theorist should defend the view that an action achieves full normative status as intentional only when the agent can, at least in principle, integrate it into her self-model through retrospective endorsement; on this account, the detour navigation is sophisticated behavioral autopilot—functionally equivalent to sleepwalking or reflexive skill—rather than genuinely intentional action, because it is permanently excluded from the agent’s authorial perspective. Both interlocutors should be equally sharp and well-versed in the philosophy of action, neither a strawman: the Continuity theorist must directly address the objection that without retrospective access there is no principled boundary between skilled driving and unconscious automatism, and the Authorization theorist must directly address the objection that demanding narrative integration implausibly strips intentionality from ordinary expert performances, such as a pianist executing a well-rehearsed phrase without explicit self-monitoring. The exchange should be a real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues, with each figure conceding minor clarifications but holding firm on the core disagreement.

Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or references to specific historical texts; I am seeking conceptual precision rather than scholarly attribution. Keep the dialogue formal and rigorous, and make sure the detour scenario—complete with the unexpected stop sign, the residential loop, and the driver’s subsequent surprise—remains the concrete focal point throughout.
