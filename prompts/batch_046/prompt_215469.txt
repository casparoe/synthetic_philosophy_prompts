As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a systematic way, I have become absorbed in Nietzsche's tripartite distinction—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—regarding the uses and disadvantages of history for life. I wish to apply this framework not to culture or politics in the usual manner, but to the internal historiography of the natural sciences: specifically, to the institutional management of obsolete experimental apparatus, superseded measurement protocols, and the archival records of experiments that failed to replicate or that once supported theories now abandoned. Please describe the most important implications of this scheme for how scientific institutions, museum collections, and graduate curricula ought to relate to their own past. Which mode should govern the preservation of old laboratory notebooks? Which should shape the narrative of progress presented in physics textbooks? And where does the critical mode become not merely useful but ethically necessary, preventing the antiquarian accumulation of dead data from stifling present inquiry? I am particularly concerned with the tension between the monumental need for heroic narratives of discovery and the critical need to expose the errors embedded within those same narratives, and with how a practicing scientist—rather than a professional historian—should navigate this tension when mentoring students.
