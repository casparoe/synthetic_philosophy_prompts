As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to the philosophy of history, I have come to defend a specific stance on presentism. I hold that historians need not be prisoners of their own epoch's normative and conceptual standards, provided they treat presentist commitments as correctable systematic errors rather than as inescapable hermeneutic conditions—analogous to calibrating a measuring instrument against known biases by cross-temporal source triangulation and explicit normative bracketing. On this view, anachronism is not a constitutive feature of understanding the past but a quantifiable distortion that disciplined methodological correction can substantially reduce. What would genuinely count against this position? Please go beyond the familiar claim that all observation is theory-laden: identify which concrete cases of apparently irreducible presentism in actual historiographical practice, which findings in the philosophy of science about theory-ladenness and conceptual incommensurability, and which hermeneutic arguments regarding the fusion of horizons would be fatal rather than merely awkward. Rank these potential defeaters accordingly, and for each indicate whether a committed defender of this calibration model should concede it as damaging or can legitimately dismiss it. Finally, state explicitly what argument or evidence would change your own assessment—where your reasoning is most vulnerable, and which consideration, if strengthened, would lead you to abandon or substantially revise the conclusion that presentism can be methodologically calibrated away.
