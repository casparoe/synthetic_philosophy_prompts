John von Neumann, in his posthumous lectures comparing the brain and the computer, maintained that neural computation is neither purely digital nor purely analog, but operates through statistical redundancy, imprecise logical steps, and an organizational language likely distinct from that of electronic machines built on Boolean logic. He was skeptical that the brain could be understood as running a discrete program on fixed hardware, emphasizing instead its tolerance for component failure and its use of probabilistic rather than deterministic error correction.

Decades after his death, a specific conceptual framing emerged in computational neuroscience: the idea that large regions of cortex function as dynamical reservoirs—recurrent, spiking networks in which information is encoded not in individual unit activations but in high-dimensional, continuous-time trajectories, and in which local synaptic plasticity rules shape dynamics without any global error signal comparable to backpropagation. On this view, behavior is produced by linear readouts from these reservoir states, and robustness derives from distributed, redundant dynamics rather than from precisely engineered redundant circuits.

Assume von Neumann was presented with this reservoir-computation framing of cortical function, together with the associated claim that the brain therefore lacks the layered, algorithmic architecture characteristic of the machines he designed. How would he likely have responded?

Address the following in your answer:
1. Would he have interpreted reservoir dynamics and local, non-global learning rules as vindicating his claim that the brain employs statistical, analog-style computation, or as contradicting his expectation that neural processes can ultimately be translated into a formal logical language?
2. Given his work on self-reproducing automata and on achieving reliability despite unreliable components, would he have seen distributed, high-dimensional representation as a deeper realization of his redundancy principle, or as an abandonment of the structured error-correction he considered essential to computation?
3. To what extent would he have regarded the absence of backpropagation and the presence of continuous-time dynamics as marking a fundamental, irreducible architectural gap between brain and computer—rather than as an engineering difference that neuromorphic hardware might eventually bridge?

In responding, map his specific commitments—his hybrid analog-digital thesis, his distinction between logical precision and statistical approximation, and his views on self-replication and component failure—onto the modern reservoir framework, and identify precisely where the analogy holds, where it fails, and what it implies about the possibility of brain-like artificial computation.
