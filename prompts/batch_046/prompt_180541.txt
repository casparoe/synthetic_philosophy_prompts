I'm working through the anti-aggregationist position associated with Taurek—roughly, that the number of people in danger does not multiply the moral reason to save them—and I have been trying to see whether it holds up once risk and probability are introduced. In discussion with a colleague, I constructed an argument that extends that intuition into probabilistic rescue scenarios, but the result seems to prove far more than I want to accept: not merely that numbers don't count, but that a rational rescuer should systematically prefer high-probability single rescues over lower-probability mass rescues, and that this preference is morally correct rather than pragmatically regrettable.

Here is the argument in steps. (1) If moral reasons are not aggregative, then n people facing danger do not generate n times the reason to intervene; each person possesses an equal, non-cumulative claim to rescue. (2) Consider Mission L, which can save 100 people with 30% success probability, and Mission S, which can save 1 person with 95% success probability; the expected lives saved are 30 and 0.95, respectively. (3) To choose between L and S, compare the claim of any single individual in L to the claim of the individual in S. Because the claims are equal in strength and do not sum, there is no stronger moral reason to undertake L. (4) Therefore, the agent should select S, or at least is not morally required to select L, regardless of the disparity in expected outcomes. (5) By repetition of this pairwise logic, whenever a single high-probability rescue competes with a multi-person lower-probability rescue, the same reasoning applies: scale enters only through probability per person, not through the number of persons. (6) Over a sequence of decisions, this yields a policy that is ex ante guaranteed to preserve far fewer lives than a policy that occasionally accepts lower per-capita probabilities for larger groups. (7) Consequently, accepting non-aggregation in probabilistic contexts commits one to a rescue strategy that predictably minimizes lives saved, and this is not a defect of the strategy but its correct moral expression. I feel that something slips between steps (3) and (5)—perhaps an illicit generalization from pairwise comparison to a standing decision rule, or a confusion between the strength of a claim and the distributive structure of risk—but I cannot isolate the error.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, is the mistake in treating risk as external to the moral weight of a claim, in inferring a general policy from a single pairwise comparison, or in failing to recognize that probabilistic rescue creates disjunctive obligations rather than aggregated ones? Please say what would change your mind—whether a formal model of distributive moral risk, a clear distinction between ex post and ex ante moral evaluation, or an argument that expected scale is itself a non-aggregative consideration—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I want a direct structural diagnosis, not a bibliography or a survey of the literature.
