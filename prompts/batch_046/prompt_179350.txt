Read Kurosawa's Rashomon through the collective-memory-vs-history lens, but please don't just do the tired "truth is subjective" take every undergrad writes. Instead, take seriously this niche framing: there's a kind of normative weight in a community's shared narrative even when it flat-out conflicts with the archival record—call it mnemonic justice—not because memory is "truer" but because it sustains social bonds the record ignores. The film gives four incompatible testimonies about a samurai's murder, framed by the woodcutter and a monk sheltering under the Rashomon gate in a downpour. The woodcutters version is both least verifiable and most humane; at the end he takes in an abandoned baby despite having lied about the dagger. Does Kurosawa side with the version that keeps community intact, or does he think unreconciled chaos is the honest price? Where does the framing with rain and gate suggest collective memory should correct the court record, and where does Rashomon resist being read as pure relativism or pure archivalism?
