I am a high school student organizing a philosophy workshop for twelve peers, aged sixteen to seventeen, with one hour available. I want a structured activity—preferably involving assigned roles, sequential choices, and small but real stakes—that allows participants to experience firsthand how intuition pumps can mislead through narrative-direction dependence rather than through logical error. By this I mean the phenomenon in which a thought experiment produces a strong intuitive verdict when its events are presented in chronological order (an agent deliberates, then a consequence unfolds), yet that same logical structure, presented in reverse temporal order (the consequence is revealed first, then the mechanism that produced it is traced backward), either collapses the intuition or produces the opposite verdict, suggesting that the pump’s persuasive force comes from temporal framing rather than from the case itself.

The activity should not begin with a lecture or with naming the phenomenon. Instead, the rules, payoffs, or role constraints should cause the group to construct or evaluate paired scenarios—one narrated forward, one narrated backward—so that they feel the asymmetry in their own judgments before it is identified. After the exercise, I need a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of this idea, explains what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run (for instance, participants who recognize the pairing too early, or who resist the framing altogether), and is explicit about which feature of narrative-direction dependence—perhaps its dependence on tacit background assumptions about agency or causation—this classroom setup cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. Please specify the setting details, the timeline, the rules or roles, the mechanism of the small stakes, and the structure of the debrief. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
