Student answer:
The poverty-of-the-stimulus argument contends that children acquire complex grammatical rules despite receiving linguistic input that is fragmentary, noisy, and never explicitly annotated for syntactic structure. The canonical case is auxiliary inversion in English yes/no questions. Given the declarative "The boy who is happy is running," the correct interrogative fronts the main-clause auxiliary—"Is the boy who is happy running?"—not the leftmost auxiliary in the linear string, which would yield "*Is the boy who happy is running?" Children consistently avoid this linear error, applying the transformation over hierarchical phrase structure without explicit instruction or correction. Because parents do not provide negative evidence for the linear mistake, and because the available input severely underdetermines the correct hierarchical rule, an innate constraint on the hypothesis space is necessary to explain how learners converge on structure-dependency.

If we grant children statistical-learning capacities, the argument is not dissolved but mischaracterized by its critics. Statistical induction requires explicit counterexamples or suppressive feedback to eliminate overgeneralized hypotheses; without direct correction of linear inversions—corrections that are essentially absent from child-directed speech—a purely probabilistic learner should retain the linear strategy, since it succeeds in simple sentences and is never penalized. Recent neural-network studies trained solely on positive corpora of child-directed speech confirm this limitation: they either fail to extend structure-dependency to novel embedded clauses or require data volumes orders of magnitude beyond what any child hears. Hence, invoking statistics does not remove the poverty problem; it relocates the explanatory burden onto the learner's architecture, which must itself be innately biased toward hierarchical representations.
