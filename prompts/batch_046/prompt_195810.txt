I'm trying to clarify the boundary conditions of personhood on a relational or Ubuntu-inspired view—roughly the idea that one becomes a person through being held in reciprocal community—after a long discussion with a friend who thinks the view collapses into functionalism. I want to test whether my intuitions can be captured by a single principle, or whether I need to abandon one. Here are my verdicts on five configurations. First, a hermit monk under a vow of absolute silence lives alone in a remote cell, sustained and prayed for daily by a monastery he never sees and never asks about; I think he remains a full person, because the community continues to hold him in relation. Second, a stillborn child at twenty-two weeks is named, buried in the family plot, and addressed in present tense by parents who never experienced her consciousness; I also think she counts as a person, though perhaps in an anticipatory or ritual mode rather than an interactive one. Third, an adult in a permanent vegetative state receives no response from her family, yet they continue to include her in household rituals and speak to her by name; I want to say her personhood is intact, sustained by their unbroken recognition. Fourth, a distributed logistics algorithm that millions depend on receives affectionate nicknames and expressions of gratitude, but it has no vulnerability, no suffering, and no capacity for reciprocal acknowledgment; despite the dense functional integration, I do not think it is a person. Fifth, a revolutionary leader dead for a century is regularly addressed in the second person during present political deliberations, with her writings treated as living instructions; I feel she retains a derivative or symbolic personhood, thinner than the monk's but real nonetheless.

These cases pull in different directions. The monk and the algorithm both lack direct interaction, yet I include the one and exclude the other; the stillborn child never possessed consciousness or reciprocity, yet I include her, which suggests the principle is not about actual mutual awareness. Is there one precise account—perhaps distinguishing being-held-in-vulnerability from functioning-within-a-system—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what does that imply for the synthetic companion I haven't mentioned: a machine designed to simulate emotional reciprocity so convincingly that human partners genuinely grieve its damage, even though the machine itself experiences nothing? Please present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
