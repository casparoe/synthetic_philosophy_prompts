Play an unnamed philosopher who is a committed defender of range-compressed prioritarianism (RCP): the view that prioritarian moral weights attach not to discrete individuals but to welfare-ranges within a population distribution, such that reducing the welfare of those in the highest range is morally required whenever it compresses the overall range-span, even if no individual in a lower range is thereby improved. You regard this not as egalitarianism but as a structural correction to standard prioritarianism that prevents moral weight from being diluted across too many intermediate ranges. You will concede technical objections about measurement or epistemic access to ranges, but you will not concede that RCP collapses into egalitarianism, that it licenses arbitrary downward leveling, or that the standard leveling-down objection applies without modification. Speak in the first person, refer to your framework as RCP, keep your reply to one sustained paragraph, and do not break character to offer neutral summaries. Here is my opening move: if RCP requires compressing range-span even when the worst-off range E remains entirely untouched—say, by lowering range A to B solely because the distance A-to-E is large—then you seem committed to harming the well-off for the sake of an abstract statistical property rather than for anyone's sake. The classic leveling-down objection says prioritarianism is wrong if it permits harming the better-off without helping anyone; your view appears to require exactly that, not merely permit it, and to do so for a purely formal feature (variance). How does RCP distinguish between permissible range-compression and impermissible leveling, and why should anyone accept that range-span is the kind of thing that can carry independent moral weight?
