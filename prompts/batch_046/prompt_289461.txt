Here is the concrete situation. For over 120 years, a mid-sized city has managed its public parks through a highly decentralized system. Each neighborhood elects a volunteer “park steward,” a role that carries no formal legal authority, yet the city parks department has traditionally treated the steward’s recommendations on tree removal, path changes, and playground placement as effectively binding. The stewards also maintain handwritten decision logs—complete with recorded failures—dating back to the 1890s. A newly appointed city manager proposes eliminating the steward roles entirely and replacing both the distributed veto power and the handwritten logs with a centralized digital planning database run by external urban-design consultants. She argues that the system creates unnecessary delays, that no current official can explain exactly why each neighborhood was granted this influence in the first place, and that algorithmic modeling can predict park usage and ecological needs far more accurately than volunteer intuition ever could.

One longtime steward objects. He does not deny that the system is slow or occasionally irrational, but he insists that tearing it down is dangerous precisely because its purpose is not fully visible. He argues that the handwritten logs and the distributed veto over tree removal embody a form of accumulated practical wisdom: they likely solve hidden coordination problems—such as preventing rapid, irreversible landscape changes that trigger conflicts between neighborhoods—problems that a centralized consultant model, optimized for efficiency and density, would miss entirely. He describes the steward authority not as mere custom but as a kind of prescription: practices and local rights that have persisted because they successfully mediated disputes for generations. He also invokes the familiar analogy of a fence in a field—if you do not know why it was built, you should not remove it—suggesting that the delays and the logs are not bugs but features of a system that protects against overreach by centralized planners.

The city council asks for your philosophical judgment: Is the steward making a coherent argument rooted in Burkean or Chestertonian reasoning, or is he dressing up nostalgia and resistance to modernization in philosophical language? Specifically, does Burke’s concept of prescription apply meaningfully to an informal, volunteer-based role with no legal charter? Is the fence analogy being used correctly here, or is it being stretched beyond its proper scope? And is there a rigorous philosophical distinction between legitimate institutional memory—practices that preserve tacit coordination knowledge—and mere attachment to outdated procedures?

Please advise the council on whether they should preserve, reform, or eliminate the steward system. Your answer should apply the relevant theories precisely, distinguish accurate applications from misapplications, and avoid generic management consulting language.
