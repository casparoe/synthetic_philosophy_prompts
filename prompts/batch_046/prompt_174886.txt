As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying post-war moral philosophy, I have been working through Karl Jaspers' distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, particularly his claim that a nation can bear political guilt for state actions without every individual being criminally culpable. I understand that he denies descendants inherit this guilt in any personal sense, yet he maintains that the collective responsibility persists beyond the generation that directly enacted the policies. In biology, a trait that outlives its originators requires some mechanism of transmission—genetic, epigenetic, or ecological—otherwise it simply dissipates. Applying that framework here, I find myself asking: if Jaspers rejects any analog to biological heredity, by what causal or structural mechanism does political guilt remain bound to the collective entity rather than vanishing with the death of the original agents? Or should I understand collective guilt not as an inherited property but as a persistent institutional state—something closer to a standing wave sustained by continuing structures rather than an allele passed between generations? More precisely, where does Jaspers draw the boundary between a nation being genuinely guilty and its subsequent citizens being merely responsible for remedy, and does his account depend on, or explicitly foreclose, any intergenerational transmission model?
