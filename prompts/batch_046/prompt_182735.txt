As a journalist who writes on the history of economic thought, I have been preparing a long-form essay for a quarterly that examines the boundary between metaphysics and monetary theory. I want to explore a reading of George Berkeley's economic writings—particularly the series of queries he circulated in the 1730s—where he treats paper currency not as a repository of intrinsic value but as a social sign or "ticket" whose efficacy depends on collective acceptance rather than on any material substrate. I am arguing that this is not an incidental application of his broader immaterialism but its political-economic corollary: if material substance is ultimately inert and only ideas possess causal efficacy, then a coin or banknote can only exercise economic power insofar as it participates in a shared system of meanings sustained by human minds. To prevent the piece from reading like a standard intellectual biography, I have introduced a deliberately provocative framing: that Berkeley's monetary semiotics anticipates, in an unexpected way, modern chartalist accounts of state money, except that for Berkeley the "state" in question is not a sovereign authority but the distributed intentionality of the economic community itself. I have also proposed that there is an unacknowledged tension between this semiotic theory of money and Berkeley's well-known hostility to abstract ideas: if money functions as a sign that stands for labour or commodities in general, it seems to require precisely the kind of abstract generalisation—value in general—that Berkeley elsewhere rejects as a dangerous Lockean fiction.

Before I submit the draft, I should welcome your conceptual criticism rather than bibliographic suggestions. First, have I distorted Berkeley's actual position on paper currency and land-backed credit in order to force the parallel with his immaterialism? Second, is the comparison with modern chartalism illuminating, or does it import anachronistic machinery—such as endogenous money creation or sovereign debt dynamics—that obscures what Berkeley actually meant by "ticket"? Third, and most importantly for the essay's architecture, does the tension I identify between monetary semiotics and Berkeley's anti-abstractionism genuinely hold up, or am I manufacturing a conflict that dissolves once one recalls that Berkeley permitted general ideas in a restricted, nominalist sense? I am particularly keen to know whether the central claim—that Berkeley's economics is continuous with his metaphysics rather than an eccentric appendix to it—is defensible, or whether it is merely journalistically convenient. Please comment on the coherence and interest of these lines, indicating where the reasoning is weakest, which analogies feel forced, and whether any of my three concerns rest on a misunderstanding that would undermine the essay's structure.
