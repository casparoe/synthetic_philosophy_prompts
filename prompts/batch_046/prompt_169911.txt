I'm trying to work out my actual view on whether famines ought to be classified as natural disasters or as political crimes, and I'm testing my intuitive judgements against a range of historical cases. Here are my verdicts; I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts I may have to abandon if consistency demands it.

Case 1 — Bengal, 1943: Rainfall was close to normal; starvation followed war-driven grain procurement, speculative hoarding, and the collapse of exchange entitlements. Verdict: not a natural disaster, but a political crime of entitlement failure.

Case 2 — The Great Leap Forward, 1959–61: No major climatic anomaly. Mass death followed forced collectivisation, false harvest reporting, and state grain requisition. Verdict: unambiguously a political crime; to describe it as natural would be a categorisation error.

Case 3 — The Irish Potato Famine, 1845–52: Phytophthora infestans genuinely destroyed the potato crop, yet other foodstuffs continued to be exported and relief was constrained by ideological commitments to laissez-faire. Verdict: still fundamentally a political crime, though here the natural trigger is real and not manufactured, which makes the case feel different from Bengal.

Case 4 — Ethiopia, 1983–85: Severe drought occurred, but the Derg's counter-insurgency campaigns, forced resettlement, and deliberate obstruction of aid corridors turned scarcity into mass mortality. Verdict: political crime using drought as an alibi.

Case 5 — The Dutch Hunger Winter, 1944–45: A Nazi blockade of food and fuel into occupied western Netherlands following the railway strike. Verdict: political crime, but the mechanism is external siege rather than internal market or state failure.

Case 6 — Bangladesh, 1974: Democratic government; national food availability existed, but local purchasing power collapsed after regional floods destroyed the rice crop. There was no malicious intent, yet entitlement protection failed. Verdict: not a natural disaster, but I hesitate to call it a "crime"; it feels like an institutional failure without a culpable agent. This is the case I keep returning to.

Case 7 — North Korea, 1994–99 (the "Arduous March"): Severe floods and droughts struck, but the state's Songun (military-first) distribution architecture, refusal to permit market adjustments, and diversion of international aid made the scale of death inevitable. Verdict: political crime embedded in institutional design, though the weather was genuinely catastrophic.

My questions: Is there one precise principle — perhaps refining entitlement theory — that captures all seven verdicts, including the distinction between deliberate or obstructive failure (Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 7) and non-malicious institutional failure (Case 6)? Or must I choose between a simpler binary principle (absence versus presence of a natural trigger) that saves consistency but collapses important distinctions, and a more complex taxonomy that saves my intuitions at the cost of elegance?

Please apply your principle to two further cases I have not listed: first, a hypothetical famine caused purely by an asteroid impact destroying global agriculture, with institutions responding as well as physically possible; second, the 2011 Horn of Africa famine in Somalia, where drought was widespread but Al-Shabaab's blockade of aid agencies and taxation of food convoys made delivery impossible in the worst-hit regions.

In your answer, please separate what is settled in the historical and philosophical literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation as consensus. State the core principle or taxonomy precisely, with the clause that addresses each listed case, its predictions for the two new cases, and an honest statement of which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.

State your final answer in a fixed, machine-readable form at the very end, with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the label of the best principle or taxonomy you defend.
