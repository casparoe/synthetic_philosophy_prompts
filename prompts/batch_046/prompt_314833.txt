I sit on the Allocation Review Board of a public-health consortium, and we must rule by Friday on whether to fund Protocol 7, an experimental neuromodulation therapy that can save one patient from terminal neurodegeneration but requires temporary, reversible cortical linking with three consenting donor volunteers, producing shared phenomenal states and pooled deliberative capacity for the duration of treatment. Proponents apply standard utilitarian aggregation across all four linked individuals; opponents invoke the separateness of persons objection, arguing that the linking collapses the boundary between benefiting one agent and distributing burdens among others, so welfare should not be summed across the cohort as if they were fungible units. Draft the brief formal opinion I will circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame for implementation or denial, treat the strongest separateness-based objection as a substantive constraint rather than a confusion about consent, and specify whether the board ought to count the linked group as one or multiple moral agents in its cost-benefit calculus. Respond in American English.
