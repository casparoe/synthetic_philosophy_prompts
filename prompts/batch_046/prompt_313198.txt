I've been studying institutional rules that are designed to bind the very authority that creates them—provisions that state, for example, that this body may not alter its own decision-making procedures except under conditions X. I keep seeing the same failure mode, and I don't know whether it carries a technical name or is merely an instance of broader ideas like regulatory capture or ordinary statutory interpretation.

Here are two concrete cases.

First, a university faculty senate enacts a statute requiring that any future restriction on tenure protections must pass by a two-thirds vote after a sixty-day public comment period. Over the next decade, the senate never repeals the statute. Instead, it issues a series of procedural clarifications: one holds that restriction applies only to direct dismissal, not to funding reallocations; another holds that actions taken by delegated subcommittees are not actions of the senate itself; a third defines public comment to include internal memos circulated among administrators. Each clarification is individually plausible, and none formally amends the original statute. Yet the protective mechanism is now triggered only in situations that do not occur in practice.

Second, a constitutional amendment clause states that any change to the clause itself must be ratified by a direct popular referendum with a supermajority. The legislature, wishing to lower the threshold, does not amend the clause. Instead, it passes an interpretive framework declaring that direct popular referendum includes online advisory polls conducted by the legislature's own commission, and that supermajority means a majority of those who vote in the poll. The clause remains verbatim intact, but its practical operation has been inverted.

In both cases, the rule is self-referential: it governs the power that enacted it. The erosion happens not through repeal or open defiance, but through cumulative internal adjustments—definitions, delegations, and scope-narrowing—that remain technically consistent with the rule's text. The actors are not external lobbyists; they are the same authority the rule was meant to constrain.

Is there an established concept for this specific pattern? I can see similarities to salami tactics, but that usually involves overt, incremental steps rather than interpretive redefinition. It also resembles the paradoxes of legal self-reference—where a rule governs its own amendment—but those paradoxes are typically logical, asking whether a rule can validly repeal itself, whereas here the logic is intact and the problem is functional hollowing-out. Could it be a variant of constitutional hardball, or is that reserved for norm violations rather than technical compliance?

If there is no precise term, I would want one that distinguishes this from ordinary statutory interpretation on one side and formal constitutional amendment on the other, and that captures the particular risk of self-binding rules being dissolved from within by the authority they bind. What would you call it, and where exactly are its boundaries—for instance, does it require that the adjustments be made by the same body, or can it occur through judicial interpretation as well?
