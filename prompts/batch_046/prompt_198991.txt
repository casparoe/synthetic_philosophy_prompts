I have been working through symmetric decision problems of the kind Douglas Hofstadter introduced: the Platonia dilemma, in which twenty isolated individuals must each decide whether to send a telegram so that exactly one does and wins a prize, and the Luring Lottery played by Scientific American readers, where each participant could submit any number of entries on a single postcard, with the prize pool divided by total submissions so that universal restraint maximized everyone's expected return. The standard literature on these cases focuses on the conflict between direct self-interest and superrational cooperation—randomizing with probability proportional to group size in the first case, submitting exactly one entry in the second.

Yet I keep encountering a failure mode that does not seem reducible to either the direct incentive to defect in the lottery or to standard game-theoretic mistrust. It arises specifically when the cooperative solution depends on a transparent, symmetric protocol—say, each agent privately simulating a random draw weighted by the anticipated number of participants, or all parties committing to the minimal entry count. Because the protocol is common knowledge and adherence is unambiguously optimal, selecting it feels, at a second-order level, like signaling an inability to model the other party's reasoning about the protocol itself. Each agent then anticipates that the other will avoid cooperation not out of greed but out of precisely this meta-reasoning—fearing the appearance of naivety—so that both converge on defection not to gain an advantage but to preserve strategic credibility. The result is a collapse of cooperation driven not by payoff structure but by reflexive suspicion about the signaling value of rational adherence.

I do not know whether decision theory or philosophy of action has an established term for this configuration—perhaps something like a sophistication trap, reflexive defection, or meta-rationality collapse—or whether it is treated as an unremarkable variant of common-knowledge failure. How exactly should it be distinguished from the standard luring mechanism in the lottery and from ordinary failures of strategic coordination? What are its boundary conditions: in which symmetric games does this second-order signaling dynamic operate, and in which does it dissolve into plain mistrust or into the first-order incentive to deviate? If no precise concept exists, how should one be defined and delimited, including the cases that should be excluded?
