I am a high school senior delivering the opening remarks at my school's intergenerational dialogue workshop tomorrow. The audience is deliberately mixed: parents who casually invoke generational stereotypes, teachers who cite cohort-based learning studies, sociology students familiar with debates about social ontology, and peers who identify strongly with Gen Z. The philosophical issue—whether generations are real ontological kinds like biological species, or rather constructed cohort labels—must do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decoration. I want you to write the remarks (strict maximum: 350 words; formal register suitable for a school auditorium) around a committed, reasoned argument: generations are not natural kinds, but they are not pure fictions either; they are historically indexed cohort categories whose apparent shared properties are partly constituted by the very institutional measurement, marketing, and pedagogical practices that claim only to discover them. Because this ontological commitment rules out essentialism, the speech must reject appeals to intrinsic Gen-Z traits—such as universal digital nativism or attention-span deficits—while still honoring genuine differences shaped by shared historical conditions. It must be accessible to parents without patronizing the sociology students, and it must end with a concrete framing for the workshop discussions that follows directly from the position taken rather than from a generic call for empathy. Do not present both sides neutrally; take one definite view, defend it, and let that commitment shape what can and cannot be said in the room.
