As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I have been trying to understand whether Charles Sanders Peirce's and William James's pragmatist accounts of truth can actually illuminate how working scientists treat theories that eventually fail. Peirce, if I understand him correctly, defines truth as the opinion that an indefinitely extended community of rational inquirers would ultimately converge upon; error is systematically corrected over time, and truth is essentially the ideal limit of inquiry rather than any present state of belief. James, in contrast, holds that truth is not a static correspondence waiting to be discovered but a property that ideas acquire through successful verification in practice—ideas become true in the course of experience by guiding action and prediction effectively.

Please compare these two theories with specific attention to the status of now-abandoned but once-predictively-successful scientific models, such as the luminiferous ether and the caloric theory of heat. For each account, determine whether such a model could have possessed genuine truth during its period of predictive success, whether its subsequent abandonment reveals an inherent falsehood that was always present, or whether it represents a change in the practical conditions that made the idea workable. Further, evaluate whether Peirce's reliance on an indefinite future convergence renders his theory operationally indistinguishable from a scientific realism that never actually reaches closure, and whether James's verificationist emphasis permits a form of temporary or context-bound truth that a scientist should regard as genuinely cognitive rather than merely instrumentally convenient.

Conclude by arguing which framework provides the more coherent account of scientific change—one that avoids both an unreachable metaphysical endpoint and a collapse into subjective utility—while explaining precisely where each theory struggles with the phenomenon of theoretical abandonment.
