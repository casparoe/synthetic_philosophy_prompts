I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, working on causal reasoning in redundant engineered and biological systems. For a graduate workshop on causal ontology, I am designing a classification instrument that sorts philosophical positions not by school affiliation but by the concrete commitments they must take in a specific scenario—call it the Symmetrical Redundant Safeguard case. The scenario is this: a critical system contains two fully independent protective mechanisms, M1 and M2, each alone sufficient to prevent catastrophic failure F; both are active; F is averted; but the system architecture allows M1 to be disabled for testing while disabling M2 would collapse the system entirely. I need a decision-tree taxonomy with four binary diagnostic splits, yielding sixteen logical cells. Each split must be phrased as a concrete judgment about this case, not as a theoretical slogan, so that a respondent can answer without knowing the literature's labels. Once the logical space is laid out, map onto it at least the following named approaches: a counterfactual-dependence account in the style of David Lewis, a structural-interventionist account in the style of Judea Pearl, a normality-sensitive selectionist account that appeals to default or statistical normal conditions, and a pluralist or purpose-relative account that denies unique causal selection. For every occupied cell, name the position and state its most awkward commitment in one sentence. For every empty cell, diagnose whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored—with particular attention to combinations that accept counterfactual symmetry but reject joint causation while also accepting purpose-relativity. Finally, flag any pairs of named positions that the tree fails to separate, explaining whether the collapse is due to shared answers across all four splits or due to ambiguity in applying the scenario.

Please present the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Taxonomy Tree (~300 words): present the four binary splits in flowchart form (textual tree), with each branch labeled by the concrete claim that divides it, and the sixteen terminal cells labeled by logical profile (e.g., Y-Y-N-Y).

2. The Diagnostic Questions (~250 words): for each split, state the exact yes/no question as applied to the Safeguard case, explain why a counterfactualist, interventionist, selectionist, and pluralist might answer differently, and note any ambiguity that could cause mis-sorting.

3. The Position Mapping (~350 words): place the four named accounts into their respective cells, describe any hybrid or unnamed positions that fall into other cells, and for each leaf provide the one-sentence awkward commitment.

4. Empty Cells and Diagnosis (~250 words): list the unoccupied logical combinations, classify each emptiness as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, and defend at least one diagnosis with reasoning specific to the Safeguard scenario.

5. Collapsed Distinctions (~150 words): identify which named positions the tree cannot separate, specify whether the problem lies in the diagnostic questions or in the positions themselves, and suggest what additional split (if any) would be needed.

The instrument is intended for advanced graduate students in philosophy of science and formal epistemology; keep the language precise but avoid bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations, since the point is conceptual sorting rather than literature review.
