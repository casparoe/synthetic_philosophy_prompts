I am preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of social choice, and I want to test whether Arrow's impossibility theorem can illuminate a real democratic breakdown rather than remaining an abstract logical result. The episode I have in mind is the 2000 United States presidential election in Florida: the plurality vote among Bush, Gore, and Nader; the subsequent machine and manual recounts with shifting county-level rules; the Supreme Court's intervention; and the familiar fact that Nader's presence likely altered the outcome. Rather than asking for a generic summary of Arrow, I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions.

First, map the election onto Arrow's framework with precision: who are the agents, what are the alternatives, and what social welfare function is being approximated by Florida's aggregation mechanism? Identify where the independence of irrelevant alternatives fails—specifically with respect to Nader voters whose rankings of Bush and Gore are not independent of Nader's presence on the ballot—and whether the recount procedures generate violations of universal domain or Pareto efficiency. Then consider the strategic dimension that Arrow's static model suppresses: ask whether any deterministic aggregation rule for this electorate would have been vulnerable to strategic misrepresentation of preferences, and whether the recount's sequential, county-by-county nature introduces a dynamic preference-aggregation problem that Arrow's conditions never anticipated. Finally, treat the Supreme Court's decision as an institutional resolution: does it function as an exogenous dictator in Arrow's sense, and if so, does that save the theorem by confirming that non-dictatorship must be violated whenever aggregation succeeds, or does it instead show that real-world democracy escapes the theorem only by abandoning the very conditions—like anonymity and universal domain—that make democratic aggregation normatively attractive?

Conclude by saying what this episode genuinely adds to the philosophical debate. Does Florida in 2000 confirm Arrow's pessimism about rational collective choice, reveal that the theorem's conditions are descriptively irrelevant to institutional politics, or demonstrate something more specific—perhaps that democratic failure takes the form not of a Condorcet cycle but of an institutional dictator stepping in to resolve a strategic ambiguity that formal aggregation cannot settle? I want the analysis honest about where Arrow's framework is silent and where it is genuinely illuminating, without reducing the crisis to a mere illustration.
