I take the view that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are not two separate puzzles but symptoms of one mistake: aggregating beliefs that belong to different epistemic registers. In the statistical register we rationally accept that a given lottery ticket will lose, guided by aggregate probability; in the authorial register we rationally accept each individual claim in a long manuscript, guided by local reliability. The paradoxes arise only when we illegitimately pool these context-tagged beliefs into a single flat set and apply consistency or deductive closure across registers. What would genuinely count against this register-separation approach? Please rank the defeaters: fatal objections showing the tagging is ad hoc or collapses into standard non-closure or probabilist solutions; awkward cases where we intuitively ought to combine beliefs across registers; formal or conceptual results about belief aggregation that undermine context-sensitive closure; and whether proponents of familiar resolutions would concede that register-separation captures something they miss. I also want to know whether the view is genuinely distinct from contextualism or relevant-alternatives accounts, or whether it risks being a mere relabeling.
