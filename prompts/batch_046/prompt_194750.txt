As a novelist constructing a long-form narrative in which the first-person pronoun functions less as a character than as a self-referential symbol that generates its own continuity, I have been trying to understand a philosophical problem that seems to lie at the heart of my project. I recently turned to David Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (Book I, Part IV, Section VI, "Of personal identity") and encountered a passage that troubles my intuition about whether such a symbol can have any anchor at all. The passage reads as follows:

"For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception. When my perceptions are remov'd for any time, as by sound sleep; so long am I insensible of myself, and may truly be said not to exist. And were all my perceptions remov'd by death, and cou'd I neither think, nor feel, nor see, nor love, nor hate after the dissolution of my body, I shou'd be entirely annihilated, nor do I conceive what is farther requisite to make me a perfect non-entity. If any one upon serious and unprejudic'd reflection, thinks he has a different notion of himself, I must confess I can reason no longer with him. All I can allow him is, that he may be in the right as well as I, and that we are essentially different in this particular. He may, perhaps, perceive something simple and continu'd, which he calls himself; tho' I am certain there is no such principle in me."

I am attempting to read this in dialogue with Douglas Hofstadter's idea of the "I" as a strange loop: a symbol that acquires meaning not by pointing to a stable substrate but by looping back upon itself. My difficulty is this: Hume denies that there is any constant impression or hidden core that could serve as the referent of "self," describing it instead as nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions in perpetual flux. Does this denial destroy the possibility of a strange loop, since a loop seems to require some minimal structure that can be traversed repeatedly? Or, conversely, can the strange loop survive precisely because it needs no substrate—because the "I" is nothing more than the pattern of its own successive references, much as my narrator might be nothing more than the pattern of perceptions that call themselves "I"? I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations to secondary literature; I am asking for a careful conceptual unpacking of whether Hume's bundle theory is compatible with, or fatal to, the notion of a self-referential symbol that generates its own continuity in the absence of any underlying unity. Any insight into how this tension might be navigated in a narrative context—where the pronoun "I" must persuade the reader of its persistence despite having no ontological weight beyond its own utterances—would be especially valuable.
