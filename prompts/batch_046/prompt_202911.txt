I hold a qualified anti-intellectualist view about knowing-how, focussed on what I call "silent calibration": the capacity of an expert practitioner to correct subtle performance deviations through direct environmental feedback, without any accompanying propositional articulation — not even a dispositional readiness to state rules — and without the correction being accessible to conscious report even after the fact. I take this to be a genuine and distinct form of knowing-how, not reducible to standard Rylean intelligent practice, because it operates below attentional thresholds and survives debriefing procedures that usually elicit tacit knowledge. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the arguments and findings that should move me, sorted by decisiveness: empirical results in skill-acquisition research that separate implicit learning from true non-propositional competence; conceptual distinctions — for instance, between tacit knowledge, procedural memory, and embodied cognition — that might absorb silent calibration into a better-established category; and formal arguments, such as refined versions of the Stanley–Williamson practical-mode analysis, showing that any apparently silent adjustment must be governed by a proposition known under a practical mode. For each, please indicate whether it would be fatal specifically to silent calibration or merely force retreat to broader anti-intellectualism, and say what defenders of my view would themselves concede as counting against it. Finally, I ask for an honest assessment of whether I have insulated the claim by defining "silent" or "calibration" so narrowly that no observation could falsify it; if so, is that insulation a vice for a thesis about cognitive architecture, or is it defensible as appropriate boundary-setting?
