I'm trying to work out my own reflective equilibrium about when an enforced rule or command is genuinely authoritative rather than merely coercive, particularly in light of Pascal's pairing of justice with force—justice without force being powerless, force without justice being tyrannical. Rather than ask for a historical exegesis, I want to test a tentative principle against concrete judgments and see whether my intuitions can be saved together or whether one must be overturned.

Here are my verdicts on six cases:

1. A city discovers that its founding charter, which grants the mayor absolute veto over all ordinances, is a 19th-century forgery never formally repealed. The mayor invokes it to block a public-health measure, backed by police. Verdict: The veto carries no authority; it is pure coercion with a legal label.

2. A democratically elected legislature passes a tax that economists widely judge inefficient and that falls heavily on a vulnerable minority, enforced by standard fines and imprisonment. Verdict: Citizens should pay; the law is genuinely binding despite being bad policy.

3. After a violent coup, a provisional government abolishes corrupt property rules and uses the existing courts and police to enforce new equitable distributions. Verdict: The new rules are binding—not because the coup was legitimate, but because justice now aligns with the operative force.

4. A private club's century-old bylaws contain a clause permitting expulsion without cause, never invoked in living memory, until the board uses private security to eject a member who exposed corruption. Verdict: Not binding. Yet this feels different from Case 2, where unpopular enforcement still produces obligation.

5. In a legitimate defensive war, a commander orders the destruction of a civilian bridge that is militarily necessary but will kill non-combatants, enforced by court-martial. Some soldiers believe the order violates the laws of war. Verdict: I am genuinely torn—the institutional justice of the war framework pulls toward obedience, but the specific injustice of the order pulls toward resistance.

6. A neighborhood has an unwritten but universally observed practice: during water shortages, households with young children receive priority, enforced by social ostracism rather than law. A new resident refuses. Verdict: He should comply; the social pressure is legitimate enforcement of an embedded justice, not mere force.

My tentative principle is that an enforced rule is genuinely binding when the operative force behind it is recognized by those subject to it as serving a justice that could not be sustained without that force, and when the rule is not arbitrary relative to that justice—where "arbitrary" means disconnected from any shared normative framework that the subjects can see as their own.

Does this principle deliver all six verdicts? If not, which case must I overturn, and what is the simplest repair—perhaps by adding a clause about institutional provenance, about the distinction between constitutive and instrumental authority, or about a threshold of recognition? Please state the repaired principle precisely, motivate each clause by the case that requires it, say what it predicts for an unlisted case (a democratic majority permanently disenfranchising a minority through standard legal mechanisms), and give an honest account of which of my verdicts it still cannot accommodate.
