After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether historians ask a malformed question when they frame the Great Divergence as “why Britain and not China?”, I want to stop talking past each other and actually map where we stand. Please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps should have produced: the Contrastive Defenders, who hold that the Needham-style comparison is well-posed and identifies genuine explanatory targets, and the Comparative Skeptics, who hold that the framing is conceptually malformed because it treats incommensurable political-economic configurations as comparable instances of a single pre-industrial type.

The document should follow this structure. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—statements about the historical record, about the existence of a divergence in industrialization timelines, about the limits of counterfactual reasoning in macro-history, and about shared rejections (for example, that any mono-causal account ignoring institutional multiplicity is inadequate). Second, a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated twice: once in wording the Contrastive Defenders accept for their own view, and once in wording the Comparative Skeptics accept for theirs. Include disputes over whether “Britain” and “Qing China” denote comparable units for the purpose of explanation; over whether divergence requires a single sufficient cause or rather a specific conjunction of conditions that are individually insufficient; and over whether counterfactual perturbation of one factor—such as coal geography—while holding other institutions constant is a coherent and informative test rather than a category mistake. Third, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, present a concrete counterfactual case or paired scenario that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance each side’s prediction about what that case shows. One crux should concern the explanatory weight of resource endowment versus institutional structure, tested through two paired counterfactuals: a Qing-like polity with British-style coal access but retaining its imperial examination system and land-tenure rules, and a British-like polity with Qing-level coal scarcity but retaining its parliamentary representation and patent institutions. Each side should state whether divergence is expected to persist, diminish, or reverse in each scenario, and what that outcome implies about overdetermination. Another crux should address whether part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether the camps use “explanation,” “cause,” “comparison,” and “divergence” with different methodological commitments, and whether aligning those definitions dissolves any portion of the disagreement.

In drafting, apply a conceptual taxonomy that is not usually foregrounded in standard treatments of this debate: classify each disagreement as ontological (disagreement about what entities or mechanisms exist in the historical process), methodological (disagreement about what counts as admissible evidence or a valid counterfactual), or semantic (disagreement about the meaning and scope of key terms). Specify which disagreements fall into which category and whether any shift from one category to another changes the burden of proof. The document should be self-contained, formal, and written so that both parties could sign the agreed-claims section and recognize their own positions in the disagreement table without feeling misrepresented. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; rely only on reasoning and shared historical knowledge available to an informed reader without web access.
