Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy of science, I find myself stuck on what looks like a deep conceptual tension in the institutional practice of modern meteorological forecasting. The operational centers run deterministic numerical weather prediction models—systems that are demonstrably chaotic, with exponential divergence of trajectories governed by nonlinear dynamics—yet they routinely issue a single deterministic best-track forecast for public and policy consumption, burying the full ensemble spread in technical appendices accessible mainly to specialists. At the same time, these very institutions invoke chaos theory and finite predictability horizons, typically ten to fourteen days, to justify their refusal to issue deterministic forecasts beyond that window, treating initial-condition sensitivity as an absolute epistemic barrier. What makes the practice puzzling is that the identical modeling architectures, complete with the same parameterized sub-grid turbulence and coupled nonlinear dynamics, are simultaneously deployed for climate projections extending many decades forward, where chaos is treated not as a barrier but as a feature to be statistically averaged into long-term trends. The institution thus seems to exercise what I can only call predictive sovereignty: it claims authoritative knowledge of specific future atmospheric states within a carefully policed temporal boundary, and deliberately surrenders that authority beyond it, despite relying on the same underlying physics in both regimes. I want to understand what philosophical commitments this hybrid practice presupposes. Does it require a metaphysically real distinction between weather and climate, or is that distinction merely a pragmatic boundary drawn to manage institutional accountability? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether it is a form of pragmatic determinism, an epistemic partition of nature, or a hidden contract about the limits of scientific responsibility—distinguishing strictly what the practice requires from what merely fits it well. Tell me whether its parts are philosophically consistent, which rival views of determinism and predictability it quietly rules out, and what would have to change in forecasting practice, public communication, and policy reliance if we replaced its implicit view with a consistently probabilistic or anti-realist one.
