I've been running a seminar on distributve justice — actually I'm co-teaching it with a colleague from politics — and we've hit a wall on workfare and the 'undeserving poor' framing. The argument about benefit fraud checks and conditionality has gone round for three sessions without anyone shifting an inch. One group says conditionality is just reciprocity: if you can work you should, otherwise you're free-riding on others' labour, and they keep saying welfare without work-requirements erodes the social solidarity that makes redistribution sustainable in the first place. The other group says that framing is a moral error — it treats economic contribution as a precondition for dignity, which they think violates the unconditional nature of basic needs claims. They both agree the empirical fraud rates are low, they both agree poverty is harmful, but they can't agree on whether the state is morally allowed to ask 'are you trying?' before giving help.

My working theory is that they're not really disagreeing about welfare policy at all, but about whether moral obligations can have what I'd call 'activtion conditions' — like, is there a hidden disagreement in the logic of obligation itself, masked by all the talk about budgets and fraud? I want a diagnosis: is this verbal (they mean different things by 'desert'), is there a hidden empirical claim about motivation and social norms doing the work, or is it just bedrock intuition about work and human worth that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction — maybe between conditional rights and conditional benefits — that could actually unblock the debate, or should I tell my students that the question is malformed and we should stop expecting progress? Please write in British English, and don't just survey positions; I want the structure of the disagreement itself, with reasons.
