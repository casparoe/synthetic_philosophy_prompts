I'm closing the final meeting of my philosophy-of-mind seminar, and our discussion of the symbol grounding problem has drifted into slightly unconventional territory. We began with the familiar circularity—symbols defined only through other symbols, never touching the world—and considered the standard causal or perceptual anchors, but then a student pressed a sharper condition she called "counterfactual vulnerability": a symbol is genuinely grounded, she argued, not merely when it is causally triggered by some state, but only when the system that uses it could be corrected by the world across a range of counterfactual scenarios, not just in the actual one. We never settled whether any syntactic system can satisfy that modal openness, or whether it demands a kind of intentionality that computation necessarily lacks. I want to end the session with a poem that takes this specific tension seriously, rather than falling back on the Chinese Room cliché.

Write a villanelle: exactly nineteen lines, five tercets followed by one quatrain, adhering to the standard rhyme scheme with two refrains (A1 and A2) that rhyme with each other. One refrain should give voice to the symbol's self-enclosure—the circular reference of sign to sign—while the other should voice the world's claim to stand outside that circle. The refrains must alternate through the tercets and then appear together in the final quatrain, so that the form itself enacts the problem: the two lines keep returning without ever quite touching, and the closing stanza must show—without resolving—whether they finally meet or remain in permanent adjacency. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the grounding debate recognizes the distinction between derivative intentionality and intrinsic anchoring, and it should leave the counterfactual-vulnerability question hanging open rather than answering it. No more than nineteen lines; every line must carry philosophical weight.
