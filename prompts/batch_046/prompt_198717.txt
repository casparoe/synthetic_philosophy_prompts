I have recently been appointed health-care proxy for a parent who suffered diffuse axonal injury and now shows only inconsistent, unreportable behavioral responses in a minimally conscious state; the clinical team speaks of access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness as if they could come apart, and invokes global workspace architecture to explain the absence of report. Before I can rely on these distinctions for substitute-judgment decisions, I need to understand what an adequate theory in this area actually owes us.

Please specify the desiderata that any theory distinguishing access consciousness (A-consciousness) from phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness)—especially one that draws on global workspace architecture—must meet to count as adequate, not merely as a neuroscience model but as a framework that can guide ethical reasoning about patients who cannot report. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (e.g., actual-report requirements, localist alternatives, integration thresholds, counterfactual reportability conditions).

Specifically, include consideration of the following. First, how the theory treats dissociation cases in which behavioral report is blocked but some form of awareness is suspected—such as locked-in conditions, anesthesia awareness, or masked sensory processing—and whether it demands actual report, counterfactual reportability under restored motor output, or only internal broadcast to workspace nodes. Second, whether global workspace integration is necessary for P-consciousness or only for A-consciousness, and what the theory says about fragmented or partial broadcast states in which long-range prefrontal-parietal coupling is disrupted but local sensory processing remains intact. Third, whether the theory can accommodate a reversibility intuition: if access mechanisms can be restored—by resolving a motor-output blockage or by pharmacological reversal of anesthesia—without altering the underlying phenomenal state, what does that imply about the causal and metaphysical relation between the workspace and phenomenal experience? Fourth, consistency with the broad neuroanatomical picture that prefrontal and parietal networks support report and working-memory access while primary sensory cortices support qualitative experience, without committing to any specific controversial localization claim.

If some subset of these desiderata proves jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, if requiring global broadcast for all phenomenal experience conflicts with the intuition that local phenomenal states persist during workspace failure—say so explicitly, identify which requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and indicate whether abandoning it is a theoretical cost or a liberation. Keep the weight on the criteria and their underlying motivations rather than on a scorecard of named theories; I am less interested in which existing model prevails than in whether the standard we are applying is itself coherent.
