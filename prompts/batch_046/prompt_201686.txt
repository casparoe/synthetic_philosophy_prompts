I've been thinking about what "possible worlds" actually mean for personal identity, and I ended up in a long argument with two friends who have read some philosophy on their own but aren't academics. Maya defended counterpart theory: when we say "I could have been a musician," we aren't claiming that this very same person is playing guitar elsewhere; rather, there's another world containing someone very similar to me who does, and that similarity relation is what makes the modal claim true. Leo pushed back with what he called straightforward transworld identity — he thinks it's literally me in that other situation, just with different properties, and that calling that person "someone else" drains modal talk of its point, since we're no longer talking about our own real possibilities.

Maya then said Leo's view needs some hidden "essential me" that stays constant no matter what changes, but none of us can actually say what that essence is — if you replace every memory and every physical trait, Leo still has to insist it's me, which sounds like magic rather than explanation. Leo answered that counterpart theory faces the mirror-image problem: similarity is a sliding scale, so in a world where I'm gradually replaced neuron by neuron until only half my memories remain, Maya can't draw a non-arbitrary line between "still my counterpart" and "just a stranger." He added what he called "the continuity problem" — that counterpart relations work for clear-cut cases but break down exactly where personal identity gets interesting, like in split-brain or gradual-upload scenarios, so her theory saves consistency mainly by avoiding hard questions rather than answering them.

Maya's reply was that Leo's "literal me" view has to deal with contradiction: if I'm literally both a musician and not a musician across worlds, and properties are world-relative, then identity isn't doing any real work. Leo said identity does the work of explaining why I care about that other-world musician — not because he's similar, but because he's me — and that caring-about-similarity is actually the stranger position.
