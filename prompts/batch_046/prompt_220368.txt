I came across a summary in an online philosophy forum that lays out the three standard responses to the epistemic regress problem as follows. Foundationalism holds that justification terminates in basic beliefs that are not supported by other beliefs; these are either self-justifying or justified immediately by non-doxastic experience, and they serve as the foundation for all other knowledge. Coherentism denies that any beliefs enjoy this privileged status, maintaining instead that a belief is justified solely by its membership in a maximally coherent system—consistency and mutual explanatory support are sufficient, and there are no epistemically basic beliefs at all. Infinitism accepts the coherentist rejection of basic beliefs but rejects coherence as sufficient; it holds that justification consists in an infinite regress of reasons, meaning that for any belief B, there must exist an actually infinite, non-repeating sequence of beliefs B1, B2, B3, ... each providing reasons for the prior one, with no belief serving as a terminus.

The author then argues that infinitism collapses on psychological grounds—no finite mind can actually host an infinite sequence of beliefs—while coherentism collapses on the "isolated system" objection, since a perfectly consistent fiction could satisfy its conditions. From this, he concludes that foundationalism is the only viable option, because it alone avoids both the impossibility of infinite belief-chains and the permissiveness of pure coherence.

This sounds plausible to me, but I want to know how fair it is. Please go through the summary position by position: identify what accurately captures the view, what is a standard simplification, and what is a substantive misrepresentation. In particular, address whether infinitism really requires an actually instantiated infinite sequence of beliefs in a finite mind, whether foundationalists claim basic beliefs are "self-justifying" in the sense of being immune to error, and whether the "isolated system" objection is presented in a form that a coherentist could accept as damaging. Finally, evaluate the author's conclusion: does the failure of the other two views, even if granted, entail foundationalism, or does the argument depend on suppressing hybrid or contextualist alternatives?
