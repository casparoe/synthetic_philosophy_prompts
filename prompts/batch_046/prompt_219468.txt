I am a retired experimental physicist who now convenes a philosophy discussion circle for retired scientists and engineers. For next month's session I must present Kierkegaard's notion of the teleological suspension of the ethical, using Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac as the central case. My audience reasons well with universalizable laws but will instinctively treat ethics analogously to physical law, so they are likely to misread the suspension either as a utilitarian exception or as evidence of religious delusion. I need a teaching plan, not an exegesis. Lay out the dependency order: which distinction—between the universal ethical order and a singular, absolute relation—must be in place before Abraham's act can register as philosophically coherent rather than as mere moral horror. Specify the carrying example: if it is the Abraham narrative, state precisely which structural features must be retained and which theological details should be bracketed for this secular audience; if another scenario serves better, justify its choice and identify what it must possess and what it must lack (in particular, no calculable greater-good payoff). Then map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—probably rule-utilitarian exemption first, then psychologizing reduction, then divine-command theory reconceived as a competing universal rule—and indicate the exact point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and dissolved. Finally, propose one diagnostic question that distinguishes grasping the suspension as a genuine rupture in ethical universalizability from grasping it merely as an exception within that framework, and state explicitly which interpretive layers—such as the pseudonymous framing of Fear and Trembling or the comparison with Agamemnon—should be left out at this level with an explicit acknowledgment of omission.
