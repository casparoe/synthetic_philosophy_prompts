I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for third-year history-of-ideas undergraduates. They have encountered Toynbee's challenge-and-response model and Spengler's cultural morphology through secondary summaries, but they have not read Polybius's account of constitutional rotation or Ibn Khaldun's theory of group solidarity and dynastic decay. I want them to understand that the cyclical versus linear divide is not a matter of geometric shape—circles versus arrows—but of explanatory structure: whether historical change is driven by an endogenous mechanism of institutional senescence that can be regenerated, or by an irreversible, cumulative transformation that outlives any particular political form.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, identify the prerequisite conceptual distinction that must be established before the cyclical/linear contrast can even be posed—specifically, what counts as the unit of analysis (civilization, dynasty, state) and what counts as an explanation rather than a narrative pattern. Second, propose the single carrying example around which the session should turn; it must display both an endogenous cyclical mechanism (the replacement of a sclerotic center by a cohesive peripheral group) and an irreversible linear residue (a technological, administrative, or epistemic change that persists across the transition). Specify what properties this example must possess and what it must avoid—particularly examples that make the cyclical element look like mere repetition. I am inclined toward the Ming-Qing transition, but recommend an alternative only if it better separates the two mechanisms.

Third, map the misconceptions students will form in predictable order: that cyclical history means exact repetition; that linear history necessarily implies moral or technological progress; that Polybius and Ibn Khaldun are offering the same explanation of decline; and that Spengler's morphology is merely Toynbee's cycle with different terminology. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence—after which conceptual step and before which—it should be preempted, and with what brief counter-distinction or miniature case.

Fourth, design one final check—an exercise or targeted question they can answer at the end without texts—that reveals whether they have grasped the structural difference in explanation rather than memorized the four names. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as such—perhaps formal parallels between Khaldun's solidarity concept and modern social-capital theory, or the full textual taxonomy of Polybius's constitutional forms—and explain why omitting it protects the arc. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations to primary texts; the deliverable is a teaching plan, not a syllabus.
