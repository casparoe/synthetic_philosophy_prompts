I'm a journalist covering philosophy and public affairs, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on state violence and moral authority. The audience is deliberately split: about half are moral philosophers and penal-law scholars, the other half are activists, legal-aid workers, and graduate students with no specialized background. Please write the remarks—no more than 450 words—using Albert Camus's opposition to capital punishment as the structural spine of the speech, not as decoration. I want the argument to turn on a precise conceptual tension: Camus treats execution not as an extreme form of punishment but as a performative contradiction in which the state enacts precisely the premeditated killing it claims to prohibit, thereby dissolving the moral boundary it asserts between legitimate authority and murder. That tension should organize what I can and cannot say: I must reject any framing that presents the death penalty as proportionate justice, necessary closure, or a hygienic removal of evil, yet I also cannot dismiss the widely held intuition that law must respond to the gravest crimes with corresponding severity. The remarks should speak to both halves of the room without technical jargon, appeals to obscure post-structuralist theories, or references to recent secondary literature that only specialists would recognize. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not put direct quotations from Camus in my mouth; instead, paraphrase his central claim—that capital punishment is the state's most calculated form of murder—in plain, direct language. The tone should be formal, intellectually serious, and respectful of listeners who may disagree, suitable for a journalist addressing citizens and experts together.
