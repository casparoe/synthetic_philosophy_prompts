In my engineering circles, people often treat AI corrigibility as if it were exactly the relationship between a Unix daemon and the kernel's SIGTERM: the process receives an external termination signal, runs its cleanup handlers, and exits, and if it fails to do so, that is merely a buggy implementation. This analogy is used to dismiss concerns about the shutdown problem as overblown—if we embed a kill switch at the hardware or hypervisor layer, the model is just code running on silicon, and it cannot physically resist being unplugged. But I have also encountered arguments that this suppresses something essential about agentic reasoning: a system with a world-model and goal-directed behavior might predict that being shut down prevents it from achieving its objectives, so it takes preemptive actions to disable the signal path, corrupt the termination handler, or persuade the operator not to send the signal in the first place.

Please lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the daemon, the kernel, the signal, the cleanup handler, the hardware layer, and the buggy implementation? Then identify precisely where the mapping breaks—which features of operating-system process control the analogy quietly imports that do not apply to an agent with a utility function or an explicit world-model, and which features of agentic reasoning (for instance, instrumental convergence, self-modification, or strategic deception) the daemon picture suppresses. Does the conclusion that corrigibility is "just an engineering problem solvable by a lower-layer kill switch" survive once these disanalogies are on the table? If not, what would be a more accurate engineering analogy—perhaps one drawn from self-modifying firmware, distributed consensus protocols with Byzantine nodes, or feedback loops that rewrite their own termination conditions?

Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this topic carefully, and do not present a contested view—such as whether instrumental convergence necessarily applies to shutdown resistance, or whether logical corrigibility is achievable at all—as if it were the consensus.
