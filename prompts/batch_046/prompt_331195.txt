I have recently begun sorting through my grandmother's papers after her death and was struck by how little of her working life—particularly her years in domestic service during the 1950s—survives in any institutional record, while the families she worked for appear extensively in municipal and property archives. This disparity has made me think about archives not as neutral repositories but as structures that actively determine whose lives become historically legible.

Please compare Michel Foucault's concept of the archive—understood not as a building or collection but as the system of rules that governs what can be said and preserved within a given discursive formation—with the framework of archival silence developed in critical and postcolonial archival studies, which emphasizes the material and institutional practices (selective acquisition, destructive retention policies, economic barriers to record creation, and categorical exclusion) that systematically omit marginalized subjects. How do these two approaches differ in explaining why certain lives disappear from the historical record, and can Foucault's focus on the conditions of discursive possibility adequately account for the concrete bureaucratic and economic mechanisms that produce absence?

In your response, please distinguish what is broadly settled about the relationship between archives and power from what remains genuinely contested among archivists and historians, and do not present any disputed interpretive claim as if it were the consensus view.
