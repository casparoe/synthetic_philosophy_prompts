Write the document that two camps—call them the Transparency-Skeptics and the Authority-Realists—would jointly sign after a year of arguing past each other about whether Glaucon's ring-of-Gyges challenge can be answered without collapsing morality into prudence. The Transparency-Skeptics hold that if moral behavior offers no reputational or material payoff and carries private costs, a fully rational agent should defect, and that any adequate reply must therefore show that moral dispositions are instrumentally rational in perfect secrecy, probably through identity-based commitment, reflective equilibrium of preferences, or game-theoretic self-binding. The Authority-Realists hold that this framing already concedes too much: moral reasons are categorical, not hypothetical, and a rational agent who grasps them has reason to act regardless of observation; they think the challenge mistakes morality for a coordination scheme that needs enforcement rather than a constraint on practical reasoning itself. Produce this as a single continuous prose text—no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists—structured like a formal letter that moves through shared ground, then the precise disagreements, then the cruxes and predictive tests. In the shared-ground section, state what both camps accept about the structure of Glaucon's scenario (the invisibility condition, the absence of punishment, the presence of temptations like theft or betrayal), about empirical psychology (that people do sometimes act well when they believe they are unobserved, but also that corruption often follows from perceived impunity), and about rational agency (that agents can in principle revise their preferences and that such revision is not automatically irrational). Then, in continuous paragraphs, lay out the exact disagreements, phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position: for instance, whether the possibility of undetected defection genuinely undermines the authority of moral reasons, or whether it merely reveals a failure of moral comprehension; whether self-modification to induce moral concern counts as answering Glaucon or as changing the subject by making the agent no longer the kind of being the challenge targets; and whether there is a non-circular way to derive moral obligations from premises about rational consistency alone. Next, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp. I take the deepest cruxes to be first, whether a rational agent can bootstrap genuine moral concern through a Ulysses-style precommitment without that concern being merely instrumental; second, whether there exists a phenomenological or conceptual distinction between prudential regret and moral remorse in the private case that both sides can agree describes the data; and third, whether transparency-independent moral properties, if they exist, can do any explanatory work or whether they are redundant with evolved social-cognitive mechanisms. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or empirical pattern both sides agree is relevant—for example, the documented behavior of individuals in genuinely anonymous high-stakes settings, or the logical structure of reflective preference revision—and record each side's explicit prediction about what that case shows. The Transparency-Skeptics should predict that anonymity correlates with defection when stakes are high and that preference revision toward morality is best explained by anticipated social reintegration; the Authority-Realists should predict that some anonymous agents still exhibit constraint and that the best explanation invokes grasp of categorical reasons rather than hidden expectation of reward. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific scholars; do not quote any text verbatim; and avoid framing the debate around niche formalisms or highly technical theories that a well-read generalist model could not be expected to have encountered in training. Write in the voice of someone who has spent years in rationalist forums thinking about instrumental convergence, self-modification, and whether ethics can survive perfect secrecy, but who wants the disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical, and present the whole thing as continuous prose suitable for a long, carefully composed email.
