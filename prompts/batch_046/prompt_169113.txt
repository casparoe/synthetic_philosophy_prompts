I spent much of last evening in a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether tearing down statues and renaming streets is an act of historical erasure or a necessary correction of a municipality’s moral ledger. We kept returning to the same familiar cases—monuments to colonial administrators, avenues honoring slaveholders, bronze figures of generals whose causes are now widely condemned—but I gradually realized our disagreement was not really about any particular statue. It was about whether a city owes its present citizens a form of ethical accounting in its public spaces: if putting a name on a street sign or erecting a monument functions not merely as geographical labeling but as an ongoing act of honor, then should that honor be subject to periodic review, depreciation, and eventual write-off like any other public asset?

Please write a deadpan satirical parody of an official municipal ordinance—approximately 700 words, formatted as numbered administrative clauses with subsections—titled something like the “Public Commemoration Maintenance and Moral Amortization Protocol,” issued by a fictional Department of Toponymic Ethics. The voice should be that of an overworked, genuinely earnest municipal lawyer who treats commemorative honor as a quantifiable public asset governed by rigorous accounting logic. Introduce the niche bureaucratic framework of “retroactive moral depreciation,” in which historical figures are assigned commemorative half-lives and public monuments must undergo periodic “recertification cycles” based on a weighted index of documented injustices, offset by calculable “historical context dividends.” Include specific clauses addressing contradictory depreciation schedules when the same figure is honored in different neighborhoods, a refund and notification policy for residents affected by a renamed street, and the problem of collective guilt versus individual culpability—perhaps framed as a sub-section on “Joint and Several Liability for Group Commemoration.” The satire should land the standard philosophical and practical objections—who calibrates the moral index, whether removing a name erases the past or merely stops celebrating it, the arbitrariness of drawing temporal boundaries, and whether a municipality can be said to owe moral consistency across generations—without ever stating them explicitly. The humor must come from pushing the actual logic of commemorative ethics to its bureaucratic extreme rather than inventing unrelated absurdities. Keep the document readable to someone unfamiliar with the debate and fair enough that a specialist recognizes the underlying tensions are being rendered with understanding rather than caricature. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for external verification; the ordinance should stand as a fully self-contained administrative fiction.
