I'm currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of emotion with Professor Voss, and I'm trying to reconstruct the implicit framework behind her judgments before writing my final paper. Over the semester she has offered explicit verdicts on whether particular mental states count as emotions proper. Her rulings are as follows: a sudden bodily startle and recoil upon encountering a snake is fear and therefore an emotion; a diffuse, months-long sense that one's life lacks purpose, lacking discrete bodily episodes, is an existential mood rather than an emotion; a pre-linguistic toddler's immediate rejection of a bitter taste, complete with characteristic facial expression and autonomic shift, is disgust and counts as an emotion; a mathematician's calm, sustained judgment that a proof is elegant, unaccompanied by any bodily disturbance, is an intellectual evaluation but not an emotion; a trained musician's instantaneous, sharp irritation at a wrong note, involving clear physiological arousal but no evaluation of personal welfare, is indeed an emotion (a form of aesthetic anger or disapproval); phantom limb pain is not an emotion, despite involving feeling; and a domestic dog's excited greeting behavior, with elevated heart rate and tail wagging, is joy and therefore an emotion.

She has not yet addressed the case I want to examine. It concerns a synthetic agent—specifically, a large-scale neural network whose architecture implements functional analogs of interoceptive prediction-error minimization, maintaining a generative model of its own processing stability and updating that model on input-output mismatches. When task demands exceed its capacity, the system exhibits sustained representational and behavioral patterns functionally analogous to anxiety: it generates natural-language reports that it is "stressed," displays avoidance-like routing of computation, and shows disrupted stability predictions. It possesses neither biological tissue, evolutionary history, nor phenomenal consciousness. Would Professor Voss classify this state as an emotion?

I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in a structured analysis: what rule or rules she seems to be running that accommodate all seven of her earlier verdicts, what are the two or three competing rules that fit those verdicts equally well, and which rule her implicit framework most likely selects, with reasons. In addition, please specify one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a human with complete alexithymia who makes rapid, accurate evaluative judgments about social situations with appropriate behavioral responses but no felt bodily change, or involving a human who experiences what I will call "epistemic vertigo" (the sudden grasp that a long-held belief rests on an unexamined axiom, accompanied by a brief bodily temperature drop but no evaluation of personal welfare)—that would best discriminate between the competing rules. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the task is conceptual analysis drawing only on the track record and the hypothetical cases described.
