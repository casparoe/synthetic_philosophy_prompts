I'm working on a short research proposal for a 3-month philosophy project and need help framing it. The topic is AI paternalism — when should an assistant refuse or override a user request? My hunch is that the debate is stuck because people treat it as binary: either you obey or you block. I want to look at what I'm calling "epistemic asymmetry contracts" — cases where the AI has reason to think the user is temporarily not in full command of their own priorities (fatigue, emotional flooding, narrow framing) and the question isn't whether to refuse outright, but whether the AI can legitimately shift the burden back onto the user before executing. So the precise question: is there a stable, principled middle ground between full compliance and hard refusal — something like "execute only after explicit reaffirmation under corrected conditions" — and can we draw that line without just importing the designer's values? Existing answers don't settle it because harm-prevention frameworks ignore retained user authority, and autonomy-maximizing frameworks ignore that authority can be temporarily fragmented. Approach: work through three clusters — self-harm queries in acute distress, high-stakes financial decisions made impulsively, and requests to suppress one's own creative work — and formalize conditions for legitimate burden-shifting. No empirical studies; it's conceptual analysis with hypothetical case construction. A useful result is a taxonomy of intervention types with clear failure conditions for each. The project fails if every case collapses into either "never interfere" or "block whenever risk is detected." Needs to be realistic for one semester with no lab access. Write it up in American English, please, and don't include a bibliography — I'll add references myself.
