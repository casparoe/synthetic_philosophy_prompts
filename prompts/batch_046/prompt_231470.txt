In my advanced philosophy course we have been working through Nelson Goodman’s “new riddle of induction,” and I have gradually adopted a somewhat unconventional interpretation of his solution. Rather than treating predicate entrenchment as a logical or metaphysical feature of categories like “green” as opposed to “grue,” I now hold that entrenchment is fundamentally a sociological phenomenon: the projectibility of a predicate depends entirely on the contingent historical practices of scientific communities, including the linguistic habits, institutional norms, and repeated observational protocols that make certain generalizations appear natural. On this conventionalist reading, there is nothing intrinsically more rational about predicting that emeralds remain green after time t; a culture that had systematically entrenched “grue” through its educational and experimental institutions would be equally justified in projecting grue-like properties. Before I defend this view in my final essay, I need to understand exactly how exposed it is.

What would have to be true for this sociological interpretation of Goodman’s entrenchment to be wrong? I want an honest map of its vulnerabilities, not merely the objections that critics typically raise. Specifically, which of Goodman’s own assumptions—about the relativity of projectibility, the absence of natural-kind structure, or the irrelevance of syntactic simplicity—would need to fail, and what kind of evidence would be decisive rather than merely awkward? For instance, could empirical findings about how human infants or artificial learning systems spontaneously generalize properties across temporal boundaries undermine the claim that entrenchment is purely conventional? Or could a formal result in inductive logic demonstrate that some predicates are projectible regardless of community practice? I also want to know whether defenders of the standard Goodmanian account themselves concede any internal limitation—perhaps that entrenchment cannot fully explain novel scientific revolutions—that I should take seriously. Please reach a judgment about whether my conventionalist position is well insulated or genuinely risky, and justify that assessment with reference to what its defenders would themselves admit counts against it.
