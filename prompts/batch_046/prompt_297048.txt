I have no formal background in philosophy or statistics, but I have been thinking about risk and fairness since a discussion with friends about computer systems used in court sentencing. I would like to check whether four commitments I have reached hang together consistently. First, I think that any probability assigned to a specific person always depends on choosing a reference class, and that there is no uniquely correct class to pick. Second, I believe it is perfectly sensible for a doctor to use my family medical history as a reference class when judging my health risks. Third, I hold that it is unfair for a sentencing authority to use postcode-level data as a reference class when predicting recidivism, because individuals do not choose their neighbourhoods. Fourth, I maintain that since the reference class problem shows all group-based statistical prediction is fundamentally arbitrary, such tools ought to be rejected in criminal justice, though they may remain permissible in medicine. Do these positions form a coherent package? I am particularly uncertain whether the reason I reject postcode data—unchosen group membership—also undermines my acceptance of family history, and whether my claim that prediction is arbitrary in principle can accommodate treating medicine as an exception. Could you provide an audit of the tensions, ordered by severity, distinguishing any conflict that is only apparent? For each genuine tension, please indicate what would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me, and identify the cheapest way to restore consistency.
