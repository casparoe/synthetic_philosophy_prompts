Write the full specification of desiderata that a philosophical theory of future-contingent statements—statements about events, such as a naval battle, that have not yet occurred and seem neither necessary nor impossible—must satisfy to count as adequate. Start from the classical tension: if bivalence applies to all propositions regardless of tense, then a statement made today about tomorrow's battle is already determinately true or false, which appears to eliminate the contingency of the future and to undermine the intuition that present actions can influence whether it happens. From there, lay out the complete requirements list: (i) what the theory must say about the applicability of bivalence and the law of excluded middle across temporal contexts; (ii) how it must account for the asymmetry between past and future—specifically, why we treat past events as fixed and open to knowledge in ways that future events are not, without merely appealing to ignorance; (iii) whether it must preserve a substantive notion of future-directed agency, such that human deliberation is not reduced to an epiphenomenal shadow over a predetermined course; (iv) how it handles the retroactive-truth problem, namely whether the occurrence of the battle makes the prior statement true in a way that was already fixed, and whether that threatens openness; (v) whether it needs to accommodate or reject branching-time ontologies, and what it owes us if it rejects them; (vi) how it relates to physical theories of time—whether it must be compatible with a block-universe picture, with presentism, or with neither; and (vii) what it must deliver for counterfactual reasoning about unrealized futures. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or argument behind it, flag which ones are non-negotiable constraints (those whose failure renders the theory unable to preserve the phenomenon it purports to explain) and which are camp-specific preferences (for instance, commitments to determinism, to modal realism, or to a particular ontology of time), and indicate whether any pair or subset is jointly unsatisfiable given standard logical or metaphysical results—if there is such an impossibility, that result should be highlighted as the central finding of the specification. Keep the weight on the criteria and their interrelations rather than on scoring rivals, but do briefly assess how three broad families of view—those that restrict bivalence for future tense statements, those that retain bivalence in linear time by distinguishing truth from determinacy, and those that invoke a branching structure of possibilities—fare against the list. The aim is the spec itself: a clear map of what we should demand of any answer to the problem before we evaluate particular answers.
