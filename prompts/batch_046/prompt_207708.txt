In debates about Zygmunt Bauman’s argument that modern rational bureaucracy made the Holocaust possible, a common analogy compares the camp system to an industrial factory: division of labor, efficiency metrics, and instrumental rationality are said to map onto the genocide’s organization, licensing the conclusion that this was not a premodern atrocity but a product of modern civilization itself. In a seminar I recently attended, a participant extended this analogy by proposing that the camp and transit network functioned less like a stockpiling factory and more like a just-in-time logistics hub—victims moved continuously through nodes with minimal warehousing, throughput was optimized, overhead was minimized, and the system behaved like lean supply-chain management applied to annihilation. From this, the participant inferred that modernity’s specific contribution was not merely bureaucratic detachment but the rationality of continuous-flow logistics, making the Holocaust structurally closer to contemporary global supply chains than to traditional mass violence.
