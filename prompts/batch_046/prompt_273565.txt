I'm working through a conceptual puzzle about personal identity under gradual mind uploading, and I keep returning to the idea of the self not as a substance but as a strange loop: a symbol or representational structure that refers back to the very system that produces it. I want to test whether my intuitions about survival across different replacement regimes actually cohere, but I'm not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—just for a principle, or a tight family of candidates, that captures the pattern, together with predictions for cases I haven't listed and an honest statement of which verdicts have to be abandoned.

Here are my judgments on six concrete scenarios. (i) Over a decade, every neuron is replaced by a synthetic analogue that preserves synaptic weights, firing dynamics, and global connectivity; the self-referential loop updates continuously, and the subject reports an unbroken first-person perspective. I think the original self survives—this is genuine continuity, not replication. (ii) The brain is scanned at high resolution, the biological original is destroyed, and an identical digital replica is activated on a distant server. The loop is reconstructed with the same internal content, but there is no causal bridge linking the biological loop to the digital one. I think this is not survival but copying; the original is dead, and the replica is a successor with false memories of being continuous. (iii) Replacement proceeds in discrete monthly blocks, but between the destruction of an old block and the integration of the new one, the loop's state is saved to an external buffer and then reloaded after a forty-eight-hour gap during which no substrate is running the loop. Even though the content is perfectly preserved, I feel survival is broken—the reloaded loop is a reconstructed successor, not the same running loop. (iv) Two subjects undergo parallel replacement; at the point where each is fifty percent synthetic, their substrates are merged into a single system. The resulting loop references both prior histories with equal weight and claims a unified "I" that points to both origins. I think neither original survives; a new loop with bifurcated ancestry has emerged. (v) Full replacement succeeds, but the synthetic loop is hard-coded so that its self-reference always points to the now-destroyed biological brain as its sole legitimate origin, even though no biological tissue remains. The loop functions, plans, and remembers, but its anchor is factually false. I am inclined to say the person survives, yet only as a kind of anchored fiction, and I am uneasy treating false-content continuity as full-blooded survival. (vi) At the final stage, the loop is accidentally duplicated: two causally isolated but structurally identical loops run in parallel, both claiming to be the original. I think duplication destroys uniqueness; both are copies, and the original died at the moment of branching.

Is there one account of identity through gradual replacement—perhaps centered on causal continuity of the loop, on non-branching uniqueness, on the accuracy of self-reference, or on some combination—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which principle saves the most and forces me to overturn the least intuitive case? And what would your favored principle predict for a scenario I haven't considered: gradual replacement in which the loop's symbolic form is not preserved but slowly translated, over years, from a singular "I" into a distributed, plural self-reference ("we are the system") that still tracks a single continuous history?
