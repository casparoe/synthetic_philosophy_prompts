In the United Kingdom, Sweden, and Japan, constitutional conventions require that hereditary monarchs perform formal state acts—giving royal assent to legislation, appointing prime ministers, and dissolving parliaments—yet they must do so exclusively on the binding advice of elected ministers, and in Japan and Sweden they possess virtually no discretionary authority whatsoever. At the same time, defenders present these offices not as decorative relics but as structurally necessary: they are said to embody the continuity of the state, to stand above party politics, and to provide a non-partisan locus of sovereignty that democratic institutions alone cannot supply. Meanwhile, these same democracies have systematically eliminated hereditary or unelected authority everywhere else in government. What implicit theory of sovereignty, representation, and democratic legitimacy does this institutional design presuppose? Does it require a substantive distinction between the state and the government, with the monarch representing a pre-political or trans-political source of authority that elected bodies merely exercise? Or can the same practice be explained by purely symbolic or path-dependent reasons that presuppose no particular political philosophy? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the convention strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and say whether a consistently democratic republic—one that eliminated the crown entirely and placed its formal functions in an elected or rotating office—would have to reject that theory or could absorb its practical benefits without contradiction.
