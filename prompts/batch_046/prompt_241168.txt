I've constructed an argument that extends Hannah Arendt's account of political lying into the territory of pseudohistorical revisionism, and the conclusion now strikes me as intolerable. I want to know exactly which step fails, though I am especially reluctant to surrender (1) or (9).

(1) For Arendt, political lying is destructive not because it asserts falsehoods in general, but because it specifically undermines factual truths—contingent, witnessed events that depend on collective testimony and form the shared basis of public life.

(2) Fictional narratives present false contingent events, yet they do not damage this shared basis, because they are framed as art rather than as testimony; so the harm lies not in the false content itself but in the mode of presentation—claiming to correct the collective record.

(3) Therefore, any discourse that adopts the apparatus of testimony or scholarship to present false contingent events as corrected history inflicts the same structural damage to the common world that Arendt attributes to state-sponsored lies.

(4) Pseudohistorical revisionism—chronologies that deny the established timeline of ancient civilizations and present themselves as rigorous scholarly corrections—does precisely this.

(5) By (2) and (3), such revisionism is not merely erroneous but politically toxic in the same structural sense as propaganda: it erodes the factual ground required for collective judgment.

(6) But legitimate historiographical revision also denies prior collective attestation and claims to correct the record; there is no non-circular criterion within Arendt's framework to distinguish a valid revision from a pseudohistorical one, since both challenge the existing consensus of witnesses and records.

(7) Hence Arendt's view either licenses the suppression of all revisionist history in order to protect the common world, or it must concede that the danger of lying is not structural but depends on an independent moral feature—namely, the deceiver's intent.

(8) If we take the second option and make intent decisive, then (1) is false: the damage is not an objective assault on the shared fabric of facts but a betrayal of trust between speaker and audience.

(9) Yet Arendt explicitly resists reducing political lying to an individual moral failing, insisting that its danger is precisely structural and independent of the liar's private motives.

(10) So the argument proves too much: it forces a choice between endorsing censorship of historical revision and abandoning the structural account that makes Arendt's view distinctive.

If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (9) than accept (10). Where does the argument go wrong? Is there a hidden equivocation between "testimony" and "scholarship," a scope shift in how collective attestation operates across disciplines, or a modal slide between "damages the common world" and "should be suppressed"? Please identify which premise or step to reject and price the alternatives—especially whether saving Arendt's view requires importing a criterion she explicitly rejects.
