As a novelist accustomed to tracing how institutions silently shape the lives within them, I find myself returning to the architecture of the modern research university: the division of knowledge into departmental silos, the allocation of credit by standardized hours, and the examination systems that reward the accurate reproduction of discrete propositions rather than any transformation in the student’s capacity to relate, feel, or create. This arrangement is presented as administratively necessary, yet it seems to carry a hidden ontology. What philosophical commitments does it presuppose about the nature of knowledge, the learner, and the world? Must it assume a substance-based metaphysics in which subjects are separable entities and learning is the accumulation of static facts, or could a relational, process-oriented view—such as Whitehead’s, with its claim that reality is constituted by prehensive connections and that education should move in a cyclical rhythm through romance, precision, and generalization—license the same institutional form? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in departmental boundaries and assessment rubrics: distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely suits it well, and examine whether its tendency to treat knowledge as inert—disconnected from the student’s lived experience and from other domains—is an unavoidable feature or a contingent habit. Then describe what a university genuinely organized around Whiteheadian principles would have to change, particularly in its treatment of aesthetic experience not as ornament but as a primary mode of knowing, and in its refusal to separate pure inquiry from applied engagement. In your answer, please indicate how confident you are in each reconstruction, and single out the specific claim—whether about Whitehead’s metaphysics, the interpretation of disciplinary boundaries, or the feasibility of institutional change—of which you are least sure.
