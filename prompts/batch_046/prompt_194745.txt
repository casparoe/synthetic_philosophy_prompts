Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the Enlightenment-on-trial lens—specifcally the idea that instrumental reason doesn't just exclude the other but produces it as a threat it must then suppress (the Horkheimer/Adorno line). I'm interested in the creature's self-education with Milton and Plutarch and his demand for a mate: does the novel show that enlightenment learning creates a rational subject who then demands rights, and that Victor's refusal is structurally necessary to the project of mastering nature? Or does Victor's visceral horror at the creature's body point to something the Dialectic misses—maybe that domination is aesthetic and bodily, not only rational? I want a reading that takes both the text and the philosophy seriously, not just using the creature as a symbol for the proletariate. Where does the metaphor hold, and where does the book resist being reduced to it?
