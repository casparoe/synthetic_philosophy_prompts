As a novelist, I often find that arguments unfolding over late-night dinners provide richer material for fiction than any plot I could invent alone. Last autumn, I hosted a small gathering for a philosopher and a neuroscientist, and the conversation turned—rather improbably—to Avicenna's flying man. The philosopher began by sketching the scenario in its usual form: a fully formed human being suspended in empty space, eyes covered, limbs separated, never having experienced bodily sensation, yet still able to affirm that he exists. From this immediate self-awareness, she argued, Avicenna establishes that the soul is essentially an immaterial substance capable of knowing itself without any mediation through the body. My other guest, the neuroscientist, interrupted with a counter-reading drawn from embodied cognition: the flying man's affirmation is not proof of a substantial soul but merely a pre-reflective awareness of presence, a bare phenomenological given that requires no metaphysical commitment to dualism; and since Avicenna is broadly a hylomorphist who treats the soul as the form of the body rather than a separate ghost, the argument is better read as showing that self-awareness is immediate and non-inferential, not that it can subsist apart from matter.

The philosopher conceded the hylomorphic framework but insisted that the flying man demonstrates precisely that this form can operate in awareness independently of the matter it ordinarily organizes, since all bodily sensation and memory have been stripped away and yet awareness remains. The neuroscientist replied that this interpretation misreads the argument's purpose and illicitly assumes a fully formed cognitive architecture—complete with concepts of self and existence—while simultaneously denying the embodied history through which such concepts are acquired; on this view, the scenario is conceptually incoherent rather than metaphysically revelatory, because without prior embodied interaction there would be no conceptual apparatus to produce the thought "I exist" at all. The philosopher then offered a sharper rejoinder: if awareness were genuinely embodied, then the complete absence of bodily input should yield no awareness whatsoever, yet Avicenna claims it does, which means the awareness must belong to something whose operation does not depend on the body. The neuroscientist countered that one can grant an immediate, non-inferential sense of being present without granting that this presence belongs to a substance separable from the organism, comparing it to the way a single note can be heard in isolation even though music as such requires an instrument.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess the philosopher's claim that the argument proves substantial independence, the neuroscientist's appeal to embodied cognition and hylomorphism, the philosopher's rejoinder about stripped-away awareness, and the neuroscientist's charge of conceptual incoherence. Say who is more right overall, but also specify what would change your mind—whether a more precise reading of Avicenna's text, a logical clarification about the conditions for self-reference, or an empirical consideration about minimal selfhood—and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Write in American English as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a thoughtful letter written to an old friend.
