I've been trying to understand why I actively seek out sad movies and books, and I think I've built an explanation that collapses if I push it one step further. Here is the argument, laid out carefully. (1) If an experience is genuinely painful, then rational people avoid it unless it produces a greater good that clearly outweighs the pain. (2) Tragedy produces real sadness and grief, not merely pretend feelings; I leave the theater with a heavy chest and a lingering sense of loss, not just amusement. (3) So tragedy must provide some compensating good that outweighs that real pain. (4) The best explanation I can find is emotional rehearsal: by safely feeling grief and fear in a fictional frame, I build emotional capacity for real crises, like practicing fire drills without the actual fire. (5) But if rehearsal value is what justifies the pain, then any negative emotion should be worth rehearsing, including ones I almost never face—pure moral disgust, the specific shame of public failure, or existential dread without narrative resolution. (6) In fact, if the only conditions are intensity and safety, there should be a thriving genre of art designed purely to induce shame or disgust, and I should seek it out for the same reason I seek out tragedy. (7) There is no such thriving genre, and I actively avoid art that makes me feel genuinely ashamed or disgusted, even when I know it is entirely fictional and physically safe. (8) So either rehearsal is not the real reason, or it applies selectively in a way that simply restates my preferences rather than explaining them. (9) But if I drop the rehearsal idea entirely, I am left saying that I pursue real emotional pain for no compensating reason, which seems plainly irrational. (10) Therefore either I am irrational, or tragedy does not really hurt, which I know from direct experience is false. Where does this chain break? I would prefer to keep (2) and (4) if possible, because they match my experience closely, but I need to know exactly what saving them costs and whether I must accept some hidden principle that distinguishes grief from disgust.
