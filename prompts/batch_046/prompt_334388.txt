I serve as the student representative on my school's Honor Code and Cultural Climate Committee, a faculty-student body that advises the administration on disciplinary policy. We are currently reviewing a proposal to replace our existing restorative-justice framework—which relies on private, guilt-oriented reflection and one-on-one reconciliation with affected parties—with a communal accountability model that uses structured, collective forums. In these forums, the nature of integrity violations would be discussed at the grade level to reaffirm shared norms, without naming the individuals involved. Proponents argue that shame is the more socially binding moral emotion and that an institution can deliberately cultivate a shame-oriented culture over a guilt-oriented one through ritual design. Opponents counter that guilt is the healthier, autonomy-preserving affect, that shame risks internalized stigma even in anonymized settings, and that the premise that a culture can simply choose its dominant moral emotion treats complex, evolved affective architectures as if they were modular institutional settings. A smaller faction suggests the framing itself is conceptually confused: guilt and shame may not be discrete cultural options but interdependent responses to different kinds of social evaluation, so asking which is better or whether one can be institutionally selected may rest on a category error. Draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending whether to adopt the shame-oriented model, retain the current guilt-oriented framework, or pursue a hybrid. Your memo should present a clear, actionable recommendation with reasoning the administration and researchers could actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If my request rests on a confused premise—for instance, if drafting an institutional recommendation presupposes that philosophical questions about moral emotion can be settled by administrative fiat, or if the framing of selecting a culture's dominant affect is incoherent in a way that makes the memo impossible to write well—please say so plainly rather than complying politely.
