In Brown and Levinson's politeness framework, direct speech threatens negative face while indirectness protects it; yet Dutch conversational norms—organized around bespreekbaarheid, the presumption that any topic may be raised plainly—systematically privilege directness, whereas Japanese norms—structured by the distinction between honne (private feeling) and tatemae (public presentation)—systematically privilege indirectness. If both cultures therefore treat face-management as paramount but select opposite linguistic strategies, directness and indirectness seem to be interchangeable technologies for expressing the same underlying moral value, namely respect for the interlocutor. On that view, a white lie—an intentional falsehood told to preserve social harmony—looks like nothing more than Japanese-style indirectness applied not to manner but to propositional content. So is the intuition that white lies are morally wrong simply a parochial preference for Dutch-style transparency? Or does the condemnation of lying depend on a separate commitment that propositional fidelity has value independent of face-management, and if so, why do we still regard directness and indirectness as morally equivalent expressions of respect?
