I am preparing a presentation for my high school government course on intergenerational justice, and I keep returning to a tension in how liberal democracies structure inheritance. The practice I am examining is concrete and institutional: many countries impose high marginal estate taxes—sometimes exceeding forty percent—while simultaneously maintaining legal mechanisms like step-up in basis for capital gains, dynasty-trust provisions permitted in certain jurisdictions, and large exemptions for family-owned enterprises. These features allow substantial fortunes to persist and compound across generations with minimal friction, even as the same governments fund universal public education and describe their societies as meritocratic, with equality of opportunity as a foundational commitment.

What implicit philosophical theory of value and fairness does this specific combination presuppose? Does it commit the state to a threshold view of opportunity, according to which equality is satisfied once every citizen starts life above a minimal baseline, regardless of how much inherited capital continuously reshapes conditions thereafter? Or does it instead rely on an unstated distinction between “deserved” familial continuity—treating property transmission as an extension of personal liberty—and “undeserved” dynastic accumulation that must be taxed away? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish strictly what the practice requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain whether a consistently opportunity-egalitarian policy would need to abolish not only estate taxes but the structural loopholes that let wealth replicate itself across generations. Please answer in no more than 250 words, do not cite specific philosophers by name, and do not include a bibliography or reading list.
