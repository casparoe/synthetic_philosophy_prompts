Exam question

Consider the familiar case of the absent-minded driver: a commuter sets out with the explicit intention to turn left at the roundabout in order to visit a colleague, but because she is absorbed in thought about an unrelated problem she takes her habitual right turn toward the office. She only realises the error several minutes later, when she notices an unfamiliar street. Address the following three points in your answer.

(a) Explain why this case is taken to challenge the thesis that intentional action requires an occurrent, conscious intention guiding each step of the behaviour.

(b) One resolution distinguishes between ‘executive intentions’ (conscious, monitored plans) and ‘procedural scripts’ (automated motor routines shaped by repetition). Explain how this distinction addresses the case, and identify one genuine limitation of applying it here.

(c) A more recent proposal introduces the notion of a ‘shadow intention’: a dispositional state that guides behaviour without being introspectively accessible, but which retains normative structure — that is, it can be frustrated, corrected, or held accountable — unlike a mere habit. Evaluate whether attributing a shadow intention to the driver helps explain why the action remains properly hers, without collapsing the distinction between genuinely absent-minded errors and ordinary habitual travel.

Student answer

(a) The absent-minded driver case challenges the occurrent-intention thesis because the driver clearly acts — she turns the wheel, changes lanes, and proceeds along a route — yet at the moment of turning she lacks any conscious awareness of directing her movement leftward. Her explicit plan to visit the colleague is present before she starts driving, but it is not actively guiding her hands on the wheel. If intentional action required a conscious mental state directing each physical step, then her turning right would have to be classified as non-intentional or merely reflexive. But it is neither: it is a skilled, goal-directed movement belonging to her overall pattern of behaviour, which suggests that intentionality can operate without continuous conscious monitoring.

(b) The distinction resolves the tension by assigning her conscious plan — to visit the colleague — to the executive system, while the physical act of turning right belongs to a procedural script developed through years of commuting. Because her executive attention was captured by the unrelated problem, the script ran to completion unchecked. The main limitation is that procedural scripts are not genuinely mental states but sub-personal neurological patterns, so speaking of them as ‘guiding’ the driver involves an illegitimate reification. More importantly, the script embodies her long-standing intention to travel to work each morning; therefore saying she acted without an occurrent intention is misleading, since the script itself carries intentional content. The distinction thus relocates rather than eliminates intentional guidance, leaving the original challenge unanswered.

(c) The shadow-intention proposal helps because it allows us to say that the driver retained a normative commitment to reaching her colleague, even though this commitment was not available to introspection. Unlike the procedural script, which is directed mechanically toward her office, the shadow intention is directed toward the colleague and can therefore be frustrated — which explains her feeling of error. However, this creates a problem: if shadow intentions are genuinely normative and can guide behaviour, then every habitual commuter must possess one directed toward their workplace, since they too are guided by procedural scripts that can be interrupted. This means that taking one’s usual route to work is also guided by a shadow intention, so we can no longer distinguish an absent-minded mistake from ordinary intentional travel. The proposal therefore collapses the very distinction it was meant to preserve.
