ive been chewing on Bentham's sexual ethics lately — the line that conventional rules about sex are mostly just irrational prejudice, and all that really matters is whether an act produces pleasure or pain according to his dimensional calculus. It feels convincing whenever you notice that taboos against homosexuality or consensual non-monogamy don't map onto suffering in any direct way. But I'm stuck on a weirder objection: maybe sexual norms function less like harm-rules and more like coordination devices — Schelling points that stabilize long-term cooperative reproduction by locking in shared expectations about commitment. If so, then Bentham's framework treats preferences and social structures as exogenus inputs to the calculus, when in fact the norms partly constitute the stable preference-sets that make cooperative child-rearing work. That'd mean his harm-only view misses a kind of systemic fragility it can't measure.

So here's the position I want stress-tested: Bentham's harm-only sexual ethics is basically right, and appeals to 'social stability' or tradition are just status-quo bias unless they can show a concrete utility loss that survives his intensity-duration-certainty analysis. What would actually count against it? I want arguments or evidence that should move me — not just disgust responses, but maybe formal results from evolutionary game theory showing norm-erosion collapses cooperative networks, or historical cases where liberalization produced coordination failures that Bentham's calculus can't capture because it takes the preference landscape as fixed. Also, has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability — like, every counterexample gets redescribed as 'hidden harm'? And if so, is that fatal for a utilitarian position? I'd especially like to know what defenders of Bentham's sexual liberalism themselves concede as a real defeater, versus what's just noise.
