I have recently found myself defending a sharpened separability thesis in legal philosophy—specifically, that Fuller's eight principles of legality and the grudge-informer problem illustrate institutional efficacy conditions rather than an "inner morality" of law, and that treating them as moral constraints commits a category error analogous to treating chess rules as ethical norms merely because violating them collapses the game. On my view, retroactive punishment fails not because it violates moral law but because it undermines the predictive function that makes legal direction possible; the Nazi informant statute was therefore defective as law only in the sense that a broken calculator is defective, not in the sense that an unjust act is wrong. What would have to be true for this efficacy-only reading to be wrong? I want decisive defeaters separated from merely awkward ones: for instance, whether there are clear cases of fully efficacious yet radically immoral legal systems that function without internal contradiction, or whether the concept of efficacy in collective action already smuggles in normative commitments that collapse my distinction. Which of these would proponents of this view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as confusion between morality and functional success? Rank them from fatal to merely inconvenient, and say whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—and if so, whether that is a problem for a conceptual claim of this kind.
