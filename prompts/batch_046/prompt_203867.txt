Please provide a careful conceptual explanation of Russell's paradox that moves well beyond the familiar popular-science formulation. First, articulate precisely why naive comprehension—the principle that every well-defined property determines a set of things satisfying it—collapses when applied to the property of non-self-membership, and clarify whether the contradiction arises from the property itself, from treating the totality of sets as itself a set, or from an interaction of both. Second, contrast the two principal containment strategies: restricting comprehension by stratifying or axiomatizing which conditions are legitimate (thereby blocking the formation of the problematic collection altogether) versus allowing the collection to exist but classifying it as a proper class that cannot be a member of anything, including itself. Third, examine whether either strategy genuinely resolves the underlying philosophical difficulty about unrestricted quantification—specifically, whether drawing a formal boundary between sets and classes can be done without implicitly presupposing a domain of discourse that already encompasses the boundary-drawing mechanism itself. In your analysis, distinguish sharply between the technical achievement of avoiding contradiction and the conceptual achievement of explaining why self-applicability of membership is illegitimate, harmless, or conceptually confused. Conclude by assessing which containment strategy, if either, addresses the problem of self-applicability rather than merely relocating it to a different level of the ontology.
