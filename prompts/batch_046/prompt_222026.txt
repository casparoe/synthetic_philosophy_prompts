I'm delivering the eulogy at the memorial for Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a philosopher of comparative religion who died last month. The audience is split: family and old friends who never read philosophy, and colleagues and former students who spent years debating her work. Amara's central claim—developed across her essays on the Zhuangzi—was that death should be understood not as a metaphysical consolation in which the dead are transformed into something larger, but as an ethical dissolution of the boundary between mourner and mourned. She called this "the abolition of the mourner": the idea that to grieve as if the dead have been taken away from us is to cling to a false separation between self and other, living and dead. She insisted this made love more demanding, not less, because it stripped away the alibi of distance. Her family asked explicitly that I not say she is watching over us; her students asked that I not reduce her view to a platitude about nature's cycles. Write a five-minute eulogy that takes her interpretation as its structural backbone—showing what it asks of those who remain, honoring its difficulty rather than softening it, and making the idea legible to both halves of the room without turning it into false comfort or an academic lecture.
