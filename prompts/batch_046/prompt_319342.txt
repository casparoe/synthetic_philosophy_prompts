I'm a retired physicist who recently started reading philosophy for fun and got kind of obsessed with Bostrom's simluation argument and all the longtermist reasoning that spins out of it. Last week I was arguing with friends about whether being inside a simulation should make us less fanatical about the far future, and I noticed a recurring move that I don't have a word for.

The pattern goes like this. Someone invokes simulations to expand moral concern horizontally: they say if digital minds are cheap then there are vastly more existant simulated minds than biological ones, so we ought to care about future digital suffering and include them in our calculus. But in the same breath they stay totally fanatical about preserving base-reality conditions for billions of years, acting as though the chance we're in a temporary simulation doesn't contract the temporal stakes at all. They apply the argument to blow up who counts, but suppress it when it would shrink when or whether the real universe's far future actually matters. I've seen this in a couple essays and podcasts -- one writer said simulations mean we should prioritize virtual welfare, then immediately argued we need to safeguard Earth for ten billion years without ever addressing the tension. It feels more specific than generic motivated reasoning, because it's directional: one axis gets amplified, the other gets filtered out.

Does this configuration have an established name? I know about confirmation bias and scope neglect, but those don't capture the selective deployment of a single argument across different dimensions. If there's no term, can you carve out exactly what belongs and what doesn't -- what's the boundary between this and ordinary hypocrisy, and what would you call it? Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. I don't need citations or a reading list -- just the conceptual map, since I can't look up obscure literature from my armchair.
