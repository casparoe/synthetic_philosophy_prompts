While reporting on the history of scientific visualization, I have been returning to Otto Neurath's picture-language project and its ambition to replace linguistic ambiguity with directly readable, cross-cultural symbols. I find myself holding a set of judgments about modern representational practices that I suspect should hang together, but I cannot tell whether they do. Standardized chemical-hazard pictograms—the flame, the skull, the corrosive liquid—strike me as genuinely successful in Neurath's terms: they travel across languages and seem to be grasped perceptually rather than decoded. By contrast, the elaborate icon sets now used in synthetic-biology protocols, where a double helix overlaid with a scissor denotes CRISPR-mediated excision, feel like failures, despite being pictographic and international, because they demand extensive domain-specific interpretation rather than immediate recognition. At the same time, I judge that simplified geometric glyphs derived from neural-network attention heat-maps, when used in public-facing reports to represent invisible computational processes, do succeed, even though they are highly mediated representations of entities no one can observe directly. Meanwhile, the small icon arrays that museum curators deploy to summarize archaeological stratigraphy—stacked triangles for sediment layers, circles for artifacts—seem to me to fail, not because they are ambiguous, but because they quietly embed theoretical commitments about causal sequence that Neurath wanted visual languages to avoid. Airport wayfinding symbols, such as the running figure or the baggage-claim icon, appear closer to the chemical-pictogram successes than to the synthetic-biology failures, yet I am unsure whether that is because they depict actions rather than theoretical entities, or simply because they are more austere.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing perceptual immediacy from theoretical loading, or action-representation from entity-representation—that can accommodate all of these verdicts without collapsing into ad hoc distinctions? Or does consistency force me to overturn one of them, and if so, which? And if such a principle can be articulated precisely, what should it predict about a case I have not yet settled: the proposed ISO standard for quantum-error-correction codes that represents logical qubit states through nested hexagonal frames?
