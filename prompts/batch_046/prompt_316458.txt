I'm a novelist running this weird prison literacy program — every other month I have to adapt a real person's life into a forty-page novel for guys inside, and I'm tired of accidentally writing hagiograpy. I was reading about Plutarch's paired biographies and how moral learning actually works through biography, and I think the key is something like 'failure visibility': the reader has to see the subject's actual moral mistakes in high resolution, not compressed into stepping stones for later grace. There's this asymetry of exemplarity — we learn more from lives that nearly went wrong than from ones that just went right — but hagiography does the opposite, it applies moral compression so every flaw becomes proof of destiny. I need a reusable proceedure, like a flowchart in words with test questions in order, that I can run in about ninety minutes before I sign off on a manuscript. No research staff, just me and my judgment, and the stakes feel high because if I smooth over the wrong failure these guys will think virtue is just something saints are born with.

The proceedure should ask the right things in sequence: first, is there a documented failure that the subject actually faced rather than invented? Second, does the narrative arc let a reader see that failure as a choice with real alternatives, not as fate? Third, is the failure visible enough that an incarcerated reader won't mistake it for a saint's stumble, but not so horrific that it becomes a how-to guide? At each step say what counts as enough and where it stops with a hard no. Then flag the cases where it has to hand back to me because it's values — like whether a particular crime is too close to what some readers have done themselves. Apply it to three cases: a nurse who secretly euthanized dying patients against the law, a union leader who sold out a strike then spent decades making amends, and a civil rights organizer whose private betrayals hurt people near her. I want one of those to come back with a recommendation I probably won't like. Also be honest about what the proceedure can't do — where it's just my taste in a trenchcoat — and don't ask me for citations or reading lists, I literally don't have time.
