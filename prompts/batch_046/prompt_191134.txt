For an independent study in philosophy I have tried to formalize a spectrum argument against the transitivity of “better than,” but the result seems to prove far more than I intended—possibly that extreme concentration of well-being is morally mandatory. I want to know exactly which premise is the weak link and what abandoning it costs.

(1) Consider a continuous family of worlds W(t) indexed by a real parameter t in [0,1]. At t = 0 every person enjoys an identical baseline well-being; as t increases, total well-being rises continuously while no individual ever falls below her baseline—this is pure Pareto improvement across the spectrum.

(2) For any two points s < t, W(t) is strictly better than W(s), since everyone is at least as well off and the aggregate is greater.

(3) If “better than” is transitive over this family, then W(1)—the world of maximal aggregate well-being with the same baseline for all—is strictly better than W(0).

(4) Now construct a second, orthogonal family U(u) inside W(1), where u ∈ [0,1] redistributes the fixed total of W(1) with imperceptible shifts toward one individual, each step still being Pareto-superior within the affected sub-population. At u = 1, one person holds all surplus well-being; everyone else remains exactly at the baseline.

(5) By the same reasoning as (2), U(1) is best within its family. And if transitivity holds not only within families but across them—since W(1) is the origin point of U—then U(1) is strictly better than W(0).

(6) But U(1) is morally repugnant: it licenses total concentration provided the baseline is untouched. If the argument is sound, then any world that is not U(1) is impermissible whenever U(1) is available, which collapses ethics into a single maximization point.

(7) I am committed to keeping (1) and (2); I also want to preserve transitivity in ordinary cases. I suspect the slide occurs either in (3)—applying transitivity across an infinite or continuous index—or in (5)—illegitimately crossing from intra-family comparison to inter-family comparison. But I cannot pinpoint the error, and I worry there may be a hidden equivocation between aggregate “better than” and distributive “better for.”

Which step should I reject? If I save transitivity by blocking (5), what does that do to our ability to compare outcomes across different policy dimensions? And if the flaw is instead in (3), why does continuity seem to break transitivity here when it does not in discrete cases?
