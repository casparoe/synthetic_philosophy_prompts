I'm running a philosophy seminar for grad students, about 11 people, 80 minutes, and we keep circling the meta-problem of consciousness without anyone actually feeling it in their bones. I want an activity where they generate the "hard problem" judgment themselves, before anyone says Chalmers or mentions explanatory gaps. Could you design a classroom game?

Split them into four groups of three and hand out "explanation packets" for four mental processes — thermoregulation, synactic parsing, visual edge detection, and the subjective experience of crimson. Every packet has the exact same layout: neural mechanism, evolutionary payoff, predictive accuracy. The groups have eight minutes to read, then each group secretly votes "case closed" or "mystery remains" and writes one sentence why. You collect the cards and reveal the split; the crimson group should almost always land on "mystery" even though they can't point to anything structurally missing from that packet that isn't also missing from thermoregulation. Then swap packets and force every group to defend the opposite verdict in round two.

For the debrief, map the first-round split onto the meta-problem — it's not that crimson is under-explained, it's that our explanatory standards shift — and ask them to notice they produced the gap rather than discovered it. I want you to smuggle in a niche wrinkle without naming it: in the crimson packet, include a line about phenomenal overflow, saying that reportable access tracks only a fraction of the total phenomenal field, but don't cite Block or call it by name. Include materials list (just printed cards and a timer), timeline, and an honest note about what the game can't reproduce — probably the first-person transparency that makes the judgment feel like direct seeing rather than comparative bias. Also warn me what goes wrong first time: likely one student names the hard problem in minute five, or groups fixate on complexity instead of subjectivity.
