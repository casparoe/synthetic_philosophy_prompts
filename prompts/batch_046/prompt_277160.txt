I work as a case coordinator for a local restorative-justice programme in Leeds — we recieve referals from magistrates for minor assaults and property damage, and I have about thirty minutes per file to decide whether the offender goes through our restorative track (where we treat them as a moral interlocutor), gets diverted to a social-work or clinical track (objective attitude), or is sent back for formal prosecution, with anything hitting a values-judgement point signed off by the senior mediator. I want a reusable decision procedre based on Strawson’s reactive-attitudes framework, but with a twist that takes it into the weeds: I want it to distinguish not just participant reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude) from objective attitudes, but also “bracketed reactive attitudes” — where you still address the person as responsible and keep them inside the moral community, but suspend the emotional force because there is an epistemic gap about whether they genuinely grasped the normative reason for the wrong. I also want it to flag vicarious reactive attitudes — when I’m reacting on behalf of the victim rather than as the wronged party — since that changes what’s appropriate.

Please write me a checklist or flowchart-in-words my team could actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedre stops with “no — not this track,” and where it must hand over to human judgement because the issue is values rather than facts. Make sure it handles hard cases: mental-health exemptions, cultural differences in expressing remorse, and situations where the victim wants dialogue but the offender seems incapable of understanding it. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a first-time offender who smashed a neighbour’s fence in a drunken rage and is now genuinely remorseful; (2) an offender with untreated schizophrenia who pushed someone during a paranoid episode and shows no grasp of wrongdoing; and (3) a repeat offender who seems sorry only after being caught, where the victim is demanding punishment rather than dialogue — this should be the case where the procedre gives an answer I’ll probably dislike. Be honest about what the procedre can’t do.
