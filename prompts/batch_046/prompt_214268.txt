At our philosophy seminar, two participants—Marcus and Elena—debated whether Parfit-style nonidentity considerations defeat obligations to mitigate climate change for future generations. Marcus opened by arguing that if our high-emissions choices bring into existence different people who live good lives, we have harmed no one, since those particular individuals depend for their very existence on the choices that also cause climate damage. Elena replied that this reasoning proves too much: on the same logic, parents who intentionally select for a serious but non-fatal genetic condition in their child have done nothing wrong to the child, who likewise owes her existence to that choice, yet we clearly regard such selection as wrongful. Marcus distinguished the cases, claiming that climate policy affects a diffuse, indefinite population without specific victims, whereas reproductive selection involves a direct, intentional act with an identifiable bearer of the condition. Elena rejected this as ad hoc, insisting that collective climate choices are equally intentional and that if we can wrong specific people through selection, we can wrong the collective pattern of people brought about by a harmful institutional trajectory, even if no single individual within that set is comparatively worse off. Marcus then shifted ground: the wrong in reproductive selection is not non-comparative harm to the child but a breach of parental duty of care towards whichever child results; climate policymakers, however, stand in no such relational duty to unspecified future strangers, so the nonidentity problem genuinely dissolves climate-based obligations. Elena countered that this merely relocates rather than resolves the difficulty: if there is no victim in the climate case, then there is no corresponding bearer of the duty, leaving Marcus with a practical conclusion—mitigation may still be justified on other grounds—without a philosophical account of why climate choices are wrong that survives the nonidentity challenge.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of Marcus's and Elena's contributions from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating; say who is more right overall; and identify what both parties miss—especially whether there is any coherent way to maintain that high-emission climate pathways are wrongful without presupposing that the actual people who exist under them are the ones harmed. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
