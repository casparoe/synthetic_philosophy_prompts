As a software engineer working on autonomous decision systems that can revise their own policies, I have been trying to understand a formal argument—often associated with Löb's theorem—that there is a structural obstacle preventing a sufficiently rational, self-reflective agent from fully trusting its own future reasoning. The position, as I understand it, holds that if an agent is powerful enough to recognize that it will only accept a conclusion once it has a proof of that conclusion, then it cannot consistently assign full confidence to that very pattern holding for its own future outputs without encountering a kind of self-referential instability. I recently tried to map this onto system architecture: imagine a deployment pipeline in which the build script not only verifies the next release but also rewrites its own verification rules, and yet is expected to treat its rewritten self as fully authoritative. The obstacle seems to suggest that no amount of engineering redundancy—redundant monitors, formal-methods checkers, or distributed consensus—can overcome the logical block, because the issue is not empirical failure but the structure of self-reference itself. I am inclined to take this seriously, but I want to stress-test the view rather than accept it as settled.

What would actually count against this position? I am not looking for standard skeptical objections that merely shift the burden; I want an honest map of its exposure. Which of the underlying assumptions—say, about the agent's logical omniscience, the identification of belief with provability, or the applicability of the theorem to probabilistic or resource-bounded reasoning—could fail, and what would the world (or the system) have to look like for each failure? In particular, I have been wondering whether the obstacle dissolves if we replace the classical proof-based framework with a bounded, resource-rational model in which an agent assigns graded confidence to future sub-modules—more like a microservice architecture with timeout-based fallbacks and probabilistic health checks than like a single theorem prover verifying its own outputs. Would defenders of the Löbian obstacle concede that such a framing undermines the result, or would they dismiss it as changing the subject? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender would themselves acknowledge as genuine pressure points, and assess whether the view has been qualified so heavily—say, by restricting it to idealized agents or to particular logical languages—that it risks becoming unfalsifiable, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a claim with this kind of scope.
