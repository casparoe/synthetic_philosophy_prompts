Having spent the last year working first in Amsterdam and then in Tokyo, I have become convinced that the moral comparison between Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness is usually drawn too superficially. I want to defend the position that directness is ethically preferable—not merely culturally different—because indirectness, including the white lies it often requires, creates what I call interpretive debt: an asymmetrical cognitive and moral burden placed on the listener to reconstruct unstated intentions without explicit warrant, which undermines the conditions for symmetrical consent and mutual accountability. White lies are therefore not benign social lubricants but structural violations of communicative justice. Please make a rigorous argument for this position, addressing specifically whether the preservation of relational harmony can justify the imposition of interpretive debt, and do so without invoking cultural relativism or citing external authorities.
