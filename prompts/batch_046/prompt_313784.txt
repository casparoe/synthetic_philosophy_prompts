Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on epistemic dependence that now strikes me as overreaching. (1) Genuine understanding of a claim requires an internal grasp of the explanatory path from evidence to conclusion, not merely access to a correct answer. (2) When a person uses an AI to generate an explanation, the inferential path runs through opaque weights and training dynamics that the user does not grasp; the user sees only the prompt and the output. (3) Therefore AI-mediated belief formation produces what I will call borrowed conviction — correct belief without the internal structure that makes it understanding. (4) But the same opacity applies whenever a patient accepts a physician's diagnosis based on clinical trials and biochemical mechanisms she does not personally master, or whenever a student learns calculus from a textbook she cannot derive from axioms. (5) So if (3) holds, then understanding is impossible in any domain that exceeds unaided cognition — that is, in every domain worth knowing. (6) We do not in fact think that medical or scientific understanding has vanished; we think it has been distributed across people and tools. (7) The defender might reply that distribution is precisely the erosion: individual minds now hold hollow beliefs, and collective understanding is a fiction that masks this loss. (8) But that reply makes the concept of expert understanding incoherent, since every specialist depends on instruments, databases, and colleagues she does not fully understand. (9) So either (1) is false — understanding does not require internal grasp of the full path — or (3) is false — AI mediation is not special in eroding it. I want to keep the intuition that there is something genuinely lost when we outsource reasoning to an opaque system, so I am reluctant to abandon (3) entirely. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost me, and is there a way to preserve (3) for AI specifically without falling into (8)?
