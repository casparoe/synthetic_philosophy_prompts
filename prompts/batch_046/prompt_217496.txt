I am writing a paper for a philosophy of medicine course, and I have become convinced that the standard debate between naturalism and normativism about health and disease misses a dimension that should be central: the fit between an organism’s functional architecture and the specific environment it actually inhabits. Rather than defining disease solely as a deviation from species-typical biological functioning—the biostatistical approach associated with thinkers like Christopher Boorse—nor solely as a condition that is harmful or undesirable according to social values, I want to defend what I am calling functional-environmental fit. On this account, a state counts as a disorder when it involves a sustained mismatch between an organism’s intrinsic functional capacities and the demands of its actual environment, where that mismatch systematically undermines the organism’s ability to pursue ends that are genuinely its own. This seems to explain why the same neurological or physiological profile might be severely disabling in one social or physical context yet largely neutral in another, without reducing medicine to pure social construction; at the same time, it avoids medicalizing every statistical outlier by requiring that the mismatch actually impair the organism’s practical agency in its specific setting.

Yet I worry this may be nothing more than an unstable stitching-together of two incompatible traditions, and I want to know exactly where it stands exposed. What would have to be true for the functional-environmental fit view to be wrong? Which clinical cases, empirical discoveries, or theoretical objections would genuinely count against it rather than being merely awkward adjustments? For instance, if we encountered a condition that causes severe suffering and functional limitation purely through hostile social structures—without any underlying deviation from statistical functional norms—would that defeat the view, or could it be absorbed by emphasizing the environmental side of the mismatch? Conversely, if we found a statistically atypical condition that consistently enhances an individual’s pursuit of their own ends across every realistic environment, does my framework wrongly classify it as a disease, or can it accommodate such cases without ad hoc qualification? I also want to know whether the position risks anything distinctive: does it make predictions about classification that pure naturalism and pure normativism do not share, and if so, what evidence would show those predictions to be false?

Please give me an honest assessment of how well-insulated this position actually is. Where does it risk collapsing back into pure naturalism, and where does it risk sliding into pure normativism? What would its own defenders have to concede as genuinely damaging—say, a result showing that environment-relative impairment can never be measured independently of social values, or a case study that splits the functional and environmental components so cleanly that the synthesis becomes redundant? If the view turns out to be protected only by movable qualifications, say so plainly; I am trying to determine whether I should continue defending it or abandon it for a more traditional account, and I want to know what I am actually risking by holding it.
