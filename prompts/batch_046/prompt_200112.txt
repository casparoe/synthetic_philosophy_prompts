I'm currently working through some material on social epistemology for an independent study project, and I keep returning to a tension I think I've misframed. Miranda Fricker's analysis of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice seems to turn on a fairly specific interaction: one speaker suffers a credibility deficit because of a listener's prejudicial stereotype, operating within a shared hermeneutical resource pool that both parties draw upon. From this, I take it that the wrong is essentially dyadic and requires an identifiable epistemic agent whose prejudice directly distorts the exchange.

But I've also been thinking about what I am calling "distributed hermeneutical occlusion"—a scenario I have constructed for my project—where a multinational climate-adaptation database aggregates local agricultural knowledge using an ontology developed entirely in temperate-zone agronomy. The taxonomy has no category for intercropping rhythms tied to monsoon variability, so submissions describing such practices are parsed as noise rather than data. No individual curator holds a prejudice against the submitters; the exclusion is structural, embedded in the classification architecture, and distributed across engineers, funding bodies, and legacy software. If epistemic injustice is essentially the wrong one agent does to another through identity-based credibility deficit, then this case seems to be merely a technical design flaw rather than an injustice. Yet it feels like the same kind of harm—certain knowers are rendered unintelligible, and their capacity to contribute to collective understanding is systematically undermined. So my question is: if we preserve the dyadic, agent-centred core of epistemic injustice, must we conclude that structural, architecture-driven exclusions like this fall outside the concept's scope? Or is there a coherent way to extend the framework that does not dilute its moral specificity into a general complaint about bad data infrastructure? Please reply in British English, and do correct me if my framing rests on a misunderstanding of how the concept is meant to apply.
