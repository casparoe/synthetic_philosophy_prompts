I have been working through the standard framing of anthropic reasoning—specifically the tension between the self-sampling assumption, which directs us to reason as if we were randomly selected from all observers in a reference class, and the self-indication assumption, which weights our location by the total number of observers in each world. In my view, both miss something essential: they treat observer-moments as bare existences to be counted, ignoring that an agent's reasoning is only available to certain kinds of cognitive architectures. I want to defend a modified principle—call it procedural-proximity weighting—which holds that credence over self-location should be distributed not uniformly (as SSA demands) nor by raw population size (as SIA demands), but by the degree to which a candidate observer-moment could have executed the very reasoning procedure I am now running. This is meant to block the presumptuous philosopher's reductio—familiar from discussions of anthropic bias—without buying into SIA's inflationary consequences, because it restricts the reference class to moments structurally capable of hosting this specific inferential process rather than to all conscious beings in a civilization.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or at least seriously weakened? I want an honest map of its exposure: which formal results or conceptual arguments would show that procedural-proximity weighting either collapses back into SSA or SIA once properly formalized; whether the notion of executing the same reasoning procedure can be defined without circular dependence on the very self-location it is meant to constrain; and whether the move genuinely dissolves the presumptuous philosopher problem or merely relocates it to a choice about which procedures count as accessible. Please distinguish between evidence that would be decisive—say, a proof that any weighting scheme avoiding the philosopher's conclusion must be equivalent to SIA under some transformation—from evidence that would merely be awkward, such as intuitive resistance to narrowing the reference class. Say what a defender of this view should themselves concede as genuinely damaging, not only what critics have pressed. Finally, assess whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be absorbed by redrawing the boundary of procedurally accessible moments, is that a fatal methodological flaw for a principle of this kind, or a legitimate feature of reference-class selection?
