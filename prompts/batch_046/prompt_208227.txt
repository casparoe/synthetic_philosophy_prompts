I'm delivering a seminar defense of a modified eliminative-materialist position on propositional attitudes at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind workshop. The audience is precisely composed of five physicalist philosophers of mind—mostly functionalists and type-identity theorists—one cognitive neuroscientist who works on predictive-coding models but remains ontologically agnostic, and two philosophers of language who are concerned that eliminativism erodes the normative framework of rational disagreement. My thesis holds that beliefs and desires are not discrete, causally efficacious internal representations. Instead, I argue that the phenomena folk psychology labels belief should be reconstructed as distributed, precision-weighted prediction-error dynamics across cortical hierarchies, while desire should be reconstructed as an active-inference policy that minimizes expected free energy. I grant that folk-psychological vocabulary functions as a pragmatic macro-scale heuristic—analogous to using center of mass in physics—without tracking natural kinds or providing mechanistic explanations. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: list the seven or eight objections I will actually receive from this specific audience, ordered from the most damaging to the least damaging, each phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework its subgroup would employ. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for about ninety seconds of speaking time, together with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes ground. Conclude by identifying the single question I am hoping nobody raises—the one that forces me to confront the gap between my predictive-processing reconstruction and clinical evidence regarding fixed false beliefs in certain neuropsychiatric conditions. The objections should target my specific argument, not generic anti-eliminativist complaints.
