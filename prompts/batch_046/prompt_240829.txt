I want a specification of what an adequate normative theory of performance legitimacy must deliver, where performance legitimacy is the claim that sustained economic prosperity and social order can justify authoritarian or non-democratic governance, with China and Singapore treated as the central empirical reference points but not as exclusive data. Do not provide a literature review, reading list, or citation guide; I want the conceptual architecture.

Write the full list of desiderata: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases and distinctions it must handle correctly, the empirical and normative constraints it must respect, and which pairs of requirements are in tension. Specifically, include the following seven requirements, with the consideration behind each and a judgment about whether it is non-negotiable or camp-dependent.

First, the baseline and modal-robustness condition: the theory must specify the counterfactual comparator (what democratic or hybrid alternative is implicitly rejected), and whether legitimacy requires that the authoritarian path was necessary, sufficient, or merely actual for the observed prosperity and order. Explain why leaving this unspecified collapses the argument into a post hoc rationalization, and call this the modal robustness condition.

Second, the substitution problem: if the same aggregate level of prosperity and order could plausibly have been achieved under democratic institutions—perhaps with a different temporal profile or distributional shape—does performance still confer legitimacy? The theory must take a stand on whether substitutability undermines justification, and if so, under what evidentiary standard we could ever establish non-substitutability without access to counterfactual worlds.

Third, the disaggregation of goods: the theory must distinguish predictive order (low variance in outcomes, stable growth, low violent crime) from procedural order (predictable, impartial rules that constrain arbitrary power and protect against expropriation). State whether both are required for legitimacy, whether one can compensate for the absence of the other, and whether authoritarian regimes can supply procedural order without democratic accountability or judicial independence.

Fourth, the distribution and exclusion problem: prosperity and order are aggregate categories. The theory must specify whose prosperity and whose order count—must it address internal inequality, regional disparity, or the exclusion of specific groups from the benefits? And must it weigh rights violations (suppression of dissent, absence of political participation, coercive social control) as costs that can be offset by aggregate welfare, or as non-commodifiable side-constraints?

Fifth, the intertemporal fragility problem: the theory must explain whether legitimacy is granted ex post (once prosperity is achieved) or must be sustained ex ante by credible institutional mechanisms. Specifically, must it account for the risk that authoritarian prosperity creates path-dependent fragility—for example, the absence of error-correcting feedback mechanisms, information suppression, or succession crises—and does this fragility retroactively undermine the initial justification?

Sixth, the moral-status-of-performance problem: the theory must explain why prosperity and order are morally relevant to legitimacy at all, rather than merely descriptively associated with regime survival. Is performance a source of political obligation, a replacement for consent, or a constraint on the conditions under which consent can be withheld? Distinguish instrumental justifications (prosperity is valuable, therefore the regime that delivers it is legitimate) from intrinsic justifications (the regime’s right to rule derives directly from its efficacy).

Seventh, the non-circularity condition: the theory must not define prosperity or order in terms of the regime’s own metrics, growth statistics, or survival, and must not treat the absence of visible dissent as evidence of voluntary acceptance rather than as an artifact of suppression or preference falsification.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable for any normative theory, or whether it reflects the prejudice of a particular camp (e.g., liberal-democratic, developmental-state, or pure consequentialist).

Then, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given standard commitments in political philosophy—for instance, whether the combination of (a) non-substitutability, (b) procedural-order-as-required, and (c) rights-as-non-commodifiable can be satisfied simultaneously by any existing authoritarian case. If you find such a tension, present it as a formal result: the list itself generates an impossibility or forces a choice.

Finally, very briefly and without constructing a full taxonomy, indicate how three broad orientations fare against the list: (i) the East Asian developmental-state view that treats prosperity as generating a provisional, revocable mandate contingent on continued delivery; (ii) the liberal-democratic rebuttal that treats procedural rights and participatory mechanisms as side-constraints regardless of output; and (iii) a hypothetical hybrid view that attempts to separate predictive from procedural order and allow substitutability only under strict evidentiary conditions, with rights protected only in domains unrelated to economic planning. Keep the scoring subordinate to the criteria; the conceptual work is in the requirements and their tensions.
