I recently joined the board of a small housing cooperative whose charter revision has turned into a bitter dispute over voting rights—specifically whether subletters, long-term guests who share the kitchen, and former members now in debt should count as part of the demos when rules change. The conflict has made the democratic boundary problem feel less like an abstract exercise in normative theory and more like a live question about whether inclusion is earned by subjection, by affected interest, or by some threshold of participatory capacity. I want to teach George Orwell’s Animal Farm to a group of fellow residents as a philosophical text rather than a simple allegory of Stalinism, and I need a rigorous reading that takes both the novel and the boundary problem seriously.

Read the farm’s successive assemblies through the lens of who belongs to the demos: the initial inclusion of all animals under the Seven Commandments; the gradual exclusion of the hens who refuse to surrender their eggs; the restriction of Sunday meetings to pigs on the grounds of literacy and administrative burden; and the final reduction to a single speaker. Does Orwell present these exclusions as inevitable power grabs, or does the text implicitly endorse any particular criterion—perhaps that democratic standing requires both subjection to the farm’s labor regime and the cognitive capacity to deliberate? I want the reading to engage the standard philosophical distinctions—affected-interests versus all-subjected versus stakeholder models—without reducing the novel to an illustration of them, and I want you to point to specific scenes (the hens’ rebellion, Mollie’s departure for sugar cubes, Boxer’s exclusion from strategic decisions despite his labor) where the novel either clarifies or resists a given criterion.

Crucially, I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the democratic-theory literature; I want a critical interpretation that asks what Orwell’s fable adds to the philosophical debate that pure argument cannot—particularly whether the boundary problem is soluble at all, or whether any attempt to draw the line is always already an act of domination by those who control the barn and the commandments. If the novel ultimately suggests that the demos cannot be defined before power defines it, say so; if it offers a hidden normative standard that the pigs violate, identify it and defend the identification against the objection that Orwell is merely cynical. I will use this to lead a discussion among people who have never read democratic theory but are living its failures.
