As a retired biochemist who spent several decades studying metabolic networks and enzymatic regulation, and who only recently turned to philosophy of biology, I find myself returning to a familiar analogy: a living organism is like a flame. Both maintain a coherent, bounded form only through continuous exchange with their surroundings; both are dissipative structures operating far from thermodynamic equilibrium; and both vanish not when any particular constituent is lost, but when the self-sustaining process of transformation ceases. In popular and philosophical writing, this analogy is frequently used to argue that life is essentially a dynamic pattern rather than a specific material substance, which in turn is taken to resolve borderline cases—viruses are excluded because they lack autonomous metabolic "combustion"; synthetic minimal cells, such as JCVI-syn3.0, are included precisely because they re-establish this dissipative loop; and dormant spores are said to be alive in potentiality, just as an unlit match contains the flame in potentia.

I would like to audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of fuel, oxidation, boundary, heat loss, reproduction (striking a new match), and extinction? Then identify the disanalogies that matter: flames do not encode information to repair their own structure, they do not evolve by natural selection, and they lack the organizational closure that Maturana and Varela describe as autopoiesis. Does the analogy quietly smuggle in an allopoietic framework—treating life as something produced for an external end rather than as a self-producing network—and does that distort how we should judge synthetic or minimal life? Finally, assess whether the central conclusion—that life is a process-type rather than a substance-type and that this alone settles the borderline cases—survives once these breaks are acknowledged, or whether a better analogy might be drawn from autocatalytic chemical sets or from the organizational closure of metabolic networks themselves.
