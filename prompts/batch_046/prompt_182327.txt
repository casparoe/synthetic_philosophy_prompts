I have been trying to work through a puzzle about rational belief that I encountered in some informal philosophical reading. The idea is called the Reflection principle, usually associated with the philosopher Bas van Fraassen, and it appears to state that if you are now certain you will hold a specific belief in the future—once you have received some anticipated information—then rationality requires you to hold that belief already. At first this sounded like common sense, but the more I thought about it, the less clear it became, especially when I tried to apply it to a concrete choice I am facing.

Here is the situation I have constructed to test the principle, which I have not seen discussed in exactly this form. I must decide whether to sign a two-year lease on an apartment in a city where I may or may not be working. Before signing, I can pay for a private, well-calibrated predictive assessment that will forecast my beliefs after I learn my final job assignment in six months. The model predicts that, upon receiving that assignment, I will be about 85 percent confident that remaining in this city is the wrong choice. According to the Reflection principle, should that forecast alone be enough to make me avoid signing the lease right now?

Rather than asking for a simple yes or no, I would like you to rank the following four responses to this dilemma in order of how well each respects the Reflection principle, with rank 1 being the stance that best honors it and rank 4 being the stance that abandons or violates it most thoroughly. The four stances are: (A) Accept the forecast as decisive and refuse to sign the lease immediately, treating my future belief as already authoritative; (B) Sign the lease now, but build in an automatic contractual escape clause that activates if my future belief crosses a specified threshold, thereby coordinating present action with anticipated belief without actually adopting that belief today; (C) Acknowledge the forecast but insist that my present self retains full authority over my future self, so I should sign if my current reasons support it regardless of what I will later think; and (D) Reject the principle altogether on the grounds that belief is not a passive measurement—like a thermometer reading—that can be 'reflected' backward in time, but rather an active commitment that I can revise for good reasons.

Please provide the ranking with a brief, precise explanation for each position, avoiding unnecessary technical jargon where possible since I am preparing this for a discussion group without formal philosophical training. I am not asking for citations, further reading, or a bibliography—just a clear evaluation of these four positions against the principle itself. I am particularly interested in whether stance B exposes a hidden tension in the principle: it tries to respect the future belief through action without adopting it in belief, and I want to know whether that split is coherent or whether the principle forces us to choose between belief and action in a way that makes no practical sense.
