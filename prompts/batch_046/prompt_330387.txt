I serve as the clinical ethics chair for a regional health authority's treatment-authorization panel, and over the past year we have faced a growing stream of appeals that expose a gap I can no longer ignore: we have no shared procedure for deciding whether a presented symptom cluster counts as a disease that merits funded intervention. Recently I had to sign off on two appeals—one for a condition with measurable physiological dysregulation but whose reported suffering seemed entirely mediated by a high-productivity social environment, and another for a neurodevelopmental variation that caused severe exclusion and distress despite showing no internal dysfunction by any standard biomarker—where the same evidence led three panelists to opposite conclusions. I need a reusable decision procedure my three-person panel can run in about thirty minutes per case, using only the clinical summary and basic epidemiological data we already have, that operationalizes the core distinction between naturalism (disease as internal dysfunction reducing statistical or evolutionary fitness) and normativism (disease as harmful deviation from valued norms or capacities). The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist with explicit stopping points: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the flow ends in a clear denial of authorization, and where it must hand the decision back to a human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than clinical fact. Crucially, it must include the step that both traditions actually require but rarely make explicit—specifying the reference class (species-typical, age-typical, culturally local) and the purpose (survival, reproduction, social participation, subjective well-being) against which dysfunction or harm is assessed—because I have realized that disagreements on our panel usually trace back to unstated differences in these baselines rather than to the theories themselves. It must also flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, where an editor—here, a panelist—must decide. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: first, a patient with documented adrenal dysregulation and chronic fatigue living in a culture that penalizes reduced work capacity; second, a persistent attention-divergence pattern with no biomarker dysfunction but severe social exclusion and subjective distress; and third, a condition that is statistically abnormal and reduces one functional capacity but simultaneously enhances another valued capacity in the patient's specific environment, so that the procedure yields an answer I personally find unwelcome because it denies authorization despite visible suffering. For each, show the path through the checklist, note where judgment enters, and state the result. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure is not designed to do—where it will misfire, which ethical questions it deliberately leaves open, and why it cannot replace the final sign-off.
