As I was discussing with a colleague over coffee last week—she works in philosophy of technology, I in metaphysics—I realized that a puzzle I have long carried about Dennett's account of real patterns and the three predictive stances might actually be the seed of a short essay rather than merely a seminar question. I have been revisiting the distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances in preparation for a graduate seminar I am running next month, and I keep returning to what happens when we explain the behavior of complex engineered systems, particularly distributed software architectures. In the standard commentary, the three stances are presented as equally legitimate explanatory strategies, ontologically on a par, selected solely for predictive utility. But I want to argue that in artifacts—unlike in biological organisms—the design stance and the intentional stance do not merely overlap in practice but actually collapse into one another in ways that the framework fails to accommodate. When engineers explain a compiler's behavior—saying that it refuses malformed input, expects a specific schema, or misinterprets a user command—they are not simply borrowing intentional vocabulary as a convenient heuristic; rather, they are applying the intentional stance in a manner that is only possible because the design stance has already been applied to the system's representational specification files. My sketch claims that this reveals the design stance as ontologically prior to the intentional stance in technological domains, since we can only project belief-like patterns onto an artifact after we have projected functional-design patterns onto its architecture. By contrast, in biological organisms evolutionary design is not encoded in any specification accessible to the system itself, which is why the intentional stance toward animals remains genuinely independent of any design-stance explanation. Before I circulate this argument, I would appreciate targeted comments on several points: whether my reconstruction of Dennett's criterion of projectibility and pattern reality is accurate, or whether I am conflating predictive utility with ontological commitment; whether the collapse argument is philosophically interesting or merely trades on ambiguous language common in engineering practice; and whether I have ignored relevant biological counterexamples—say, in plant signaling or microbial cooperation—where design and intentional explanations might similarly collapse. I do not need bibliographic suggestions or a reading list; I am working from Dennett's original essays and the standard secondary literature already in hand, and I want to keep the focus squarely on the conceptual structure of the argument itself.
