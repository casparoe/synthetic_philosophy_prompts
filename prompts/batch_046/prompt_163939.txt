As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I spend my working life reasoning about data retention policies, the ethics of garbage collection versus immutable audit logs, and what happens when deletion rights are distributed across a system with no single administrator. I have been trying to understand a philosophical dispute about collective traumatic memory: one camp, associated with the ethics-of-memory tradition represented by Avishai Margalit, holds that societies bear an obligation to preserve accurate records of past wrongs, especially to victims, and that erasure is often a second harm; the opposing camp, associated with the case-for-forgetting tradition represented by David Rieff, holds that forced remembrance can perpetuate trauma, that societies need active and structured forgetting to achieve reconciliation, and that not every record should be permanently retained. Rather than a summary or adjudication, I want you to draft the document that these two camps could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration, with the discussion anchored specifically to digital-era archives—systems where cryptographic deletion rights, algorithmic curation, generational access controls, and distributed immutable storage are technically real options, not metaphors.

Structure the deliverable in three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: what collective trauma is and why it raises special obligations distinct from ordinary historical record-keeping; what digital archiving actually enables and risks (including the difference between passive decay in analog storage and active cryptographic erasure); the distinction between maintaining an accurate record and performing ritual commemoration; and the limits of analog metaphors such as treating an archive like a monument or a library.

Second, a disagreements table in neutral wording. For each row, provide the crux in language both sides accept, then the preservationist formulation phrased so that camp would sign it as its own view, then the forgetting-camp formulation phrased so that camp would sign it as its own view. I expect the central cruxes to include: (a) whether digital immutability creates a genuinely new moral category not present in analog memory; (b) whether descendants or affected communities possess an ethical right to permanently erase specific testimonies through cryptographic deletion, or whether such erasure is necessarily a betrayal of victims; and (c) whether algorithmic curation—ranking, summarizing, or selectively surfacing testimony—introduces an interpretive layer that undermines the authenticity required for ethical remembrance.

Third, for each crux, provide a concrete technical scenario that both sides agree is relevant and that does not depend on obscure historical facts I could not verify without searching. For example: a national post-conflict archive stored on an immutable distributed ledger with time-locked decryption keys that allow a community to trigger permanent deletion after a generational threshold; or an archive that uses machine-learning curation to organize victim testimony according to engagement metrics rather than chronological or evidentiary order. For each scenario, record in advance what each camp predicts—e.g., whether preservationists predict that time-locked access strengthens long-term reconciliation or merely delays trauma, and whether the forgetting camp predicts that cryptographic erasure restores communal agency or enables historical denial. Also specify, for each crux, the argument or piece of reasoning both sides accept as genuinely bearing on it, separating substantive disagreement from verbal disagreement. If any portion of the dispute turns out to be about words—such as if "remembering" is being used to mean both "maintaining an unaltered database record" and "performing an annual ritual of acknowledgment"—identify that portion precisely and explain why the disagreement dissolves once the ambiguity is removed.

Keep the tone formal and constructive: this is a joint statement, not a dialogue, not an adjudication, and not a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck.
