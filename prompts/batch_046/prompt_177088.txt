I serve on a small allocation board for a rural clinic that receives unpredictable shipments of a single pain-management drug. This month we have enough to fully treat one patient with end-stage suffering for her remaining three weeks, or to partially treat six patients with severe but non-terminal chronic pain. I argued for distributing across the six—not because it minimizes total suffering, since it clearly does not, but because selecting the one patient for full relief requires me to treat the suffering of the other five as expendable. In my view, the ethical weight of the decision rests less on the clinical outcome and more on the mental root behind the choice: if I allocate to the one, my decision is rooted in a selective attachment that privileges proximity and narrative coherence—she is the one who needs it most—over the equal reality of the others' pain. Even if I tell myself I am being compassionate, the root mental state feels closer to aversion, specifically aversion to facing six people I cannot fully save, masked as targeted care. A fellow board member, who reasons purely by aggregate outcomes, said I am confusing my emotional comfort with ethics, and she cited a tradition that allows deliberately harming one being if it spares many greater harm, arguing that this proves outcomes should dominate intention. I think she misapplies that tradition: in my understanding, an action's moral quality is set primarily by the root mental state—whether it arises from non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion—while the result is secondary, and skillful means does not license an action rooted in aversion just because the numbers work out. Is my reasoning coherent, or am I using the language of mental roots to justify an emotionally easier choice? In particular, address whether my claim about root mental states holds up against the view that foreseeable suffering is the primary moral datum, and identify where my reasoning is most likely to fail. State explicitly what argument or finding—say, about how karmic weight is calculated when intention and foreseeable result sharply conflict—would change your assessment of whether my position holds up.
