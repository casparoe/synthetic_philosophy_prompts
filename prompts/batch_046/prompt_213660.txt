Read these notes for a short essay I am drafting, tentatively titled "The Continuity Trap in Official State Apologies."

Thesis: State apologies for historical wrongs suffer from a concealed logical tension between the identity conditions required for moral apology and those required for moral disavowal.

Outline:
1. Apologies by present governments for past state actions—forced assimilation, colonial expropriation, internment—are usually justified by institutional continuity: the legal personality of the state persists across regimes.
2. Yet moral apology seems to require more than causal or legal continuity; the apologizer must identify with the wrong in a way that makes the apology genuinely self-directed.
3. This creates a dilemma. If the present state's relation to the past is only that of a legal successor—analogous to a corporation inheriting assets—then moral culpability does not automatically transfer; one inherits debts, not guilt. If, however, the relation is a stronger moral-collective identity, then framing the past as alien to present values undermines the very continuity needed to ground the apology.
4. I call this the "continuity trap": the speech act needs robust identity to be meaningful, but needs a clean break with the past to be morally coherent.
