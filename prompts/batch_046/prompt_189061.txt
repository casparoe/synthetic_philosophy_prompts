I'm a journalist who covers economics, history, and the philosophy of social science. Next month I am delivering the keynote lecture at an interdisciplinary symposium on development and historical explanation, and I am defending a thesis I call narrative overdetermination: that institutional, geographic, and cultural accounts of national wealth are each internally coherent and jointly sufficient to explain the same observed divergences, so that the historical record lacks the counterfactual leverage to adjudicate among them. On my view, claims that institutions "explain" why some nations are rich are therefore not empirical discoveries extracted from data but interpretive commitments disguised as causal inference—particularly when regression coefficients and natural experiments are presented as if they broke a symmetry that the data themselves never resolved.

The audience will be about thirty scholars: roughly a third are economic historians and development economists working in the institutionalist tradition, several of whom have published large-n quantitative comparisons of institutions and growth; another third are geographers and macro-historians who defend geographic determinism through arguments about exogenous resource endowments, climate persistence, and long-run path dependence; and the final third are philosophers of science and social epistemologists who study causal identification, theory-ladenness, and the limits of inference to the best explanation in historical cases. Two of the institutionalists are known for treating synthetic control and instrumental-variable designs as resolving identification problems in development; one geographer has argued that institutional explanations are endogenous to geographic conditions; and one philosopher in the group has published on the claim that historical economics faces an irreparable underdetermination problem.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need the eight or so objections I will actually face—not generic rebuttals, but the specific ones this particular mix of institutionalists, geographic determinists, and philosophers will raise, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they would be to my thesis. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that kind of audience member would phrase it: the institutionalists in the language of causal identification, exogenous variation, and mechanism design; the geographic determinists in the language of deep parameters, climate persistence, and endogenous institutions; and the philosophers in the language of underdetermination, inference to the best explanation, and the distinction between epistemic and metaphysical causation. Include a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus a brief note on where that reply is weakest, where it concedes too much, or where it relies on a move that this audience will see through. Tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks so that the objections lose some of their force, and what I must hold at all costs if my thesis is to survive. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the objection that, if pressed, exposes the softest joint in my own position and threatens to collapse narrative overdetermination into either skepticism or a hidden preference for one of the three explanations I claim are symmetric.
