A municipal committee must recommend one of two vacant parcels for a new public park. Independent assessments confirm the parcels are genuinely equivalent across every evaluated dimension: ecological value, accessibility, maintenance cost, and neighborhood demand. The committee's charter requires a unanimous recommendation, yet members are divided not over which parcel is superior, but over how rational agency ought to behave when no preference exists. One faction proposes resolving the tie by random lot, arguing that once every relevant reason has been exhausted, delegating the decision to chance is the only intellectually honest acknowledgment that preference is absent. A second faction insists that rational choice cannot be outsourced to a coin flip; they advocate inventing a trivial, post-hoc distinction—such as proximity to the committee's usual meeting place—to generate a genuine reason for selection, even though that factor was not among the original evaluation criteria. A third faction urges indefinite postponement, maintaining that patience will eventually reveal a hidden, relevant difference that current assessments have missed. You have been asked to advise the committee. Is there a coherent philosophical distinction between breaking the tie through randomness, through manufactured reason, and through refusal to choose? In particular, does constructing a trivial reason after the fact respect rational autonomy, or does it constitute a form of self-deception about the nature of one's own preferences? Identify the underlying conceptual structure and apply the relevant theoretical framework, without appealing to bibliographic authorities or named doctrines.
