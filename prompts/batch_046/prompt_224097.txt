I'm preparing a philosophy-of-action unit and want to pair a literary text with experimental-philosophy findings rather than with abstract vignettes. Read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the lens of the Knobe effect — the documented asymmetry by which observers are more willing to attribute intentionality to an agent for a harmful side-effect than for a beneficial one, holding the agent's direct aim and causal structure constant. Concentrate on the killing of Simon during the ritual dance: Simon's death is not the direct purpose of the frenzied performance, yet it is its catastrophic collateral outcome. Where does Golding's narration, and the other boys' retrospective insistence that the event was an accident rather than murder, align with or diverge from the folk-psychological pattern Knobe identified? Does the communal, ecstatic context supply a reason for denying intentionality that the standard business-vignette experiments do not model, or do Ralph and Piggy's denials represent precisely the kind of post-hoc moral reasoning that critics of the effect claim drives it? Conversely, does the reader's likely judgment that the boys bear deep moral culpability depend on attributing intentionality, or does the novel separate responsibility from the mental-state label in a way that challenges a conceptual link between harm and intention? I want a reading that takes both the novel's moral psychology and the experimental literature seriously: not reducing Golding to an illustration, but asking what his depiction of group dynamics and retrospective self-exculpation adds to the debate over whether the Knobe asymmetry reflects a genuine conceptual connection or a confound with normative evaluation.
