Having retired from a career in experimental physiology, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of medicine and have become preoccupied with Thomas Szasz’s thesis that mental illness is a myth—by which he meant not that psychological suffering is unreal, but that psychiatric diagnoses classify not literal biological diseases analogous to diabetes or pneumonia, but rather socially disvalued patterns of behavior and experience. In one seminar I found myself mediating between two interlocutors whose disagreement seemed simultaneously sharper and narrower than they realized. The first, whom I shall call the Szaszian literalist, maintains that no mental disorder belongs to the same ontological category as a bodily disease; that all psychiatric taxonomy is essentially a system of moral-social labeling; and that involuntary psychiatric intervention is therefore always an illegitimate exercise of state power masked as medicine. The second, whom I shall call the critical realist, accepts Szasz’s warning that psychiatric institutions have historically exercised coercive social control, that diagnostic manuals embed normative judgments about acceptable cognition and affect, and that the category of “mental illness” is partly constructed; yet she insists that conditions such as schizophrenia and severe bipolar disorder involve empirically observable dysfunctions of neurobiology, perception, and reasoning that can be investigated without reducing them to mere metaphors, and that voluntary, consent-based psychiatric care is therefore legitimate and sometimes necessary.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration statement these two should have produced after a year of good-faith dispute. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered list of jointly signed claims about what is formally settled—concerning the structure of psychiatric diagnosis, the documented history of coercive practice, and the empirical findings about neurobiology and phenomenology that neither side disputes; (2) a disagreements table giving, in neutral wording each party would accept, the precise formulations of their residual differences; (3) an explicit identification of cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party—including, specifically, whether anosognosia in schizophrenia is best explained as a neurocognitive deficit or as rational resistance to a stigmatizing social label, and whether “species-typical functioning” can be defined in psychiatry without smuggling in normative assumptions about rational agency; and, for each crux, one concrete observational case or empirical pattern both sides agree is relevant, together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that case shows. If any portion of their dispute turns out to be verbal—say, over the term “disease” itself—state which portion and why. Keep the entire document to no more than 650 words. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; write for a reader without access to external sources.
