I'm trying to work out what I actually think about individual moral responsibility for collective harms—climate damage, factory farming, democratic outcomes—going by my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than by theory. I was discussing this with a friend who takes the standard inefficacy objection seriously: if my single act makes no difference to whether the harm occurs, there can't be a duty to refrain, and vice versa. But my verdicts don't line up neatly with that. Driving my old SUV alone to work in a city with reliable transit feels clearly not fine; voting in a national election where my ballot is statistically certain not to decide the outcome feels like it's neither obligatory nor wrong to skip; eating beef regularly feels wrong, but somehow less wrong than the SUV, even though my individual order almost certainly doesn't change supply; taking a job optimizing coal-plant efficiency—lowering emissions per kilowatt-hour but extending the plant's profitable life—feels more wrong than the beef, despite the "efficiency" framing; having a second child in a high-consumption country feels genuinely ambiguous to me, different in kind from both the driving and the job cases; purchasing a carbon-offset flight package I know is largely unverified feels not fine, but the wrongness seems closer to self-deception or free-riding than to direct harm; and withholding the portion of my taxes that subsidizes fossil-fuel extraction—knowing others will cover the gap—feels permissible, perhaps even commendable, which sits oddly with the SUV verdict since the harm proceeds either way.

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures this pattern without simply overturning half my judgments? I'm open to something like "wrongness tracks whether you are treating a collective harm as a private benefit you could forgo without sacrifice," but that struggles with the child case and doesn't obviously distinguish the coal-efficiency job from the beef. Or perhaps the distinction turns on whether you are entering or sustaining a causal role versus merely failing to stop one, but then I need an account of why voting and tax refusal are treated differently. I'd like the principle stated precisely, with the specific case that motivates each clause, its verdict on at least two cases I haven't mentioned—say, working as a software engineer on ad-targeting algorithms that increase consumption, and refusing to serve on a jury in a case you think will be decided correctly without you—and an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts it must reject to stay coherent. If no single principle saves them all, I'd rather have two competing candidates, each with its cost, than a forced unity.
