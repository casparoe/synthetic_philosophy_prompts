Consider a constitutional system containing Article X, which states that it may be amended only by unanimous vote of the constitutional court and that no other provision may alter that procedure. A philosophy-of-law exam asks students to analyze whether this creates a paradox of legal self-reference by addressing three things: (1) whether the clause yields a logical contradiction analogous to the self-amendment problems discussed in jurisprudence; (2) how Hart’s distinctions among primary rules, rules of change, and rules of recognition apply, and whether they avoid infinite regress without invoking a Kelsenian Grundnorm; and (3) a further complication—suppose the system contains two competing official practices, Practice A and Practice B, each claiming to be the authoritative rule of recognition for constitutional amendments, with Practice A validating court-vote amendments and Practice B validating only referendum-based amendments. If Article X is amended by court vote, Practice A accepts it but Practice B rejects it. How should validity be determined, and does this split undermine Hart’s claim that a legal system ultimately has one rule of recognition?

Student answer:
Article X does generate a logical paradox of self-reference, essentially the same problem Ross identified: a rule governing its own alteration can change the conditions for its alteration, yielding contradiction. Hart resolves this by separating primary rules from secondary rules—specifically rules of change and rules of recognition. Article X functions as a rule of change empowering officials to modify primary rules, while the rule of recognition is the social practice among officials that determines validity. Because this practice is accepted as a matter of fact, there is no need for Kelsen’s Grundnorm, which is only a theoretical presupposition. The problem returns, however, if the rule of recognition itself falls under Article X: then amending the recognition criteria changes the authority validating the amendment, producing an infinite regress. Hart avoids this by treating the ultimate rule of recognition as standing outside legal amendment altogether—though he calls it social acceptance, it functions exactly like Kelsen’s basic norm placed outside the system. Regarding the split between Practice A and Practice B, Hart’s theory clearly requires one ultimate rule of recognition; if officials are genuinely divided, there is no legal system but only competing systems. So the court-vote amendment is valid only if Practice A is the actual social practice of the majority of officials. If Practice B dominates, the amendment fails, but there is no deeper paradox—just a factual disagreement about which practice is authoritative.
