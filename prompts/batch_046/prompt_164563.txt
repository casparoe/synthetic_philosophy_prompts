I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on British empiricism, and for our final colloquium I want to present an original poem that treats Berkeley's master argument with real structural precision rather than merely alluding to the familiar formula about being and being perceived. I would like a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each in roughly iambic pentameter, followed by a three-line envoi, with the six end-words rotating in the standard pattern—using these end-words: perceive, idea, matter, spirit, likeness, and exist. The rotation should do conceptual work: in the early stanzas matter should be paired with perceptual terms, then in the middle stanzas it should be displaced by idea and likeness, and in the envoi all six words should appear together in an order that enacts the dependency relation at the heart of the master argument, specifically that any putative absolute existence of matter collapses into the conditions of its being perceived by spirit. The poem's turn should occur in the fifth stanza, where the speaker confronts the continuity problem—what happens to a tree or a table when no human perceiver is present—and the closing lines should leave the divine-guarantee resolution structurally suspended rather than settled. The content should be accurate enough that a specialist in Berkeley's idealism recognizes the likeness principle, the argument from the inconceivability of unperceived matter, and the appeal to constant divine perception, without reducing them to mere labels. Please write this poem, keeping the philosophical tension open and avoiding any sermon about whether the argument ultimately succeeds.
