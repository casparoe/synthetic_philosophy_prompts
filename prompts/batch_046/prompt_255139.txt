I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy. I am organizing a 75-minute workshop for sixteen colleagues at my company—mostly engineers with no philosophy background—to explore the tension between retroactive criminal law and victors' justice, using the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals as the conceptual anchor. I want participants to feel, through action rather than lecture, why writing rules after the fact feels structurally unfair even when the underlying acts are widely condemned, and I want them to encounter a specific wrinkle: the problem of applying those retroactive rules to institutional successors (e.g., successor states or reorganized regimes) that claim they are not identical to the entities that existed when the acts occurred. I will call this the jurisdictional-identity problem, and I want it built into the mechanics of the activity, not just mentioned in discussion.

Please design a structured simulation or role-play, not a free-form debate, with clear roles, hidden information, and a forced decision point. The group should be split into three factions: representatives drafting the tribunal charter (the rule-makers), representatives of accused institutional actors from the prior regime, and neutral adjudicators who must apply whatever charter is written. Crucially, some accused representatives should receive a hidden card stating that their state or organization has legally dissolved and reformed, so they can argue they are not the same legal subject to which retroactive rules apply. The rule-makers should not know which accused parties hold this card until after they have finalized the charter. Use only symbolic stakes—reputation points or team prestige, with no real money—through a payoff scheme that rewards the rule-makers for securing convictions but penalizes them if the neutral adjudicators rule the charter invalid due to identity or retroactivity objections.

Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, including when roles and hidden cards are distributed, when the charter is drafted, when accusations are revealed, and when adjudication happens. Ensure the facilitator says almost nothing about philosophy before the debrief; the unfairness should emerge from the participants' own arguments and the adjudicators' rulings.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation onto: (1) the nullum crimen sine lege principle, (2) the structural advantage of the victor in defining both the crime and the court, and (3) the jurisdictional-identity problem I described. Be explicit about which feature of the real-world Nuremberg and Tokyo context this activity cannot reproduce—likely the actual power asymmetry of occupying forces versus defeated parties, or the absence of genuine enforcement alternatives—and explain why that omission matters. Also list what will predictably fail the first time (for example, the rule-makers ignoring retroactivity concerns, or the adjudicators refusing to apply the charter) and how to handle it without breaking the simulation.

Constraints: Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure philosophical theories that require specialist knowledge; keep the framing to the well-known retroactivity and victors'-justice issues, with only the jurisdictional-identity wrinkle added. Write in a formal, polished tone appropriate for a technically minded audience.
