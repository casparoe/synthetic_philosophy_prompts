I run a philosophy-of-mind seminar for about twelve rationalist-leaning adults; we meet for 75 minutes with paper and pens but no screens. Design a paired exercise that forces participants to live Hofstadter’s claim that an “I” is a symbol-pattern that can persist across brains—not as metaphor, but as an operational fracture. Give each pair split custody of one fictional self: Partner A holds all episodic-memory cards; Partner B holds the evaluative framework (values, goals, emotional weights) but zero episodes. Together they must reach a single joint first-person decision—e.g., whether to “sacrifice” a relationship for a career move—speaking only in the first-person singular, with either partner able to veto. Use only social-status points (no money) for payoff, and structure the rules so coordination collapses precisely when both feel like the original “I” yet each controls only half the strange loop. Include materials, the payoff scheme, a debrief that maps the breakdown onto pattern-continuity versus biological-continuity, and be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to salvage it. Also state the one feature of distributed selfhood this exercise can never reproduce and that you must supply verbally. Keep the response under 350 words; format as Rules / Payoff / Debrief / Predictable failure / Unreproducible gap; and lead with a one-sentence bottom line naming the single rule that makes the distributed-I split.
