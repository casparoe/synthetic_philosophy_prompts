Next month I present to a graduate seminar in moral philosophy and political ethics. The audience includes two Catholic moral theologians who work extensively on the doctrine of double effect, three analytic philosophers of action who are skeptical of thick moral vocabulary and institutional blame, and one historian of science whose research focuses on the administrative chain of command in the Manhattan Project. The thesis I will defend is that Anscombe's protest against Oxford's decision to honor Truman with an honorary degree is best understood not as a direct application of the principle that intentionally killing innocents is always wrong, but as an argument about a distinct failure mode in practical reasoning: the substitution of quantitative strategic comparison for the recognition of an absolute moral boundary, specifically the boundary that distinguishes treating civilian deaths as a genuinely foreseen but unintended side-effect from treating them as a negotiable parameter within a cost-benefit calculus. I argue that this corruption of practical reasoning can be structurally embedded in institutional decision procedures rather than residing solely in an individual agent's psychology, which explains why Anscombe directed her objection at the university's act of conferring legitimacy rather than only at Truman's personal choice. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face. Order them by likelihood and damage, phrasing each as the relevant subgroup would phrase it. For each objection, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. Finally, specify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises — in particular, whether my framing illegitimately projects a modern concern with bureaucratic moral distancing onto a mid-twentieth-century protest that was primarily about personal moral character — and advise me on whether to preempt it or let it lie. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; I need conceptual and strategic preparation only.
