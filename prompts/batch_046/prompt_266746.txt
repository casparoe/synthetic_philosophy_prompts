I have been working through Dennett’s “Where Am I?” brain-transplant thought experiment in a philosophy-of-mind reading group, and a particular structural variant keeps generating disagreement that I think touches a deeper conceptual issue. In the classic version, Dennett’s brain is placed in a vat and remotely controls his body; the puzzle asks whether he is located with the brain or with the body. But I want to consider a more distributed case: imagine that the interface does not merely transmit signals but genuinely partitions cognitive labor, so that abstract reasoning and memory retrieval occur on the vat’s processors while emotional integration, proprioceptive mapping, and affective evaluation remain localized in the biological body’s residual neural architecture. There is no single hardware site that contains the full functional profile we ordinarily associate with a unified conscious subject.

The conceptual problem this raises is about the determinacy of first-person indexical location. If “I am here” normally tracks the spatial position of the system that integrates experience into a single point of view, but integration itself is now split across two bodies—one in the vat, one in the room—what fixes the truth-conditions of that utterance? Please explain, without appealing to bibliographic references, how the three main competing frameworks—functional-system unity, embodied sensorimotor coupling, and narrative self-modeling—each attempt to locate the self in this partitioned scenario, and why each framework seems to capture something the others miss. In particular, I want to understand whether this case reveals a hidden presupposition that indexical self-location requires a singular computational locus, or whether it shows that asking “where am I?” was always a category mistake that confuses spatial presence with the distributed architecture of personhood.
