I am designing a final exam for an upper-level philosophy of action course. Draft one essay question, suitable for 45 minutes, on the moral distinction between doing harm and allowing harm. The question must not be answerable by summarizing standard textbook treatments—such as the doctrine of double effect or familiar switch-and-bridge scenarios—and should instead turn on a specific conceptual injection: a "reverse-intervention" case in which an agent can either (a) actively withdraw a sustaining resource currently delivered through a mechanism that already includes the agent's body, redirecting it to save five others while one dies, or (b) refrain from initiating an identical new mechanism that would have saved the one but not the five, with both options requiring the same physical movement and producing identical causal and distributive outcomes. Ask students whether this collapses the doing/allowing distinction, whether preserving it requires a normative "status quo" baseline rather than a purely causal one, and what that implies for attributing moral responsibility.

Provide the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by specifying the exact philosophical moves required—e.g., recognizing that causal topology alone is insufficient, identifying the baseline-dependency of the distinction, and constructing or rebutting a counterexample showing the distinction survives even when causal structures are mirrored. For each of five required moves, list the marks, the correct execution, and the typical mistake that costs points.

Finally, in your response, say explicitly what consideration or argument would change your verdict about whether the distinction survives this case, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable.
