Since I began consulting on an agricultural robotics programme that automates targeted pest elimination, I have been unable to stop thinking about the moral weight of insects. I am drafting a short philosophical essay on the scale argument in insect sentience, and I would welcome your comments on its coherence, whether it avoids special pleading, and where it is weakest.

The essay proceeds as follows. The familiar scale argument holds that because there are perhaps 10^19 insects alive at any moment, even a very low per-individual probability of sentience yields an enormous expected number of moral patients—enough, allegedly, to dwarf all farmed vertebrates combined. I want to resist the conclusion that this immediately generates overwhelming obligations to avoid harming insects.

My central move is to distinguish between potential moral patienthood and what I call actualised moral addressability. Potential patienthood generates diffuse, precautionary reasons to minimise harm where it is easy to do so. Actualised addressability, however, requires that an agent can identify the specific interests of a being and direct action towards or away from that being in a morally non-arbitrary way. The scale argument, I claim, illicitly aggregates potential patienthood across vast numbers without asking whether our actions are addressable to those specific individuals.

I apply this to two cases. In automated pest control, the system targets specific organisms with identifiable behavioural signatures; addressability is high, so the moral stakes are genuinely elevated. By contrast, when I walk across a lawn and inadvertently crush insects, my action is statistically unavoidable background noise with near-zero addressability. Here, I argue, the scale argument fails to generate a specific prohibition; at most, it supports a weak general duty to reduce unnecessary harm.

I also consider an objection: if addressability depends on human cognitive or technological capacities, does this not collapse into anthropocentrism? I reply that addressability is relational but not necessarily species-relative; a more advanced being could address insects in ways I cannot, and my obligations are indexed to my actual epistemic and causal position.

Please comment specifically on whether the distinction between addressability and aggregation holds up, whether I have missed a standard reply to the scale argument that undermines my position, and whether the robotics application is consistent with the framework. Please keep your response to no more than 350 words, and do not include bibliographic references, as I am working without library access.
