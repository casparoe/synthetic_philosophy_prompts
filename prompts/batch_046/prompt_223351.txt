I just spent a long, circular evening arguing with a friend about whether Newton's bucket experiment genuinely proves spacetime substantivalism, or whether a sufficiently rigorous relationist can absorb it without changing the physics. To break our stalemate, I want you to adjudicate a reconstructed, sharper version of the dispute—presented below as an exchange between two interlocutors, Alex and Jordan, neither of whom is me or my friend. I am reporting their exchange purely as a bystander, and I genuinely do not know which side holds up.

You are an expert philosopher of physics with deep familiarity with the metaphysics of space, time, and motion, particularly the Newtonian bucket argument, Mach's principle, and the distinction between substantivalism, relationism, and structural realism. Before delivering your verdict, please take a moment to brainstorm and map out the logical architecture of each position: identify hidden premises, distinguish kinematical claims from dynamical ones, and flag any equivocations between "absolute motion" and "absolute space." Think carefully, and do not rush to a partisan conclusion.

Here is the exchange.

Alex (1): Newton's rotating-bucket experiment proves that rotation is not merely relative to other bodies. Imagine the bucket at rest relative to the fixed stars, then spin it. The water climbs the sides. If relationism were correct, and rotation were only rotation-relative-to-other-bodies, then once the bucket is at rest relative to those bodies there should be no inertial effect. But there is. Therefore there is a fact of the matter about rotation independent of relations—rotation relative to absolute space itself.

Jordan (2): That only works if you assume the universe contains other bodies that could serve as relational anchors. But in our actual universe distant masses contribute to local inertial frames through frame-dragging and global boundary conditions. So the water climbs not because of an invisible container called space, but because it is rotating relative to the global distribution of matter and energy.

Alex (3): You are importing general-relativistic dynamics into a Newtonian thought experiment. Newton had no gravitational mechanism linking distant stars to local water surfaces. Within pure Newtonian mechanics, distant bodies exert no force on the bucket water. So appealing to Mach's principle or frame-dragging changes the physics, not merely the interpretation. Within Newton's own framework, the relationalist has no causal story.

Jordan (4): Granted—relationism is an interpretational thesis, not just a causal one. Still, let me push a coherence challenge within Newtonian mechanics itself. If there is truly nothing in the universe except the bucket and water, then there is no fact of the matter about whether the bucket is rotating. The premise "the bucket spins in an empty universe" is not a coherent physical description; it is a misapplication of a relational concept to a non-relational context. So Newton's experiment begs the question by assuming that "spinning" can be defined without relations.

Alex (5): That is too quick. We can certainly conceive of accelerating the bucket by applying a torque. The conception does not require an external reference frame to be intelligible—we feel the torque, we observe the parabolic water surface. You are conflating epistemology with ontology: just because we usually detect rotation by comparing positions does not mean rotation is constituted by those comparisons.

Jordan (6): I am not making an epistemic point. Here is a more specific challenge. Suppose we accept, for the sake of argument, that the universe has a global topological structure—say, flat spatial sections with periodic boundary conditions on a 3-torus—so that there are no distant stars, no asymptotically flat region, and no "rest of the matter" outside the subsystem, yet the local geometry remains Euclidean. In such a universe, if the bucket and water form an isolated subsystem, the relationalist cannot formulate the claim that the subsystem rotates relative to "the rest of the matter," since there is no rest of the matter. The substantivalist can still say "it rotates relative to the manifold," but that seems to reintroduce space as a substance precisely through the back door of global topology. So the bucket argument either assumes what it needs to prove, or it forces us to treat topological structure as ontologically prior to matter—which is just substantivalism in structuralist clothing.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say, overall, who is more right about what, and identify anything important that both sides are missing—particularly whether the bucket argument actually distinguishes full-blooded substantivalism from structural realism about spacetime, and whether Jordan's topological-relationalist challenge succeeds in undermining the argument without invoking GR dynamics. End with a clear overall verdict rather than a non-committal summary.
