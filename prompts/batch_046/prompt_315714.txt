Analyze the 1986 Challenger launch decision—specifically Morton Thiokol engineer Roger Boisjoly's attempt to halt the flight—through Kant's account of moral worth. Treat the episode as a problem of overdetermined motivation: Boisjoly's technical objection was clearly in accordance with duty, but if his resistance was partly driven by self-protective fear of being blamed for a failure he had warned about, does it still qualify as acting from duty (aus Pflicht) rather than merely in accordance with it (pflichtgemäß)? Map the players—Boisjoly, his managers, NASA officials—their available options, and the institutional pressures shaping their motives. Then state precisely what Kant's binary of pure duty versus inclination predicts about the moral status of Boisjoly's act, and identify where the framework is silent: for instance, whether there is a meaningful distinction between dominant duty with residual inclination and pure duty, or whether institutional coercion introduces a category of compelled moral action that Kant never anticipated. Finally, argue whether the Challenger case supports Kant's claim that only actions done solely from respect for the moral law possess unconditional worth, or whether it shows that the purity condition is too strict for evaluating real-world moral agency.
