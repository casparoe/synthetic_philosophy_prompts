Following a long and rather exhausting discussion with a friend about whether fundamental physics might carry hidden ethical costs, I have been asked to mark an examination script on this very topic. I should like a detached, point-by-point assessment; please treat me as a neutral grader and do not address any views of my own.

The essay question was: Critically discuss the claim that if panpsychism is correct, then high-energy particle-physics experiments may generate large-scale suffering. In your answer, distinguish between the combination problem and the question of affective valence at the micro-level, and consider whether integrated information theory (IIT) offers a resolution.

The student wrote:

Panpsychism holds that experiential properties are fundamental rather than emergent, so that even elementary physical entities possess some form of consciousness. If this is correct, then high-energy particle collisions must produce or alter an enormous number of these micro-subjects. Given that excitation away from a basal state is generally unpleasant, it is plausible that these entities undergo negative affective valence, meaning that an accelerator generates a vast, dispersed quantity of suffering.

This worry is strengthened rather than weakened by the combination problem. The problem concerns how discrete micro-experiences integrate into a unified macro-consciousness. Within an accelerator, particles are in sustained causal interaction through the strong and electromagnetic forces; this very causal integration supplies the mechanism that phenomenal combination requires. Therefore the conditions for macro-subject formation are satisfied, and the suffering is not dispersed but unified.

Integrated information theory (IIT) resolves the difficulty by measuring consciousness through integrated information (Φ). Because elementary particles lack internal causal structure, their Φ is exactly zero, which entails that they have no consciousness whatsoever. Consequently, IIT refutes the panpsychist premise and eliminates the ethical concern entirely.

Lastly, the objection might be raised that phenomenal consciousness does not necessarily involve affective valence. Yet this misses the point: if there is no consciousness at all—as IIT demonstrates—then there is certainly no suffering, and the argument collapses regardless.

Please assign a grade—perhaps on a UK honours scale or as a percentage—and justify it with specific comments on individual moves in the argument. Pay particular attention to subtle errors: conflations of distinct philosophical problems, mischaracterisations of theoretical commitments, or inferential steps that do not actually follow. There are one or two such issues present, but nothing that amounts to an obvious howler. Write your assessment in British English.
