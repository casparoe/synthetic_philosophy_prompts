I am writing a feature on how urban policy is beginning to treat meaning in life as an infrastructure problem. In several northern European cities, planning authorities now require developers of large public spaces to submit a “meaning-impact assessment” — modelled on environmental-impact reports — that projects how the redesign will affect residents’ sense of purpose. The assessment uses a composite index: it weights subjective life-satisfaction scores against what the framework calls “objective narrative resources” (access to archives, communal rituals, spaces for unstructured encounter) and explicitly excludes purely hedonic or consumption-based metrics. The underlying guidance document treats meaning neither as a private psychological state nor as a transcendent given, but as a kind of civic utility that can be damaged or repaired by spatial design.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning in life that makes such an assessment coherent: does it require a hybrid account that combines subjective fulfilment with objective worth, or can it be sustained by a purely objective-list theory, or by a purely subjective one? Does it quietly rule out the absurdist claim — familiar from the tradition of Camus — that meaning is not something institutions can distribute but rather something individuals must construct in the face of an indifferent universe? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where rival theories of meaning would license the same bureaucratic form. Finally, describe what would have to change in the assessment’s design, its metrics, and its legal standing if the implicit theory were replaced by a rigorously absurdist view that denies meaning can be a public good engineered through planning.
