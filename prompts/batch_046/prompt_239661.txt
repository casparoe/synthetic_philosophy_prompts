I am preparing a four-month dissertation prospectus in philosophy of mind and epistemology for a fellowship that funds conceptual rather than empirical work. The question I want to defend is this: does epistemic dependence on AI erode a distinct form of understanding—call it friction literacy, meaning the mental representation of where in a domain reasoning dead-ends, distinctions collapse, and claims become brittle—separate from both propositional knowledge loss and procedural deskilling? Existing treatments tend to split between a skills-degradation thesis (users forget how to write or calculate) and a trust-calibration thesis (users accept outputs they cannot verify). I think both miss the harm that occurs when a user retains correct beliefs and can still generate answers, yet no longer possesses a map of which parts of the problem space are genuinely contested or unstable. Write the prospectus: state the precise research question; explain why the standard skill-degradation and trust-calibration frameworks fail to settle it and what gap they leave open; defend a conceptual methodology—taxonomy construction, conceptual distinction, and designed thought experiments—rather than empirical user studies, and say why this makes progress where obvious alternatives (survey data, neuroimaging, interface redesign) do not; specify the deliverable (a framework that identifies conditions under which friction literacy is preserved, dissolved, or delegated) and what counts as failure (if friction literacy collapses into ordinary meta-cognitive awareness that survives delegation intact); and outline a realistic four-month scope for a single researcher with no lab access. Do not include a bibliography, citation requests, or references to specific papers by author and date; I will supply citations myself. Keep the framing rigorous but avoid invoking niche theoretical machinery that requires specialized memorization beyond standard epistemology and philosophy of mind.
