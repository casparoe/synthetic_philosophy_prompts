I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on political philosophy and found myself stuck on a comparison that my colleagues treat as obvious but that I think hides a deeper conceptual divergence. The practice I have in mind is the institutional management of religious visibility in public education: specifically, France's 2004 prohibition on conspicuous religious symbols in state schools, which targets headscarves, large crosses, and turbans, set against the Anglo-American pattern—visible in British policing uniform codes, Canadian provincial school-board accommodations, and several U.S. state employment rules—where religious dress is permitted unless it generates a direct, demonstrable functional conflict. What puzzles me is that both regimes claim to uphold neutrality, yet they operationalize it in opposite directions: one by stripping visible particularity from shared space, the other by distributing exemptions that preserve visible particularity within shared procedures. I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments that make the French practice coherent rather than merely arbitrary. Does it presuppose what I have been calling a "perceptual neutrality" thesis—that republican equality requires the public sphere to be emptied of markers that signal group membership—and if so, does that thesis depend on a specific conception of the self as possessing a private interior identity that can be separated from its public expression? Or can the French rule be defended without that metaphysics of the masked self?

Then compare this to the accommodationist regime: does the Anglo-American practice presuppose an incompatible view—perhaps that identity is partly constituted through public recognition and that neutrality is therefore procedural rather than visual—or can both practices be derived from the same underlying principle of equal respect, merely applied to different empirical assumptions about coercion and social pressure? I am not asking for a survey of positions; I want a committed analysis. Take a definite stance on whether the divergence reflects a genuine philosophical disagreement about the nature of neutrality and equality, or whether it is primarily a path-dependent institutional settlement that borrows philosophical language post hoc. Furthermore, if we replaced the French perceptual-neutrality premise with a procedural-equality framework, specify precisely which institutional practices beyond the headscarf prohibition itself—for instance, rules about holiday calendars, curricular canon selection, or staff dress codes—would necessarily have to be revised to maintain consistency, and which could remain unchanged without contradiction. Give your reasons clearly, and do not hedge between the two main interpretations; I want to know which account you think is correct and where the weaker view fails.
