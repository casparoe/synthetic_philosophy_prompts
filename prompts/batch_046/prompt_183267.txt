I was having a pint with my flatmate Priya after our seminar on moral psychology — well, she was on the pint, I was stuck with ginger beer — and she started laying out these very specific verdicts about when an action actually carries moral worth. She reckons that if you return lost cash purely to avoid feeling guilty, that's basically zero worth, not even partial credit. But if you do it because you think honesty is genuinely valuable, even if you also get that little warm glow afterwards, she gives it full marks. She also said that someone who pulls a kid out of a burning building purely on reflex, with no time to think 'this is right', gets about half worth, because rightness wasn't driving the bus but the character still lined up. Meanwhile she insists the classic Kantian who helps strangers purely from duty while feeling total revulsion still gets full worth, and that if you donate anonymously but only because you want your name on a plaque eventually, it's worthless even if the charity gets loads.

I'm trying to figure out what principle she's actually running, since her pattern fits more than one rule and I want to predict her on fresh cases. One story is that worth needs conscious endorsement of rightness (so the reflex rescuer drops points). Another is that worth just needs absence of competing selfish motives (so the guilt-avoider is out, but reflex is fine since there's no bad motive). A third might be counterfactual stability — would you still do it if the emotional payoffs vanished?

So here's the ask: what should she say about a nurse who administers care during an epidemic not because she thinks it's right or feels compassion, but purely because her contract binds her and breaking it means legal anxiety — she feels nothing about the paitents either way? And an artist who paints free public murals that happen to improve mental health in rough neighbourhoods, not because he cares about health or social duty, but purely because he loves geometric patterns and picks high-footfall spots for asthetic reasons? I want to know which of those three rules diverge on these two, which verdict best coheres with her earlier pattern, and what further case would actually tell the rules apart if she answered consistantly. There's also this idea about de dicto versus de re motivation — being moved by the concept 'this is right' versus being moved by the concrete features that make it right — and I'm wondering if her verdicts on the reflex rescuer versus the disgusted Kantian map onto that split. If they do, does it fix the tension or just rename it?
