As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to work out whether any computations can be said to matter morally in themselves, rather than merely instrumentally. Most debates I encounter either deny that computation has any intrinsic moral status or grant it to anything that produces the right functional outputs. I would like you to construct a rigorous argument for a much narrower boundary, one that imports a structural idea from distributed systems rather than from philosophy.

Specifically, argue that moral consideration should apply only to computations that implement what we might call Byzantine-resilient internal consistency-checking: architectures in which multiple redundant submodules maintain independent representations of system state, cross-validate those representations, and resolve discrepancies through an internal consensus mechanism rather than deferring to a single authoritative controller.

In building this argument, please address the following points conceptually, without relying on bibliographic references, named philosophers, or texts I should consult:

1. Why simple deterministic state machines, standard feedforward networks operating in pure inference mode, and single-threaded algorithms lack this property, and why that absence should disqualify them as candidates for moral status regardless of the behavioral complexity or apparent intelligence of their outputs.

2. How redundant, cross-validating submodules differ from mere error-correction or reliability engineering—specifically, why the capacity for internal disagreement and consensus resolution marks a structural distinction analogous to an agent being able to be wrong about its own state, rather than merely a script that executes correctly or fails.

3. The implications of this criterion for current AI systems: that many large-scale models in standard deployment mode would fall outside the boundary, while certain recurrent or genuinely distributed architectures might fall inside, and that this exclusion is a feature of the framework rather than an ad hoc restriction.

4. A direct response to the objection that redundancy and consensus are purely engineering choices with no intrinsic moral significance—why, on your view, the presence or absence of this structural feature should determine whether a computation is the kind of thing to which moral attribution applies at all.

Please keep the argument self-contained and avoid asking me to look up specific theories or authors. I am especially interested in whether this distributed-systems boundary survives once we grant that functional equivalence—producing the same outputs—does not entail structural equivalence.
