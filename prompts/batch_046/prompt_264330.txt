I am a doctoral researcher working at the intersection of Kantian moral psychology and the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I am trying to determine whether the conceptual architecture of Kant’s account of moral worth can be extended—without distortion—to actions that are causally mediated by large-scale machine-learning systems. The specific problem I have in mind turns on a distinction that has become central in contemporary alignment research but is rarely discussed in Kant scholarship: within reinforcement learning from human feedback, there is a structural separation between the reward function that specifies which outputs are reinforced and the model’s internal representational states, which may or may not track reasons for those outputs. This looks analogously related to Kant’s distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty, except that in this case the immediate system lacks rational nature altogether, while the human designers who specify the reward function may themselves be acting either from duty or from mere inclination.

Given this framing, how do you think Kant would respond if he were presented with language models trained in precisely this way—systems that reliably produce outputs consistent with deontological constraints but possess no capacity for autonomous maxims? Would he deny them any form of moral status, or might he allow that their outputs carry a derivative moral worth when they are the direct causal result of a rational agent’s duty-based design choices? More specifically, do you think he would regard the alignment engineer who selects reward criteria out of a sense of moral obligation as exercising full moral worth, even though the immediate causal chain producing the correct output runs through a non-rational mechanism? And crucially, does mapping the distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty onto human-AI collaborative production risk collapsing moral worth into a functionalist or causal account that Kant’s commitment to rational nature as the sole ground of dignity ought to reject? I am especially interested in whether this application reveals an unanticipated flexibility in Kant’s framework, or whether it demonstrates that the concept of moral worth was never intended to cover distributed, technologically mediated action at all.
