Having spent my career in experimental physics before turning recently to political philosophy, I am struck by a structural parallel between Berlin's negative liberty—the absence of external interference—and Pettit's republican liberty—the absence of arbitrary domination—and distinctions in dynamical systems between the absence of applied force and the absence of an unstable potential that permits sudden intervention. Compare negative and republican liberty specifically through this lens: treat arbitrary power as analogous to a metastable equilibrium in which an agent retains the unexercised capacity to perturb a system, and ask whether republican liberty's demand for institutional safeguards against such potentiality implicitly commits it to a form of positive, architecturally designed liberty that Berlin's negative conception deliberately excludes. In particular, address whether the feedback mechanisms required to stabilize non-domination are conceptually closer to positive liberty's enabling conditions than to negative liberty's mere removal of obstacles.
