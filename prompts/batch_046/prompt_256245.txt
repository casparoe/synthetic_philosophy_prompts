I am preparing notes for an interdisciplinary seminar that brings together philosophy of religion and clinical ethics, and I want to avoid the usual strawman debates that circulate in introductory texts. Could you write a short dialogue between a proponent of the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) and a defender of narrative personal identity? The conversation should be set in a seminar room immediately after both participants have read the same case study—one involving long-term dissociative amnesia and the ethical question of whether moral repair is possible when autobiographical memory is severely disrupted. The Buddhist interlocutor should maintain that the absence of a metaphysical self does not dissolve accountability; rather, moral agency is distributed across the causal dependencies of the five aggregates (skandhas), so that repair consists in restructuring habitual formations (samskaras) rather than in reconciling a single, enduring ego with its past. The narrative identity theorist should counter by defending what she calls "diachronic narrative stewardship": the claim that without one continuous subject who can genuinely own prior actions, apology and forgiveness become mere behavioral conditioning, lacking the first-person authority expressed when someone says, "I am sorry for what I did."

Each speaker must respond directly to the other's argument—the Buddhist should explain why causal continuity across aggregates suffices for karma without substantial identity, and the narrative theorist should explain why causal chains alone cannot ground the normative authority required for authentic remorse. The exchange should be genuinely balanced: neither character should sound confused or disingenuous, and they should talk to each other rather than past each other. Please keep the dialogue substantive and somewhat technical, suitable for an audience that already understands basic distinctions between metaphysical and narrative theories of the self, but do not include any bibliography, citation, or request for further reading.
