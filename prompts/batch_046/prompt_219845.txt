I spent this evening in a long, circular argument with a friend about Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life. We kept returning to his central claim: that asking what the meaning of life is itself arises from a category mistake—treating life as work, pursued for an external end—whereas genuine meaning is found in play, in activity valued for its own sake, which Schlick associates with youth not as a chronological stage but as a structural attitude available at any age. I want to close our exchange not with a summary but with a poem that enacts this distinction structurally rather than merely naming it.

Write a Petrarchan sonnet—octave and sestet, with the volta falling precisely at line nine—whose form deliberately subverts the usual logic of problem and resolution. In the octave, construct the "work" conception of existence through the first-person voice of an artifact of completed labor, such as a sealed architectural blueprint or a closed ledger, describing life as accumulation, deferred gratification, and teleological closure. At line nine, do not resolve the tension; instead, have the speaker realize that its own significance was never in the finished form but in the intrinsic, goal-less play of its making, and that this youthful mode can return even after labor is complete. The sestet should dissolve the initial question rather than answer it, closing with an image of circular, rule-bound but winless activity—children at play, or a game whose rules generate motion without destination. Crucially, break the rhyme scheme at the final couplet: lines thirteen and fourteen should be unrhymed, so the formal rupture at the poem's end mirrors Schlick's claim that meaning is not found in closure or achievement but in an open, self-contained present. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Schlick's distinction between intrinsic play and instrumental labor recognizes the philosophical argument, and coherent enough that a reader without that background finds it a complete, self-standing poem.
