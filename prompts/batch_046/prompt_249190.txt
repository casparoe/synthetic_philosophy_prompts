I'm taking a phil of mind course and we got into whether LLMs are agents or just simulators, and I tried to formalize my intuition but now it seems to prove way too much. Here's the arguement: (1) If a system counts as an agent, then attributing beliefs and desires to it must be inescapable — you can't replace that explanation with a mechanistic one without losing predictive power. (2) For humans, psychological explanation is inescapable: neuroscience doesn't replace it, it just underwrites it. (3) For LLMs, the mechanistic story (next-token prediction over learned weights) fully replaces the intentional story; you never need to say "it believes p" to predict its output, you just need the weights and context. (4) So LLMs aren't agents, they're simulators — they generate text that looks belief-driven without actually being driven by beliefs. (5) But now apply (1) to a thermostat with a predictive PID controller: if you don't know the physics, you have to say "it thinks the room is cold and wants to fix it" to predict its behavior, and knowing the circuit diagram doesn't always help you predict failure modes without the control-theoretic framing. (6) So by (1)-(2), that thermostat is an agent, and in fact any feedback loop that requires an intentional gloss is an agent. (7) That's clearly wrong, so one of the steps has to go. (8) If I drop (1), then being an agent doesn't require inescapability, and LLMs might still qualify, which I don't want. If I drop (3), then LLMs are agents after all, which also feels wrong. Where's the slip? I feel like "simulator" is doing hidden work between (3) and (4) but I can't pin it down. Which premise should I reject, and what does it cost me?
