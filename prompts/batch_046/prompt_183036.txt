I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and bioethics serving on the Institutional Neuro-Ethics Review Board at my university's medical center. Next Thursday we must vote on Protocol GCSR-9, an experimental intervention for progressive cortical degeneration in which a patient's neurons are replaced sequentially—left hemisphere first, then right—by synthetic analogues that preserve both synaptic connectivity and global information integration over a six-month window. The investigators maintain that personal identity and decision-making capacity remain continuous throughout because the replacement is gradual and function-preserving. The patient's family, however, has raised an objection that invokes the logic of Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle: during the intermediate stage, when biological tissue and synthetic substrate are simultaneously active but not yet fully integrated, the patient's self—what Dennett might describe as the center of narrative gravity—could be ambiguously distributed or split between substrates. They argue that this undermines the validity of informed consent obtained at the outset and suggests we may be authorizing experimentation on a being whose location of selfhood is genuinely indeterminate. Our board policy requires us either to approve with specific conditions, to defer pending further review with a clear timeline, or to reject; yet we have no regulatory precedent for cases of partial-substrate identity. Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of these three courses of action, include a concrete time frame and specific conditions if approval or deferral is chosen, and treat the family's concern about narrative fragmentation seriously rather than reducing it to a metaphor. In particular, address whether the gradual-replacement scenario genuinely instantiates the brain-transplant problem or whether the continuity of functional integration dissolves the ambiguity, and explain how that philosophical judgment should shape our institutional decision.
