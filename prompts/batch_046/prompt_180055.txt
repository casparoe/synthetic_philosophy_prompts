As a high school student reading philosophy of mind outside my curriculum, I have become stuck on what looks like a stalled debate in my discussion group about Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of creatures—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—and whether it can be applied to modern large language models. One side insists that these models are essentially Skinnerian pattern-matchers, shaped by reinforcement from training data without internal hypotheses. Another side argues they are Popperian: next-token prediction functions like a conjecture that is constantly tested and falsified by the corpus, allowing the system to die in place of its errors. A smaller group claims they are Gregorian, manipulating linguistic symbols as tools that restructure cognition, not merely reacting or predicting. We all agree on the underlying architecture—transformers, attention mechanisms, next-token prediction—yet nobody has moved their position in months. I want a diagnosis: is this a substantive disagreement about cognitive kinds, a verbal dispute over what 'mind' or 'creature' means, or a case where we are answering different questions—evolutionary origin versus functional architecture—under a single heading? I also suspect there may be a hidden empirical question we are avoiding, perhaps about whether Gregorian status requires embodied interaction or a specific kind of recursive self-modeling that current systems lack, and whether that absence collapses them back into the Popperian category. Please examine what each side actually commits to, say which kind of disagreement is doing the work, and tell me what would count as progress: a formal distinction, an experiment, or a change of question. And if my framing is confused—if, for example, Dennett's taxonomy was designed only for evolved biological minds and was never meant to classify engineered systems—say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise.
