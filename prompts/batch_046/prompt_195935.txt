As a retired geochemist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science, I have been following the dispute over whether the Anthropocene should be recognized as a formal geological epoch. The disagreement involves stratigraphers insisting on a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, historians treating the term as a historiographical framing rather than a claim about rock layers, and climate scientists employing it as an established reality regardless of formal status. Despite decades of discussion, the sides do not converge. Is this stall due to substantive empirical disagreement—say, over whether mid-twentieth-century nuclear signals or earlier industrial markers provide the appropriate boundary—or does it reflect a deeper divergence about what geological periodization is meant to do? In particular, do the parties differ about facts, about the norms of scientific classification, or about whether naming an epoch is a purely descriptive act versus one that assigns collective moral responsibility? I also suspect a hidden disagreement about authority: geologists as custodians of the stratigraphic record versus a broader interdisciplinary community. Please diagnose the structure of this impasse rather than adjudicating it, and indicate what—if anything—would count as progress: a refined stratigraphic criterion, a conceptual distinction, or a reformulation of the question. In your response, please state how confident you are in your diagnosis and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
