I'm a retired field entomologist who spent about thirty years studying hymenoptera behavior, and since I retired I've been trying to read philosphy of mind to keep my brain from turning to mush. I recently got obsessed with this old observation about Sphex ichneumoneus -- you know, the wasp that paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow, and then if you move the cricket a few inches away it starts the whole inspection-and-dragging ritual all over again. In the philosophy books they always use it to illustrate determinism versus free will, but I think that's missing the actual mechanistic point. I wrote up some notes for an essay arguing that sphexishness isn't really about metaphysical determinism at all; it's about running an open-loop control sequence without any back-trak subroutine that can abort and rewind the state machine. The wasp isn't "lacking free will" in some spooky sense, it's just hardcoded without sensory feedback that interrupts the loop.

My idea is to frame human flexibility -- and maybe what people call free will -- as just being further along the same continuum: a high-dimensional closed-loop system with nested error-correction and local reversibility, not some magical escape from causation. I want to compare the wasp's rigidity to behavioral architectures in more flexible insects or even mammalian foraging, where you see genuine state revision. The part I'm unsure about is whether this actually dissolves the philosophical problem or if I'm just smuggling compatabalism in under engineering vocabulary. I also wonder if I'm unfairly conflating mechanical rigidity with an absence of learning -- maybe the wasp could learn in other contexts, and I'm overreading one ritual. Could you look over these notes and tell me if the argument holds water? I'm especially curious whether the open-loop versus closed-loop framing is genuinely illuminating here or just a retired biologist's metaphor running wild. Does this miss something important that philosophers actually care about in the free-will debate? I don't need citations or a reading list -- just honest comments on whether the idea sounds interesting or if I'm reinventing a wheel that's already been beaten to death. Any suggestions for tightening the logic, especially on the distinction between behavioral flexibility and representational decoupling, would be great. I have a feeling I might be blurring those two and I want to check before I go further.
