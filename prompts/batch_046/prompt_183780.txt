I'm a PhD student in philosophy of psychology. At a recent interdisciplinary workshop on Stoicism's modern clinical revival, a facilitator repeatedly invoked the line, "You control your judgments, not the world," using it to argue that a client's anxiety about workplace discrimination was misdirected: since organizational culture lies outside direct personal control, emotional energy should be withdrawn from it and reinvested solely in internal appraisal. I want you to treat that sentence not as a platitude but as a compressed argument that could hide several distinct theses. Separate the precise claims it might be making—for example, that voluntary mental acts are the only locus of direct agency; that practical rationality requires ignoring every target that is not directly mental; that moral responsibility tracks only what is immediately controllable; that all affective responses are judgment-mediated and therefore always revisable; or that social structures, being external, permanently fall outside the boundary of ethical concern. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, or false, and specify which one (if any) actually licenses the facilitator's inference from "you do not control the culture" to "you should stop caring about it structurally." I also want you to test whether injecting distinctions like diachronic control—agency over extended processes rather than momentary acts—and socially distributed agency—control partly constituted by collective practices—breaks the binary that makes the slogan sound compelling in the first place. Present the results as a menu of readings with brief verdicts rather than picking one winner, identify which reading was being invoked in the workshop argument, and say whether that reading survives once those distinctions are on the table. Keep the response under 350 words.
