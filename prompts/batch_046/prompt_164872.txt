I am writing as a high school student currently enrolled in an advanced ethics seminar, where we recently examined the classic comparison between the severe torture of one individual and the distribution of an almost imperceptible irritation—like a single dust particle in the eye—across an enormous population. During our discussion, I developed a line of reasoning that I cannot seem to shake, and I would like a direct evaluation of it rather than a neutral survey of positions.

My reasoning runs as follows. The dust-speck scenario is usually presented to test whether moral harms can be summed across people when each instance falls below any meaningful threshold of suffering. I take the intuitive resistance to aggregation here to imply something stronger: that a harm which never enters conscious experience lacks intrinsic moral weight entirely, not merely that it is too small to accumulate. From this, I derive a principle I will call the Phenomenal Boundary Thesis—namely, that moral wrongness requires a subject to actually experience the badness in question. If this thesis is correct, then statistical or distributed harms that operate entirely beneath the level of awareness—such as a hypothetical environmental exposure that produces no felt symptoms but measurably degrades cellular repair mechanisms across millions—should generate no direct moral obligation, no matter how large the affected population. By contrast, torture remains unambiguously wrong because it violently crosses that phenomenal boundary.

So my question is this: assuming the torture-versus-dust-specks comparison genuinely supports the Phenomenal Boundary Thesis, does it follow that policies or conditions causing only sub-phenomenal damage are morally neutral even at massive scale? Or does the original thought experiment fail to establish anything about the moral status of unfelt harm, in which case my application to the cellular-repair case collapses? I ask for a committed answer—please take a definite stand on whether imperceptible harms can ever aggregate into a serious wrong, defend that stand with clear reasons, and identify the precise mistake in my reasoning if you reject the Phenomenal Boundary Thesis. I am not looking for a literature survey; I want the argument evaluated on its merits.
