My friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether zoning categories can survive once you take conceptual fluidity seriously. I tried to formalize the worry into an argument, and now I think it proves far too much—probably by smuggling in an equivocation about category membership—but I cannot pinpoint the slide. Here is the chain as I laid it out:

(1) Zoning law treats “residential” and “commercial” as mutually exclusive categories with determinate boundaries.
(2) But conceptual categories are not classically bounded; they have radial structures with central prototypes and graded peripheral members, as shown in analogical-reasoning research.
(3) Every residential parcel contains peripheral commercial activity (home offices, delivery logistics, digital labor), and every commercial parcel contains residential traces (caretaker units, live-work lofts, overnight security presence).
(4) If peripheral members belong to a category, then residential zones are already commercial zones, and vice versa.
(5) Therefore the zoning distinction is conceptually incoherent.
(6) The same radial-structure reasoning applies to any regulatory boundary—vehicle versus pedestrian, private versus public, permitted versus prohibited use.
(7) So if zoning collapses, all legal categorization collapses.
(8) But legal categorization is not globally incoherent; courts apply it successfully every day.
(9) Hence the argument must contain a hidden error.

I feel the pressure is between (3) and (4): membership in a legal category might be governed by dominant use or legislative intent rather than by analogical proximity. But my friend insists that if you are genuinely committed to fluid concepts, you cannot simply switch to classical logic when it is convenient for urban planning. Where exactly does the argument slip—at (2), by overstating the universality of radial structure in legal concepts; at (4), by conflating analogical similarity with categorical membership; or at (6), by overgeneralizing? And if I have to defend one step to keep zoning intact without abandoning conceptual fluidity altogether, which one is least costly to reject?
