I am a senior software engineer with no formal philosophy background. At my company I chair a quarterly peer-recognition review: engineers submit nominations describing a colleague’s exceptional judgment, and I must write a brief evaluation that distinguishes not merely whether the outcome was good, but whether the action carries genuine moral worth—whether the engineer acted for the right reasons rather than producing the right result accidentally or for the wrong motives. I need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as a flowchart in words with ordered tests, that another tech lead with no philosophy training could run in about ten minutes per nomination using only the nomination form and one brief clarifying question. The procedure must be grounded in what philosophy actually holds about moral worth and acting for right reasons, but it should not ask me to study texts; it should translate the relevant distinctions into practical steps. It must explicitly flag which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, must say where it will misfire or fail to settle a case, and must state clearly what it cannot do—especially where mental-state opacity makes judgment unreliable. The procedure should incorporate two specific conceptual tools defined in plain language: a Counterfactual Stability Test (would the agent still have acted if the right-making feature were absent but the self-interested feature remained?) and a Public Justification Filter (can the agent cite the right reason in a transparent explanation without contradiction?). Apply the procedure to three hypothetical nominations: (1) an engineer quietly refactored a brittle authentication module that eliminated a serious security vulnerability, but only began after learning the CTO was auditing security debt and they needed visibility for promotion; (2) an engineer stayed overnight fixing a production outage caused by their own earlier deployment error, repairing it without disclosing their fault, with the nominator calling them heroic; (3) an engineer advocated adopting a less efficient open-source library because it improves screen-reader accessibility, knowing the CTO tracks accessibility metrics, and genuinely caring about inclusion but also aware of the career benefit. One of these applications should yield an answer I will find unwelcome—the procedure must be honest even when it undermines my instinct to reward good outcomes. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not use verbatim quotations from philosophical texts; if you refer to a specific idea, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Keep the tone formal and polished, fix the role and stakes, and mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.
