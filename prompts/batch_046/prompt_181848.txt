I have been trying to understand the debate about free-floating rationales—reasons that are supposed to exist independently of any particular agent or reasoner. Some defenders of this view talk about reasons being “archived” in situations themselves, almost like normative data stored in the world that retains its force even if nobody ever reads it. From this, I assumed that if such an archived reason really exists, then any rational agent who happens never to encounter or recognize it is still failing in some way—like ignoring a signal that was objectively addressed to them. But then I wondered: doesn’t this mean that internalists, who claim every genuine reason must connect to an agent’s actual or potential motivations, are forced to deny that there can ever be unknown moral obligations? After all, if a reason only exists once it hooks into someone’s psychology, then an obligation that remains completely hidden from an agent wouldn’t really be a reason for them at all. Is the archival model just a confused attempt to give normative weight to inert facts, or is there a coherent way for free-floating rationales to generate demands on agents who have no cognitive access to them?
