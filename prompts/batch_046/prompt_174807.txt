For my final-year independent study in moral philosophy, I have been examining luck egalitarianism and the longstanding objection that its central distinction—between outcomes for which individuals are responsible and those that are merely the product of fortune—cannot be applied consistently without either abandoning people to avoidable hardship or collapsing into a more general egalitarianism. The debate has persisted for decades: defenders maintain that the distinction captures an intuitive difference between a reckless gambler and a victim of congenital illness, while critics argue that every choice is shaped by unchosen social and psychological conditions, so the boundary is always arbitrarily drawn.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not really about distributive justice but about two incompatible conceptions of responsibility. One side treats responsibility as requiring causal authorship—an agent must be the genuine origin of her choice—while the other treats it as requiring only epistemic access, meaning an agent is responsible if she knew the risks and could have acted differently, regardless of whether her preferences were themselves determined. If this is correct, then the disagreement is partly conceptual: the sides are answering different questions about what makes a choice genuinely one’s own, and they share no common metric for where to draw the boundary.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical claim—perhaps about the psychology of decision-making or the sociology of risk exposure—that is doing the real work? Could the impasse be resolved by a new formal distinction, for example one that separates modal control (what was genuinely avoidable in nearby possible circumstances) from normative expectation (what society may reasonably demand of an agent), or is the question itself ill-posed because choice and chance are not natural kinds but rhetorical devices? I want an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual clarification, an empirical finding, or a shift in the framing question—could actually move the debate forward, and if not, why the disagreement is structurally immune to resolution. I am not asking for a survey of positions or a bibliography; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s architecture, supported by what proponents and critics actually say about cases like voluntary risk-taking versus inherited disadvantage, together with a clear statement of what progress would have to look like given your diagnosis.
