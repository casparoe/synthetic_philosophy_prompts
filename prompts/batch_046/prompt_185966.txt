I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have noticed a recurring pattern in technical and personal decision-making that I cannot name, and I want to know whether philosophy has already carved it out. Here is the concrete structure. A lead architect wants to migrate a monolith to microservices. Rather than defending the preference directly, she designs an evaluation framework that weights scalability, team autonomy, and deployment velocity highly—values that happen to align with microservices—while treating operational simplicity and data consistency as secondary. She does not falsify data; she sincerely believes these weights are the correct ones. Once the framework outputs a recommendation matching her initial preference, she treats the output as independent corroboration, citing it in meetings as “what the model says.” She would defend the weights sincerely if challenged, yet an outside engineer can see that the framework was calibrated—perhaps unconsciously—to produce exactly this result.

I have seen a parallel in personal life: someone builds a spreadsheet to decide whether to accept a job offer, assigns heavy weights to prestige and commute time because those happen to match the offer they want, then describes the choice as “data-driven.” In both cases there is an external artifact—metrics, weights, a formal process—that sits between the agent and their belief. The agent genuinely endorses the artifact’s neutrality and genuinely holds the resulting preference, yet there is no felt conflict, no conscious deception, and no obvious post-hoc filtering of evidence.

Is there an established concept for this specific structure—where something like self-deception, if that category even applies, is mediated through an instrument or procedure that the agent sincerely treats as authoritative? I am familiar with motivated reasoning and confirmation bias, but those seem to describe filtering or interpreting evidence after one already has a preference, not constructing an evaluation apparatus whose parameters are already aligned with one’s desired conclusion and then outsourcing belief-formation to it. I also know the phrase “bad faith” only in a loose, popular sense, and it does not seem to capture the role of the intermediate layer.

If philosophy does not have a precise term, I would like one defined in a way that distinguishes this phenomenon from three nearby cases: plain rationalization (where I defend a belief I already hold with arguments I do not fully endorse), unmediated self-deception (where I hold contradictory beliefs without an external scaffold), and deliberate rigging (where I knowingly design the weights to reach a predetermined result and hide that fact). What should we call the boundary case in which an agent is neither fully transparent to themselves nor deliberately deceptive, but has calibrated—likely without full awareness—a formalized tool to match their preference, and then treats the tool’s output as an independent reason?

What exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not, and where do its edges lie? I am less interested in a bare label than in a careful carving that explains whether self-deception is even possible in this mediated form, and whether the presence of the artifact changes the moral or epistemic evaluation of the agent.
