I have recently begun drafting a paper on determinism in gravitational physics, and a senior colleague summarized Einstein's hole argument for me in the following terms: "In 1913 Einstein discovered that if the field equations of general relativity are fully generally covariant, then within any matter-free region—what he called a hole—one can apply an active diffeomorphism that shifts the metric values from point to point while leaving all matter fields and boundary conditions outside untouched. This generates infinitely many distinct metric configurations compatible with the same physical data, which means the theory cannot uniquely determine the geometry inside the hole from conditions outside, and therefore determinism fails. Einstein regarded this failure as so severe that he temporarily rejected full general covariance, adopting the restricted Entwurf equations instead, and he only reinstated general covariance in 1915 once he had convinced himself that spacetime points possess no independent physical identity—what he called the point-coincidence argument—so that the apparently different metrics are in fact one and the same state. Contemporary philosophers of physics often treat this episode as decisive proof that spacetime is not a substantial container but a purely relational structure, and that any ontology of points as individual entities is metaphysically obsolete." I am unsure whether I should rely on this account in my own work. Is it a fair summary? Please distinguish what is historically and technically accurate, what is a defensible modern interpretation, what subtly misstates Einstein's reasoning or the logical structure of the argument—particularly regarding whether the resolution truly establishes pure relationalism or merely removes a gauge redundancy—and whether the philosophical conclusion drawn from it actually follows.
