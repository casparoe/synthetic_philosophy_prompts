Design a final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of language, gender, and conceptual framing, together with a model answer of approximately 700 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The question must center on Douglas Hofstadter’s satire “A Person Paper on Purity in Language,” but it should be unanswerable by mere summary: students must reconstruct the specific “default assumption” that Hofstadter targets—namely, the treatment of masculine pronouns and occupational nouns as linguistically unmarked and therefore genuinely generic—then evaluate whether his strategy of satirical reversal (substituting feminine terms as the purported generic) actually dismantles that default or instead preserves an underlying binary framework by treating gender as a symmetric pair. The prompt should also require students to address one standard meta-objection: that satirical exaggeration, however effective as rhetoric, fails as philosophical argument because it offers no normative account of how language ought to change and risks substituting inversion for analysis. The model answer should distinguish linguistic markedness from conceptual framing, identify at least one place where Hofstadter’s reversal might leave non-binary or gender-neutral linguistic practices unexamined, and clarify whether the essay’s force depends on an implicit theory of linguistic determinism. The marking scheme should specify five must-hit moves, the marks each carries, and a brief note on how candidates typically miss it; separate an A answer (which must contain all five moves executed with philosophical precision) from a B answer (which reconstructs the satire accurately but treats the binary-preservation objection superficially or omits the distinction between markedness and framing). Do not ask examinees to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature beyond Hofstadter’s essay itself, and do not frame the task around obscure post-1985 debates that would require specialized knowledge outside the essay’s immediate conceptual terrain. Write the prompt in a formal, polished register suitable for a course syllabus.
