I'm giving a seminar next tuesday defending this weird hybrid view I cooked up: I think Williamson's attack on the KK principle only works if you model knowledge as a binary on/off state, like a light switch being fully luminous or not. But if you model knowing as a graded signal-detection problem—basically a dimmer switch calibrated to noise, false-positive rates, and contextual thresholds—then you can save a statistical version of KK without buying full luminosity. I'm calling it the calibrated-transparency view, though I'm not married to the name. My argument sketch is that once you treat "knowing that p" as a calibrated confidence state rather than an all-or-nothing illumination, the margin-for-error cases stop being counterexamples and become expected calibration drift, so the second-order claim "if I know p then I know I know p" survives as a probabilistic expectation rather than a logical guarantee.
