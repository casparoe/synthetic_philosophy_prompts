I am preparing a position paper for a philosophy seminar on meaning and value, and I have been trying to crystallise my own interpretation of Moritz Schlick's thesis that the significance of life lies in play rather than in the attainment of ends. Before I present it, I would like a consistency audit of the following commitments. First, I read Schlick's claim about play as a normative doctrine about the proper allocation of attention—an ethical perfectionism concerning how consciousness ought to be structured—rather than as a merely descriptive psychological observation. Second, I maintain that this normative claim is fully compatible with a physicalist ontology of mind, since playful engagement can be cashed out in functional terms without invoking any non-natural properties. Third, I argue that Schlick's distinction between a youthful attitude of pure play and a mature attitude of instrumental purpose is ultimately unstable, because sustained playful activity tends to become instrumentalised by biological or social aims; yet, fourth, I also want to resist the subjectivist conclusion that meaning is therefore purely private, proposing instead that there are intersubjective ludic norms—conditions of genuine playfulness that are publicly assessable and that constrain which activities can confer meaning. Fifth, and finally, I treat Schlick's view as closer to a Stoic practice of attentive presence than to either hedonism or utilitarian maximisation.

Do these five commitments form a coherent package, or do they pull in incompatible directions? I am especially concerned about whether the instability claim in my third point undermines the normative foundation in my first: if play is invariably instrumentalised, can it still ground the perfectionist demand I attribute to Schlick? There is also a potential conflict between my fourth point—the intersubjective ludic norms—and Schlick's well-known logical-empiricist hostility to metaphysical or mind-independent normative facts; does this commit me to a kind of moral realism that sits awkwardly with his anti-metaphysical stance? At the same time, I suspect there may be only an apparent tension between my second commitment (physicalist ontology) and my fifth (the Stoic-perfectionist framing), since functional descriptions of mental states do not by themselves determine normative structure, though I want this checked rather than assumed. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, indicate for each which single revision would be least costly—whether abandoning the intersubjective constraint, softening the instrumentalisation claim, or dropping the perfectionist analogy—and clarify which pairings dissolve on closer inspection. I should stress that I am not asking for a reading list, bibliography, or citation of specific passages; I need only the conceptual audit. Please answer in British English.
