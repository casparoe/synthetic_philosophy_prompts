But the structure of the critique applies to conceptual history too. Consider the standard narrative of 'the scientific method' as linear progress from Baconian induction through Mill's canons to Popper's falsificationism, with each stage correcting the deficiencies of the last. Is this narrative Whiggish? In your answer, distinguish carefully between (i) retrospective normative assessment—judging past thinkers by epistemic standards they did not hold—and (ii) genetic fallacy in conceptual genealogy—treating historical development of an idea as directed toward its present form. Does the narrative commit (i), (ii), both, or neither? The student's answer (approx. 300 words):

"The narrative is clearly Whiggish, though the diagnosis requires care. Butterfield's central claim is that any evaluation of past thinkers by present conceptual schemes commits us to the genetic fallacy in conceptual genealogy, because to measure Bacon against Popper is already to treat Popper's framework as the hidden endpoint of Bacon's project. This is the core of Whig historiography: not just judging the past, but organizing it as an incomplete version of the present.

We should distinguish, however, between retrospective normative assessment—asking whether past methods achieved their own stated aims using our best current criteria—and this genetic fallacy, which arranges historical development into a linear trajectory. The standard scientific-method narrative commits both. It evaluates Bacon's inductivism as deficient because it cannot survive Popperian criticism (retrospective assessment), and it presents Mill's canons as transitional refinements that existed mainly to bridge the gap between naive induction and rigorous falsification (genetic fallacy). In fact, Popper developed falsificationism specifically to resolve the logical asymmetry that Mill's canons left unaddressed, so it is fair to see Mill as working inside a problem-space that only makes full sense from a Popperian vantage.

Still, Butterfield's original critique targets only the genetic fallacy. Because science pursues explicit normative goals—truth and predictive success—retrospective assessment is not merely allowed but obligatory. We can legitimately ask whether Bacon's methods produced reliable knowledge, using Popperian standards, without being Whiggish, so long as we do not also claim that Bacon was implicitly aiming at falsificationism. The narrative therefore fails only on its structural claim: it treats conceptual history as destiny rather than as rational reconstruction. Once that structural error is removed, cross-temporal normative evaluation remains fully legitimate.

So the answer is both, but only the genetic-fallacy half is genuinely Whiggish by Butterfield's lights."
