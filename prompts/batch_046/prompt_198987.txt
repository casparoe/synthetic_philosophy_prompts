Bad faith, then, is the self-deception by which a person treats one facticity—say, a social role, a psychological trait, or a past choice—as if it exhaustively defines the for-itself, thereby fleeing the anguish of total responsibility. I want to extend this by suggesting that in an era of algorithmic profiling and predictive behavioral modeling, bad faith takes on a new structural form: the subject treats externally generated predictive scores, such as recommendation algorithms, risk assessments, or personality forecasts, not merely as heuristic tools but as ontological definitions of the self. Rather than using these predictions as part of the given situation that freedom must transcend, the subject in bad faith allows them to function as a kind of pre-written narrative that closes off the openness of the future—in effect, allowing a dynamically updated statistical profile to serve as an institutionalized spirit of seriousness.

I'm particularly unsure about two moves. First, am I right to think that Sartre's critique of deterministic psychology—his dismissal of unconscious motives or fixed character traits as excuses—can be analogously applied to predictive models that claim only probabilistic authority over behavior? Or does the statistical, non-causal nature of these predictions break the analogy? Second, does treating an algorithmic profile as facticity confuse Sartre's distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-itself, since the profile is itself a constructed representation rather than a brute fact? Please comment on the accuracy of the Sartrean interpretation, on whether the analogy to algorithmic prediction is philosophically interesting or strained, and on whether I've missed an obvious objection—especially any that turns on Sartre's own remarks about situation and facticity. In your response, please separate what is well-established in Sartre scholarship from what remains genuinely contested or speculative, and avoid presenting any contested interpretive claim—such as the precise scope of radical freedom in Sartre's early phenomenological ontology versus his later Marxist turn—as if it were the consensus view.
