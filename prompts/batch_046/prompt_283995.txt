In a recent discussion of rescue obligations, an interlocutor appealed to the maxim that numbers should count to defend the claim that, other things being equal, a rational agent ought to direct scarce aid toward the larger of two groups facing identical risk. I found the maxim ambiguous in precisely the way that allows it to do argumentative work: it slides between statistical, axiological, and deontic readings without marking the shift. Could you disentangle the distinct precise theses it might express—distinguishing, for instance, (i) a tautological claim about cardinal comparison; (ii) an aggregation principle for individual well-being; (iii) an agent-neutral reason whose strength scales with group size; (iv) an epistemic heuristic for identifying the correct course; (v) a tie-breaking rule for cases of moral equality; and (vi) a claim about the constant or diminishing marginal moral value of saving additional lives? For each, please indicate whether it is trivially true, genuinely substantive and defensible, or false. Then specify which of these readings actually licenses the inference from the larger group being larger to the agent being required to save it rather than the smaller group in a scenario where only one can be rescued, and which leave the inference unsupported—particularly if the agent bears a prior special obligation to a member of the smaller group, or if saving the larger group requires the active sacrifice of the smaller group's sole member.
