People defending compulsory voting love the jury-duty analogy: voting is like being summoned for jury service, both are brief civic burdens that keep democracy from being captured by the motivated few, and if we accept one we ought to accept the other. Lately I've seen this stretched further -- lowering the voting age to 16 is like letting younger people sit on juries, and giving local votes to long-term non-citizens is like letting permanent residents serve on juries in the districts where they actually live. I'm not convinced the family holds together definatly.

Can you audit the analogy properly? Give me a correspondence table -- what plays the role of the summons, the pool, the verdict, the excuse for absence, the penalty for skipping -- then the points where the mapping breaks, especially for the stretched versions (16-year-olds, non-citizens). Which disanalogies actually undermine the justification for compulsion, and which are just cosmetic? And does the conclusion -- that we ought to have compulsory voting, maybe with these extensions -- survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work?
