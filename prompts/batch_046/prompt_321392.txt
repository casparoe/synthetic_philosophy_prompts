In discussions of Buridan’s ass and the problem of choice without preference, it is common to hear the agent compared to a digital logic gate fed by two identical input signals: both pathways remain open, the error signal is exactly zero, and without some injected noise or arbitrary tie-breaker the circuit deadlocks and never produces an output. The comparison is usually invoked to argue that pure rationality—understood as perfect equilibrium between reasons—is not merely impractical but structurally paralytic, and that functional agency therefore requires an asymmetrical “noise” component (a random bit, a habit, a visceral lean) that rational deliberation itself cannot generate. Audit this analogy carefully: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the ass, the two bales, the starvation outcome, the tie-breaking mechanism, and the observer onto their counterparts in the circuit model; then identify where the mapping fractures—particularly with respect to whether the agent is passive hardware or an active chooser, whether “starvation” is a meaningful analog of a suspended computation, and whether the need for noise in engineered systems genuinely transfers to normative reasoning about human or rational agency. Separate the disanalogies that merely decorate the comparison from those that undermine the conclusion that rationality must be supplemented by non-rational disruption. Finally, say whether the argument that perfect balance produces dysfunction survives once the analogy is fully unpacked, and if not, suggest a more apt comparison for what actually happens when an agent faces genuinely equipollent options.
