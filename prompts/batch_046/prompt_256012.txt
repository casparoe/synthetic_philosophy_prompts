I am a retired experimental physicist who, in retirement, have taken up philosophy of science and mind. I now serve on an interdisciplinary review board that evaluates autonomous field instruments—biomimetic drones, adaptive sensor networks, and self-modifying ecological sampling devices—before they are cleared for deployment in protected reserves. Our board needs a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Dennett’s framework of the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—that determines which explanatory and predictive posture is appropriate for a given system or subsystem, specifies the conditions under which a shift between stances is legitimate, and marks the precise points where the framework runs out and a human judgment call is unavoidable. The procedure must be operable by board members without philosophical training, run as an ordered sequence of questions, state what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. It should preserve the distinctions that actually carry explanatory weight—predictive reliability, strategic explanation, and ontological commitment—while dropping technical refinements that do not survive translation into practice. Please include a compact set of test questions that can be applied in minutes, note where the procedure will misfire, and apply it to two proposals: first, a swarm of aerial drones that use only local interaction rules to construct temporary nesting structures, a case in which some board members are inclined to attribute collective intentionality but the procedure should resist or severely qualify that attribution; second, an individual underwater sensor that continuously rewrites its own calibration thresholds in response to salinity fluctuations, a case where the physical stance seems adequate but a design-stance reading is temptingly available. For each application, show the step-by-step outcome, including one result that conflicts with the board’s initial intuition. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—specifically, whether it can ever license attributing moral standing or welfare interests to a system, and exactly where that question must be handed off to ethics rather than to philosophy of explanation. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable should stand on its own for a practitioner who will not search the literature.
