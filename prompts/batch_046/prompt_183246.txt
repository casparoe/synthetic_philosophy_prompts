I'm drafting a proposal for a three-month philosophy-of-law independent-study grant and need a concise, formal document—about two pages—framed for an interdisciplinary review panel. The project uses Norway's Halden prison and American mass incarceration not as empirical case studies to be evaluated for recidivism rates, but as competing institutional expressions of theories about moral personhood over time. Halden's normalization principle holds that prison life should mirror outside life, preserving future-oriented planning and social roles. American long-term incarceration, by contrast, structurally enforces a kind of moral suspension: the prisoner is separated not only physically but narratively from the continuity of their pre-carceral life through sentence length, collateral consequences, and carceral architecture. My core question is philosophical rather than sociological: is normalization compatible with any non-instrumental theory of punishment? More precisely, does a defensible retributive or expressive account require that the offender's moral status be suspended during the sentence, or can punishment be executed without disrupting the temporal continuity that normalization seeks to preserve? The proposal should include: (1) the precise question, stated so that institutional comparison alone cannot answer it; (2) why the standard retributivism-versus-rehabilitation debate leaves this open—specifically, why treating rehabilitation as merely instrumental misses the conceptual tension; (3) the method: conceptual analysis of moral suspension and temporal continuity applied to prison design features, with an argument for why this philosophical approach makes progress where normative sociology or legal history would not, and why obvious alternatives—such as direct empirical evaluation—are inappropriate for the question asked; (4) what success looks like (for example, demonstrating that normalization is conceptually incompatible with strong retributivism, or that moral suspension collapses into a contingent feature of American practice lacking philosophical justification) and what failure looks like (for instance, if the distinction between suspension and mere hardship proves unstable, or if retributive punishment turns out to require only physical separation rather than narrative disruption); (5) a realistic scope for three months of conceptual work with no field access, and a clear deliverable—a structured philosophical argument illustrated by institutional features, not a bibliography, literature review, or citation list, which I will supply separately. Write in a formal, polished register.
