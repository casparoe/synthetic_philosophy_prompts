Here is an argument my study partner constructed about bounded rationality that I think collapses into something absurd, but I cannot locate the crack. (1) Rational choice requires selecting the option with the highest expected utility among those that are evaluated. (2) Because of bounded rationality, agents lack the computational resources to evaluate all available options, so they must rely on a stopping rule—accepting the first option that exceeds an aspiration level. Call this first-order satisficing. (3) But selecting the aspiration level itself, and selecting the stopping rule that applies it, are also choices with significant utility consequences. (4) By (2), computing the optimal aspiration level and the optimal first-order stopping rule exceeds the agent's bounded resources. So the agent must apply a second-order stopping rule to choose the first-order one—call this second-order satisficing. (5) The same reasoning applies at every level: choosing the n-th-order decision rule requires an (n+1)-th-order stopping rule. (6) Thus bounded rationality generates an infinite hierarchy of meta-satisficing procedures. (7) An infinite hierarchy cannot be executed; a finite hierarchy terminates arbitrarily at some level N, which receives no rational justification and is exempt from the very constraint that generated the hierarchy. (8) Therefore, if bounded rationality holds, rational choice either never begins or rests on an unjustified base. (9) But rational choice clearly occurs and is not arbitrary. (10) So bounded rationality is incompatible with rationality, and we must either be unbounded optimizers or abandon rational choice altogether. I want to preserve (2) and (9). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a principled way to halt the regress at a base heuristic without reintroducing unbounded computation?
