I'm taking an undergraduate philosophy of law course, and I encountered a popular essay that summarizes the Lockean justification for intellectual property through what it calls the rivalrousness gap. The summary states: "Locke's labor-mixing argument applies only to tangible goods because its proviso—that appropriation must leave enough and as good for others—assumes rivalrous consumption. Because ideas are non-rival, claiming one does not deplete the common stock, so the proviso is automatically satisfied; yet patents and copyrights artificially restrict access, which harms others by blocking use of something they could have employed without diminishing anyone's share. Therefore, Locke's theory actually prohibits intellectual property, and modern IP regimes must rely entirely on utilitarian incentive arguments rather than natural rights. The essay also notes that defenders of IP often retreat from labor-mixing to personality-based or expression-based arguments precisely because the rivalrousness gap undermines the original Lockean case." How fair is this characterization? Please evaluate it point by point: which parts correctly capture Locke's proviso and the economics of non-rival goods, which subtly invert the logic of the proviso, and whether the conclusion that Lockean rights forbid IP actually follows. I also want to know if the claim that IP defenders retreat to personality-based arguments is an accurate description of the literature, and whether the natural-rights versus utilitarian dichotomy survives these corrections. Please answer in American English.
