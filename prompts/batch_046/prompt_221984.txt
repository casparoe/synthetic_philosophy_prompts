I am writing a consistency check for my seminar paper on EU political theory, and I want to know whether the following commitments can be jointly defended or whether they secretly undermine one another. Here is my position: (1) The democratic deficit should be assessed through a polycentric standard rather than a Westminster benchmark—democratic authorization in the Union is distributed across national parliaments, the European Parliament, and deliberative forums, with no single institution being sufficient but the ensemble being adequate; (2) Sovereignty is pooled, not divided: member states do not hold reserved domains of exclusive authority comparable to U.S. federalism, since EU law enjoys primacy and direct effect, yet they collectively retain ultimate constituent authority through the formal right of withdrawal and unanimous treaty revision, which means there is no sovereign locus either above or below the member states; (3) Ever-closer union is normatively legitimate only if it eventually produces a singular European constituent power—a unified demos capable of authorizing constitutional change—because without such a subject, deeper integration remains an elite-driven project that lacks democratic pedigree; (4) The correct institutional remedy is therefore differentiated integration, in which a federal core possesses its own parliamentary chamber and autonomous budgetary authority, while an intergovernmental outer ring retains veto rights over core decisions that affect it. Do these hang together? I am particularly concerned that (2) and (4) may be incompatible, since pooled sovereignty seems to preclude the functional division of authority implied by a core-and-ring structure, and that (1) and (3) conflict because a genuinely polycentric legitimacy should not require a singular demos in order to justify further integration. At the same time, (1) and (2) feel complementary, though I want to be certain I am not overlooking a hidden contradiction there. Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish genuine incompatibilities from merely apparent ones, and identify the least costly single revision that would restore consistency without sacrificing my central concern about democratic authorization.
