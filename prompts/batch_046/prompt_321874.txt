I'm currently enrolled in a philosophy of logic and formal epistemology seminar, and we've been working through the mechanics of self-reference in formal systems—specifically Hofstadter's idea of jumping out of the system (JOOTSing) and the MU-puzzle's demonstration that a formal system cannot prove its own completeness from within. I drafted the following argument for our discussion board, but I find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to overreach, and I can't isolate the slip.

(1) In the MU-puzzle, whether the string MU is derivable from MI is mechanically decidable within the formal rules; what cannot happen inside the system is a proof that MU is permanently non-derivable, since that requires stepping outside to arithmetic.

(2) So if a system S can represent its own syntax and derivation rules, then any claim about S's own limits—such as "X is not derivable in S"—must be established by a meta-system S' that treats S's rules as objects rather than operating within them.

(3) Now consider a rational agent whose beliefs are governed by a consistency principle analogous to the MU-rules; call this belief-system B. Suppose B can represent its own belief-forming rules.

(4) Then any belief that B is incomplete, inconsistent, or bounded must be justified from within B'—a meta-belief framework—not from within B itself.

(5) But if B' is just B operating at a higher reflective level, then B can never genuinely acknowledge its own hard limits without already having transcended them; the acknowledgment is either a mechanical derivation inside B (hence subject to B's rules and not truly about B's limits) or an unjustified leap that violates B's own consistency conditions.

(6) Therefore, no rational agent can coherently believe that their own reasoning system has absolute or structural limitations.

(7) Yet we do seem to coherently hold beliefs like "inductive inference does not guarantee truth" or "human cognition has bounded rationality."

(8) So either we are not rational agents in the relevant sense, or the analogy between formal derivability and rational belief collapses entirely.

My instructor hinted that something slips between (4) and (5), possibly involving the distinction between object-level derivation and reflective endorsement. But I'm unsure whether the real damage is done by the analogy in (3), the identification of B' with B in (5), or the move from formal undecidability to epistemic limitation in (6). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost for the broader project of using formal self-reference models to illuminate epistemic humility? I'd prefer to preserve (7) if possible, since it tracks a genuine feature of our self-understanding, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying to keep it.
