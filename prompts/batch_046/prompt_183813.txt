I've been trying to formalize my views on death, identity, and clinical ethics for a discussion group, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is where I've landed:

(1) The philosophically correct account of death is the irreversible loss of the brain's capacity for global information integration—the breakdown of the unified workspace dynamics that underwrite conscious experience—rather than the mere cessation of heartbeat or respiration.

(2) Despite (1), I think the law should retain whole-brain death (including brainstem reflexes) as the operative clinical-legal criterion, not because brainstem function is intrinsically valuable, but because it is currently the most determinable observable proxy for the condition in (1).

(3) I hold that circulatory-respiratory failure is conceptually prior to brain death: absent artificial ventilation, brain death always follows circulatory arrest, so brain death is a technologically mediated decoupling rather than an independent biological kind.

(4) My view of personal identity is psychological-continuity based: once the condition described in (1) is satisfied, the person no longer exists, regardless of whether the heart continues to beat with mechanical support.

(5) I support permitting organ procurement immediately upon legal declaration of brain death, but I interpret this not as harvesting from a biologically dead organism in the traditional sense, but as discontinuing life-support for an organism that has permanently lost the capacity for conscious existence.

(6) I reject the organismal-integration view (that death is the loss of the body's self-maintaining homeostasis), because that view would declare a ventilator-supported, brain-dead patient alive, which conflicts with both (1) and (4).

Please examine these commitments systematically. For each pair, say whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, rank the real tensions by severity, and specify the least costly revision and what it costs. I am especially concerned that (3) undermines the conceptual independence of (2), and that (5) relies on a notion of death that (3) denies; please scrutinize those connections. Also determine whether (6) introduces a hidden consistency between (2) and (4), or whether an apparent conflict between them dissolves once (6) is in play. Be precise about which single revision, if any, would clean up the most conflicts, and do not treat all tensions as equally damaging.
