Next month I defend, in a joint philosophy-neuroscience seminar, a modified Dennettian view I call weakly-coupled narrative gravity. The thesis is that the self is not a singular center of narrative gravity but a federation of autobiographical attractors held together by a variable coupling strength—what I term the decoupling coefficient—and that apparent breakdowns of personal identity, whether in severe dissociation, split-brain conflict, or engineered modular partition in artificial agents, do not eliminate the self but expose this plural structure. I claim this preserves Dennett’s anti-substantialist insight while accommodating the first-personal stability we observe, because the federation typically operates with a high effective coupling constant even though no unitary core exists.

The audience is precise: roughly half are analytic metaphysicians of personal identity who mostly defend psychological-continuity or animalist accounts and regard Dennett’s view as either eliminativist or too weak to ground moral responsibility; about a quarter are cognitive neuroscientists who study default-mode self-referential processing as a unified predictive model and are skeptical of philosophical multiplicity; the rest are phenomenologically inclined philosophers who accept that narrative shapes experience but insist that any distributed account misses the irreducible mineness of consciousness.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in the rough order they will arise. For each objection: phrase it exactly as that subgroup would phrase it; state whether it has real force against my position; specify what I should concede up front and what I must hold; provide a concise reply suitable for a five-minute exchange; and flag the weakest point in that reply. Order them by combined likelihood and damage. Finally, identify the single objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that targets the decoupling coefficient directly—explain why it is especially dangerous for my view, and sketch how I should respond if it is pressed.
