I'm taking a seminar on normative ethics and the structure of moral reasons, and I've constructed an argument about indirect normativity that seems to drive itself into a wall. I'd like help finding the exit.

Consider the view that a norm binds an agent only if it survives endorsement by an ideally rational deliberator—where "ideal" means possessing perfect information, unrestricted computational capacity, and reasoning from a position that abstracts away from particular social roles and affective attachments, but retains the basic structure of practical reason.

Here is the argument:

(1) If indirect normativity is correct, then for any agent A and norm N, N applies to A only if A's idealized counterpart would endorse N.

(2) An actual agent A and their idealized counterpart are not identical; they share rational structure but differ in information, bias, and affective constitution.

(3) Therefore, if N applies to A's idealized counterpart, it applies to A only derivatively—through a relation of structural similarity, not direct address.

(4) But moral obligations are directed: to say that A ought to do X is to address a demand to A, not to a hypothetical construct.

(5) So under indirect normativity, either (a) actual agents are never directly addressed by moral demands, or (b) we must reinterpret "A ought to do X" as meaning "A's ideal counterpart endorses X," which eliminates the prescriptive force of morality for actual people.

(6) Moreover, for intergenerational policies—say, carbon mitigation—the ideal deliberator knows exactly which future individuals will exist under each policy path, but the actual policymaker does not. So the ideal counterpart endorses norms for specific future people, while the actual agent cannot identify those people.

(7) This means actual agents cannot even know which norms apply to them in these domains, since they cannot determine which ideal-endorsement conditions are met.

(8) Therefore indirect normativity either collapses into moral skepticism for actual agents, or it demands omniscience as a precondition for moral knowledge.

(9) Both results are unacceptable.

I want to preserve (1) and (6), because the idealization conditions seem essential to avoiding parochialism, and the intergenerational case—where demographic identity depends on the very policies being evaluated—seems like a genuine stress test rather than a contrivance. But I'm willing to abandon (4) if that's where the damage is done.

Which step should I reject? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what does giving it up require—say, adopting a direct-normativity view, or reconceiving moral address? Also, what does your diagnosis predict for a case I haven't listed: suppose an actual agent, through no fault of her own, has a cognitive bias that her ideal counterpart lacks, and she sincerely endorses a harmful norm—does indirect normativity say she is not bound by the correct norm, or that she is bound but unaware?
