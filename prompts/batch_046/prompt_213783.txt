I have come to accept a reconstructed version of Simone Weil's argument for the abolition of political parties: not merely that parties tend to corrupt individual judgement, but that the party form is structurally incompatible with justice because it substitutes collective passion and loyalty for the individual moral attention required to perceive truth, and that representative democracy therefore cannot be repaired by better parties, since any such institution manufactures the false consensus it claims to represent. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: empirical cases where parties have served as vehicles for moral clarity rather than obscurity; conceptual arguments that individual moral attention is itself socially constituted and cannot be purified from collective structures; and internal tensions, such as whether Weil's own involvement in resistance networks and factory labour undermines her thesis, or whether her distinction between legitimate association and illegitimate party can be sustained without arbitrary line-drawing. Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the concept of 'party' or 'attention'? Please also assess whether the view risks being insulated by definitional qualifications into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a problem for a normative thesis of this kind. Write the response in British English.
