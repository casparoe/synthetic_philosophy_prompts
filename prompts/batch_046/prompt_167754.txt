I have been working through the standard philosophy-of-history literature on explanation, and I find the familiar opposition between Hempel-style covering-law accounts and narrative or configurational accounts somewhat exhausted—especially in seminar discussions where everyone quickly defaults to citing the same canonical positions. For a one-semester dissertation prospectus, I want to frame a more focused question that still lives inside that debate but pushes into territory that is not usually mapped out in introductory surveys. Specifically, I am interested in what I will call sequential contingency in historical explanations of institutional collapse: the idea that a narrative’s explanatory force derives from the irreversibility of a particular ordering of events—say, the staged breakdown of a centralized resource-allocation mechanism—rather than from subsumption under general laws, yet still supports robust counterfactual reasoning about whether altering an earlier stage would have prevented the later outcome.

Please write a short research proposal, suitable for a graduate workshop pitch of roughly eight hundred to one thousand words, that turns this idea into a tractable project. The proposal should state the central question with precision—namely, whether sequential contingency can be reconstructed within a modified covering-law framework that accommodates ordered concatenations of conditions, or whether it requires a distinct explanatory ontology that narrative explanation alone can supply. You should explain why existing treatments of the covering-law versus narrative distinction leave this specific problem open, particularly regarding path-dependent institutional failure. Then describe your proposed approach: a combination of conceptual analysis of counterfactual dependency in ordered event-structures and logical reconstruction of how narrative historians implicitly stabilize or destabilize particular sequences. Specify clearly what a successful result would look like—perhaps a demonstration that sequencing adds irreducible explanatory content beyond what concatenated law-like regularities can capture—and what failure would look like, such as showing that all apparent sequential dependencies reduce to independent causal regularities linked only by temporal ordering. Finally, include an honest assessment of scope and risk, noting in particular that engaging with actual historiographical cases of institutional breakdown could drag the project into empirical archive work that exceeds a single semester’s capacity, and propose how to limit the case material to keep the work conceptual.

Please do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading list; I will supply references myself. You may refer in general terms to positions associated with Hempel, Danto, or later philosophers of history, but do not invent specific archival sources or quote historians verbatim—paraphrase any referenced arguments without quotation marks. Keep the framing conceptual so that the proposal can stand on its own logical structure without requiring access to specialized empirical literature.
