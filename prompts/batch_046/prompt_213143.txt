I want to stress-test an argument a friend built that applies Good's theorem to generate an epistemic paralysis regress. He writes it out as follows.

(1) Good's theorem: for any decision problem, if an agent can observe a variable X at zero direct cost and then choose an action contingent on X, her ex-ante expected utility is weakly greater than if she commits to a fixed action now.

(2) Take any proposed action A, undertaken only if belief B holds. There always exists some proposition E such that if E were true, B is false and A should not be done.

(3) By (1), observing E before acting is weakly beneficial; since observation is free, I ought to do it.

(4) Any observation of E relies on background beliefs C—about calibration, reliability, consistency. For each C there is a defeater D that, if true, undermines C.

(5) Applying (1)-(3) recursively, I must check D before trusting the E-observation, then check D's defeaters, ad infinitum.

(6) So a rational agent never reaches a belief state stable enough to justify action—yet rational agents clearly act.

(7) Therefore either Good's theorem is false, or practical reasoning does not depend on beliefs as (2) assumes.

I want to keep (1) and (6). Where does the chain actually slip—at (2), (3), (4), or (5)? Is the error that (3) ignores computation and delay costs, breaking the "free" condition? Or that (4) overstates universal defeasibility, collapsing once priors are bounded? Or is there a hidden modal or scope shift between (1) and (3) about what "contingent on X" allows? Diagnose the faulty step, explain why it initially looks sound, and map what the standard Bayesian, bounded-rationality, and functional-decision-theoretic / updateless responses each must pay to reject it.
