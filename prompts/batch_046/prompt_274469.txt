In my philosophy of religion course we have been working through Hume’s discussion of miracles in his 1748 Enquiry, and I am struggling with a specific move in his reasoning. Hume treats a miracle as a transgression of a law of nature fixed by firm and uniform experience, and he argues that a wise person proportions belief strictly to the evidence, weighing the direct testimony for the event against the indirect proof supplied by the entire course of nature. He concludes that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would itself be more miraculous than the fact it attempts to establish, and he urges us to reject reports that conflict with our steady experience of regularity.

What I find particularly difficult is whether Hume is treating the reliability of testimony as an empirical generalization that could, in principle, be overturned by a sufficient mass of independent reports—say, distributed across disparate cultures and digital archives—or whether he builds in an a priori exclusion of any such overturning.
