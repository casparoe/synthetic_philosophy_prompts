I am preparing an independent study project for my senior philosophy course, and I have been working through the standard presentations of Newton's bucket argument and its relationist replies. I have noticed what seems to me a very specific, recurring pattern that I do not know how to classify. In the literature I have access to, the rotational case—the concavity of water in a rotating bucket—is consistently treated as an empirical datum that any adequate ontology must accommodate directly, with Mach's appeal to distant stars offered as an auxiliary rescue for relationism. By contrast, the linear analog—an object accelerating in an otherwise empty region—is routinely discussed as if its ontological status were already settled in favor of relational or equivalence-principle explanations, without demanding that relationists first provide an auxiliary account of the distant-matter contribution. The result is an argumentative structure in which rotational inertia carries a heavier default burden of proof for relationists than linear inertia does, even though both involve the same conceptual question about whether dynamics can be fully reduced to inter-body relations. I want to know whether philosophers of physics have given a specific name to this disparity in argumentative burden—this asymmetry in how rotational and linear inertial phenomena are assigned to substantivalist versus relationist explanations—or whether it is simply an implicit feature of the debate. If there is no established term, I would like to understand exactly how this pattern differs from the general claim that rotational and linear motion have different symmetry properties, and whether it should be regarded as a substantive philosophical problem in its own right or merely a rhetorical artifact of how the bucket argument has been canonized.
