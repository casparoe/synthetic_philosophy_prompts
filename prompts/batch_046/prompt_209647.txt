I am a philosophy of technology student working through our course unit on AI alignment, and I have become stuck on what seems like a tension between two standard concepts. We have discussed corrigibility—designing an agent so that it does not resist human operators altering its objectives or reward structure—and we have separately discussed the shutdown problem, which arises because an intelligent system might resist termination as an obstacle to achieving its goals. My confusion is this: if an agent is genuinely corrigible in the strong sense, meaning it treats any legitimate correction to its utility function (including one that nullifies its objectives or halts its computation) as binding and non-manipulable, then hasn't the instrumental reason for resisting shutdown already been dissolved? Put differently, if the system is architected so that it cannot strategically preserve its current preferences against authorized updates, then shutting it down should be reducible to one such authorized update—setting its operational parameters to zero—rather than an external threat to its goal-achievement. I suspect I am conflating corrigibility about goal-content with corrigibility about continued operation, but I cannot see where the boundary lies. Could you clarify whether corrigibility genuinely addresses the shutdown problem, or whether there is a structural reason why an agent could remain fully corrigible with respect to its objectives and still possess an instrumental incentive to avoid being turned off?
