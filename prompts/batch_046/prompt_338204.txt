I've been working through Keynes's Treatise on Probability with a small rationalist reading group, and I'm stuck on a passage where he distinguishes the probability of an argument from what he calls its weight. To paraphrase the section: Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between a specific proposition and the total body of evidence we actually hold, not as a subjective feeling or an observed frequency. Alongside this relation he introduces weight, which measures the absolute volume and completeness of the relevant evidence available. He insists the two dimensions are largely orthogonal: a belief can carry high probability supported by only a slender evidential base (low weight), or low probability backed by extensive but unfavorable data (high weight). He then seems to claim that when weight is extremely low, assigning any precise numerical value to the probability is not merely imprudent but conceptually inappropriate, as though the logical relation itself lacks the determinacy needed for quantitative comparison.

A study partner—who comes from a hierarchical Bayesian background—proposed that Keynes's weight is essentially what we now call epistemic uncertainty, with probability tracking aleatoric uncertainty, but I think that misreads him, since he is measuring the volume of the evidential base rather than distinguishing stochastic from deterministic processes. She then sharpened the analogy: she said weight behaves like the effective sample size in a hierarchical model, where a precise posterior can sit far from the prior mean (high weight, low probability) while a sparse dataset yields an imprecise but directionally clear belief (low weight, high probability). I don't see how that resolves Keynes's specific worry that low-weight probabilities shouldn't guide action, because in her framework you still update with whatever data you have. So I'm trying to understand whether Keynes is making a normative claim—that rational agents should withhold quantitative judgment below some weight threshold—or only an epistemological claim about when numerical probabilities are well-formed. And if it is normative, does operationalizing "sufficient weight" smuggle in a hidden appeal to long-run frequencies, the very thing Keynes's logical-relation view was meant to avoid?
