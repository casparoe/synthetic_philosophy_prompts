You serve as the senior review officer for a national public-history board that evaluates proposals for permanent museum installations and mandatory school-curriculum modules on contested national events. The board meets monthly, must sign off with one historian, one civic educator, and one ethics officer, and needs a reusable procedure that can be applied in a single session without requiring members to have read the underlying philosophy. Your board has adopted a working framework drawn from Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation on history's service to life: history is valuable only insofar as it operates in one of three metabolic modes—monumental, inspiring action through exemplary greatness; antiquarian, preserving continuity through faithful preservation; and critical, liberating the present by breaking with the past—while each mode becomes toxic when overdosed, producing respectively heroic inflation without agency, archival paralysis without change, and destructive negation without reconstructive replacement. To operationalize this, the board treats every proposal as competing for collective attention across three measurable axes: agonic charge (the monumental demand), archival fidelity (the antiquarian demand), and negation half-life (the critical demand, defined as the explicit timeline within which destructive framing must yield a constructive alternative). A proposal is life-serving only if no single axis exceeds its compensatory threshold without proportional support from the others.

Write a formal decision procedure your team can run in sequence: the ordered questions to ask of any proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point at which the procedure issues an automatic rejection, the points where the question is one of values rather than facts and must be handed to a vote rather than resolved by the checklist, and the safeguards against the three pathologies. The procedure must be usable by someone without philosophical training, must flag hard cases—such as a proposal with high agonic charge but no critical negation, or one with total archival fidelity but zero reconstructive timeline—and must state honestly what it cannot do, including whose life is being served when groups disagree.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals. First, a permanent monument-style exhibition that glorifies a revolutionary leader in order to inspire youth civic activism, containing no critical framing and proposing no mechanism for constructive forgetting; your board's civic educator strongly favors approval for its motivational value, but the procedure should yield the answer it yields regardless. Second, a comprehensive digitization and public display of every administrative document from a colonial period, presented with full archival fidelity and no interpretive framing, intended to make the record transparent but risking antiquarian paralysis; here the ethics officer argues for approval on transparency grounds alone. Include one case where the procedure produces an answer the asker—acting as board chair—will find unwelcome, and explain what the procedure reveals that intuitive judgment misses.

Keep the procedure conceptually faithful to the triad and its pathologies, but do not reproduce Nietzsche's text verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure secondary literature the board could not be expected to know.
