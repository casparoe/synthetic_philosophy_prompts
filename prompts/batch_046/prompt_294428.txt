Design a classroom activity for a philosophy of language seminar of about 14 postgraduate students, with 80 minutes available, that makes participants feel the force of Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation — specifically the “gavagai” case, but pushed into the underdetermination of logical quantifiers and temporal-identity conditions rather than left at the familiar rabbit example. The session must not open with a lecture; the rules, card deck, and scoring system should be arranged so that the groups construct rival translation manuals from identical behavioural evidence, discover that every manual predicts the same observed responses, and only then learn that this deadlock has a name.

Divide the group into four teams of three or four. Each team is an independent “radical translation committee” decoding a constructed alien protocol using only 24 “field observation cards” that record assent, dissent, and pointing behaviours in the presence of stimuli, across temporal sequences, with no meta-linguistic commentary or direct ostension permitted. Give the teams 25 minutes to write a formal translation manual for three target expressions: a nominal behaving like “gavagai”, an assent-marker, and a logical quantifier. Introduce a “funding-point” payoff: teams earn points for predictive success on four hidden “test scenarios” revealed at minute 30, with the highest-scoring manual receiving a symbolic “grant” for further research. Design the card deck and hidden scenarios so that empirical equivalence is unavoidable: for instance, one manual may translate the quantifier as universal, treat the nominal as a kind-term with persistence conditions, and interpret assent as agreement with a proposition; another may translate the quantifier as existential over a restricted domain, treat the nominal as a temporal stage, and interpret assent as willingness to continue interaction — yet both score full marks. At minute 35, reveal the tie, ask the teams to vote on which manual is correct, and record the deadlock. Only afterwards name Quine’s thesis and map the exercise onto it.
