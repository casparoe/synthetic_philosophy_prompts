I have been trying to articulate my own ethical outlook since I became the primary caregiver for my grandmother through her dementia, and I have tentatively called the view "situated interdependence," though I am unsure whether that label survives scrutiny. Before I present these ideas to a reading group I have joined, I want to know whether the pieces actually fit together.

Here is where I have landed: first, I hold that moral obligations arise within concrete caring relationships rather than being derived from universal rules that apply impersonally, which means that my partiality toward my grandmother is not a moral failure but the very core of ethical life; second, I take vulnerability and dependency not as deficiencies to overcome but as the fundamental human condition, so that autonomy ought to be replaced by what I call "recognized interdependence" as our central moral value; third, I nevertheless believe we bear serious obligations toward strangers we will never meet—specifically, future generations threatened by climate collapse—because they are vulnerable to our present actions even though they do not yet exist to enter any reciprocal caring bond with us; fourth, I think a just society should make care labor visible and distribute it equitably, yet people should remain free to choose whether to enter caring roles, since coerced care would destroy the relational quality that makes it morally significant; fifth, I maintain that if someone genuinely rejects all dependency and all caring connections—say, through total and permanent isolation—then they are not acting immorally but are simply living outside the domain in which moral obligations apply, much as an inanimate object lies outside it. Do these five commitments form a coherent package? I am particularly worried that my third claim violates the first by extending care beyond concrete relationships, that the fourth smuggles autonomy back in through the language of choice despite my rejection of it in the second, and that the fifth conflicts with the universality of vulnerability asserted in the second. Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, distinguish real conflicts from ones that only appear problematic, and identify the single least costly revision that would preserve the core conviction that care is prior to justice.
