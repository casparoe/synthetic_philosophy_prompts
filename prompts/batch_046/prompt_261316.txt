As a novelist drafting a narrative in which an entomologist is forced to confront the ethics of agricultural pesticides, I have been observing a debate in animal ethics and effective altruism that has circled the same ground for well over a decade without resolution, and I should like a philosophical diagnosis rather than a partisan verdict. The dispute concerns insect sentience and the so-called scale argument: proponents observe that, with roughly ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, even a one-in-a-thousand probability that an individual possesses subjective experience yields an aggregate of suffering or wellbeing that dwarfs that of all farmed vertebrates combined; sceptics reply that the architectural distance between insect nervous systems and mammalian consciousness is so vast that the probability of phenomenal experience is vanishingly small, that our epistemic position does not justify treating every invertebrate as a moral patient, and that adopting a precautionary stance collapses practical moral reasoning into paralysis. Both sides appear to accept the same biological facts about nociception, the same probabilistic reasoning, and the same normative premise that suffering matters, yet neither has persuaded the other, and the literature now merely repeats the same moves—expected-value calculus versus burden of proof, precautionary principle versus epistemic humility—without discernible progress.

My working theory is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical but concerns the standards for attributing moral patienthood under radical uncertainty, and that beneath this lies a hidden divergence about whether moral status is binary and individual or graded and distributed. Specifically, I wonder whether the interventionists implicitly treat sentience as a property that, once present however faintly, generates full-scale moral consideration, while the sceptics treat it as a threshold concept requiring a specific kind of integrated phenomenal binding—something like global workspace dynamics—that insects almost certainly lack. If so, the sides may be answering different questions: one asks what we ought to do given uncertain harm, the other asks what we are justified in believing about harm. To push this further, I should also like you to consider a conceptual innovation: if we adopted a framework in which moral considerability tracks not binary sentience but "fragmentary phenomenal fields"—discrete, non-integrated affective states that do not constitute a unified subject—would that dissolve the scale argument, or merely relocate it?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of both sides answering different questions under one name? Is there a concealed empirical disagreement—perhaps about the evolutionary integration of nociceptive pathways in hymenoptera—that is actually doing the work, or is it a divergence in what counts as a moral reason? Given your diagnosis, is there any distinction, formal result, neurobiological finding, or reconceptualisation—such as treating moral patienthood as graded and distributed rather than binary and individual—that could genuinely move the debate, or is the stalemate a sign that the question is ill-posed?
