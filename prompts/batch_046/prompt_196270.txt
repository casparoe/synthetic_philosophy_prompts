At a recent graduate seminar on idealisation and collective agency, two of my colleagues — Dr. Elena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen — became locked in a disagreement about the normative structure of coherent extrapolated volition and its relation to idealised preference theories. I should like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, indicating where each party is correct, where each errs, and what both are overlooking.

Voss opened by proposing that CEV is best understood as a collective analogue of individual idealised preference satisfaction: we take the actual volitional structure of humanity, correct for informational deficits and logical inconsistencies, and extrapolate to a coherent endpoint. She argued that this process automatically addresses the problem of adaptive preferences — preferences formed under conditions of deprivation or coercion — because full information and coherence would lead agents to repudiate the conditions that shaped those preferences.

Chen replied that coherence is a formal, not a normative, filter. He proposed a thought experiment: imagine a large, stable community whose members have all developed mutually reinforcing adaptive preferences for a rigid social hierarchy. Each preference is consistent with the others, the community’s volition is highly coherent, and full information about the origins of these preferences is available to every member, yet none are repudiated because the preference-formation mechanisms themselves have been systematically distorted. If CEV is applied to this community, Chen claimed, it would extrapolate a stable, coherent collective volition in favour of the hierarchy. This, he said, shows that coherence alone cannot distinguish a corrupted volition from an acceptable one.

Voss countered that Chen had mischaracterised the extrapolation condition. CEV does not merely apply logical consistency; it includes reflection upon what one wants when one knows more and thinks more clearly. This reflective component, she insisted, functions analogously to the cognitive psychotherapy model found in rational-preference theories — it does not impose an external standard but allows the agent’s own deeper commitments to surface.

Chen conceded that reflection is part of the picture, but pressed a further objection: if reflection is permitted to correct or reject initial preferences, then CEV is no longer extrapolating volition but substituting a philosopher’s conception of rationality for the actual preferences of the agents. He asked whether, if the fully extrapolated volition of humanity turned out to coherently endorse a caste system, we should accept that result. If we reject it, he said, we are admitting that coherence is not the governing criterion; if we accept it, then CEV is normatively empty and cannot do the work its proponents intend.

Voss responded that this presents a false dichotomy. Even within idealised preference frameworks, full rationality and information do not guarantee unanimity; different agents may coherently endorse different values. The point of CEV is not to guarantee a particular liberal outcome but to discover what we would want if we were more like the people we wish we were. That the result might be disturbing is not an objection to the method but a feature of taking human volition seriously.

Chen concluded by noting that, without an independent normative criterion to determine which reflective revisions count as legitimate, CEV risks either collapsing into arbitrary preference aggregation or smuggling in substantive moral commitments under the guise of formal coherence. He suggested that this ambiguity is rarely acknowledged in discussions of collective extrapolation, and he added that the debate may also conflate diachronic coherence — stability of preference through sustained reflection — with synchronic coherence — consistency among preferences at a single time — in ways that obscure whether the corruption lies in the preferences themselves or in their temporal trajectory.

Please adjudicate this exchange. For each of the following moves — the adaptive-preference correction claim, the coherence-as-formal-filter objection, the false-dichotomy reply, and the charge of smuggled normative commitments — say which party is more nearly right, and explain why. Then identify what, if anything, both are missing: in particular, whether the distinction between diachronic and synchronic coherence resolves any part of their dispute, and whether they have adequately addressed the difference between individual and collective extrapolation. Write your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
