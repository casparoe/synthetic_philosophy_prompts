Consider the institutional practice in professional historiography — shaped since Herodotus’s separation of direct observation from reported testimony and refined through Thucydidean source criticism — by which contradictory primary accounts of the same distant event are not eliminated in favor of the single most probable narrative, but are preserved within a synthetic framework that explains the conditions under which each account could have been produced. The same discipline simultaneously penalizes presentism, the application of contemporary moral or conceptual categories to past actors, yet requires scholars to reconstruct past horizons of expectation precisely in order to identify distortion and correct for source bias. What theory of historical knowledge does this paired norm presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it commit to a particular view about the accessibility of past mental states, about the evidentiary status of contradiction, or about the separation between explanatory and evaluative judgment? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether it is philosophically underdetermined so that rival epistemologies could license the same norms, and describe what would have to change in archival training and peer evaluation if the discipline replaced this framework with a straightforwardly probabilistic aggregation of testimonies that weighted each source by estimated error rate and merged them into one best-estimate narrative, discarding structural contradictions once the weights settled.
