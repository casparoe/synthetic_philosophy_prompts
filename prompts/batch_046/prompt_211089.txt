Design a 90-minute structured deliberation for an upper-level metaphysics seminar of 14 students that makes the statue-and-clay problem unavoidable before anyone names it. Inject this twist: the clay is not inert but a cultivated mycelium block with its own biological regeneration cycle, so its persistence conditions are genuinely distinct from the artistic form carved into it. Divide participants into three committees—Conservation, Insurance, and Legal Title—each assigned one criterion: spatiotemporal continuity, functional or artistic continuity, or biological-substrate continuity. Stage four sequential changes to the object: a small chip is replaced with fresh mycelium; half the mass is reshaped into a different form; the entire substrate is replaced cell-by-cell over months while the shape is held constant; and finally the shape is accidentally crushed while the mycelium remains intact. At each stage every committee must vote on whether the original object survives, whether ownership persists, and whether an insurance claim is valid, using only their assigned criterion. Use a cumulative point system with real but small stakes—e.g., the committee with the most consistent votes across stages chooses the seminar’s closing discussion question—so that contradictions between committees become personally felt rather than abstract. Provide explicit roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, rules preventing committees from adopting hybrid criteria, and a debrief that maps each vote onto the coincidence/non-identity distinction in material constitution. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—such as participants smuggling artistic identity into biological continuity judgments—and be explicit about which feature of the classic argument, particularly the modal claim about squashing the statue but not the clay, the staged biological replacement cannot reproduce and must be supplied in the facilitator’s closing remarks.
