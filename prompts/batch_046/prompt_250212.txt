I have spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics and, since retiring, have been working through philosophy texts in a small salon I host for former colleagues—mostly experimentalists and one applied mathematician, none with formal philosophical training. Next month I want to lead a session on Nietzsche’s eternal recurrence, but I am determined not to present it as the familiar cosmological proof involving finite energy states and infinite time, which my audience will immediately dismiss as outdated thermodynamics. Instead, I want to teach what I have been calling an “indexical recurrence” framing: the claim that if time is infinite and the possible configurations of matter are finite, then this exact present—including the particular quality of one’s awareness right now—must occur again, not as a loop one travels through, but as an independent, indistinguishable occurrence. The ethical weight, on this reading, comes not from cosmic determinism but from asking whether one can affirm the precise details of one’s life as worthy of being instantiated repeatedly without variation.

Plan the explanatory sequence for a ninety-minute session with this audience. Specify the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—why the metaphysical premise must be separated from the ethical injunction, and where the distinction between cyclical time and recurring indexical states belongs—together with the one concrete carrying example that should anchor the discussion. I intend to use a scenario drawn from laboratory practice: a meticulously kept notebook describing a failed calibration that produced a particular, unremarkable error pattern, and asking whether the experimenter could genuinely will that exact page, with its mundane disappointment, to be rewritten identically across an infinite timeline. State what this example must include and what it must deliberately exclude—particularly elements that would let the audience confuse the thought experiment with predictive physics or with personal immortality. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they are likely to arise: the impulse to invoke Poincaré recurrence, the conflation with deterministic chaos or entropy reversal, the assumption that recurrence implies personal survival or memory continuity, and the objection that quantum indeterminacy blocks exact repetition. For each, indicate exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off, not after it has derailed the discussion. Include a check—one precise question I can pose at the end—to determine whether they have grasped the idea as an ethical calibration rather than as a cosmological claim. Also state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level and say so openly.
