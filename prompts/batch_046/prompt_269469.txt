I have recently been assisting in the design of evaluation benchmarks for multi-agent language-model systems, and I have encountered a scoring convention that puzzles me: when two agents face structurally identical payoff matrices in a one-shot interaction and their reasoning traces are made fully transparent to one another, the benchmark awards full cooperation points only if both choose the cooperative strategy—treating this outcome as the uniquely rational one, with no credit for defection even when defection dominates in standard game-theoretic terms. What conception of practical rationality does that scoring rule presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on a Hofstadter-style superrationality in which logical recognition of symmetry between agents collapses the strategic distinction between self and other? Or can an instrumentalist, agent-neutral framework license the same practice? Distinguish what the benchmark strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what would have to change in the evaluation protocol if we replaced its implicit view with a straightforwardly payoff-maximizing, identity-independent theory of rational choice.
