So I was arguing with my friends last night about whether a group can literally have a mind, not just metaphorically. We started with that classic Hofstadter ant-colony thing—Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue—where the colony acts like one creature. I said yeah, I think Aunt Hillary has a mind, but it's a really dim, ache-y kind of mind, nothing like a human's. But then we tried to apply the same idea elsewhere and everything got messier.

Here are my verdicts, and I'm not sure they fit together. A slime mold solving a maze? Not a mind, just clever chemistry—no matter how smart it looks. A corporation with executives and AI tools? Also no, even though it makes decisions; the parts are too seperate and symbol-driven. But a drone swarm running one shared neural net, where no single drone controls anything thier own? That feels like a real mind to me, maybe even sharper than Aunt Hillary. Now here's the weird one I made up: imagine thousands of people in a "Weave," linked by neural interfaces that only share emotional valence—no shared thoughts, no leader—so the group moves together based on collective mood but no one knows the plan. I want to say the Weave is NOT a mind, but honestley it's structurally closer to the drone swarm than Aunt Hillary is, and I can't defend the difference. So either I'm wrong about the drones, wrong about the Weave, or there's some hidden principle I'm missing.

Can you lay out a precise principle for when a collective counts as having a mind? I want it to cover Aunt Hillary, the slime mold, the corporation, the drone net, and the Weave. For each clause, tell me which case motivates it and what it says about ones I didn't mention. And be honest—which of my five verdicts does your principle force me to drop? If there's more than one consistent principle, give me the simpler one and the one that saves more of my intuitions, even if it's messier.
