You chair the Governance and Ethics Panel of the Transboundary Civic Data Cooperative, a consortium of municipal archives, community land trusts, and environmental-monitoring groups operating across jurisdictions with incompatible transparency regimes. The Panel must issue a binding recommendation on Proposal 7-B, the Graduated Visibility Covenant for member-submitted records.

Under the proposal, all citizen-contributed data—ranging from water-quality readings to informal housing-occupancy maps—would be sorted into three tiers. Tier I applies American administrative-transparency norms: fully searchable by any state agency or private party, with contributor attribution, to prevent corruption and enable democratic oversight. Tier II applies German informational-self-determination logic: citizens retain granular control over which state actors may view their contributions, with a default presumption of opacity and a right to contextual deletion. Tier III adopts a modified social-credit mechanism: access to aggregated datasets is gated not by identity but by a reputation score tied to prior verification accuracy, allowing high-scoring contributors broader visibility privileges while restricting direct state surveillance of anonymous or low-scoring participants.

The concrete case is the cooperative's pilot archive in the Lower Rhine Basin. German municipal partners have refused to submit groundwater-extraction logs unless Tier II protections are guaranteed; American partner NGOs threaten withdrawal if Tier I openness is not the baseline for all environmental data; and one affiliated research partner has offered funding contingent on implementing Tier III scoring to harmonize data quality with social accountability.

Your memo must recommend whether the Panel should approve Proposal 7-B as written, reject it in favor of a single-tier default, or approve it with binding conditions—specifying which tier should govern which data category and why. The recommendation must be actionable: the cooperative's board can vote yes or no on your conditions. You must treat the strongest argument from the losing framework with full honesty—if you reject the social-credit tier, explain precisely what problem of free-riding or data corruption it was designed to solve and why your alternative addresses that problem without adopting its visibility logic. Likewise, if you limit American openness, acknowledge what democratic-accountability goods are sacrificed and how they are preserved elsewhere in the covenant. Do not cite specific legal statutes by section number; ground your reasoning in the philosophical rationales of informational autonomy, democratic visibility, and collective reputation management.
