I serve as the senior conceptual-standards referee for a national synthetic-biology regulatory board that reviews patent and biosafety applications involving engineered genetic sequences. Roughly twice a month I must adjudicate disputes over whether a given synthetic DNA construct counts as an invented informational code—meriting patent protection and stricter containment—or as a chemically modified natural substrate governed by different rules. The board has no in-house philosopher of biology, each decision must be rendered within forty-eight hours, and my sign-off is required before any case proceeds to legal review. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run on every proposal: an ordered sequence of conceptual tests, with early clear answers that stop the process, explicit markers for where judgment is unavoidable, and designated stop-points where the procedure hands the decision back to the board because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

The theoretical tension at the heart of each case is whether DNA functions as a literal semantic code—carrying arbitrary, syntactically structured meaning interpreted by cellular machinery analogous to a reader—or whether “code” is only a causal-chemical metaphor describing regular biochemical correlations without genuine syntax, semantics, or an interpreting agent with independent functional goals. I want the procedure grounded in the distinctions philosophy of biology and philosophy of language actually use to separate literal codes from causal correlations—such as arbitrary mapping, combinatorial syntax, error-correction independence from chemical affinity, and the presence of an interpreter with its own teleonomic ends—without asking me to cite named theories, produce bibliographies, or invoke scholars I cannot verify without access to literature. The procedure should be honest about which distinctions survive practical simplification and which collapse, and it should explicitly state what it cannot resolve, particularly whether being classified as a “code” should ever determine patent eligibility or biosafety level.

Please structure your response exactly as follows. First, The Procedure (approximately 400 words): present the step-by-step flowchart in words, including the initial screening questions, the three conceptual tests applied in fixed order, the conditions yielding a definite “code” or “not-code” verdict, the conditions that force an immediate stop for value judgment, and a brief note on what to do when data are missing and a judgment call must be masked as procedure. Second, Hard Cases and Judgment Gaps (approximately 250 words): discuss the scenarios where the tests conflict—such as sequences using expanded alphabets beyond A-T-G-C to store non-biological digital information, or sequences that hijack endogenous ribosomal machinery for purposes unrelated to organismal metabolism—identify precisely where the procedure misfires, and flag the residual judgment call that remains uneliminated. Third, Worked Applications (approximately 350 words): apply the full procedure to two proposals—first, a bacterial genome rewritten with a compressed codon table designed solely to accelerate industrial enzyme synthesis; second, a mammalian cell line engineered with a synthetic six-base-pair alphabet to archive a literary text in its genome, readable only by an externally supplied synthetic polymerase. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer the board is likely to dislike, and conclude by stating clearly what the procedure is powerless to do, especially regarding normative questions of containment and intellectual property.
