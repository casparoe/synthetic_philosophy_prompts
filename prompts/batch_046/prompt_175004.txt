I have no formal background in philosophy, but I work as a software engineer designing a compliance-verification engine. The system holds a fixed, finite set of explicit regulatory rules and applies them only within a bounded inference depth—currently three deductive steps—to maintain sub-100ms latency per transaction. A senior auditor recently argued that we should be held liable whenever the engine fails to flag a violation that requires four or more steps to derive from those premises, on the grounds that knowing the rules means knowing everything they entail. From an engineering perspective, this seems to demand computing the full logical closure of a knowledge base in real time, which is intractable at scale. But I am trying to understand whether there is a deeper philosophical principle at stake: is there a meaningful distinction between an agent that genuinely lacks a belief and one that simply hasn't executed the derivation, especially when the agent was deliberately designed with restricted reasoning capacity? Is the auditor conflating a practical computational limit with a normative failure of rationality? If you were advising me on how to frame this disagreement—without appealing to policy manuals—how should I think about responsibility and knowledge for an artificial system whose epistemic reach is intentionally limited?
