Consider the following pattern of judgments about sexual ethics, which I take to be broadly in the spirit of Bentham's liberal writings on sexuality but which I cannot quite reduce to one rule. First, private consensual acts between informed adults that produce net pleasure and involve no broken commitments are permissible, and state interference is unjustified. Second, acts that are physically harmless but depend on material deception—say, concealing fertility status or the presence of hidden observers—are impermissible, even if all parties later report high satisfaction. Third, public acts that cause no direct injury but provoke widespread distress should generally be tolerated, because I judge that much of the distress is irrational aversion rather than genuine harm, and suppression usually produces more misery than it prevents. Fourth, acts occurring within radically coercive institutions, such as prison contexts with extreme power asymmetry, are impermissible regardless of apparent consent. Fifth, practices involving minor, fully disclosed physical risks that yield intense and lasting pleasure are permissible if genuinely consented to with full knowledge. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a principle of utility constrained by conditions on valid consent and a filter for irrational social aversion—that unifies all five? Or must I accept that the deception and institutional-coercion verdicts import non-utilitarian commitments to autonomy and equality that sit in tension with the calculus? Please formulate the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it saves and which it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it predicts for a case not listed here: a long-term consensual arrangement in which both parties deliberately maintain false beliefs about each other's identities, with no material risk involved, sustained until after both have died, and never discovered by them.
