I'm helping curate a small community heritage exhibition about defunct local industries, and we've encountered a problem that has started to feel less like logistics and more like a philosophical puzzle. In the basement of a demolished warehouse, we found twelve sealed cylindrical containers made of the same aluminum alloy, with identical dimensions and no external markings except for a faded batch number. Because the seals are corrosion-bonded, opening them would destroy the containers, and any internal documentation has long since deteriorated. Local archives mention both a pharmaceutical packaging plant and an independent film-processing cooperative operating in that warehouse during overlapping decades, and both industries are known to have used containers of precisely this specification.

My roommate, who is studying museum conservation, pointed out that our board is now divided over how to classify and preserve these objects. One faction wants to catalog them as medical-supply artifacts, which would place them in the health-history wing, require low-humidity climate controls, and frame them as evidence of the town's pharmaceutical labor. Another faction wants to treat them as film-cooperative equipment, assigning them to the arts wing with different preservation protocols and interpreting them as part of a regional cinema movement. A retired engineer who volunteered to advise us has proposed a third view: that these were standardized industrial shipping vessels used interchangeably by both sectors, so that labeling them "medical" or "film" containers imposes an ontological distinction that doesn't actually exist in the objects themselves—the categories are just functional labels that depend on context we no longer have.

What makes this feel strange is that the decision seems to require me to determine what kind of things these containers fundamentally are, yet the information that would settle that question—what's inside, which industry commissioned them, how they were actually used—is permanently inaccessible. I don't want generic curatorial advice about compromise or audience engagement. Rather, I'm asking whether there is a rational basis for committing to one classification over another when all the distinguishing evidence is lost, or whether any such commitment is necessarily a value-laden choice disguised as a factual identification. If I must proceed, is there a principled way to decide which framework to apply, and what does it mean for an object's identity when the facts that would define it are irretrievably hidden?
