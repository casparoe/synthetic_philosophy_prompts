For a workshop of fourteen philosophy and political-science graduate students meeting for eighty minutes, design a structured simulation with explicit rules, roles, and a non-monetary payoff scheme that makes participants feel the instability of defining corruption across different institutional frames. The activity should center on a fictional but precisely defined exchange system I will call the Meridian Protocol: in this system, access to public goods is legally mediated through transparent institutional payments, but social access is simultaneously governed by mandatory courtesy transfers—non-monetary gifts that are culturally required, never explicitly linked to outcomes, yet accumulated across cycles in a socially tracked ledger that creates implicit obligations over time. Participants should be divided into two rotating roles: Local Brokers, who must maintain social legitimacy by giving and receiving courtesy transfers to secure cooperation, and Institutional Auditors, who are tasked with applying a universal transparency-based anti-corruption standard that demands visible, contract-bound transactions and treats any hidden reciprocity as potential bribery. The payoff scheme should award points for both Access (securing resources) and Legitimacy (being judged uncorrupt by the auditors), but the rules should be arranged so that Brokers can only maintain high Access scores by accumulating courtesy obligations that are invisible to the audit framework, while Auditors maximize their Legitimacy scores by flagging hidden reciprocity—even when no explicit quid pro quo has occurred. The sequence should run in three rounds with escalating resource scarcity, so that by the third round Brokers are locked into dense networks of obligation they did not intentionally design, and Auditors are forced to render corruption judgments on relationships that lack any single transactional moment. The idea—the structural opacity of distributed reciprocity versus the legibility demands of Western transparency norms—must emerge from what participants do and suffer under the rules before anyone names it; do not open with a lecture.
