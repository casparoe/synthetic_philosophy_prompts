The same modern state routinely maintains three visibility regimes that pull in opposite directions: German-style data protection, which treats personal data as an aspect of dignity and demands strict minimization; American-style administrative openness, which treats government records as assets subject to broad public disclosure; and Chinese-style social-credit infrastructures, which render individual conduct continuously legible for predictive governance. What philosophical conception of citizenship and sovereignty makes this triad appear stable rather than contradictory? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether it relies on a split between a transparent political self and an opaque private self, a positional account of visibility whose value depends on who observes whom, or some other framework—say whether it is genuinely unified or an unstable compromise, and specify what legal or institutional practice would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by a uniform principle of either total citizen opacity or total transparency toward the state.
