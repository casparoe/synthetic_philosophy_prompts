I'm a high school senior in an advanced philosophy seminar, and for my capstone project I'm constructing a two-axis matrix for philosophies of punishment: one axis tracks whether determinism is true, the other tracks whether "just deserts" requires a metaphysically robust property or can be sustained as an institutional practice alone. In discussing this with my classmates, I initially mapped Daniel Dennett onto the quadrant where determinism is false but desert survives as social practice—since he famously defends moral responsibility—while I placed Gregg Caruso in the quadrant where determinism is true and desert is rejected in favor of a public-health quarantine model. My instructor suggested I had mislocated Dennett, which led me to develop what I call the "retrospective-prospective asymmetry in desert attribution": the idea that backward-looking judgments about an offender's deservingness depend on a different normative logic than forward-looking institutional designs.

If Dennett is indeed a determinist, then his defense of just deserts cannot rest on libertarian freedom, which means he must be treating desert as purely practice-internal. But if that is the case, I do not see why he resists Caruso's conclusion that we should abandon retributivism altogether, since both seem to reject a metaphysical grounding for punishment. At the same time, Caruso's quarantine model requires identifying a specific individual as dangerous based on past behavior, which appears to reintroduce a backward-looking causal attribution that mirrors the very retrospective logic he claims to eliminate. So is the real disagreement between Dennett and Caruso about the truth of determinism, about whether backward-looking institutional practices can be justified without smuggling in a false presupposition of libertarian agency, or about whether the retrospective-prospective asymmetry itself is coherent? I would like to understand how to correct my matrix without simply collapsing the two positions into the same quadrant.
