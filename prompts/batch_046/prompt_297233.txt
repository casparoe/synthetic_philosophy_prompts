I'm presenting tuesday in the phil of art seminar on the paradox of fiction, but I'm not just running through the standard framing. My actual claim is that fictional grief isn't a broken version of real grief—it's what I call a 'shadow affect,' borrowing the phenomenology but lacking the existential stake that ties emotion to action. So fiction doesn't just fail to justify our feelings; it actively decouples affect from practical commitment. I think that's a feature, not a bug, but it raises a weird spillover worry: if fiction trains us to feel intensely without acting, might it be eroding the link between affect and agency in real moral life? The room will have two people who study empathy and fiction and are convinced novels make us better people, plus a couple analytic aestheticians who think 'shadow affect' is just cognitivism wearing a hat. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would put them, what I should concede right away, which reply has to actually land for the proposal to survive, and the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend instead of praying they miss. Also include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
