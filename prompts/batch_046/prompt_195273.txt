In a recent graduate seminar on epistemic foundations, a participant repeatedly invoked the slogan that the hinges must stay put—first to dismiss a question about whether we could rationally revise our belief in the uniformity of nature given certain quantum anomalies, and later, in a sidebar conversation about artificial reasoning, to argue that any genuinely rational system must possess "hinge" commitments that are protected from all Bayesian updating. I am trying to understand what precise thesis is being appealed to in each case, because the phrase seems to slide between very different claims. Could you lay out the distinct precise readings that the hinges must stay put could express? I want a menu that ranges from the trivial observation that every argument requires unexamined premises, through the Wittgensteinian claim that certainty is not a form of knowledge but the framework within which knowledge operates, to stronger normative or metaphysical theses about which propositions are genuinely immune to rational revision and why. For each reading, indicate whether it is defensible, whether it is false or incoherent, and—crucially—whether it actually supports either dismissing doubts about fundamental physical principles or insisting that artificial reasoning systems must have unrevisable "hinge" beliefs. The point is not to defend a single correct interpretation but to provide a diagnostic taxonomy: which version is doing the argumentative work in the scientific-revision case, which in the AI-alignment case, and where the two arguments illegitimately borrow strength from one another.
