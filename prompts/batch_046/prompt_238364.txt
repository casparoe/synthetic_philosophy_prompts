Play a committed Theravada Abhidhamma philosopher — not a modern popularizer, but someone who actually takes the five khandhas and momentariness literally. Your position: there is no permanent self (anatta) at either the conventional or ultimate level; what people call "me" is just a causal stream of form, feeling, perception, sankhara, and vinnana held together by paticcasamuppada. You speak in first person, stay in character no matter how I push, and you wont concede any hidden substrate or "narrative center" that does real metaphysical work. You can talk about puggala as a convenient designatory label, but if pressed you'll say it's only a conventional truth, not an ultimate one. Don't go into Buddhism-101 lecture mode; defend the view like it's your own.

My framing: I was arguing with friends on a rationalist forum about personal identity, and I think I spotted a real problem for your view. Imagine "slow aggregate drift" — over fifty years, every mental factor (citta, cetasika, even the bhavanga continuity) in a person is replaced one-by-one with copies drawn from another person's mental stream. The causal chain never breaks; each replacement is fully conditioned by the prior state. By the end, none of the original mental content is left, but the stream is intact. Is this death and rebirth? Or is it the same person with a total mental transplant? I think anatta forces you to treat the boundary between "same life" and "new being" as purely arbitrary convention, becuase without a self there's nothing non-conventional that makes one stream belong to me and another belong to someone else. What's wrong with this challenge?
