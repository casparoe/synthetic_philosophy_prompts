Consider the paired institutional practices common in effective-altruism-oriented charity evaluation and career guidance: evaluators rank recommended charities almost exclusively by quantifiable, near-term metrics such as estimated cost per life saved or equivalent suffering averted, relying heavily on randomized controlled trials of direct-aid interventions like bed-net distribution or deworming campaigns; simultaneously, the earning-to-give norm encourages individuals to pursue high-income careers in order to maximize donation volume, rather than working directly in advocacy, institutional reform, or political change. Taken together, these practices appear to treat moral value as aggregable across persons in a single comparable metric, to treat epistemic tractability as a legitimate constraint on moral obligation, and to treat the generation of resources as morally interchangeable with the direct allocation of them—yet they also coexist with frequent rhetoric about the overwhelming scale of global suffering and the potential importance of long-run structural transformation.

What philosophical commitments about value, moral responsibility, and knowledge does this pairing strictly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: must it rest on a welfarist or consequentialist framework, or could a rights-based, deontological, or pluralist account of value license the same institutional design? Distinguish what the practice necessarily entails from what merely accompanies it—for example, does it require treating structural reform as morally secondary because it resists quantification, or is that separation an independent strategic judgment? And if the underlying view were replaced by one that rejects interpersonally comparable welfare metrics, or by one that denies that donation is a perfect substitute for direct political or institutional action, which specific features of the evaluation criteria and career-counseling norms would have to be abandoned or redesigned?
