(1) Searle says original intentionality belongs only to conscious minds; books and computers only have derived intentionality that traces back to some original mind.
(2) Dennett's two-bitser shows that if a system's internal states play the right functional roles—tracking environment, guiding behavior—we can legitimately adopt the intentional stance toward it, even without consciousness.
(3) Now imagine we wire three two-bitsers into a cascade: Bitser-1 tracks light, Bitser-2 reads Bitser-1's output and tracks temperature, and Bitser-3 integrates both to control a valve. No human is in the loop after the initial wiring.
(4) If Dennett is right, each layer has intentional states relative to its inputs, and the cascade as a whole has integrated beliefs about its environment.
(5) But then the cascade's intentionality isn't derived from any single conscious mind—it's generated recursively by the functional architecture itself.
(6) So either we accept that this valve-controller has original intentionality (which is nuts—it's just metal and silicon), or we reject Dennett's functionalist criterion altogether and go back to Searle's consciousness-only view.
(7) But if we reject (2), we lose the ability to explain why we intuitively attribute beliefs to simple animals or even to AI without smuggling in consciousness.
(8) So the argument seems to force us either into panpsychism-about-valves or into denying intentional stance to anything non-conscious.

where does this actually break? i kinda want to keep (2) and (4) if i can, because the cascade idea is cool and i think Dennett's point about functional roles is right. but maybe im conflating the intentional stance being useful with it being metaphysically real? idk. also spelled Dennett wrong somewhere probably, oops.
