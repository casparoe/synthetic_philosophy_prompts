The popular gloss of Nietzsche’s eternal recurrence is often compressed into the injunction to live every moment as though it will return, unchanged, across infinite repetitions. In discussions I have encountered, this slogan is used interchangeably as an existential litmus test for affirming one’s life (a kind of radical amor fati), as a speculative cosmological thesis about cyclical time, and as a normative principle for decision-making—yet these uses seem to pull in different directions. Can you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims this slogan might be expressing? For each reading—cosmological, existential-prescriptive, decision-theoretic, psychological-heuristic, or metaphysical—say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; clarify whose ability or attitude is at stake, at what temporal scale, and whether the claim requires literal identity of worlds or only qualitative indistinguishability. Then consider a niche variant: suppose recurrence is not literal temporal looping but the existence of indistinguishable lives across a branching multiverse or a sufficiently large modal space. Under that interpretation, does the slogan retain normative force, and can it still serve as a test for affirmation if the repetitions are not experienced by a single continuous subject? Finally, say which of these readings best captures the philosophical work Nietzsche intended the idea to perform, and whether the common one-liner collapses distinctions that actually matter.
