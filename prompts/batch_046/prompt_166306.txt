The standard framing presents Kelly betting as maximizing the geometric mean growth rate of capital, thereby avoiding ruin across repeated gambles. But I'm struck by a tension with the ergodicity critique in economic dynamics: ensemble averages over hypothetical parallel agents diverge sharply from time averages over a single agent's actual wealth trajectory. Could you explain whether Kelly's geometric-mean maximization genuinely resolves this non-ergodicity problem for an individual agent, or whether it implicitly presupposes an ergodic environment where the two averages coincide? More precisely, does geometric rationality provide an independent normative justification for risk-averse capital preservation, or does it redefine rationality in terms of a long-run growth rate that only has purchase if we already assume the agent survives to realize it? I'd like an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of how the time-ensemble distinction bears on the justification of Kelly betting, treating the distinction as philosophically substantive rather than merely mathematical.
