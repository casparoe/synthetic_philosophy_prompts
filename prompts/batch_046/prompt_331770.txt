I've been working with a specific framing of the procreation asymmetry that tries to avoid both standard harm-based person-affecting views and simple utilitarian aggregation. The position is that the asymmetry—no obligation to create a happy life, but strong reason not to create a suffering one—arises not from a metaphysical distinction between harming and failing to benefit, but from an evidential-risk structure. Specifically, any procreative decision faces an asymmetric forecasting landscape: severe suffering is easier to anticipate in outline and far harder to remedy after the fact, whereas happiness is both harder to guarantee beforehand and less urgent to secure afterward. On this view, the expected moral cost is dominated by downside tail-risk with no corresponding upside tail that could balance it, even if one accepts a total-value calculus. The asymmetry is therefore in our epistemic position and in the dynamics of remedy, not in the intrinsic weight of pleasure versus pain.

What would have to be true for this framing to collapse? Which premises—about predictability, remedy asymmetry, or the moral weighting of risk—could fail, and what would our reasoning or the world have to look like for each? Does the position make predictions that rival accounts, such as a symmetric risk-averse total view or a pure person-affecting harm view, do not make? I want an honest map of its exposure: is there a decisive defeater, like a demonstration that modal risk is always symmetric across valence regardless of remedy dynamics, or only awkward adjustments, such as finding that parents predict child well-being symmetrically? What would defenders of this evidential-risk framing themselves concede counts against them, and where have they insulated the view with qualifications that risk making it unfalsifiable? Finally, I would find it most persuasive if you could show whether the intuition that procreation is permissible-but-not-required survives once we strip away every asymmetry in forecasting and remedy—that is, whether there remains any structural reason to treat potential happiness and potential suffering differently, or whether the asymmetry was always just risk aversion in disguise.
