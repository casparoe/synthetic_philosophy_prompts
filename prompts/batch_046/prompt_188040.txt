I am writing in connection with my second-year epistemology course, where we have spent the last fortnight examining Edmund Gettier's 1963 counter-examples to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. Our tutor presented Case I—the scenario in which Smith justifiably believes that Jones, who has ten coins in his pocket, will be appointed, and therefore infers that the successful candidate has ten coins, only for Smith himself to be appointed with ten coins in his pocket by coincidence—before introducing a variant she labelled 'compensatory environmental luck'. In this variant, a laboratory technician calibrates a thermometer using a standard protocol and thereby acquires the justified belief that the ambient temperature is exactly 20°C; unknown to her, a concealed heating element has malfunctioned in a manner that precisely cancels the instrument's systematic error, so the belief is true purely through the interaction of two hidden disturbances rather than through the calibration procedure. Our tutor insisted that this is not a Gettier case at all, but rather an instance of what she called 'veritic environmental luck', and that the failure of knowledge here operates through a different mechanism altogether.

I find myself unable to accept this distinction. In both scenarios, it seems to me, the subject exercises appropriate epistemic diligence, the belief is true, and yet the truth is secured by a factor entirely outside the scope of the justification: in Gettier's example by the falsehood of the premise about Jones's appointment, and in the thermometer example by the hidden cancellation of errors. Consequently, I am inclined to think that my tutor is drawing a spurious boundary: if knowledge requires that the truth of the belief not be accidentally secured relative to the justification, then both cases violate the same underlying anti-luck condition, and we ought to treat the absence of such hidden compensating factors as an implicit, necessary condition on knowledge. My concern, however, is that adopting this condition might prove excessively demanding—would it not imply that ordinary perceptual knowledge is constantly undermined by unknown environmental interferences that happen to cancel out? Could you therefore clarify whether there is a structurally significant difference between Gettier-style failures of inferential transmission and these external compensation cases? In particular, I should like to know whether the conclusion that justified true belief is insufficient for knowledge survives only when we conflate these two forms of epistemic accident, or whether the anti-luck intuition genuinely applies across both.
