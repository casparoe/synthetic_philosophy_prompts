Here are my verdicts on whether particular scientific transitions involve genuine Kuhnian incommensurability, and I want to know whether a single principle underlies them.

Case one: the replacement of Newtonian mechanics by general relativity. I think the term mass shifts its theoretical role — it is no longer an invariant scalar — yet it retains enough referential continuity through the limiting-case relation that the two frameworks are not strongly incommensurable; they talk about the same property, corrected.

Case two: the replacement of phlogiston theory by Lavoisier’s oxygen chemistry. Here I think incommensurability is real and deep: phlogiston has no referent in the new framework, the taxonomy of substances is rebuilt, and there is no neutral observation language that describes both worlds without loss.

Case three: the move from caloric-fluid thermodynamics to the kinetic theory of heat. I feel pulled both ways. The operational measurements (temperature change, heat flow) survive intact, which suggests continuity, but the ontology — an invisible fluid versus statistical motion — is radically different. I am inclined to say there is partial incommensurability: measurement practices are commensurable, but the explanatory ontology is not.

Case four: a less-discussed shift in nineteenth-century embryology, where vital force (a directive, non-mechanical power guiding development) was replaced by biochemical gradient models. The experiments measuring growth rates remain comparable, but the very idea of what explains directionality changes. I think this is closer to case two than to case one, despite the shared vocabulary of development.

Case five: two contemporary cognitive-science programmes, one treating representation as explicit symbol manipulation in a computational architecture and the other treating it as coupled sensorimotor dynamics with no internal models. They study identical behavioural tasks, use overlapping terminology, and can sometimes predict the same outcomes. Yet I think they are genuinely incommensurable: the concept representation is embedded in such different networks of law and ontology that translating one into the other requires abandoning core explanatory commitments of at least one side.

The tension I keep returning to is between case one and case five. In case one, deep theoretical change does not destroy commensurability because there is a formal limiting relation. In case five, there is no formal reduction and predictions overlap, yet I still want to claim incommensurability. So my verdicts seem to depend both on formal reducibility and on ontological embedding, but these pull in different directions.

Is there a coherent principle — perhaps distinguishing conceptual, ontological, and methodological dimensions of incommensurability — that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, and say what it predicts for a transition I have not mentioned: the proposed shift from classical Boolean logic to quantum logic in certain interpretations of quantum mechanics, where the logical rules governing propositions about physical properties change. If no single principle can save all five, which verdict should I overturn for the sake of simplicity, and what does the streamlined principle then say about case five?
