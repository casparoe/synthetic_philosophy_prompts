In my seminar on the philosophy of historical explanation, we have been examining how retrospective knowledge reshapes the evaluation of past decision nodes. I grasp the basic mechanics of hindsight bias—how learning an outcome retroactively inflates our sense that it was predictable—and I also understand the narrative fallacy as the tendency to impose causal coherence on sequences that actually contained substantial randomness or branching. What I find conceptually elusive, however, is the precise interaction between these two distortions when applied to macro-institutional change. Specifically, I am trying to understand why explanations of path-dependent institutional lock-in—such as the consolidation of technical standards or regulatory regimes—so often present the victorious trajectory not merely as probable in retrospect, but as having been uniquely rational and structurally inevitable, even when the historical record shows that contemporaneous actors perceived multiple viable equilibria and that small perturbations could have diverted the system elsewhere.

Could you provide an intuitive, gut-level account of the conceptual mechanism through which hindsight bias and narrative fallacy jointly produce this particular illusion? I am not asking for a formal proof or a literature review, but rather for a clear conceptual dissection of how outcome knowledge enables narrative smoothing, and how that smoothing in turn suppresses the visibility of contingent branching points. In particular, I would like you to address why this conjunction makes lock-in appear optimized rather than merely actualized—why we come to see the winning path as having solved prior coordination problems with an elegance that the rejected alternatives apparently lacked—without invoking specific bibliographic references or citation-heavy historiography. A conceptual framework that distinguishes the retrospective inflation of probability from the retrospective imposition of causal coherence would be especially helpful.
