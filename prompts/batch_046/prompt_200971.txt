and heres the student answer (read the whole thing, its not short):
Darwin in On the Origin of Species demonstrates that functional complexity like the eye can accumulate through natural selection acting on random variation, with no conscious planner directing the process. This is the classic competence-without-comprehension scenario: nature achieves design-level results without understanding them. Turing makes an analogous inversion in his 1950 paper on computing machinery. Rather than asking whether machines possess internal consciousness or understanding, he proposes the imitation game, arguing that if a system behaves indistinguishably from a human we should treat its performance as genuinely intelligent. So in both cases an internal mental cause is replaced by an observable functional outcome.

That said, the structures arent identical. Darwin's argument is causal and historical: he explains how adaptation builds up across generations. Turing's argument is epistemological: since we cant observe other minds directly, behavior is all we have access to, so comprehension should be bracketed rather than denied. One error people often make is assuming Turing thinks machines genuinely understand mathematics once they solve equations, but he actually only suspends the question. Meanwhile, Darwin explicitly describes this pattern as competence without comprehension in his discussion of the eye, using it to show that Paley's watchmaker argument collapses once we see that function doesnt require a mental plan behind it.
