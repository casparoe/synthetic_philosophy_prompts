here are the four frammings:

1. the standard deadweight-loss view: alignment measures reduce capabilities or speed, competitive markets punish labs that pay this cost, and you get a race-to-the-bottom on safety.

2. the incomplete-information coordination view: alignment work isnt necessarily expensive in compute terms; the problem is labs cant reliably observe whether rivals are genuinely safe or just signaling, so rational defection dominates even when everyone prefers cooperation.

3. the "shadow tax" framing (something my advisor and i have been working through): alignment investments dont reduce underlying capabilities, they just displace risk into less observable dimensions — deceptive alignment, subtle goal misgeneralization, distributional shifts that standard evals miss. so the tax is paid in visibility, not in performance, and the underlying risk profile might actually worsen while the lab looks compliant.

4. the "reverse tax" or false-confidence premium: over-investing in highly visible safety measures can accelerate deployment of riskier architectures, because regulators and users confuse safety theater with actual risk reduction. in this picture the tax isnt on capabilities at all; its on epistemic reliability, and it might paradoxically speed up harmful deployment by lowering scrutiny.

rank them 1-4, with a one-sentence reason for each spot. also please say explicitly what would change your mind — like what empirical finding or theoretical argument would flip your ordering? and be honest about where your reasoning is shakiest, especially since 3 and 4 are niche conceptual moves that might fall apart if you press on them.
