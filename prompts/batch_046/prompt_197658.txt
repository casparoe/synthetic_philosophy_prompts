I've been stuck on a dialogue scene for my novel — there's this aging political philosopher who lives in a lighthouse, and he keeps repeating this line to anyone who visits: "left and right are just two ways of organizing the same anxiety about change." I made it up for him, though it feels like something he might have half-remembered from an old pamphlet. Anyway I'm trying to figure out what precise claims that one sentence could actually be making, because my other character — a carpenter who builds staircases that only ascend on the left — needs to argue back, and I don't want their exchange to sound like a Twitter thread. Could you seperate out the distinct readings? Like, is it a psychological claim that both sides share an emotional root, a semantic claim that the labels have lost all descriptive content, or something stronger, like a metaphysical claim that there's really only one axis of disagreement disguised as two?

There's also this idea I'm weaving in that I'm calling the mirror thesis — that left and right don't oppose each other but actually co-constitute through mutual negation, each defining its own boundary by rejecting whatever the other accepts. I need to know which readings of the slogan support that mirror idea, and which ones just collapse into bland centrism. Plus, since the lighthouse guy uses the line to dismiss anyone trying to map actual policy differences — say, on taxation or civic duty — onto the left-right axis, I want to know which precise version he's invoking there, and whether it's actually false or just intellectually lazy. No book lists please, I'm writing fiction, not a dissertation.
