Here's an argument I sketched for a labor-philosophy seminar that now strikes me as proving too much, since it seems to show that recognizing leisure as a human right necessarily authorizes comprehensive state control over citizens' time.

(1) A right to leisure is meaningful only if it protects against being compelled to labor beyond a sustainable limit; without enforceable caps, economic and social pressure can eliminate rest entirely, as karoshi demonstrates when unregulated labor destroys the worker.
(2) Rights are inalienable: if leisure is a genuine right, individuals cannot waive it by consenting to overwork, just as they cannot contract away bodily integrity.
(3) The Protestant work ethic treats continuous labor as a moral calling rather than a burden, meaning that for some people enforced rest is not liberation but a violation of conscience.
(4) Therefore, if leisure is universal and inalienable, the state must impose mandatory limits—such as the 35-hour week, compulsory vacation, or retirement ages—even against those who sincerely reject them.
(5) But once the state holds authority to dictate the boundary between work and rest for every citizen, nothing in principle prevents it from dictating the content of that rest, the permissible forms of labor, or the temporal structure of the entire calendar.
(6) So accepting leisure as an inalienable right commits us to a paternalistic governance of citizens' hours that extends far beyond preventing exploitation.
(7) That conclusion is absurd: we want to defend workers from karoshi-style destruction without endorsing authoritarian time-management.
(8) Hence leisure cannot be an inalienable right in the same category as free speech or bodily integrity.

Tell me which premise or step is the weakest link, explain why it initially looked sound, and price what giving it up costs—particularly whether (1) and (2) can be preserved. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
