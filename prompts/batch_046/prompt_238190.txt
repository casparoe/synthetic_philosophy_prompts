As a retired physicist who has recently taken up moral philosophy, I am convening an adversarial collaboration on the mechanisms of moral revolution. The two camps are: (A) the honor-conceptual view, in the spirit of Appiah and Bernard Williams, according to which norms such as dueling and chattel slavery collapse when the internal honor-economy that sustains them becomes conceptually unstable; and (B) the structural-interest view, according to which such norms dissolve primarily when underlying material incentives and power arrangements shift, with honor-language functioning mainly as ex post rationalization.

Please draft the joint statement both camps could sign. Organize it into four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the historical trajectory of one practice (either dueling or chattel slavery); about what distinguishes genuine normative change from mere suppression or displacement; and about why rational argument alone is insufficient to account for the timing of collapse.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present, in wording that the respective camp accepts for itself, the precise causal mechanism proposed; the point of divergence must be stated neutrally, with particular attention to whether shifts in honor-status can occur independently of shifts in structural interests.

Third, the cruxes. One crux must address normative hysteresis — understood here, by analogy with physical hysteresis, as the persistence of a moral norm after the structural conditions that originally supported it have disappeared — including whether this phenomenon is real and which framework better explains it. A second crux must address whether a practice can disappear without any prior internal destabilization of its honor-logic.

Fourth, for each crux, identify one concrete historical comparison or observation both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance the prediction each camp makes regarding it.

Keep the document formal and concise. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to obscure specialist literature. The objective is not to resolve the dispute but to render the remaining disagreement exact and empirically tractable.
