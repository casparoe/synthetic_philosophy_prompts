I'm prepping Friday's session on Williams's integrity objection — you know, that one-thought-too-many problem where saving your wife is fine, but thinking 'it's my wife, therefore permitted' ruins the direct attachment. My grad students keep reducing it to 'utilitarianism ignores feelings,' which totally misses the deeper structural point about ground projects being constitutive of practicle identity, not just preferences you happen to have. So I want to close with a poem, specifically a villanelle, 19 lines, two refrains that you pick. The speaker should be a musician in a burning concert hall deciding whether to grab her own cello or a stranger's interchangable identical one — not the usual husband-and-wife case, but something that still makes the attachment non-interchangable. One refrain should be the direct reaching ('I take the wood I know'), the other should be the moral justification that keeps intruding ('any instrument is equal'). The villanelle's repetition should show how the justification refrain slowly hollows out the first one, and in the final couplet they should sit side by side but not resolve — maybe the direct line is silent and the justification line is all that's left. Make it accurate to Williams's point that routing your project through impartial morality alienates it not because morality is wrong but because it's the wrong kind of reason for this agent. Don't quote from the essay, don't cite page numbers, and please don't end with some uplifting line about ethics and art being friends — leave the tension hanging, since that's literally where our semanar ended up.
