Here is roughly where I've landed on race, gender, and money as social kinds, and I want an audit of whether the package hangs together: (1) Race lacks a biological foundation but is real as a structurally embedded kind that produces causal effects—such as health disparities—independent of individual prejudice. (2) Gender is also constructed, but through bottom-up performative iteration rather than top-down institutional assignment, so it is constantly re-enacted rather than assigned. (3) Money demonstrates that pure collective intentionality can generate objective institutional facts with no physical basis beyond the representations we use. (4) I take genuine social construction to entail that membership conditions are conventional, which means boundaries are in principle open to collective revision, even if path-dependent lock-in usually renders revision practically impossible. (5) Nevertheless, I think gender and race are more revision-resistant than money because their embedding—performative for gender, structural for race—runs deeper than collective acceptance alone. Is this consistent? I worry that (2) should make gender especially susceptible to subversion through changed performance, which pulls against (5)'s claim that gender is more entrenched than money; I also wonder whether (4)'s "in principle open" does any work if (5) makes revision impossible across the board. Rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment is the cheapest to weaken or drop, distinguish any conflict that only looks serious, and deliver the answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
