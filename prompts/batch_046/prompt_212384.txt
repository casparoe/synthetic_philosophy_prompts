I'm preparing a module on metaphysical principles and find myself stuck on the classic challenge posed by Max Black's symmetric universe: two spheres of identical size, color, and internal constitution, suspended in an otherwise empty space with no other objects or properties to distinguish them. The standard debate turns on whether Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles survives if we allow that the spheres stand in irreflexive relational properties—each being, say, two miles from the other—even though no intrinsic feature differs. A colleague and I disagree about the force of this case. She holds that if such a universe is metaphysically possible, then PII is plainly false and must be replaced by a principle admitting primitive thisness or haecceities; her credence that PII fails in this scenario is about 0.85. I am somewhat more cautious—around 0.60—because I worry that relational distinctness might still be circular or that quantum-mechanical considerations about identical particles actually complicate rather than resolve the intuition.

Please give me your own credence that Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles is false, or must be substantially amended to allow non-qualitative individuation, specifically in a Black-style maximally symmetric universe containing only two qualitatively identical spheres. I want the reasoning decomposed into at least these terms: (1) the prior probability that Black's universe describes a genuinely metaphysically possible world rather than merely a conceivably imaginable one; (2) the probability that irreflexive spatial or structural relations can ground numerical distinctness without implicitly presupposing the very identity they are meant to explain; (3) the probability that considerations drawn from quantum mechanics—particularly the statistics and indistinguishability of identical particles—provide an independent analog that either strengthens or weakens the sphere intuition; and (4) the probability that even if PII fails for maximally symmetric macroscopic objects, the failure generalizes to ordinary objects in asymmetric worlds or remains a local anomaly. Assign a rough number and a one-line justification to each term, show how they combine into your overall figure, and identify which term is doing the most work in separating my 0.60 from my colleague's 0.85. Finally, specify what kind of argument—perhaps from structural realism, gauge-theoretic ontology, or the analysis of relational properties—would most substantially shift your number.
