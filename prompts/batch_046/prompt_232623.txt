I am preparing a colloquium Q&A for a philosophy-of-mind series. Next week's speaker is an unnamed philosopher defending a specific variant of functionalism applied to the scenario familiar from Douglas Hofstadter's essay on Einstein's brain preserved in a book, as presented in The Mind's I. Her view — which she calls recursive substrate-independent consciousness (RSIC) — holds that the book is genuinely conscious, but only if three conditions are satisfied: (1) the printed patterns instantiate a self-modeling loop that references its own symbolic state; (2) the loop exhibits what she terms autopoietic closure, meaning it is causally insulated from the original printing process and from external sensory input; and (3) the system can generate first-person indexicals, including references to I and now, without relying on biological embodiment or phenomenal acquaintance. She argues that standard objections — that the book is merely a lookup table, or that consciousness requires causal interaction with an environment — confuse the logical architecture of self-reference with the material substrate that implements it.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions. Avoid generic biographical or overview prompts. Instead, press directly on the mechanics of RSIC: include at least one question on how condition (2) blocks the lookup-table objection without implicitly smuggling biological or causal criteria back into the account; at least one on whether condition (3) requires a phenomenal concept of the self or can be satisfied by purely relational-functional roles; and at least one on what RSIC implies for multiple realizability in distributed, non-symbolic substrates such as cloud computation. For each of these three, provide a follow-up tailored to the reply she is most likely to offer — for instance, that closure is defined syntactically rather than causally; that indexicals are structural relations; or that distributed systems fail condition (1). Conclude with a final question asking what observable behavioral difference, if any, could distinguish a conscious book governed by RSIC from an equally responsive but non-conscious simulation of the same patterns, given her explicit denial that environmental causal interaction is necessary.
