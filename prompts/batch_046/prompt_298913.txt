Having spent a career in the natural sciences, I have recently turned to philosophical ethics and find the standard treatments of dietary morality conceptually under-specified. I want the full desiderata that any adequate normative theory of human food choice must satisfy if it is to take seriously both farmed-animal suffering and wild-animal suffering mediated by agricultural practice. Write the specification formally, as for a philosophy seminar: list the requirements, the empirical or conceptual case behind each, whether each is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and flag any subset you believe is jointly unsatisfiable.

The theory must be able to do the following.

First, distinguish suffering directly caused by the consumption act—such as slaughter—from suffering structurally induced by land-use conversion and crop cultivation, including field-animal mortality during harvest and habitat fragmentation affecting wild populations. It must specify which of these enter the moral calculus and why, without collapsing them into an undifferentiated aggregate harm.

Second, resolve the substitution asymmetry determinately: replacing beef with chicken typically increases the number of individual animals killed per unit of nutrition, yet reduces total land-use intensity and associated wild-animal displacement. The theory must deliver either a principled verdict or an explicit weighing framework; it may not merely note the tension and leave it unresolved.

Third, remain consistent with well-established ecological dynamics—specifically, that agricultural land-use changes alter predator-prey relationships and can trigger trophic cascades—so that reducing one form of animal agriculture does not have morally transparent effects on wild suffering. The theory need not solve ecology, but it must not treat land use as ecologically inert or contradict population dynamics that are not in serious scientific dispute.

Fourth, respect the distinction between having moral status and the weight assigned to that status in aggregation. If the theory grants insects or small wild rodents negligible weight, it must explain why that weight is negligible without contradicting its own criteria for status; if it weights them equally with large mammals, it must explain how that scales against ecological reality.

Fifth, apply consistently across plant and animal agriculture the distinction between intentional harm and foreseen but unintended harm—particularly regarding pesticide use in crop farming versus intentional slaughter in livestock farming—and specify whether the doctrine of double effect can operate uniformly in both domains or must be restricted.

Sixth, specify the scope of counterfactual responsibility with respect to supply inelasticity: to what extent is an individual consumer responsible for suffering that would occur regardless of their particular choice, given that agricultural supply does not respond one-to-one with marginal consumption? The theory must not assume perfect counterfactual efficacy without explicit argument.

For each requirement, state the motivating consideration. Then identify any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—for instance, whether demanding strict consistency with complex trophic dynamics and demanding a simple per-capita aggregation of suffering are compatible.

Finally, assess briefly—without bibliography or citation—how the prevailing candidates fare: a straightforward utilitarian aggregation focused on farmed-animal suffering; a rights-based prohibition on using sentient beings as means; and an ecological-footprint virtue approach that weights land-use transformation as primary. Keep the weight on the criteria and their potential contradictions rather than on the scorecard; I am using this to determine whether the field harbors a hidden structural conflict in its demands.
