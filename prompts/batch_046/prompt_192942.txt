Medical informed-consent protocols prohibit deception and coercion but routinely permit clinicians to present statistically identical outcomes through frames known to systematically shift patient preferences—survival-rate versus mortality-rate formulations, or relative-risk versus absolute-risk presentations—so long as the underlying figures are accurate. At the same time, the same institutions often restrict nudges that exploit cognitive biases in non-clinical settings. What theory of autonomy and manipulation does this asymmetry presuppose? Must it hold that rational self-governance is preserved whenever factual content is technically available, regardless of how it is structured? Or can the practice be explained by a distinction between altering the contents of belief and altering the processes of belief formation? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, assess whether it is consistent with the broader institutional commitment to patient sovereignty, and specify what elements of consent practice would have to change if framing were treated as a form of non-coercive manipulation.
