I have been recording a political philosopher’s rulings on institutional-design exercises modeled on the original position. In each exercise, representatives choose behind a veil that conceals their social class, but the exercises differ in what other information is disclosed. The philosopher compares two schemes and declares one permissible and one not; she offers little explicit reasoning, but her verdicts are consistent across cases.

Her rulings to date:

(1) Full veil — representatives know nothing of their talents, risk preferences, era, or psychological makeup. She permits Scheme A, which maximizes the worst-off’s share of social primary goods and allows inequalities only if they raise that share, even when they produce large status gaps; she rejects Scheme B, which achieves strict equality but with a lower worst-off share.

(2) Risk profile revealed — representatives know they are uniformly risk-averse, but class and talents remain hidden. She permits the same Scheme A; she rejects Scheme C, which offers a lower worst-off share but guarantees an absolute floor against economic volatility, despite its higher expected utility for risk-averse agents.

(3) Generational position revealed — representatives know whether they belong to the present or the future, but class and talents are hidden. She permits Scheme D, which accepts a slightly lower present worst-off share in order to invest in infrastructure that raises the future worst-off share substantially; she rejects Scheme E, which maximizes the present worst-off share but depletes capital.

(4) Talent level revealed — representatives know whether they have high or low productive capacity, but social position is hidden. She permits Scheme F, which allows high-capacity representatives to retain a portion of the surplus they generate, yielding a worst-off share slightly above that of full redistribution; she rejects Scheme G, which taxes all surplus away and results in a lower worst-off share due to reduced output.

(5) Partial positional knowledge — representatives know they are not in the bottom decile, but do not know which decile they occupy within the top nine, and they are informed of the society’s technological dynamism. She permits Scheme H, which imposes steep progressive taxation plus an absolute income cap near the 95th percentile, yielding a worst-off share that is lower than in Scheme I (the same tax structure without the cap, which produces a higher worst-off share through greater aggregate output). She rejects Scheme I.

Now consider a new exercise, Convention 6. The veil conceals class, generation, and talent, but reveals to each representative their own preference-adaptation profile — specifically, whether their life-plan satisfaction is primarily responsive to absolute consumption, relative social status, or autonomous achievement — without disclosing which profile predominates in the population. Representatives must choose between Scheme J and Scheme K.

Scheme J applies objective maximin: it maximizes the worst-off’s objective bundle of primary goods, permits steep inequalities that generate that bundle, and treats revealed preference profiles as institutionally irrelevant, on the grounds that representatives behind the veil should not design social structures around their own psychological idiosyncrasies.

Scheme K applies a corrected maximin: it calculates the worst-off’s objective bundle as in J, but then applies a discount factor if empirical regularities (supplied to the representatives) indicate that the resulting status disparities would trigger widespread downward preference adaptation — that is, satisfaction-reporting despite objective deprivation — among status-sensitive individuals who may fall in the worst-off group. Under this correction, Scheme K produces a slightly lower objective worst-off bundle than J, but it avoids the disparity pattern that triggers adaptation.

Predict the philosopher’s verdict. Identify at least two competing rules that fit her first five rulings — for example, an objective maximin rule supplemented only by information-triggered side constraints, versus a unified legitimacy or stability principle that treats self-respect and preference integrity as lexically prior to further gains — and state which scheme each predicts. Give your best guess at her actual ruling, with the reasoning that favors it, and identify Convention 5 as the closest analog or explain why another is closer. Then say whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, and if so, what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify one further convention case — varying either the information revealed or the structure of the schemes — that would best distinguish the rules you have identified.
