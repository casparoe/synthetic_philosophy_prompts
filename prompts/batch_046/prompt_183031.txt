The metaphysical debate over whether laws of nature are Humean regularities—best-system summaries of the total mosaic—or anti-Humean governing relations—typically grounded in universals or dispositional essences—has persisted for decades with remarkably little convergence. The standard arguments about ontological parsimony, explanatory asymmetry, and counterfactual support are now familiar to both sides, yet neither camp treats them as decisive. Recently, I tried to localize the dispute by asking whether the predictable trajectories of ecological succession—ordered species replacement following disturbance—should count as laws. Here the stall reproduces exactly: the Humean assimilates the trajectory to a globally optimal axiom set for ecological patterns, while the anti-Humean denies it has the modal authority to constrain particular organisms. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about ontology but about the criteria for explanation in non-fundamental sciences—global systematization versus local modal constraint—with both sides speaking past one another under the single heading "lawhood." Diagnose this impasse: is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two different questions being answered under one name? If substantive, is there a hidden empirical or formal issue—perhaps about whether best-system analyses can uniquely identify ecological patterns without gerrymandering? And given your diagnosis, what kind of move—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a reframing of scientific explanation—would actually count as progress, or should we expect none?
