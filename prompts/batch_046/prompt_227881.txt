I have noticed that France makes it illegal to collect racial or ethnic data in its national census, while the United Kingdom and United States gather exactly those statistics to enforce anti-discrimination laws and allocate multicultural funding. At the same time, France asks immigrants to sign an integration contract pledging loyalty to republican values, bans conspicuous religious symbols in public schools under laïcité, and yet still uses public money to repair historic Catholic churches as cultural heritage. To me these rules seem to pull in opposite directions: the state claims not to see race or religion, yet it actively regulates what citizens wear and believe, and it treats some religious buildings as national patrimony.

What philosophical commitments about equality, identity, and citizenship does this cluster of practices actually presuppose? Does it require the view that justice demands the state be literally blind to group differences—treating ethnicity as irrelevant in the same way as eye color—rather than merely treating those differences as immaterial to rights? Or does it depend on something stronger: that public recognition of group identity is itself harmful or hierarchical? Reconstruct the implicit theory of recognition and integration here, distinguishing what the census ban, the school symbol ban, the integration contract, and the heritage funding each strictly require from what they merely happen to fit. Are these parts consistent with one theory, or do they quietly presuppose conflicting views about whether culture is a private preference that can be bracketed in public, or a structural feature that must be actively dissolved?
