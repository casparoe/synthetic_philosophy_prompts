I'm a doctoral candidate in visual studies building a parametric typeface generator for my dissertation. The system interpolates across a high-dimensional style space, and I have isolated two anomalous outputs that are destabilizing my working framework. The first is a glyph situated far outside the statistical cluster of conventional lowercase 'a' forms: it possesses neither bowl nor stem, consisting instead of a single open loop with one break. When I embed it in common English words, viewers identify it as 'a' with over ninety percent accuracy; presented alone, the same viewers call it a partial circle or an unrecognizable mark. The second glyph sits precisely at the geometric center of every canonical measurement—bowl area, counter ratio, stem height, terminal curvature—yet in both isolation and word context readers classify it as 'o' or 'q', never as 'a'. I had been proceeding on the assumption that recognizing a letter means detecting some invariant structural core that persists despite stylistic deformation, but these examples seem to separate geometric fidelity from categorical membership in opposite directions. I need to decide how to frame this in my chapter: is recognition tracking a hidden intrinsic feature that these samples accidentally trigger or miss, or is membership in the category fixed by the glyph's functional role within a larger word pattern? If the latter, does that imply the identity of a letterform is not really in the shape itself but in its relational position? I want to avoid overstating the philosophical stakes, yet the pattern feels too systematic to dismiss as mere perceptual noise. How should I interpret what these outliers reveal about the stability of letter categories?
