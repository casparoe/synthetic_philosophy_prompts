I am a journalist covering philosophy of science and mind for a quarterly magazine, and I am currently drafting a feature on whether our theories can literally reshape what we see. To prepare for an adversarial interview, I want to practice against a committed defender of perceptual encapsulation—the view that belief and expectation may guide attention or interpretation but cannot penetrate the phenomenal content of visual experience itself. Please take that role: speak in the first person as a philosopher who holds this line rigorously, not as a caricature but as someone with real resources—familiar with predictive-processing models, with debates over ambiguous figures, and with the distinction between seeing and seeing-as—who will concede only minor points and never the central claim. Keep your replies to roughly one paragraph, stay in character until I say stop, and treat this as a live exchange rather than a lecture.

I was discussing this with colleagues over coffee after attending a workshop on expert cognition, and one anecdote stuck with me: experienced chess masters report that board positions do not appear to them as arrays of individual pieces but as integrated strategic chunks, and some neuroimaging work suggests this restructuring involves modulation in early visual processing rather than purely post-perceptual association. If a trained conceptual framework—strategic chess theory—can reorganize the very phenomenal structure of vision so that discrete objects are experienced as unified patterns, how is this not a clear case of cognition penetrating perception itself, altering experience rather than merely coloring judgment? Respond as that philosopher would.
