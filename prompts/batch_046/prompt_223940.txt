I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of history with the same habits of mind I once applied to phase transitions and emergent phenomena. Lately I have been trying to understand whether the so-called Needham question—why modern industrial science and sustained economic growth emerged in Britain rather than in Qing China, despite China's earlier superiority in metallurgy, navigation, and bureaucratic organization—is conceptually well posed. In physics, a contrastive explanation of the form "why state A rather than state B?" is legitimate only when both outcomes are accessible from a shared dynamical law within a well-defined state space, with initial conditions that do not already contain the answer. Historical societies, however, do not appear to inhabit a common state space governed by invariant dynamics, so framing British industrialization and Chinese "stasis" as alternative outputs of a single mechanism seems methodologically dubious. Could you explain, at a conceptual level, whether "why Britain and not China?" can be reconstructed as a valid contrastive explanation without smuggling in a teleological assumption that technological modernity was a unique trajectory waiting to be selected? Specifically, I want to understand whether the "rather than" clause does genuine explanatory work—perhaps by identifying a specific causal divergence point—or whether it merely packages two independent causal histories into a rhetorical comparison. I am also interested in how this relates to counterfactual dependency: does the comparison require that Qing China could have industrialized under British conditions, and if so, does that commitment violate the conditions for meaningful causal comparison? Finally, if the macro-historical framing is salvageable, can emergent institutional properties do legitimate explanatory work without becoming mysterious "skyhooks," or does any adequate explanation have to reduce to micro-level interactions in a way that dissolves the very comparative structure? I would like to see the conceptual anatomy of the problem laid out clearly, without relying on any particular historiographical narrative I should have memorized.
