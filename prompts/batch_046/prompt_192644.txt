Consider two formalized institutional communication protocols: one, drawn from Dutch administrative practice, mandates that any evaluation containing negative judgment be delivered without mitigating language or face-saving framing, on the premise that indirectness treats the recipient as incapable of bearing truth; the other, modeled on Japanese organizational norms, requires that direct refusal or criticism be mediated through contextual cues, deliberate ambiguity, or third-party intermediaries, on the premise that explicit negative statements violate relational harmony and impose an unchosen emotional burden. What philosophical commitments about autonomy, respect, and the moral status of misleading or evasive speech does each protocol presuppose? Do they share a single underlying theory of why white lies are wrong—say, one grounded in rational agency and transparency—or do they instead rely on rival moral psychologies, such as care-based non-maleficence versus dignity-based candor? If a single multicultural institution adopted both protocols in different departments, what fundamental inconsistencies about the nature of respect would it need to resolve?
