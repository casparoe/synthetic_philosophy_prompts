I'm drafting an essay on Marx's theory of exploitation and alienation, and I'm tired of the standard undergraduate gloss that just lists the four dimensions from the 1844 Manuscripts and applies them mechanically to factory labor. I want to read Charlie Chaplin's Modern Times (1936) through a more specific, somewhat idiosyncratic lens: what we might call somatic alienation, meaning not just mental estrangement or loss of control over the product, but a literal neuromuscular re-patterning in which the worker's body continues to execute machinery rhythms after leaving the plant. The Tramp's involuntary shoulder twitch after the assembly-line sequence, and the automated feeding machine that treats his mouth and digestive tract as production inputs to be optimized, both seem to dramatize this idea. I want the reading to be genuinely two-way: take the film's major scenes—the line work, the feeding machine, the department-store dance, the final sunrise walk with the gamine—seriously as cinema, and take Marx's distinction between formal subsumption (capital appropriating existing labor processes) and real subsumption (capital transforming the labor process and the worker's subjectivity from within) seriously as philosophy.

Does Modern Times depict formal or real subsumption, or does it blur them? Does the comedy—Chaplin's physical resilience and the Tramp's ultimate escape into the open road—undercut the claim that bodily alienation is total, or does it instead show that alienation is compatible with apparent freedom? I'm especially interested in whether the feeding-machine scene can be read as extending alienation into the sphere of reproduction and consumption, not just production, and whether that extension makes Marx's framework more powerful or reveals its limits when applied to industrial modernity. Where does the philosophical concept illuminate a moment that otherwise looks like pure slapstick? Where does the film resist the Marxist frame—perhaps by making the Tramp's suffering charming rather than tragic, or by suggesting that human playfulness survives mechanization intact? And what does the film add to the conceptual debate about whether alienation is fundamentally economic, phenomenological, or embodied that a purely argumentative essay could not?
