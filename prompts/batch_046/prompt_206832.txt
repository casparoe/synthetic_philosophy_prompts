I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher of biology who defends a specific variant of collective consciousness for ant colonies. Rather than treating the colony as a metaphorical agent or as a swarm with distributed but unintegrated cognition, she argues that certain colonies realize a single, unified subject of experience. She calls her view residual-trace holism. On her account, the primary substrate of the colony's mental life is not the live neural activity of individual ants but the persistent architecture of pheromone trails, soil tunnels, and chemical gradients—structures that remain causally active and information-integrating long after the ants that produced them have died. She takes this to solve the combination problem for macro-minds: there is no need to sum individual ant experiences because the colony-level mental state is realized in the structural residue itself, with living ants serving mainly as maintenance and updating mechanisms. She accepts the framing of Hofstadter's ant-fugue analogy—searching for neuron-like patterns at a higher scale—but insists that the analogy applies to the dead traces as much as to the living insects.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of the view. I want at least one question, with a tailored follow-up, on each of the following four pressure points:

1. The distinction between her view and standard functionalist accounts of swarm intelligence: if the colony behaves adaptively but we deny that the live ants contribute phenomenal states, what makes the residue itself a subject rather than merely an intelligent mechanism?

2. The combination problem in her specific form: if no individual ant experiences are being summed or merged, what is the qualitative character (if any) of the colony-level state? Is it blank, is it analogous to a single ant's experience scaled up, or is it sui generis?

What would count as the death of such a mind?
