You are an expert moral philosopher with deep familiarity in animal ethics, the conceptual history of human-animal relations, and the structure of relational moral theories. Before answering, please take a brief moment to brainstorm the main axes along which critiques of speciesism are typically organized—intrinsic capacity, relational status, and ecological embeddedness—then proceed with disciplined focus.

The position I hold is this: speciesism is best understood not primarily as a failure to weigh animal suffering equally with human suffering, but as the collapse of a specific kind of relational moral practice—what I will call an agrarian covenant—in which the historical domestication of animals entailed reciprocal obligations of care, shelter, and eventually humane death, obligations that industrial agriculture has severed without substituting any equivalent moral structure. On this view, the central wrong of modern factory farming is not that it treats pigs or chickens as mere means, but that it preserves the form of instrumental use while annihilating the reciprocal form of care that once justified it; the moral error is therefore structural and relational rather than calculative.

Please take a committed response: argue whether this framing is defensible, and in particular, map out what would genuinely count against it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also consider whether this framing implicitly privileges domesticated species over wild ones in a way that undermines its own anti-speciesist ambitions, and whether results from other domains—say, about the moral status of wild-animal suffering, or about the ontology of collective practices—expose hidden commitments the view cannot easily absorb.

Be direct, make definite choices, and do not merely lay out options for me to select among.
