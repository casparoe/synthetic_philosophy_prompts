I'm on the restorative-practice review board at my community college—mostly admin staff plus one philosophy adjunct who always makes things complicated—and we're deadlocked over whether to renew funding for the "Agency Repair Program." It's a pilot for students caught in plagiarism or cheating: they write a "responsibility narrative" where they have to affirm that they genuinely could have acted otherwise and that they're truly blameworthy for what they did, then they do restorative amends with the affected party. One board member filed a formal objection citing Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. Her point is that if our actions flow from our character, and our character is just the result of heredity and environment and prior causes we don't control, then nobody is ever causa sui—cause of themselves—so true moral responsibility is literally impossible. She says forcing students to write "I could have done otherwise" is coercing them into affirming a metaphysically false belief, and that this actually deepens psychological harm rather than repairing anything. The other members think she's overthinking it and the program has good retention numbers.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting—just to be clear, the meeting is at 3pm, not 4pm like I mistakenly put in the shared calendar. Recommend a concrete course: approve with modifications, reject outright, or suspend for six months pending an external review. The memo should include actual criteria the board can apply, like whether a pedagogical program's contested philosophical premise undermines its ethical legitimacy, and it should take the Strawsonian objection seriously rather than treating it as a distraction. Most importantly, include an honest, strong treatment of the best argument for whichever side ends up losing. Keep it formal enough to vote on, but don't turn it into a metaphysics dissertation. No need for bibliographies or outside reading lists.
