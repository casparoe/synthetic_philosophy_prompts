I will be defending, in a classics department colloquium, the thesis that Plutarch's Parallel Lives functions not as hagiographic exemplarity but as an apparatus of moral calibration. Specifically, I argue that the paired Greek and Roman biographies and their synkrisis sections are structured to block direct imitation: instead of offering a single life as an emulable model, the text forces the reader to rescale judgments of virtue and vice across incommensurable civic contexts, producing comparative moral calibration rather than exemplar-based moral learning. I further claim that modern cognitive-scientific accounts of narrative transportation and biography-based moral learning have tacitly adopted a hagiographic framework—treating biography primarily as a vehicle for identification and imitation—thereby misreading Plutarch's comparative architecture as a failure to provide clear moral targets rather than as a deliberate pedagogical design. The audience consists of: two specialists in ancient biography who read Plutarch's moralizing prefaces and death-scenes as straightforward exhortation to virtue; a virtue ethicist working on moral exemplarity who is skeptical of functionalist psychology applied to ancient texts; a cognitive psychologist who studies narrative persuasion and is familiar with the literature on transportation and identification but wary of imposing modern experimental categories on premodern narrative structures; and two doctoral students in late-antique reception who read imperial biography through the lens of hagiographic continuity. Provide a Q&A prep sheet: list the objections in the order they will actually arise, phrased in the vocabulary of each faction, ordered by the damage each would do to my position. For each objection, indicate what I should concede outright, what I should hold without qualification, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the specific objection—most likely from the psychologist—regarding the empirical basis for my claim that paired comparison suppresses rather than intensifies identification with the subject, which I hope is not raised, and draft a concise reply that does not depend on citing specific experimental studies. Conclude by identifying the single weakest point in my position—likely the question of whether moral calibration counts as a genuinely moral achievement rather than a merely epistemic one—and advise how to defend it without retreating to the claim that Plutarch was implicitly conducting modern cognitive science.
