I run a workshop on philosophy of history with about 14 people — mix of grad students and some faculty — 90 minutes, one session. I want them to feel in their bones the conflict when a community's living memory and the historical record flatly contradict each other, and specifically which one should "give way." To avoid them just recycling standard arguments, I'm injecting this framing: there's a fictional but concrete case of an island community practicing "mnemonic stewardship" — they deliberately let oral accounts of a founding migration drift over generations, not from error but because the shifting details actively maintain present kinship obligations and dispute-resolution rituals. Then a digitized colonial archive surfaces showing the migration was actually a forced relocation involving violence and different actors, which the living narrative completely erases. The participants shouldn't hear this as "fix the mistake" — they need to feel that correcting the record might literally dissolve the social structure that depends on the memory.

Design a structured activity — roles, rules, a timeline across the 90 minutes, and some kind of payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (maybe veto tokens or social-credit points) — so they run into the problem themselves before anyone names it. I want at least three roles: Memory Keepers who must defend the drifted narrative, Archival Researchers who have to present the documented evidence, and Bridge figures who can propose compromises but pay costs for each side they alienate. The rules should force them to make an actual collective decision about whether to "update" the community's official story, suppress the archive, or live with contradiction, with consequences assigned by the rules rather than by discussion alone. Afterward, a debrief that maps each moment onto the philosophical issue — epistemic authority, corrective justice, the performative role of remembrance — and explains what predictably goes wrong the first time (probably the Bridge people try to split the difference and end up satisfying nobody). Be frank about which feature of collective-memory theory — maybe that memory is constitutive of group identity in a way archival history can never replicate — the activity can only gesture at rather than fully reproduce. No upfront lecture, and don't call the theme by its usual name untill the end.
