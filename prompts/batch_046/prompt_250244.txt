I'm preparing a graduate seminar session on evolutionary explanations in philosophy of mind, and I want to press my students on a conceptual tension that I think gets glossed over in standard treatments of Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique. Most introductory discussions present spandrels as evolutionary byproducts—traits that arise not through direct selection but as necessary architectural consequences of other adaptive structures—and then contrast them with adaptations. But I think this framing understates the philosophical problem that arises once we introduce exaptation, particularly when we apply it to high-level cognitive capacities rather than morphological features.

Here is the specific issue I would like explained with real conceptual precision. Consider the human capacity for elaborate counterfactual reasoning: the ability to construct and evaluate scenarios that diverge from actual states of affairs. An adaptationist narrative might treat this as a direct selection product—perhaps evolved for social strategizing, long-term planning, or risk assessment. A spandrels-style critique, by contrast, might argue that counterfactual cognition is not itself an adaptation but an inevitable byproduct of the architecture required for language, episodic memory, and working-memory integration; its presence is structurally necessary once those other capacities are in place, not because selection favored counterfactual thinking per se. Now suppose that, once present, this capacity is exapted for scientific modeling, legal reasoning, and technological design—domains where it clearly enhances fitness in modern environments, and where cultural selection might eventually reinforce the underlying neural architecture.

The conceptual question is this: does the fact of exaptation and subsequent cultural reinforcement retroactively dissolve the spandrels explanation, or does it leave it intact? In particular, I want to understand whether there is a meaningful distinction between (a) a trait that is originally a spandrel but becomes so functionally integrated that selection eventually operates on the underlying architecture, and (b) a trait that was an adaptation all along but whose original selective environment was simply misidentified. If the distinction collapses, then the spandrels critique risks being reduced to a historical claim about our ignorance of original selective pressures rather than a structural claim about the logic of evolutionary explanation. Please explain this conceptual issue carefully, working through the logic of architectural necessity, co-optation, and diachronic selection without merely summarizing the standard position. I am especially interested in whether the spandrels framework can accommodate "locked-in" spandrels—byproducts that become indispensable—without surrendering its core challenge to adaptationist reasoning.
