ok so i'm putting together a longform piece on decision paralysis for the magazine — i cover science and ideas, mostly philosophy of mind and decision theory these days — and over drinks last week i got into it with some friends about buridan's ass, you know, the donkey starving between two identical hay bales. i ended up writing down where i actually stand, and now i'm wondering if i'm holding a consistent package or just stitching together things that sound good. here's the set of commitments i want to defend, and i need you to audit them like you're checking my copy before it goes to print (definately don't want to look sloppy).

(1) i think buridan's ass is a fake paradox: if two options are genuinely equal in value, using an arbitrary tie-breaker — even literally flipping a mental coin — is perfectly rational. there's no hidden obligation to have a preference.

(2) i also think we should keep a hard line between being genuinely indifferent (the options sit on the same scale at the same point) and being incomparable (no shared metric at all). conflating them is what makes people suspicious of tie-breaking.

(3) in moral contexts, though, i think if you're truly indifferent between saving person A or person B — identical in every relevant respect — a coin flip is fine, but if there's even the tiniest hairline preference you ought to follow it. introducing random noise near a tie feels like it disrespects the actual weight of reasons.

(4) i think evolutionary explanations of tie-breaking — random mutation, neural noise, that kind of thing — are causally interesting but normatively dead ends. they don't tell us what we should do.

(5) and i think rational decision procedures shouldn't depend on framing: presenting the choice as "pick A or B" versus "stick with the default" shouldn't change what's rational. but then i also catch myself thinking that maintaining the status quo is sometimes the right tie-breaker, just because it's the path of least resistance.

(6) finally, i think preferences can be constructed on the spot for the purpose of breaking a deadlock — they're not "fake" just because you didn't have them before the choice presented itself.

so here's the ask: do these hang together? i'm specifically worried there's a tension between (1) and (5) — if arbitrary mechanisms are fine, why do i keep reaching for the status quo as special? — and between (3) and (6) — if i can construct preferences on the fly, why can't i construct a hairline preference to avoid the coin flip in moral cases? also, is there a hidden problem between (4) and (1), like if evolutionary noise is just nature's coin flip, why is my mental coin flip rational but nature's is normatively empty? rank the tensions by severity, say which one has to go if i want the cleanest package, and flag any pair that only looks tense but actually dissolves once you look closer. don't lecture me on buridan's original text, i already read the summary, just audit the logic of my own commitments.
