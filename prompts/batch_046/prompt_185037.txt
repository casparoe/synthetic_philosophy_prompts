I retired from biophysics a few years back and lately I've been sitting in on philosophy of mind seminars, which is how I got dragged into this color-phi mess. You know the illusion — green dot flashes on the left, then red dot on the right, and people swear they saw one dot glide through the middle turning orange, even though the second dot hasn't arrived yet. A friend in my study group built this argument about Orwellian versus Stalinesque revisions of consciousness, and now I think it proves that experience has no real past at all. I can see each step, but I can't find the exit.

Here is his chain, more or less:

(1) In color phi, subjects report experiencing the intermediate color-change at the spatial midpoint before the second dot is physicaly presented.
(2) If that report is accurate, then consciousness at time t included information that wasn't neuraly available until t + about 40ms, violating causal closure.
(3) So the report can't be accurate at the moment it occurs; the brain must register both dots, construct the motion narrative, then revise the record of the first interval.
(4) Call the two revision-types Orwellian — the false draft was experienced then replaced in memory — and Stalinesque — the false draft never made it into consciousness.
(5) In phi, subjects don't say "first green, then I corrected it"; they embed the motion in the original flow, which looks Orwellian: the brain lets them experience the first dot alone briefly, then overwrites that frame.
(6) Now here's where my friend injects this newer idea he calls retrograde consolidation: working-memory buffers aren't overwritten in place but are replaced by a backward-propagating signal that reaches back to the buffer's original timestamp, so the revision doesn't just change a stored file — it changes the timestamped experience itself.
(7) If that mechanism works within an 80ms binding window, there's no principalled reason it can't work across minutes or years whenever new context arrives.
(8) So every "stream" of consciousness is just a reconstructed draft with no privileged access to earlier versions; the very idea of "the timing of experience" collapses because there is no stable temporal ordering left to measure.
(9) But this undermines (3), which assumed there was a "first interval" with a real timestamp to be revised — if all ordering is reconstructed, then "before the second dot arrived" is just another fiction.
(10) So either we reject (3) and accept backward causation in consciousness, or we reject (5) and call phi Stalinesque, which saves temporal order but makes consciousness permanently inaccessible to introspection.

My friend says (7) is the slip — he's just being arbitrary about the 80ms window. But I feel like if I drop (7) I'm paying some other price I can't name. I'd rather keep the idea that experience has real temporal thickness and that we can introspect it's basic flow. Which step should I actually reject, and what does giving it up cost? And does retrograde consolidation change anything, or is it just a fancy name for the same problem?
