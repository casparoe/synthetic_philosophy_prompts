A philosophy-of-action textbook summarizes the absent-minded driver case like this: a driver intends to take exit 42 to visit a friend, but drives past it lost in thought, only noticing miles later. The textbook claims this proves conscious intention is neither necessary nor sufficient for intentional action, because the driver clearly acted intentionally—staying in lane, obeying signals—while the conscious plan was causally inert. It then proposes a predictive-processing resolution: the brain minimized free energy by suppressing the prediction-error that should have triggered the exit, so the driver's "real" intention was always to maintain the current trajectory, and his earlier conscious plan was merely a post-hoc rationalization. Is this a fair summary of the philosophical problem and of the predictive-processing interpretation? Please separate what is accurate about the driver case, what misrepresents the standard philosophical framing—particularly regarding whether conscious intentions must be causally active to guide action—and whether the neural account genuinely resolves the tension or just redescribes it. In particular, does the claim that the conscious plan was a post-hoc rationalization actually follow from anything in the predictive-processing model, or is that an extra philosophical assumption being smuggled in?
