I am a journalist covering comparative politics and the history of ideas for a quarterly magazine, and I repeatedly have to edit or write through passages where the word “liberal” is used without clarification—by interview subjects, in translated speeches, or in cross-national comparisons. I want a reusable editorial audit my copy desk can run in about five minutes per instance, without reaching for reference works or external databases, to determine which semantic register is in play and how we should gloss, flag, or leave untouched the term. The conceptual framework I want operationalised is not the familiar geographic binary, but a three-register model—market-procedural (R1), welfare-universalist (R2), and republican-civic (R3)—crossed with an institutional-lock principle (meaning is fixed by named parties, coalition roles, or explicit meta-reference) and modified by an asymmetry-of-negation rule (the implied opposite of “liberal” differs systematically by party system). Please translate this into a step-by-step procedure: the questions in strict order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the decision tree in words, the precise points where judgment must replace algorithm and be signed off by a senior editor, and the misfires the audit is prone to. Apply it to three worked cases: a German MEP stating, “We liberals must resist this welfare expansion”; an American senator described in copy as “the liberal from Vermont”; and a British columnist writing that “the liberal establishment has betrayed the working class” in a passage that mentions both deregulation and cultural elitism but names no party. Include one case where the procedure returns a classification the writer is likely to resist for narrative reasons, and explain how to handle that editorially. Then state clearly what the procedure cannot settle—deliberate ambiguity, neologism, hybrid formations, and speakers who reject institutional anchoring altogether—and note where it will silently fail. Write the answer in British English, with British spelling and punctuation conventions throughout.
