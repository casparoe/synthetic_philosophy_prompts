Hobsbawm and Anderson both think modern nations are basically recent inventions, but they highlight different mechanisms. In The Invention of Tradition, Hobsbawm shows that rituals and symbols that feel ancient—like Scottish Highland dress or royal pageantry—were actually fabricated in the 1800s by states that needed social cohesion. He argues that older local loyalties were breaking down, so governments deliberately invented traditions from scratch to replace them.

Anderson, in Imagined Communities, emphasizes print-capitalism and the decline of Latin, but he also gives us the census, the map, and the museum. These three institutions let the state classify people, fix borders, and curate a shared past. For Anderson, older ethnic and religious ties were mostly obstacles; the modern nation had to erase local dialects and particularism to create a standardized secular identity.

Take Italy. The Risorgimento turned Garibaldi into a secular saint almost immediately, and the Alpini corps was founded in 1861 with a deliberately archaic uniform to symbolize Alpine unity. Meanwhile the 1861 census and provincial mapping turned local populations into statistical "Italians." This shows Italian ethnicity wasn't a pre-existing foundation—it was manufactured ex nihilo by these Anderson-style institutions plus Hobsbawm-style rituals. So primordialists are wrong: both authors prove that old ethnic attachments are myths that modern nationalism replaces rather than repurposes.
