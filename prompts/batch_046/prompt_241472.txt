For a 16-person upper-division philosophy or political-theory workshop running 120 minutes, design a structured constitutional-convention simulation set in the fictional post-revolutionary republic of Meridia. The participants should come away feeling—not merely being told—that debates about same-sex marriage and marriage’s purpose involve a structural feature that gender-composition arguments usually overlook: marriage operates as a triadic status institution linking couple, state, and recognitional community (witnesses, kin, civic bodies), and any purpose clause assigned to marriage automatically generates a corresponding “normative residue,” a reshaped legal and social status for intimate relationships that fall outside the definition. The activity must make participants discover that resolving who may enter marriage does not eliminate this residue, and that attempts to “shield” outsiders by amendment are always partial and costly.

Divide the group into four factions of four, each with a brief values profile (traditionalist, revisionist egalitarian, pluralist, state-minimalist). Give each faction a blank “Marriage Article” with four mandatory fields: (1) Purpose Clause (what the state recognizes marriage as being for), (2) Entry Conditions (who may enter, with gender-composition left open for them to specify), (3) Exit Conditions (divorce rules and status termination), and (4) Recognition Protocol (who beyond the couple and state must witness or validate the union—religious bodies, civic councils, kin networks, or none). Give them 30 minutes to draft.

After drafting, apply a hidden mechanical rule: each Purpose Clause triggers a pre-set “Social Impact Deck” of three cards that assign concrete legal statuses to non-marital relationships in Meridia. For example, a “child-rearing” purpose draws cards that grant co-parents outside marriage only “provisional family” status with reduced inheritance and medical-decision rights; a “mutual companionship” purpose draws cards that allow registered close friends to access some spousal benefits, diluting marital privilege and altering tax structures for single households. Deal the cards face-up to all factions simultaneously so they see that their purpose choice has restructured life for people who never sought marriage.

Then run a 25-minute amendment round. Factions may propose “Shield Clauses” to protect outsiders—e.g., extending inheritance rights to non-marital cohabitants, or barring employers from discriminating against unregistered partners—but each clause costs Political Capital points (each faction starts with 10; costs range from 2 to 6 points depending on scope). Crucially, include two “impossible shields” on the hidden cost sheet: no clause can fully neutralize the normative residue generated by the Purpose Clause, and any shield that protects one outside group tends to impose a burden on another (e.g., extending benefits to friends reduces subsidies available to single parents). Factions must negotiate trades and vote on amendments with secret ballots.

End with a 15-minute “Constitutional Vote” in which each faction ranks all four articles (including its own) and calculates a “Residue Score” you provide via a simple formula: the intensity of the Purpose Clause multiplied by the number of unshielded outsider categories. The winning article is adopted, but all factions receive the Residue Score for every article, making visible that even the “most inclusive” gender-neutral article still produces a reshaped outside.

The debrief should map moments precisely: the drafting of the Recognition Protocol onto the triadic node that debates usually ignore; the card reveal onto the automatic generation of normative residue; the failed or expensive shields onto the irreducibility of that residue; and the final vote onto the discovery that inclusion and structural outside-status are independent problems. Predict what will go wrong the first time: groups will fixate on Entry Conditions (gender eligibility) and neglect the Purpose Clause; you must enforce a rule that fields 1 and 4 are filled before field 2 is discussed. Also predict that one faction will try to “solve” residue by adopting a minimalist state-neutral purpose—anticipate this and have its Impact Deck show that neutrality itself generates residue (all intimate relationships become contractual and lose default protections). Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: it cannot capture the historical embeddedness of actual marriage institutions or the lived emotional weight of exclusion experienced by real people; the facilitator must supply that framing in the opening narrative and in the debrief’s closing reflection, explicitly naming that the game reveals structure, not suffering. Include the timeline minute-by-minute, the materials list (role cards, blank articles, Impact Decks with pre-mapped clauses, Political Capital tokens, secret ballots), and a worked example of one Purpose Clause with its three dealt cards and one failed shield attempt. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the deliverable is the runnable activity with its mechanics and honest limits.
