Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between Outside-View Reference-Class Analysts and Inside-View Structural Historians on forecasting the longevity and decline of synthetic federations — political entities formed by treaty among previously sovereign units rather than by conquest or dynastic union. The statement should address whether the outside view (reference classes drawn from empires, confederations, and composite states; base-rate estimates of institutional lifespan; predictive weight assigned to structural analogues) and the inside view (institutional design, economic integration, path dependency, and the specific treaty mechanisms that bind member units) can be reconciled, partially reconciled, or shown to talk past one another.

Structure the response in four named sections, in this order, with approximate lengths as indicated:

1. Agreed Claims (around 300 words): Present as a numbered list the propositions both camps accept regarding synthetic federations, the limitations of historical reference classes, what requires explanation in forecasting institutional endurance, and the formal conditions an adequate forecast must meet. Include agreement on at least one concrete historical case — for example, the dissolution of the United Arab Republic or the persistence of the Swiss Confederation — that both sides treat as genuinely informative, without requiring the reader to consult specialised chronologies.

2. Residual Disagreements (around 250 words): Provide a table with two columns. The left column should state each disagreement using language the Outside-View camp would accept for its own position; the right column should state the corresponding disagreement using language the Inside-View camp would accept for its own. Cover at least: whether synthetic federations constitute a coherent reference class; whether base-rate estimates should be weighted by institutional-design similarity or by temporal proximity; and whether federation failure is typically endogenous (institutional erosion) or exogenous (geopolitical shock). Ensure neither formulation misrepresents the side it represents.

3. Cruxes and Predictive Tests (around 350 words): Identify three cruxes — sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp closer to the other. For each crux specify: (a) the concrete comparative case or historical evidence both camps agree is relevant; (b) what the Outside-View camp predicts that evidence demonstrates about the reference class; (c) what the Inside-View camp predicts; and (d) what outcome or interpretation both sides acknowledge would genuinely weaken one position. At least one crux should examine whether disputes over whether 'the same institution persists despite complete turnover of member units' are partly verbal rather than substantive.

4. Meta-Ontological Assessment (around 150 words): State clearly whether any portion of the disagreement between the camps is verbal — that is, reducible to ambiguity in terms such as 'institution', 'federation', or 'base rate' — giving the reasoning both sides accept for that judgement. If they agree part of the dispute is verbal, identify which part and explain why; if they agree it is not verbal, articulate the substantive point that remains.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure formalisms that require specialised mathematical training. Write so that every claim can be assessed by an informed reader without online search, though you may refer to well-known historical entities — such as the Holy Roman Empire, the League of Nations, or the German Confederation — without supplying exhaustive dates or archival references. Ensure that at least one agreed claim and one crux incorporate the idea that institutional memory decay in synthetic federations operates differently from that in conquest-formed empires, treating this not as an established fact but as a shared working hypothesis to be tested against the cases discussed.
