I'm preparing a feature on democratic design and the metaphors used to justify institutional rigidity, and I've been trying to untangle a conceptual knot that I suspect rests on a misunderstanding I haven't quite been able to name.

The literature on constitutional precommitment often invokes the Ulysses-contract image: a polity, anticipating its own future susceptibility to populist demagoguery or majoritarian panic, binds itself through entrenched provisions and supermajoritarian amendment rules, just as Ulysses had his crew tie him to the mast. But when I press the analogy, it seems to depend on a false model of collective agency across time. In rational-choice treatments, a Ulysses contract is coherent only if the present agent correctly anticipates that her future self will hold different — and in some respect mistaken — preferences, and if the binding mechanism operates with that future self's tacit consent. Transposed to the constitutional setting, this framing appears to require that the founding generation claim sustained epistemic authority over the normative commitments of citizens generations later, and that any subsequent democratic effort to amend the framework be interpreted not as legitimate collective revision but as the very manifestation of irrationality the original contract was meant to suppress. In other words, it treats an intergenerational collective as if it were a single agent suffering from time-inconsistent preferences, and then pathologizes democratic change as a failure of rational continuity.

Given all that, doesn't the Ulysses metaphor actually presuppose that future citizens are the "irrational future self" to the founders' "present self"? And if that is a category mistake — collapsing democratic succession into diachronic personal identity — then why do constitutional theorists continue to deploy the metaphor as a defense of rigidity rather than recognizing it as an argument that undermines the legitimacy of binding future generations? I feel certain I'm missing a distinction here, but I can't see where the analogy stops being ornamental and starts being a genuine model of intergenerational binding.
