After a long argument with a friend about Buddhist anattā and its relevance to Western personal-identity debates, he summarized the doctrine for me as follows: "Buddhism denies the self by reducing the person to the five skandhas—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—so there is no enduring substrate, only a stream of impermanent aggregates. This is essentially Hume's bundle theory with religious packaging: the self is just a useful fiction constructed from successive mental states. But Buddhism also insists on karma and rebirth across lives, which requires some continuous bearer to inherit the karmic results; without a self, there is nothing that gets reborn, so the doctrine collapses into incoherence. Therefore anattā either abandons rebirth or secretly reintroduces a subtle permanent self—the so-called pudgala—to do the metaphysical work." He concluded that Western philosophers should treat anattā as either naive reductionism or self-defeating mysticism, not a serious rival to Lockean psychological-continuity theories. I suspect he is flattening distinctions—perhaps between Abhidharma and Madhyamaka, or between conventional and ultimate truth—but I am not sure exactly where the summary slips. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what captures mainstream Buddhist doctrine, what is a common oversimplification imported from Western philosophy, what misrepresents the internal Buddhist response to the rebirth objection, and whether his charge of incoherence actually follows from the accurate parts. In particular, address whether framing anattā primarily as a metaphysical claim about the absence of substance already misses something essential about the view.
