I have been attempting to apply an outside-view approach, selecting reference classes for comparison, but I notice that I oscillate between two different axes of classification without any clear rule for which should take precedence: one axis groups institutions by functional role, meaning what economic or social purpose they serve, and the other groups them by organizational morphology, meaning how authority, risk, and membership are structurally distributed. These two axes often point in opposite directions, and my confidence in a forecast seems to depend on which one I happen to invoke first.

Here are my concrete verdicts, and I want to know whether there is a single principle behind them or whether I am merely rationalizing intuition. First, if in the year 400 one had tried to forecast the dissolution of the Western Roman administrative apparatus within the next century by using the reference class of large territorial empires with multi-ethnic professional armies, I believe the base rate should have yielded moderate confidence in eventual decline but not in rapid, fragmented collapse; the actual speed still feels like an outlier that the reference class alone should not have predicted with high precision, yet I am unsure whether that feeling is justified or just hindsight bias. Second, forecasting the sudden end of Soviet central planning in 1988 using only the reference class of single-party Marxist-Leninist states should, I think, have produced a very low prior for peaceful rapid dissolution, since none in that class had previously collapsed that way, but I also feel that adding the morphological feature of ethnically defined federal republics should have raised the probability substantially, though I cannot specify the quantitative adjustment and wonder if I am smuggling in knowledge of the actual outcome. Third, predicting that the European Union will disintegrate within two decades using the reference class of customs unions and loose confederal leagues feels intuitively wrong to me despite the poor historical base rate for such arrangements, because the density of legal, fiscal, and regulatory interdependence seems to break the analogy, yet I worry this intuition is nothing more than familiarity bias rather than a legitimate structural update. Fourth, when I consider whether a dominant social networking platform will survive thirty years, using the reference class of communication protocols versus the reference class of branded consumer ecosystems produces radically different forecasts, and I feel strongly that the morphological classification should dominate, but I have no principled reason for that preference. Fifth, and perhaps most troubling, I think that forecasting climate-driven state failure for a small island federation should rely on reference classes of past environmental migrations and territorial abandonment, yielding a high risk estimate, but when I apply what I take to be the same functional reference class—states facing gradual, irreversible environmental degradation—to a large continental federal system, I feel the forecast should be much lower, not because the empirical base rate differs but because I implicitly weight morphological redundancy and geographic dispersal differently, and I cannot tell whether this weighting is coherent.

What I need is a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures how I am actually weighing these reference classes, together with an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot save without ad hoc complexity. Specifically, does the principle distinguish formally between functional and morphological similarity, and if they conflict, which takes precedence and under what conditions? How should it treat the case I keep returning to—the cooperative banking federation, which functionally resembles a modern credit union but morphologically resembles the mutual savings networks of the 1930s that collapsed in regional waves—should I forecast its twenty-year survival by the functional class, the morphological class, or by some interaction rule involving both?
