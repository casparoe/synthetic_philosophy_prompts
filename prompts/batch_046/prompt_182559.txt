I'm a doctoral student in philosophy specializing in moral psychology and metaethics, and I'm trying to work through a disagreement that arose in our department's reading group on cross-cultural variation in folk morality and its implications for moral objectivity. Two of my colleagues—let's call them Maya and Julian—have been arguing over how to interpret the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence in moral cognition, particularly the finding that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations weight individualizing foundations like harm and fairness far more heavily than binding foundations like loyalty, authority, and purity, relative to many non-WEIRD societies.

Maya takes this divergence as strong evidence against moral objectivity. She argues that if moral judgments tracked mind-independent facts, we should expect convergence comparable to what we see in basic perceptual or scientific domains, rather than the kind of systematic, culturally clustered variation we actually observe. She proposes what she calls the "indexical calibration thesis": the idea that even apparently universal moral responses are implicitly indexed to local cooperative frames, so that a judgment like "this distribution is unfair" does not assert an objective property of the world but rather signals a deviation from the locally dominant coordination equilibrium. On this view, moral disagreement between cultures is not a case of one side being correct and the other mistaken, but rather a clash between differently calibrated social technologies. Julian objects that this conflates moral ontology with moral epistemology. He grants that cultural transmission explains variation in explicit moral codes, but insists that deep structural features—such as reciprocity norms and harm aversion—function like parameter settings in a universal moral architecture rather than like arbitrary conventions. He further argues that Maya's thesis cannot account for cross-cultural moral learning, in which individuals genuinely revise their beliefs upon exposure to other cultures rather than simply switching coordination frames. Maya responds that Julian's analogy to a universal moral grammar illicitly imports Chomskyan nativism into ethics without the corresponding empirical support—moral development, she notes, lacks the poverty-of-the-stimulus features that motivate linguistic nativism. Julian concedes the disanalogy but presses that the burden remains on the relativist to explain why moral disagreement feels like error rather than mere difference, something he claims the calibration thesis fails to do without circularity.

Specifically address whether the indexical calibration thesis genuinely undermines objectivity or merely relocates it, whether the universal-grammar analogy is defensible in this domain despite the poverty-of-stimulus objection, and whether Julian's phenomenological appeal to the experience of moral error succeeds against Maya's coordination-based framework.
