At a philosophy discussion group, two members—Mara and Jules—argued about lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and whether suffering and pleasure are genuinely incommensurable.

(1) Mara: The view is incoherent. To say suffering takes lexical priority over pleasure is to rank them, and ranking requires comparability; if the two are truly incommensurable, there is no scale on which one can dominate the other, not even lexically.

(2) Jules: Incommensurability blocks aggregation, not ordering. Health and artistic achievement may lack a shared metric, yet saving a life rationally takes precedence over funding a gallery without any hidden translation of values.

(3) Mara: That analogy conceals the problem. Both options are evaluated against a single background value—human flourishing—whereas lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism applies both suffering and pleasure within one decision procedure and orders them, which just is to place them on an ordinal scale.

(4) Jules: Lexical ordering is a non-metric ordering, like dictionary ordering: once suffering falls below an empirical threshold—say, the absence of deprivation severe enough to undermine agency—the evaluative framework shifts. No translation of suffering-units into pleasure-units is required.

(5) Mara: Then explain the boundary without smuggling in comparison. To know we have reached that absence, and to decide whether to stay on the suffering-reduction side—curing a headache versus funding a lifetime of art—one must still weigh across the boundary; if the values are genuinely incommensurable, there is no reason either to stay or to cross.

(6) Jules: The boundary is fixed by the internal logic of suffering itself, not by cross-domain weighing. Once the kind of distress that undermines agency is absent, the moral urgency of suffering is exhausted. Demanding a continuous metric is precisely the aggregative mistake the view was built to reject.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both sides miss—especially whether lexical ordering genuinely avoids commensurability or merely disguises a hidden comparison.
