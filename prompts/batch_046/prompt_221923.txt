I am designing a ninety-minute seminar session for first-year doctoral students in history who have completed introductory coursework in both European and East Asian historiography but have not yet taken a dedicated global-history methods course. The session aims to teach one specific conceptual problem: whether the past can be narrated from everywhere at once without covertly reinstating a Eurocentric backbone. I want the students to see that simply expanding the archive—adding Chinese, Indian, or West African sources to a narrative still structured by Atlantic revolutions or the long nineteenth century—does not by itself decenter the frame; rather, the deeper issue is chronological and causal incommensurability, in which different regions operate with distinct periodization schemes and causal densities that resist integration into a single diachronic line.

Please provide a teaching plan rather than a full lecture script. First, specify the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced and justify the dependency structure: for instance, why the distinction between archival pluralism and chronological decentering must come before any discussion of narrative coherence, and where the problem of asynchronous modernities should enter relative to the critique of universal stage theories. Second, identify the single concrete comparative case that should carry the idea—this case must contain genuinely incommensurable timelines and causal structures, yet still allow some limited comparison; specify what the case must include and what it must deliberately lack, particularly avoiding scenarios in which one region clearly dominates the causal chain. Third, map the misconceptions students predictably bring or form: at minimum, address the assumption that global history is merely additive, the confusion between moral inclusion and structural decentering, and the belief that incommensurability makes comparison impossible rather than merely difficult. For each misconception, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Fourth, propose a brief check for understanding that would demonstrate the idea has landed—not a quiz, but a targeted task or question that reveals whether students grasp the dependency structure rather than memorizing slogans. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—particular named theoretical frameworks, technical apparatus, or highly specialized historiographical disputes—and say so openly. Finally, say how confident you are in the pedagogical sequencing you propose and single out the one point—whether in the dependency order, the choice of carrying case, or the misconception map—about which you are least certain.
