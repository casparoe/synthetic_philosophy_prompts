I'm trying to design a workshop activity for my philosophy of distributive justice seminar — about 13 people, 90 minutes, mostly grad students but a couple undergrads too. The topic is prioritarianism and that leveling-down objection where egalitarians seem forced to say it's good to make everyone equally miserable. I need this because my mom was just diagnosed with something long-term and I've been sitting in on these family care-planning meetings where they talk about "fair allocation" like it's just arithmetic, and it made me realize I don't actually know what I think about whether equality itself matters or just helping the worst off. So I want the students to feel the tension in their bones before anyone names it.

Design a runnable game or structured deliberation — no lectures — where the group acts as a community aid board distributing limited resources among four case profiles with very different baseline welfare (say 2/10, 4/10, 7/10, 9/10). The rules must include: (1) a prioritarian option that gives everything to the worst off without touching the best off; (2) an egalitarian option that achieves perfect equality by reducing the top person's resources down to match the bottom with no net gain to anyone (pure leveling down); and (3) a hidden "sufficientarian" twist where one profile reports high subjective satisfaction despite low objective resources due to adaptive preferences, so they have to vote on whether priority tracks objective or subjective welfare. Use roles, a clear timeline (maybe 20 min deliberation, 10 min vote, rest debrief), and a payoff structure that doesn't use real money — maybe the board's decision determines which case study the class spends the second half analyzing, plus token "reputation points" that affect presentation order. Make the mechanics do the work: they should want to choose equality for its own sake and then feel weird about destroying resources to get it.

In the debrief, map the vote patterns onto prioritarian weighting vs intrinsic equality vs sufficientarian thresholds. Be honest about which feature of prioritarianism the activity can't reproduce — probably the interpersonal comparison problem or the continuous weighting function? — and say what you have to supply verbally. Also predict what will definatly go wrong the first run (maybe they ignore the adaptive-preference case, or they vote for leveling down just to feel the symmetry?). And please, tell me how confident you are in this design and single out the point you're least sure about — I think it's the payoff scheme, but I'm not sure wich part will actually motivate them. This needs to work without anyone looking up citations or bibliographies — just the idea in the rules.
