I'm running an ethics of mind seminar this term — 13 grad students, 90 minutes, no prereading needed. I want them to actually feel the scale argument about insects, not just understand it on paper. You know the move: assign a beetle like a 0.1% credance of sentience, multiply by 10^18, and suddenly the expected suffering dwarfs anything we do for humans. I want the discomfort of that multiplication to hit them through action, not lecture.

Design a runnable in-class game. Call it Allocation Lottery if you want. Here is the skeleton I have in mind, but I need you to flesh out rules, materials, timeline, and debrief.

Setting: seminar table, 13 people. Materials: sealed envelopes with patient profiles — one human mental-health program (high credence, ~2 million affectd) and one agricultural pesticide reduction (low credence, ~10^18 insects). Before opening anything, each student makes a blind preliminary budget vote: 100 points split between the two. Only then do they open envelopes, see the numbers, do the expected-value calculation together, and defend whether they revise their split.

Payoff scheme: no real money. Instead, they earn coherence points — after the reveal, each student gives a 60-second public defense of their revised (or unrevised) allocation, and the group anonymously ranks whose reasoning was sharpest. The winner picks the reading for next week. So there's social stakes but no cash.

Timeline I imagine: 10 min rules, 15 min blind vote, 20 min reveal and math, 25 min defenses and ranking, 15 min debrief, 5 min buffer.

The debrief must map specific moments onto the formal argument: (1) the pre-opening intuition, (2) the multiplication step, (3) the comparison that makes human priority look arbitrary. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce — probably the qualitative gap in suffering intensity, and that real-world credences aren't neat point values. And tell me what will go wrong: I'm sure some students will laugh off the beetle envelope before opening it, and I need a concrete rule or framing that stops that mockery cold.

Also, don't ask them to cite anyone or read ahead. This needs to work cold for people who haven't seen this argument before.
