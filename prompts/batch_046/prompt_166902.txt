I am currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on philosophy of science, and I have encountered a practical decision at my part-time work that seems to reproduce a conceptual puzzle from our readings—though I cannot quite name it, and I need to know whether my reasoning is sound. For the past six years I have assisted a small cooperative that produces a traditional fermented infusion using a continuous culture maintained since 2019 and a fixed protocol: water heated to precisely 82°C, a specific regional wildflower honey, and a thirty-day anaerobic cycle in ceramic vessels. Across every batch we have produced—roughly forty iterations—those prepared under these conditions have yielded an identical sensory profile: a deep amber hue, a pronounced acidic sharpness on the palate, and a sediment pattern that settles into a distinctive radial, star-like formation. Because the protocol and the culture have never changed, we have always treated this profile as a stable biochemical signature, not as a coincidence.

We are now preparing to initiate a batch that will begin its fermentation cycle on January 3, 2026—the first time we will cross that specific calendar boundary since we started tracking outcomes. A senior member of the collective insists that we should commit resources expecting the same amber, sharp, star-sediment result, because none of the causal conditions have altered. I understand her point, but I find myself unable to dismiss an alternative regularity that seems equally compatible with our entire observational history: that batches are amber-sharp-star-shaped if prepared before January 1, 2026, and—regardless of protocol—assume an entirely different character (perhaps a muted copper tone with a sweet, flat finish) if prepared after that date. We have never observed the post-2026 case, of course, but we had never observed any case before 2019 either; the data underdetermine both projections.

She dismisses my counter-possibility as a contrived time-bomb predicate, gerrymandered solely to undermine our confidence. Yet I struggle to articulate why her biochemical projection is epistemically privileged without simply assuming the very uniformity I am trying to defend. So my question is concrete: should I advise the collective to proceed with the expectation of our traditional profile, and if so, on what grounds can I reject my time-dependent alternative without begging the question? Or is my hesitation merely sophistry, and I should treat the calendar boundary as irrelevant in exactly the way she suggests?
