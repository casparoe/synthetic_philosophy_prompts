I'm preparing a brief seminar contribution on consciousness and find the standard split-brain literature somewhat constrained by its reliance on verbal and manual report. Could you design a thought experiment—no longer than a single paragraph in its narrative form—that engineers a modified commissurotomy case in which emotional integration, rather than linguistic or motor divergence, serves as the isolating variable?

The specifications are these. Present stimuli simultaneously to the left visual field (right hemisphere) and the right visual field (left hemisphere): one conveys information that, taken alone, warrants distress, the other information that, taken alone, warrants reassurance. The patient must respond through a graded, non-verbal emotional indicator—such as a calibrated facial expression or galvanic skin response—rather than through speech or manual pointing. The observable behavioural output should be ambiguous or intermediate, so that both a unified global-workspace account and a divided-streams hypothesis predict the same emotional indicator. Nevertheless, the two theories must diverge ontologically: the unified view should entail one subject of experience with a single, conflicted emotional state integrating both inputs, whereas the divided-streams view should entail two discrete subjects, each with its own emotional evaluation, with the ambiguity arising from subcortical competition or averaging rather than from unified consciousness.

Please present the scenario, then demonstrate that it satisfies each specification—giving the reasoning for both theories, identifying the precise structural feature that forces their separation, and explaining what happens if that feature is removed (for instance, if the response modality is switched to verbal report, collapsing the case into the familiar split-brain paradigm). Finally, note how a defender of unified consciousness might attack the scenario, particularly by disputing whether non-verbal emotional indicators are legitimate evidence for separate streams of experience. Keep the neuroscience mainstream—no exotic physics, no named historical patients, and no reliance on theoretical apparatus beyond basic familiarity with the corpus callosum—so that the case remains accessible to students encountering these debates for the first time.
