I serve on a municipal technology-ethics review board, and our published precedents include the following rulings: we blocked a city-wide deployment of mood-responsive street lighting that adjusted color temperature based on aggregated pedestrian biometric signals drawn from existing security-camera feeds, citing unknown psychological effects and the involuntary nature of public-space exposure; we approved—subject to explicit informed consent and immediate debriefing—a clinical trial of a nootropic compound suspected of altering emotional-memory consolidation, on the grounds that participation was voluntary and the effects were expected to be temporary; we mandated a phased, opt-in pilot for an AI tutoring system that adapted pedagogy using real-time facial-expression analysis, before any mandatory adoption, citing unknown risks to student self-esteem and autonomy; we rejected a proposal to disperse trace amounts of an untested pheromone analog through office HVAC systems to boost productivity, even though exposure levels were minimal and likely harmless, because the intervention was covert and targeted a captive population; and we prohibited a newborn genetic-screening program for non-medical traits—such as predicted athletic potential—arguing that the resulting information could inflict irreversible social harm regardless of biological impact.

A new proposal is now before us. The public library system wishes to install attention-guidance shelving in all branches: books are arranged and illuminated according to real-time eye-tracking of patrons via ceiling-mounted sensors, in order to increase circulation of under-represented genres. Individual tracking data are not retained, but the algorithm's long-term influence on the formation of literary taste and on autonomous choice is unknown. Patrons are not notified of the system, though they may request that staff disable it for their visit. The board must render a decision.

Assuming we wish to remain consistent with our own record, what verdict should we reach?
