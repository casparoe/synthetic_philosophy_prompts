At a graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, two doctoral students—Maya and Leo—fell into a sustained disagreement over how to read the Mencius-Xunzi debate on human nature, specifically whether the dispute is genuinely about moral psychology or about the conditions under which morality becomes possible. I was present for the exchange, did not take sides, and still cannot see how to settle it. Maya began by arguing that Mencius’s doctrine of the four sprouts (siduan) is routinely misread as a claim that human beings are born with fully formed moral virtues; in her view, Mencius only asserts that we possess innate affective precursors—spontaneous compassion at the sight of a child falling into a well, an innate sense of shame and dislike, deference, and a basic capacity to approve and disapprove—which remain latent unless cultivated through ritual and education. She insisted that this makes Mencius closer to a developmental interactionist than to a naive nativist: the sprouts are not good in themselves, but they are genuinely other-regarding and provide the necessary psychological material for virtue. Leo countered that this interpretation domesticates Mencius to the point of incoherence; if the sprouts are merely proto-social preferences that require extensive cultural scaffolding to become moral, then Mencius is not really claiming that human nature is good (xing shan) in any meaningful sense, but only that humans are socially plastic animals. Leo then defended Xunzi’s position that human nature is structurally oriented toward self-interest—seeking profit, desiring sensory gratification, and resenting harm—not as a moral condemnation but as a descriptive claim about unmediated desire. He argued that Xunzi’s analogy of human nature being like crooked wood that must be straightened by the carpenter’s tools (ritual and propriety) is not a claim that people are evil in essence, but that innate tendencies, left to themselves, generate conflict and disorder. Maya responded that Leo was importing anachronistic frameworks—behavioral economics, present-bias models—onto Xunzi, and that Xunzi’s text explicitly describes innate human tendencies as actively destructive rather than merely underdeveloped; she pointed to Xunzi’s claim that people are born with a love of profit and a hatred of harm that, without ritual, leads them to contend and steal. Leo replied that Maya was herself anachronistic in reading evolutionary-psychology-style domain-specific moral modules into Mencius’s child-by-the-well example, and that the example demonstrates nothing more than an affective reflex or kin-selection analog rather than an innate moral virtue. He concluded that the core disagreement is not about whether humans have some biological predisposition toward social behavior, but about whether morality is discovered within human nature (Mencius) or constructed upon it through artificial institutions (Xunzi), and that Maya’s middle position collapses this distinction by treating the sprouts as both innate and insufficient without reducing either claim to triviality.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point: who is right about the interpretation of the four sprouts, about whether Xunzi’s xing e is a descriptive or normative claim, about whether modern analogies illuminate or distort the classical texts, and about what, if anything, both Maya and Leo are missing in framing the dispute this way.
