At an interdisciplinary seminar on computation and cognition, two participants—Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of science, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a computational neuroscientist—debated how to interpret von Neumann's comparison of the brain and digital computers in his posthumous lectures, later collected in The Computer and the Brain. Vasquez maintained that von Neumann was advancing a thesis of principled incommensurability: the brain computes through analog, statistically redundant, low-precision pulses, distributes errors so that no single failure is catastrophic, and operates with an energy efficiency that reflects a fundamentally different logical architecture from the high-precision, brittle, digital machines built in the mid-twentieth century. She argued that when von Neumann observed that the brain's descriptive language differs from mathematics, he meant that neural computation cannot be captured by the formal logic underlying digital computers, and therefore that scaling up digital hardware could never, in principle, replicate human cognition. Chen replied that von Neumann was describing contingent engineering constraints—speed, reliability mechanisms, and representational notation—not logical ones. He pointed out that von Neumann calculated the brain's information capacity and found it comparable to large digital systems, and that von Neumann's discussion of error tolerance merely identified a design problem that digital error-correction codes could solve. Chen concluded that the comparison supports functional equivalence across substrates, with differences being implementation details. Vasquez then introduced a modern complication: large language models now perform complex reasoning through statistical pattern matching on digital hardware rather than through formal deduction. She claimed this partly vindicates von Neumann's idea that intelligence can rest on a non-mathematical descriptive language, but insisted it does not vindicate Chen's functional-equivalence view, because these systems remain brittle outside their training distribution in precisely the way von Neumann thought brains are not. Chen responded that this actually supports his reading: if statistical architectures on digital substrates can approximate brain-like flexibility, then von Neumann's concerns have been addressed by architectural innovation rather than substrate change, confirming that the brain-computer gap was always about engineering, not principle. They turned to me, asking for a point-by-point adjudication of who is right about what, and whether either is missing something crucial about the relationship between substrate, architecture, and logical form in von Neumann's argument. In particular, address whether von Neumann's remarks about distributed error tolerance and about the brain's descriptive language support an incommensurability thesis or an implementation thesis. Finally, state clearly what argument or evidence—whether a closer reading of the lectures, an empirical result in neuroscience, or a conceptual distinction—would cause you to change your verdict, and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
