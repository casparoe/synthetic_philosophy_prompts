A philosophy graduate student friend summarized Grice's account of implicature and speaker meaning for me in roughly these terms: Grice holds that rational conversation is governed by a Cooperative Principle, not as a moral rule but as a condition of successful coordination, and from it he derives four maxims—Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner—that regulate explicit content much as grammar regulates syntax. When a speaker flouts one of these maxims, the audience detects the breach and calculates an implicature that restores coherence; so if someone says "Some of my students passed" when in fact all did, the Quantity maxim is violated and the audience infers that not all passed. My friend insisted that, for Grice, an implicature only counts as present if both speaker and hearer actually share recognition of the specific maxim breach—if the hearer misses it entirely, there is no implicature, only a failure to understand. He drew a stronger conclusion from this: because speaker meaning depends on this intersubjective recognition, Grice's theory allows no genuinely private intentions that fail to reach the audience, and therefore it cannot explain strategic silence in negotiation, where one party deliberately withholds information precisely so that the other does not recognize any maxim violation, which on his reading means the silence communicates nothing at all. I suspect he has collapsed Grice's intention-based framework into a purely public one and has misapplied the maxim structure to strategic contexts, but I am not sure where the line falls between fair simplification and misrepresentation. Is this summary accurate? Please separate what it gets right about Grice's actual view, what is a defensible popularization, and what distorts his position—especially regarding whether speaker intentions can exist without audience uptake, whether every implicature requires a recognized maxim breach, and whether strategic silence genuinely falls outside the theory's scope. I also want to know whether his conclusion about negotiation follows from the accurate parts or collapses once the errors are corrected.
