I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of technology and normative ethics, and after a long series of discussions with colleagues in longtermist circles I have settled on what feels like a somewhat heterodox but defensible view: that Bostrom-style simulation arguments should not be treated as a mere epistemic curiosity, but should actively dampen what I have come to call far-future fanaticism—the tendency to assign overwhelming moral weight to astronomically distant futures on the basis of high-confidence expected-value reasoning. My argument is not simply that being simulated might reduce our causal efficacy in base reality, but that the argument introduces what I term a recursive substrate-indifference problem: if we allow that our ontology might be nested inside a simulation whose creators are themselves possibly simulated, then we lack any non-circular criterion for identifying which substrate level is the correct one for moral accounting, and the confidence with which we project value across cosmic timescales collapses. I also hold that the argument undermines the probabilistic premises of longtermist calculus—specifically, the assumption that we can assign stable prior probabilities to our being in base reality versus in a simulation—without falling into a skeptical paralysis that would equally undermine near-term ethical obligations.

Still, I want to subject this position to rigorous stress-testing rather than insulating it. What would genuinely count against it? I am not asking for generic dismissals of simulation hypotheses, nor for standard defenses of longtermism. Rather, I want to know which specific empirical findings, formal results in decision theory, or philosophical arguments about substrate-independent value or moral patienthood would have to obtain for my dampening thesis to fail. For instance, would a rigorous proof that moral weight is fully substrate-invariant restore the longtermist calculus regardless of nesting? Would empirical or theoretical evidence that simulated civilizations cannot achieve the computational density required for large-scale ancestor simulations undermine the prior probabilities on which my argument rests? Or might there be a decision-theoretic framework—perhaps one involving indexical uncertainty across worlds or causal decision theory applied to nested substrates—that allows longtermists to absorb simulation risk without reducing the expected value of far-future actions?

Please rank the potential defeaters by decisiveness: those that would require me to abandon the position entirely, those that would force substantial qualification, and those that are merely awkward. Be explicit about whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by retreating to the claim that simulation risk is just one more uncertainty factor to be multiplied into an expected-value calculation rather than a structural defeater for far-future reasoning—and whether such a retreat is legitimate for a view that claims to be normatively consequential rather than merely skeptical. I want an honest assessment of how exposed the dampening thesis actually is, including the points at which its defenders must concede ground.
