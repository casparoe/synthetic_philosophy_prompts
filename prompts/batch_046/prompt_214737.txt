I'm taking a seminar on conceptual categories and design cognition, and last week my professor—who works on letterform recognition and has been thinking in the tradition of Letter Spirit—walked us through a series of judgments about whether various distorted or stylized shapes count as instances of the lowercase letter 'a'. Her rulings so far: the classic Roman double-story serif form is an 'a'; the geometric single-story circular form found in faces like Futura is an 'a'; a handwritten version with an open top, essentially a 'u' with a tail extending upward on the right, is still an 'a'; a perfect circle with a straight vertical line placed near its right edge but not actually touching or connecting to it is not an 'a'; a form with a triangular bowl and a curved stem, maintaining the usual proportions but built entirely from straight lines, is an 'a'; and a lowercase 'e' that has an extra ascending stroke added to its right side is not an 'a'.

Next week she will consider a new case. The form is structurally perfect by any geometric account: a fully enclosed counter with a right stem extending clearly above the x-height, placed in the word "cat" where an 'a' is expected. But every other letter in the surrounding font is executed in an ornate blackletter calligraphic style with thick-and-thin contrast, while this particular shape is rendered with mechanically uniform stroke width and perfectly straight edges that match no other glyph in the family. Predict her verdict, identify at least two distinct rules that are consistent with her earlier rulings but diverge on this case—say, one focused on topological or structural features and one focused on stylistic coherence or the "spirit" of the letterform family—give your best guess about which rule she is actually running with reasons, and name one further form that would best tell the two rules apart. Please separate what is settled about letterform categorization from what is genuinely contested among people who study typographic theory and recognition, and do not present a contested theoretical commitment as if it were the consensus.
