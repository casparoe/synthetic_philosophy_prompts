I have come to hold the view that free will is not the antithesis of sphexishness—the mechanical, subroutine-driven rigidity characteristic of instinct-bound behavior—but rather a specific architectural arrangement of it: a hierarchically nested sphexishness in which locally rigid, deterministic subroutines are encapsulated within flexible meta-routines capable of suspending, revising, or recombining them. On this account, freedom consists not in global flexibility but in the structured insulation of local determinism, preventing rigidity at lower levels from cascading into determinism at the level of the agent as a whole. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position. Beyond the familiar determinist objection that meta-routines are themselves sphex-like, identify which empirical findings in cognitive architecture, which thought experiments about modular minds or split-brain cases, and which formal results about hierarchical control or emergence could undermine it. In particular, indicate which of these defeaters a defender of nested sphexishness would be forced to concede as damaging, and which they could absorb by refining the insulation mechanism. Rank the challenges from fatal—those that would require abandoning the core claim—to merely awkward—those that demand only auxiliary adjustments—and note whether any cluster of objections risks collapsing the view into either pure determinism or an unstructured libertarianism it explicitly rejects.
