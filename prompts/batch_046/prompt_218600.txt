In rationalist forums we usually expect that once a paradox is formalized with precision, disagreement collapses to an identifiable crux; the Fitch knowability paradox has resisted that treatment for decades. The derivation is canonical—if every truth is in principle knowable, then every truth is actually known—yet the standard responses have ossified into camps that appear to talk past one another: those who reject the factivity of knowledge, those who restrict the knowability operator to block omniscience, those who deny the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, and those who dismiss the modal step as an illegitimate mixing of object-language and meta-language. Despite this formal transparency, the literature shows little convergence, which suggests the stalemate is not about logic per se but about what the logic is being asked to model. I want a structural diagnosis rather than another defense of one camp. Is the split verbal, turning on whether "knowable" denotes a property of propositions, a modal relation between agents and worlds, or a normative standard of epistemic accessibility? Is there a hidden empirical claim about bounded human cognition that renders omniscience implausible for one side and conceptually irrelevant for the other? Or are the camps actually addressing different questions—one about logical possibility, one about agent-level feasibility, one about epistemic modesty—under a single formal schema? Please map what each position is genuinely defending, identify the buried crux that should resolve the dispute but consistently fails to move anyone, and assess whether progress requires a conceptual distinction we have not yet drawn—perhaps between propositional accessibility and agent-level feasibility—or whether the paradox reveals that "all truths are knowable" was never a single thesis to begin with, and the debate is stalled precisely because it is several debates in disguise.
