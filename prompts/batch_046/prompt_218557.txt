I have recently been reflecting on the antinatalist position associated with David Benatar, particularly his asymmetry between pleasure and pain: that suffering is bad for those who exist, and its absence is good for those who do not; yet pleasure is good for those who exist, while its absence is not bad for those who do not exist. I find the reasoning elegant, but I struggle to see how far its implications extend beyond the ordinary ethics of having children. To push the idea further, I have been wondering about a scenario that feels somewhat futuristic but not impossible: suppose that within the next century we develop the capacity to generate fully conscious, sentient beings within controlled virtual environments — beings whose lives we could design with near-perfect precision, ensuring either a steady state of mild satisfaction punctuated by brief discomfort, or a life of unremitting but not extreme suffering.

Given this possibility, I would like you to describe the most important implications of Benatar's asymmetry for the ethics of deliberately bringing such artificial or virtual conscious entities into existence. Does the asymmetry entail that we ought never to create them, no matter how pleasant their designed lives might be, because any risk of suffering outweighs the absence of good that non-creation avoids? Or does the framework allow for a threshold — perhaps a life so overwhelmingly positive that creation becomes permissible? Furthermore, please address whether there is a morally relevant difference, within Benatar's logic, between failing to create a being who might have enjoyed existence and actively preventing such a being from coming into being, especially when the potential beings are not human and lack any prior claim to existence. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, but rather a careful working-out of what the asymmetry itself demands in this somewhat unusual context.
