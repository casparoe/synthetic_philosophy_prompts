I'm a senior curator writing permanent gallery labels for a museum exhibition on scientific institutions, 1600–1900, and I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered tests with explicit stopping rules—drawn from anti-Whig historiography. The procedure must tell me when I may describe an institutional change as oriented toward modern practice and when I must frame it only as a local problem-solving effort, operating under these constraints: thirty minutes per label, no consulting historian available, visitors with no specialist background, and an institutional mandate not to portray past actors as deficient drafts of ourselves. Structure it around three specific failure modes I must catch: teleological contamination (reading later outcomes back into actors' motives), anachronistic projection (applying modern categories such as peer review or controlled trial to practices that served different functions), and inevitability bias (treating contingent institutional paths as necessary). For each test, state the judgment call that cannot be eliminated, the specific misfire mode, and what to do when information is missing. Then apply the full procedure to three worked cases: a seventeenth-century correspondence network that eventually enabled journal publication; a nineteenth-century clinical comparison protocol driven by local administrative needs rather than by generalizable knowledge; and an institutional reform that looks like progress in hindsight but failed in its own terms and was abandoned. In the applications, show what the procedure permits, what it forbids, where it produces an answer I will not want to publish, and where it remains genuinely ambiguous. End by stating exactly what the procedure cannot do, regardless of how carefully it is run.
