I keep noticing a particular argumentative pattern in debates about who should count as part of the democratic community—the boundary problem, in other words—and I am not sure whether it has a standard name or is just an instance of something more general.

Here are two concrete cases. First, someone argues that anyone who will live with the long-term consequences of a decision should have a say in it, using this to justify extending voting rights to teenagers or long-term residents who are not citizens. But when you point out that the same criterion would include people in neighboring countries affected by cross-border pollution, or nonhuman animals affected by environmental policy, they retreat to an unstated condition—something like being part of the relevant social cooperation—without explaining why that condition does not also exclude the teenagers they wanted to include. Second, in discussions of workplace democracy, an advocate says all people who contribute labor to an enterprise should have governance rights, invoking this to include contractors. But when asked about one-time customers whose purchases also contribute value, they invoke ongoing dependency on the organization as a hidden filter, even though some of the contractors they want to include are more transient than some of those customers.

The pattern seems to be this: advancing a broad, apparently principled criterion for membership in the demos to justify including one specific group, then relying on an unacknowledged, narrower restriction to block the inclusion of another group that clearly satisfies the broad criterion—while continuing to treat the broad criterion as the real justification for the first inclusion. It is not exactly special pleading, because the speaker is not asking for an exception to a general rule; the broad rule and the hidden restriction are both presented, or at least implied, as part of the same position, but only the broad part gets defended.

Is there an established concept in democratic theory—perhaps within discussions of the boundary problem or the all-affected interests principle—that captures this specific two-step structure? If not, can you coin and define a term that distinguishes it from plain inconsistency, from motte-and-bailey, and from ad hoc qualification? I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are, so please do not stop at a bare label.
