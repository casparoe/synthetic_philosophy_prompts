Consider the epistemic problem of forecasting the remaining institutional lifespan of a large, territorially dispersed, resource-dependent empire using strictly the outside view—that is, through reference-class forecasting and base-rate reasoning, while deliberately excluding inside-view narratives about current leadership, cultural exceptionalism, or contingent geopolitical events. Rank the following five proposed reference classes in order of predictive reliability for this task, placing each into one of three tiers—High, Moderate, or Low—and providing a brief, one-sentence justification for its placement. The classes are: (i) all agrarian and industrial empires that exceeded approximately twenty million square kilometers at their territorial peak; (ii) all federated or composite states that dissolved primarily through legal-constitutional secession mechanisms rather than through external conquest or internal revolution; (iii) all hegemonic powers that experienced concurrent fiscal crisis and ecological stress—defined as sustained resource depletion combined with sovereign debt default—within a single thirty-year window; (iv) all multi-ethnic polities governed through a hybrid regime combining direct bureaucratic administration with indirect tributary or client-state arrangements; and (v) the undifferentiated base rate of sovereign-state failure calculated across all internationally recognized states from the early modern period onward, regardless of scale, regime type, or economic structure. In your evaluation, attend specifically to the methodological tension between reference-class specificity and statistical power: a narrowly tailored class may capture structural homologies—such as administrative heterogeneity or fiscal overextension—that genuinely drive collapse, yet suffer from small-sample unreliability, whereas an expansive base rate risks diluting the very mechanisms that make imperial decline predictable.
