I am a novelist preparing a seminar defense of the view that an ant colony can possess a genuine, if minimal, collective mind—not through analogy to human consciousness, but through what I term distributed narrative coherence: the colony sustains a continuous, self-correcting representational trajectory about its ecological state via pheromone trails and trophallaxis, functioning as a form of distributed autobiographical memory rather than centralized awareness. Drawing on the Ant Fugue framework in which individual ants correspond to neurons and Aunt Hillary emerges as a nascent subject, I argue that mental unity requires not shared intentional content but functional continuity across temporal scales, though I concede that no individual ant accesses the whole trajectory. The audience is an interdisciplinary philosophy of mind colloquium comprising two Searle-style skeptics of group intentionality, two cognitive ethologists who reject mind-talk as anthropomorphic projection, and a literary theorist of narrative identity who might support me if I avoid overreaching into empirical neuroscience I do not command.

I need a prep sheet ordering the objections by likelihood and damage, each phrased in the vocabulary of its speaker—the philosophers pressing the absence of we-intentions and shared propositional attitudes, the ethologists charging explanatory redundancy with swarm algorithms, the theorist probing whether narrative coherence collapses back into smuggled first-person phenomenology—paired with a concise reply suited to a five-minute exchange, an explicit note on what I should concede versus hold, and where each reply is soft. Crucially, include the objection I am dreading: that my criterion of distributed narrative coherence reintroduces precisely the subjective unity I claim to have eliminated, and how to respond without retreating into mere metaphor. Finally, flag the weakest structural point in my overall argument—the analogical leap from individual autobiographical memory to distributed chemical trace networks—so that I defend it actively rather than hope the audience overlooks it.
