In a scene I am drafting, a philosophy instructor grades an undergraduate examination on Douglas Hofstadter’s strange-loop theory of the self. The examination asks: “Explain what Hofstadter means by a ‘strange loop,’ describe how the symbol ‘I’ functions as a self-referential pattern rather than a Cartesian ego, and identify one structural feature that distinguishes such a loop from ordinary recursion.” The student responds: that a strange loop is a hierarchical feedback circuit in which an abstract symbol points downward to the neural substrate that generates it, comparable to a video camera displaying its own feed; that the symbol “I” is therefore an emergent, distributed pattern that creates the convincing illusion of an immaterial observer; and that the crucial distinction from recursion is temporal latency—the loop unfolds over time, whereas recursion is instantaneous. Please assess this answer point by point with a justified grade, separating settled elements of Hofstadter’s view from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and avoid presenting any disputed reading as the consensus.
