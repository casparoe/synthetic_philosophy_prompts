I am drafting the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on rational choice and philosophical paradox. Please write one essay question that uses Buridan's ass as its point of departure but is unanswerable by merely recounting the standard story about rational paralysis between identical options. To achieve this, inject the following novel variant: the ass stands equidistant between two hay bales that are identical in every property accessible to its perceptual and cognitive apparatus, yet one bale contains a dormant compound that, if ingested, will restructure the ass's future preference-ordering without its present awareness, while the other bale lacks this compound entirely. The question should ask students to determine whether this hidden asymmetry supplies the ass with a reason to choose one bale, whether the absence of epistemic access nullifies that reason, and whether a deterministic tie-breaking rule—such as an innate left-bias—can be rationally endorsed ex ante or is only causally explicable ex post. Specifically, students must address whether the paradox is dissolved by the hidden difference or merely relocated into a problem about rational ignorance and inaccessible reasons. Provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme organized around four required conceptual moves: (1) distinguishing metaphysical symmetry from epistemic symmetry; (2) assessing whether properties unknown to the agent can generate reasons for choice; (3) evaluating whether arbitrary deterministic resolution is rationally justifiable from the agent's ex-ante perspective; and (4) diagnosing whether the resolution eliminates Buridan's problem or displaces it into a different domain. For each move, assign a specific mark weight (total 20 marks) and state in one line the characteristic error that separates a B-level answer from an A-level answer—for instance, conflating causal explanation with rational justification, or treating hidden properties as automatically reason-giving without addressing access conditions. The difficulty should come from the conceptual crux, not from historical scholarship, and the question must not request citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
