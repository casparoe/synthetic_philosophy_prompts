I am currently taking a seminar in the philosophy of mathematics in which we have discussed Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's diagonal argument, and the finitist rejection of actual infinity in favor of merely potential infinity. For our final session I want to close with a short poem that holds open the tension we kept circling: the hotel manager who insists there is always another room, and the finitist who maintains that a completed totality of all rooms is impossible. I would like the poem written as a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with exactly two refrains and only two rhymes—so that the circular repetition of the refrains enacts the endless assignment of guests, while the strict finite form of the poem itself embodies the finitist refusal to complete an infinite set. The two refrains should take turns through the tercets and meet side by side in the final couplet, one giving the manager's assurance and the other the finitist's denial; a reader who knows the debate should be able to identify which refrain belongs to which position and see why they remain adjacent without being reconciled. Please render Cantor's argument not as an abstract comparison of cardinalities but as an event occurring inside the hotel—every room is filled, yet one more guest arrives, and the assignment breaks—without declaring either the manager or the finitist correct. The poem should leave the paradox unresolved, since that is where our discussion ended. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I only need the poem to be conceptually accurate enough that a specialist in the philosophy of mathematics would recognize the positions rather than feel they have been caricatured.
