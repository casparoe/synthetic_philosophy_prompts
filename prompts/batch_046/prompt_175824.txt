I'm running a 75-minute graduate seminar on consciousness studies with about fifteen participants, mixing philosophy and cognitive-science students. Design an in-class simulation called "The Interpreter's Tribunal" that makes them feel the methodological force of heterophenomenology—Dennett's proposal that we treat first-person reports as texts describing a heterophenomenological world rather than as direct, transparent evidence about consciousness—without delivering it as a lecture. The simulation should assign three roles by lot: Reporters, who undergo a brief ambiguous perceptual task (for example, a stabilized-image or auditory-illusion exposure) and write a structured first-person narrative using only assigned vocabulary cards that constrain descriptions to either phenomenal-character or functional-access terms; Heterophenomenological Interpreters, who receive only these narratives and must construct a predictive model of the Reporter's subsequent behavioral choices—such as which perceptual variant they will select or how long they will persist—without asking clarifying questions, appealing to shared experience, or claiming access to "what it is like"; and Introspectionist Auditors, who receive the same narratives but are instructed to reconstruct the Reporter's "actual conscious experience" and defend its accuracy against competing interpretations. Use a point system in which Interpreters and Auditors earn points tied solely to predictive success on the behavioral-choice task, while Reporters separately assign a "felt-authority score" judging whether each interpreter "took my report seriously as authoritative about my consciousness." The rules must be arranged so that Interpreters predict behavior more accurately yet receive low authority scores from Reporters, while Auditors earn high authority scores but poor predictive marks—making the tension between predictive utility and first-person authority visceral rather than abstract. Specify the exact materials (the vocabulary cards with permitted and forbidden terms, the prediction form with forced-choice behavioral outcomes, the behavioral-choice task itself), the round-by-round sequence including a hidden "clarification temptation" round in which Reporters are given an opportunity to smuggle extra information, and a debrief that maps each round onto Dennett's distinction between the heterophenomenological world (the fictional world described by the report, treated as real data) and the real-world mechanisms of consciousness. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run—Reporters will try to "help" Interpreters by violating the vocabulary constraints, Auditors will mistake predictive failure for methodological disrespect, and participants will conflate "not accessing my experience" with "not caring about it"—and say how to correct each in real time. Finally, be frank about which feature of heterophenomenology the activity can only approximate—specifically, the claim that the heterophenomenological world is itself a real, explainable phenomenon that deserves scientific study but carries no special ontological privilege over neural mechanisms—and that you as facilitator must supply this point verbally after the debrief. Do not ask for readings, citations, or bibliographies.
