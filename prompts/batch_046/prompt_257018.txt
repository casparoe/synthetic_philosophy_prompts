For a debate seminar I am organizing at my high school, I am building a handout on the normative question of how much rule-following a society should demand—moving beyond the descriptive anthropological contrast between "tight" and "loose" cultures to ask what level of normative rigidity is actually justified. I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions by the concrete judgments they make about cases, not by the labels people prefer.

Please construct the tree so that each branch point is phrased as a claim or scenario that a respondent could evaluate without already knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to. The root should ask whether a society ought to tolerate a deliberately visible, locally beneficial violation of a widely shared social norm.

From there, include branches that separate positions on the following four distinctions—though you should order them logically rather than listing them in sequence:

1. Are social rules primarily coordination instruments whose authority derives from solving collective-action problems, or are they partly expressions of collective moral identity that carry weight even when coordination benefits are unclear?
2. Does the strength of the case for strict enforcement depend on contextual conditions such as ecological threat, resource scarcity, or external conflict, or should normative tightness be evaluated by the same standard regardless of environment?
3. When norms are violated, is a tacit, unacknowledged exception more damaging to social authority than an explicit exception that is publicly debated and formally permitted?
4. Is there a scale or complexity threshold—say, in population size, institutional density, or technological interdependence—beyond which loose norms become practically unworkable, irrespective of moral arguments?

Each leaf should carry a descriptive name (for instance, "Contextual Instrumentalism," "Universal Identity-Based Tightness," "Explicit-Exception Looseness," or "Scale-Dependent Pragmatism") or be marked "unnamed" if the combination seems unexplored. For every leaf, provide one sentence stating its core normative commitment and one sentence stating its most awkward practical implication—the kind of bullet a defender would have to accept.

Please also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining briefly whether they collapse conceptually or whether the branching question is ambiguous between them. The tree should aim for six to eight leaves, and unoccupied logical combinations should be noted with a brief diagnosis: is the gap due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere lack of exploration?

Keep the framing accessible to high school debaters: do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to highly technical literatures that a general reader would not recognize. Instead, build the taxonomy from the logical space of the answers themselves.
