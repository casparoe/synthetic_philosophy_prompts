I sit on our university’s humanities publication-grant committee, and by Thursday we must vote on whether to fund a proposed critical edition that translates a cycle of sixteenth-century French lyric poems in the manner of Clément Marot into contemporary English. The applicant proposes a “double-binding” method: each line must preserve the original syllable count, rhyme scheme, and caesura placement, but must also observe an arbitrary additional constraint—no occurrence of the letter “e” in any translated line. She argues that this honors the generative logic of the original poetic culture, in which formal restriction is constitutive of meaning rather than merely decorative, and that relaxing these constraints for semantic transparency would misrepresent the work’s aesthetic ontology. Two opposing reviewers contend that the result is unreadable, that unavoidable semantic distortion makes the text a formalist puzzle rather than a translation, and that fidelity to propositional content must take precedence over the reproduction of generative rules. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the meeting: recommend approval, approval with conditions, or rejection, together with a concrete time-bound course of action; treat the applicant’s formal-reproductive conception of translation seriously, including its strongest defense; and identify the philosophical stake—whether translation is primarily an act of semantic fidelity or of formal reproduction—without appealing to named theoretical authorities that the committee does not share. The memo should also give honest weight to the reviewers’ concern that preserving form at this cost collapses poetry into an exercise in constraint satisfaction.
