You are a professional philosopher working in metaethics and normative theory, with particular attention to the architecture of practical reasoning and the debate between moral particularism and generalism. Here are my verdicts about when moral reasoning ought to appeal to general principles and when it ought to remain responsive to particular features, and I want to know whether there is a single principle—perhaps something like authority-sensitive particularism or structured discretion—that captures the pattern.

Case 1: A physician refuses to prescribe opioids to a chronic-pain patient because institutional guidelines prohibit it, even though she knows this patient has an unusually high pain tolerance, no history of substance use, and an objectively verified severe injury. Verdict: wrong—she should have overridden the guideline in this particular instance.

Case 2: A judge imposes the statutory maximum on a first-time offender for a non-violent property crime because the sentencing grid mandates it, despite recognizing that the offender is the sole caregiver to a disabled sibling, has made full restitution, and poses no future risk. Verdict: wrong—she should have found a procedural mechanism to depart downward.

Case 3: A parent insists that her child share a favorite toy with a visiting friend because “we always share,” ignoring that the toy is the child’s only comfort object following a recent trauma and that the visitor is a complete stranger in an unfamiliar environment. Verdict: wrong—the particular emotional context should have overridden the family rule.

Case 4: A military officer orders the destruction of a civilian structure that intelligence reports identify as an enemy communications node, applying the rules of engagement exactly, but fails to notice visible signage indicating a historic orphanage and the presence of local children in the courtyard. Verdict: wrong—she should have halted and verified despite full procedural compliance.

Case 5: A university admissions committee rejects an applicant with exceptional but non-standard credentials—published original translations of dead languages, self-taught mastery—because the rubric requires standardized test scores, which the applicant lacks due to a documented learning disability. Verdict: wrong—the particular achievement profile should have triggered an exception.

Case 6: A diplomat breaks explicit protocol and treaty terms to shelter a dissident in an embassy, violating host-state sovereignty norms, because she recognizes a specific, imminent threat of torture that the protocol was not designed to address and that no general exception clause clearly covers. Verdict: right—the particular moral urgency justified departure from the rule.

Now observe the tension. In cases 1–5, rigid adherence to well-designed general rules seems like a moral failure. So I want a principle that distinguishes legitimate particularist override from illegitimate whim, but that also does not collapse into “follow the rule unless you have a good reason,” since that seems too permissive and fails to explain why the officer in case 4 is specifically blameworthy for not noticing the orphanage.
