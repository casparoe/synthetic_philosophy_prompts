I hold the view that in modern democratic states, citizens ought to cultivate what I call structured opacity — a deliberate practice of maintaining informational gaps, strategic ambiguity, and limited legibility in their dealings with government institutions — rather than embracing transparency as a default virtue. I draw this partly from the contrast between Germany’s tradition of informational self-determination, the American framework of administrative openness, and China’s social-credit architecture, which together suggest that visibility is asymmetrical: the state becomes more readable to itself while citizens become more readable to the state. I further claim that this opacity is not merely defensive privacy but constitutive of democratic agency; full legibility makes citizens individually addressable and fragments collective capacity. What would actually count against this position? I want the evidence, arguments, and possible observations ranked by how decisive they would be, including things that have arguably already happened in democratic societies. Specifically: are there well-documented cases where increased citizen transparency to state institutions strengthened democratic accountability without enabling surveillance? Could the view be qualified so heavily — for instance, by saying opacity is only good for marginalized groups — that it becomes unfalsifiable, and is that insulation a problem for a normative claim of this kind? Would it be fatal if critics showed that structured opacity is mainly available to privileged citizens with access to encryption and legal counsel, thereby reinforcing inequality rather than resisting state power? I also want to know which objections defenders of this view would themselves concede as damaging, versus which they would dismiss as missing the point. Give me an honest map of where this position is exposed.
