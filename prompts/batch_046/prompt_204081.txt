I'm trying to work out what I actually think about phenomenal judgemnt if epiphenominalism is true, using some homemade gadget scenarios because the usual zombie examples feel tired. Here are my gut verdicts.

Case 1: I stub my toe, feel pain, and say "that hurts." The pain doesn't cause the words—the neurons do—but I still think my judgment is correct and genuinely mine.

Case 2: Now imagine a "phenomenal translator" chip in my skull that reads the same nociceptive signal and voices "that hurts" through a speaker. I hear it and agree. The pain is present but still causally inert. I think this is still my judgment, just mediated.

Case 3: The chip is in a zombie twin with identical signals but zero consciousness. It voices the same sentence. Here I don't think there's any phenomenal judgment—just grammatically correct noise.

Case 4: Split-brain version: the chip reads right-hemisphere fear and voices "I'm afraid" to the left hemisphere, which nods along. Real fear exists in the right half, but I think the left half's agreement is confabulation, not a legitimate judgment about that fear.

Case 5: A blindsight patient reports "I see nothing" in the blind field despite guessing shapes correctly. I think this report is spot-on—there's genuinely no phenomenal vision there.

Case 6: A drug severs consciousness from speech, but the speech centers stay active and keep saying "I'm joyful" in a flat voice. Even if the neural correlates of joy are still firing, I think these are false reports, not judgments.

Is there one principle that captures all six? The pair that keeps bugging me is 2 vs 4: both involve mediation and causal inertness, yet one feels okay and the other doesn't. I also want to know what the principle says about a case I haven't listed—say, the chip voices the correct report but I'm deaf and never register it. And if I have to drop one verdict to keep the principle clean and simple, which one should go?
