I'm part of a working group assisting a municipal planning authority that is modernising its bylaws and internal protocols. Over successive rounds of staff turnover, the institutional memory behind many legacy safeguards—archaic zoning buffers, dual-authorisation requirements for minor grants, ritualised cooling-off periods—has been lost, and we are now reviewing them systematically for possible abolition or streamlining. In our discussions we have kept returning to Burkean conservatism's warning against rationalist reconstruction of institutions whose wisdom is distributed and historical, and to Chesterton's principle that one ought not to tear down a fence until one knows why it was put up. I want these ideas translated into a practical, reusable decision procedure.

The procedure should be designed for a compliance officer who must evaluate between five and ten such proposals each month, usually under a forty-eight-hour deadline, with no budget for deep archival research and no specialist historians on call. It should take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, specifying the tests to apply in sequence, the distinctions that must survive simplification—above all, the difference between a purpose that is genuinely obsolete and one that remains embedded but unrecognised in the wider system—and the precise points at which judgement cannot be eliminated and the officer must sign off on personal responsibility rather than procedural output. The conceptual grounding should remain visible: the officer should understand, without being given a lecture, why Burkean scepticism about deliberate design applies to administrative practice and why Chesterton's fence functions as an epistemic rather than merely aesthetic constraint.

Please apply the procedure to three worked cases: first, a redundant paper logbook that appears to serve no current regulatory purpose; second, a mandatory cooling-off period for disciplinary appeals that seems only to delay justice but may protect an unseen procedural dependency; and third, a cumbersome dual-signature rule for small financial disbursements that the authority is strongly inclined to abolish for efficiency reasons, but where the procedure should deliver a retention verdict that the officer will not welcome. For each case, show the procedure's reasoning step by step. Finish by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly where historical reconstruction fails, where competing goods must be weighed, or where unintended consequences lie beyond its predictive reach. Write in formal British English, keep the prompt self-contained, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or suggested readings; a model without web access should be able to respond fully from its training.
