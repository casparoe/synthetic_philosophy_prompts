I'm convening a ninety-minute interdisciplinary workshop for roughly eighteen graduate students in history and philosophy of science, and I want a runnable simulation that makes Fernand Braudel's distinction between the longue durée and the history of events visceral rather than textual. The participants should leave feeling how geographic and climatic structures can quietly nullify intentional human projects across generations, without anyone lecturing them about it in advance.

Design a tabletop role-play divided into three compressed "centuries" of rounds. Split the group into four factions—coastal state administrators, trans-regional merchant guilds, highland agrarian communes, and harbor engineers—each with distinct victory conditions they set themselves in round one, such as maintaining a trade surplus, achieving political autonomy, or preventing famine. Provide each faction with visible resources—labor tokens, grain reserves, and reputation points—but also assign them hidden structural cards representing soil salinization rates, alluvial silting of the harbor mouth, and multi-decadal rainfall oscillations that shift only slowly according to a predetermined schedule unknown to the players. The rules should require that every round each faction propose one collective action, whether a treaty, an irrigation project, or a tariff reform, but the outcome is determined not only by negotiation with other factions but by drawing from a structural deck whose cumulative effects gradually erode the feasibility of the factions' original goals. Use a scoring system that rewards both short-term negotiation success and long-term adaptation, but weight the structural deck so that by the third round every group has been forced to abandon at least one founding objective not because of opposition but because the physical environment has made it structurally impossible.

Specify the materials—map layout, token types, card decks—the exact round timeline with minutes allocated, the payoff scheme without real money, and the rules for revealing hidden parameters. Then lay out the debrief: identify which round events correspond to Braudel's layers of historical time, predict the failure mode in the first run—participants will likely attribute their losses to betrayal by other factions rather than to the slow variables—explain how to handle that misattribution in the moment, and state honestly which aspect of the longue durée, specifically the fact that actors living inside it rarely possess the temporal scale to recognize it, this simulation reproduces well and which aspect it necessarily cannot reproduce, leaving that gap for the facilitator to address verbally. Do not require any in-session reading of primary sources; the conceptual point must emerge from the rules and the accumulated choices, not from citation.
