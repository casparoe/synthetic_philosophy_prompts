I have been covering the recent resurgence of interpretive work on Berkeley's Three Dialogues, and I have come to hold a specific, somewhat unconventional reading of the master argument. Rather than treating it as a question-begging conflation of mental representation with mental presence, I read it as advancing a structural claim about the architecture of conscious attention: the act of attempting to direct first-person awareness toward an object conceived as existing independently of all perception necessarily introduces a representational vehicle into awareness, so that the intended mind-independent target is always replaced by the mental content through which it is apprehended. On this view, Berkeley is not asserting a logical impossibility but describing an attentional collapse built into the conditions of perceptual presence—something any adequate theory of perception must accommodate rather than dismiss. Before I commit to this interpretation in print, I want a rigorous stress test. What exactly would have to be true for this structural reading to fail? Which empirical results about subliminal or unconscious representation, which formal models of attentional architecture, which interpretive discoveries about Berkeley's engagement with Locke or Malebranche, or which phenomenological counterexamples would genuinely count against it? I also want to know what this reading predicts that rival interpretations—whether the traditional question-begging charge or more charitable conceptual readings—do not predict, and whether those predictions expose it to risk or merely insulate it. Please distinguish the defeaters you regard as decisive from those that are merely awkward, indicate which objections defenders of this structural reading would themselves concede as damaging versus those they would dismiss as misdirected, and assess honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is a problem for an interpretive claim of this kind.
