For decades, historians and economists have debated the Great Divergence by asking why Britain industrialized rather than China—the so-called Needham question. Some scholars, working in comparative economic history, treat this as a well-formed contrastive explanation: they identify institutional, geographic, or resource variables present in Britain and absent in Qing China, then test whether those differences are causally decisive using counterfactual reasoning about alternative development paths. Others, particularly in global and cultural history, argue the question is malformed: Britain and China were not comparable units in the relevant period, the counterfactual requires holding constant social dynamics that were structurally interdependent across Eurasia, and asking "why not China" implicitly assumes a single linear trajectory of modernization that both regions were competing to achieve.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about empirical evidence—both sides draw on overlapping archives, price data, and demographic reconstructions—but about what a contrastive historical explanation must accomplish. One camp demands a difference-maker within a multivariate causal model; the other demands a narrative account of contingency that respects the internal coherence of each society's development, treating comparison as an anachronistic projection.
