Here is an argument I have been trying to formalize for a seminar on comparative moral status, and I am concerned it collapses into an unacceptable conclusion unless I abandon a view I would rather keep.

(1) Consciousness is distributed continuously across the animal kingdom: there are no sharp metaphysical boundaries separating full phenomenal subjects from non-subjects, only differences in the richness, integration, and intensity of experience. Call this gradualism about phenomenal consciousness.

(2) If gradualism is correct, then moral patienthood should also come in degrees, proportional to the quantity and quality of conscious experience possessed, rather than being governed by an arbitrary species threshold.

(3) Accept what I will call the Continuity of Aggregation: if harming one subject is wrong for reason R, and harming a second, distinct subject is wrong for the same reason R, then harming both together is wrong to a degree that tracks the combined magnitude of R-affected consciousness, absent some special defeating condition.

(4) Apply this to a rescue choice. You can save either one human being or ten thousand sentient invertebrates whose individual phenomenal lives are real but far less rich. By (2) and (3), if the aggregate phenomenal density of the invertebrates exceeds that of the single human, you are morally required to save the invertebrates.

(5) Now extend the same reasoning beyond rescue cases to ordinary action. Every routine human activity—walking, farming, using electricity—indirectly destroys or frustrates enormous numbers of minimally conscious organisms. By (2) and (3), the aggregate phenomenal harm of ordinary life dwarfs the harm of a single human death. So if (4) is correct, then preserving one human life is rarely the right choice, and our everyday conduct involves constant, massive moral failure.

(6) The conclusion in (5) is intolerable; we do not live in a world where saving a child from a burning building is typically wrong because of the insects nearby.

(7) Therefore, gradualism must be false: there must be a sharp line that grants humans (or perhaps all mammals) full, non-aggregable moral status, and denies it entirely to beings below that line.

My colleague points out that rejecting (3) to save gradualism seems to push us toward a Taurek-style refusal to aggregate within our own species, which makes public health and disaster relief conceptually puzzling. I want to retain gradualism and also maintain that saving the human in (4) is clearly correct, without accepting (5). Which step—(2), (3), or the hidden inference from (4) to (5)—should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? Is there a principled way to allow aggregation in rescue contexts while blocking the slide into routine harm, or does the argument expose a deeper tension in continuous views of consciousness?
