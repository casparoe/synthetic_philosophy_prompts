I'm trying to fix my judgments about Machiavelli's distinction between fortune and virtù by testing them against five constructed cases rather than the standard historical examples.

Case 1: A ruler builds an elaborate defense tailored to one specific enemy tactic; an entirely different tactic appears and defeats him. I judge fortune to have overwhelmed virtù.

Case 2: A ruler inherits chaos and, through rapid institutional innovation and relentless personal adaptation, consolidates power despite unpredictable resistance. I judge virtù to have mastered fortune.

Case 3: A ruler takes no meaningful initiative for a decade, but every serious rival dies of unrelated natural causes, leaving him dominant. I judge this pure fortune—no virtù whatsoever.

Case 4: A ruler is habitually reckless, takes enormous risks repeatedly, and succeeds in most of them solely because unlikely outcomes keep breaking in his favor; he learns almost nothing from failure. I judge this not virtù, despite the appearance of success.

Case 5: A ruler prepares exhaustively for drought, but an unprecedented flood arrives; he immediately converts his irrigation infrastructure into flood defenses and saves the realm. I judge this virtù, yet I am uncertain whether it belongs with case 2 or stands apart.

What principle—perhaps concerning adaptability, the origin of contingency, or the restructuring of one's own capacities—captures all five verdicts? What does it predict for a further case in which a ruler's meticulous preparation succeeds exactly as planned, with no surprises at all? And if the principle cannot save every verdict, which should be overturned: the denial of virtù in case 4, the attribution of fortune alone in case 3, or the pairing of cases 2 and 5?
