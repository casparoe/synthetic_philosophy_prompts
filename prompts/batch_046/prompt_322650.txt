I am grading a final exam for an upper-division seminar on philosophy of social science. The exam asked: Weber’s Protestant Ethic thesis is often summarized as claiming that Calvinist values caused modern capitalism; assess this summary by explaining (i) how Weber distinguishes causal explanation (kausale Erklärung) from interpretive understanding (deutendes Verstehen), (ii) why elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) is neither coincidence nor deterministic causation, and (iii) whether his account permits downward cultural causation—values shaping institutional outcomes without reducing to individual psychology or material conditions alone.

Here is one student answer. It is genuinely mixed: correct on the more visible elements, but it contains one or two subtle mistakes—a conflation, a misattributed view, or a step that does not follow—that a hasty grader might overlook. There are no obvious howlers.

Student answer: Weber rejects downward cultural causation altogether, insisting that values can only be interpreted, not explained causally. The Protestant ethic displayed an elective affinity with capitalism, which is essentially a statistical correlation lacking any mechanistic or meaningful link between religious motivation and economic behavior. Because Weber denies that culture can function as an efficient cause, he treats ideal types as purely descriptive classifications rather than explanatory models. He does allow that the capitalist spirit eventually formed an iron cage, but this demonstrates only that economic structures cause cultural persistence, not the reverse. Consequently, Weber should be read as a strict methodological individualist who reduces all macro-level patterns to the subjective meanings held by individual Calvinists.
