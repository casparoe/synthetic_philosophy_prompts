I have been working through the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism for an independent study project, and I think I have found an extension that makes the objection prove far too much. Here is the argument as I see it: (1) The separateness of persons entails that there is no collective moral subject over whom benefits and burdens can be summed; moral reasons are always indexed to individual agents. (2) Any social choice rule—including not only utilitarian aggregation but also Rawlsian maximin, prioritarian weighting, or even contractualist unanimous agreement—requires comparing outcomes across different people in order to justify selecting one distribution over another. (3) Such cross-person comparison treats the group as if it possessed a single point of view from which the fates of its members can be weighed together, which is precisely the kind of "super-person" thinking the separateness thesis rejects. (4) Therefore, every social choice rule violates the separateness of persons. (5) But if no social choice rule is permissible, then no institution—whether a legislature, a court, or a school board—can legitimately make collective decisions that affect different people differently. (6) That conclusion is clearly false: institutions do make such decisions, and we do not think they are all morally illegitimate. (7) So the argument must go wrong somewhere. I am especially attached to premises (1) and (5), because (1) captures exactly what I find wrong with utilitarianism and (5) shows how radical the objection really is. Is the flaw in (2)—that social choice necessarily involves the forbidden comparison—or in (3)—that comparison implies a collective subject? Or is there a hidden scope shift between steps? Please tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked plausible, and then explain what the standard responses—those who defend the objection narrowly, those who allow limited interpersonal comparison, and those who reject the framing entirely—each have to pay to reject that step.
