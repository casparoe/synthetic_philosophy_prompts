Following a long argument with a friend about whether historical contingency is an objective feature of the world or merely an artifact of the linear narratives we impose, I want to close the discussion with a poem. Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—using the standard rotating end-word pattern. The six end-words, in initial order, should be: nail, shoe, horse, rider, battle, kingdom. Do not trace the proverb's causal chain as a simple deterministic sequence; instead, treat the six terms as nodes in a nonlinear dynamical system, with each stanza introducing feedback, amplification, or dampening so that the initial loss may cascade unpredictably, dissipate, or loop back upon itself. The strict deterministic rotation of the form should thereby stand in structural tension with the chaotic dynamics inside the lines. In the envoi, gather all six words without resolving whether the kingdom falls or is saved, leaving the tension between narrative linearity and genuine contingency exactly where our debate ended. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with chaos theory recognizes the departure from linear causation, but should function as verse in its own right for a reader without that background.
