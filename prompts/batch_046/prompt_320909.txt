My grandfather writes the same journal entry every October 14: a word-for-word account of a conversation he had with his late brother in 1972, including the same punctuation and the same note about the weather. Over forty years the entries are nearly indistinguishable. He now refuses to vary anything about that day—the same breakfast, the same walk, the same hours of silence—and says that altering the pattern would mean accepting that the moment is “past,” which he denies. He insists that certain moments are not destroyed by time but remain permanently present, and that repeating their exact form is fidelity to reality rather than a compulsion. His therapist describes this as a ritualized grief response borrowing philosophical language. When I asked whether he might be rationalizing, he replied that I was assuming linear time without argument, just as everyone does. I want to take him seriously without ignoring that this may be harming him. Is there a philosophically serious view of time that actually supports his practice, or is he necessarily conflating a psychological need with a metaphysical claim? And if such a view is genuinely available, does that change how I ought to respond to his refusal to vary the day?
