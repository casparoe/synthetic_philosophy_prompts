I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether moral compromise is merely mutual concession or can generate a kind of moral surplus—value that neither side possessed before the agreement—and whether that idea survives contact with genuinely incommensurable commitments. I sit on a municipal mediation board that reviews development proposals pitting deep value conflicts against one another, such as ecological preservation against affordable housing or indigenous data sovereignty against open-science mandates, and I need a reusable decision procedure my colleagues can apply without philosophical training. Please design an ordered framework that treats compromise not as splitting the difference but as identifying a package in which each party trades away a lower-order commitment for a higher-order one, with explicit checks for false trade—where a non-negotiable core value is diluted, disguised, or rendered fungible—and with clear stopping conditions: a definite “no—this is betrayal, not trade”; a hand-off point where quantitative reasoning ends and human value judgment must take over; and a final check that the outcome is not merely Pareto-improving in preference space but genuinely respects the structure of each value system involved. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: constructing a wind farm on culturally significant but legally unprotected land in exchange for community equity and co-management rights; anonymizing health records for research on the condition that the community gains veto power over all future data uses; and relaxing historic-preservation zoning to allow high-density transit-oriented housing. Make one of these yield an answer that the procedure approves but that ethics should reject, and explain precisely where the framework fails to catch the problem. Keep the underlying concept of gains-from-trade in values intact, but do not reduce it to simple preference aggregation or utility calculus.
