Write an essay on whether a single self, an 'I', can be genuinely distributed across multiple physical brains. Take as your starting point Douglas Hofstadter's view that personal identity is not bound to one brain but is instead a high-level, self-referential pattern that could in principle be shared or copied. Weigh this against the phenomenological objection that subjective experience requires a unified point of view, and examine the intermediate case of split-brain patients in whom two hemispheres appear to host distinct preferences while both claim ownership of the body. To sharpen the analysis, distinguish between a 'weakly plural' distributed self—where brains share a continuous narrative memory stream but lack unified phenomenal access—and a 'strongly plural' distributed self, where they maintain independent phenomenal fields yet still identify with one another. Reach and defend a conclusion about whether either form is coherent, or whether the pattern-based view ultimately collapses into an eliminativist denial of the first-person perspective.
