Hey, I've been reading some old debates about whether society is just a bunch of individuals or if people are basically shaped by their groups, and I keep noticing a rhetorical move I don't have a word for.

Here's the pattern. Someone proposes a policy to force more social interaction -- like a city rule that new apartment blocks have to include shared courtyards, or a school curriculum that requires group service projects. The supporters talk like hardline liberals: they frame it as expanding individual choice sets or giving people optional resources for connection, treating community like a personal asset you can engineer. Meanwhile the opponents talk like classic communitarians: they say real trust can't be zoned into existence, that state-mandated proximity crowds out the voluntary associations that used to grow organically, and that once the goverment designs your neighborhood, the genuine social bonds get replaced by compliance.

I've seen it flip other ways too. Liberals defend mandatory civic education as building the capacity for autonomous reflection, while conservatives defend local dialects or rituals by arguing they only stay alive if the state doesn't try to preserve them. It's not just strategic framing, because both sides seem genuinely convinced they're defending their own core value. Is there an established term for this inversion -- where the side usually associated with communal goods ends up protecting unregulated social life from state design, and the individualist side ends up using state tools to manufacture collective life? How is it different from plain equivocation or motte-and-bailey, and if there's no name, how would you carve out the phenomenon so it doesn't just cover every case of someone borrowing the other side's language?
