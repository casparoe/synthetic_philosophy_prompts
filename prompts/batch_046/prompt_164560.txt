Suppose Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz had lived long enough to encounter Max Black's thought experiment involving two qualitatively identical spheres suspended in an otherwise empty, symmetric space, and that he was also made aware of the quantum-mechanical treatment of identical particles, which are indistinguishable even in principle rather than merely unknown to us. I am not asking for a generic restatement of the principle of the identity of indiscernibles; rather, I want you to reconstruct, in conceptual detail, how Leibniz might have responded to Black's scenario in light of these later developments. Specifically, please distinguish between two defensive strategies he might adopt: a relationalist defence, in which the spheres differ by their spatiotemporal relations to one another or to the boundaries of space; and a monadic defence, in which he posits some intrinsic, non-qualitative feature—perhaps a primitive thisness or haecceity—to preserve the principle. Would he have regarded Black's universe as a genuine metaphysical possibility, or as an impossible world described with insufficient precision? Furthermore, do you think he would have drawn an analogy between the spheres and quantum identical particles, treating the latter as confirming that indistinguishability arises only when relational properties are absent, or would he have insisted that even quantum particles possess hidden relational or monadic differences? Please write your response in British English. In your answer, please state how confident you are in each major part of your reconstruction, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
