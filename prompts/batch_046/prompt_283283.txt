I am putting together the final examination for an advanced seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of probability—aimed at students who have already worked through the St. Petersburg literature and can recite the standard utility-bounding and truncation responses without thinking. I need a single essay question that can be answered in roughly ninety minutes (expected response: 800–1,000 words) and that earns twenty-five marks total. The core topic must be the Pasadena paradox and the specific problem of genuinely undefined expected values, not merely infinite ones. To prevent simple recitation, the prompt should present students with the alternating-payoff Pasadena game—fair coin tossed until heads, with payoff (−1)^{k+1}·2^{k−1} on the kth toss, yielding term-by-term contributions of ±½ and an expectation that oscillates without converging—together with a constructed twist: a compound version in which the agent may, at no cost, switch after observing the first toss to an isomorphic mirror-game whose payoff sequence is the negative of the original shifted by one index. The essay should ask students to evaluate whether three proposed resolutions—(i) a hard truncation at horizon N, (ii) a bounded utility function that remains linear in the neighborhood of zero, and (iii) a non-Archimedean preference ordering that ranks gambles solely by the sign of the expected-utility difference—actually resolve the paradox in this undefined-expectation setting, and specifically why each either succeeds or fails for reasons that do not apply to the ordinary St. Petersburg case. They must distinguish an expectation that diverges to +∞ from one that is genuinely undefined (the Grandi-series structure), explain why pairing arguments that cancel successive terms are illegitimate in expected-value calculation, and address whether dynamic consistency requires the agent to switch in the compound version even though the value of the switch is itself undefined.

Please provide: (a) the exam question itself, clearly specifying the time limit, length expectation, and total marks; (b) a model answer of about 650–750 words that demonstrates the required conceptual moves rather than summarizing familiar literature; and (c) a marking scheme organized around four essential analytical moves, allocating marks so that they sum to 25, with each entry stating the point's weight, the specific correct content it must contain, and a one-line description of how candidates typically lose marks on it. Do not include any request for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; assessment should turn entirely on internal conceptual precision and on the student’s ability to navigate the compound mirror-game twist.
