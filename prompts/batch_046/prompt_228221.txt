I'm preparing a feature on the philosophy of collective remembrance, and I've been mapping a debate that sits at the intersection of social epistemology and philosophy of history. One position—let's call it mnemonic sovereignty—holds that when a community's living memory conflicts with the documentary record, the community's account should prevail, not out of mere sentiment but because collective memory operates through embodied practices and identity-formation that archives are structurally unable to capture; defenders often distinguish between thick memory, embedded in ritual and social practice, and thin propositional belief, arguing that empirical correction applies only to the latter. The opposing view—archival correctionism—maintains that this thick-thin distinction collapses under scrutiny: if an archive demonstrates that a community's origin narrative is causally false, then the practices sustaining it are not preserving an incommensurable form of knowledge but are propagating error, and insulating thickness from revision is arbitrary. My difficulty arises when the archive itself was produced by the institutional powers that oppressed the community—consider cases where colonial administrative records contradict indigenous oral histories not merely on details but on the very framing of events. Can you compare these two positions in light of that complication? Specifically, can the sovereignty view defend its distinction without sliding into a problematic relativism about causal history, and can the correctionist view acknowledge the constructed nature of archives without surrendering the claim that some records remain epistemically better positioned than communal memory to establish what actually occurred?
