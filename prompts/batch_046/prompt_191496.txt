I recently left a long career in engineering to become the primary caregiver for a parent, and the sudden absence of structured purpose led me back to Schlick’s claim that meaning resides not in achievement but in play—activity valued for itself rather than for its results. I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who is reviving this account and connecting it to contemporary psychology’s research on self-rewarding, intrinsically motivated activity. I need eight to ten questions that avoid mere exposition: one on whether Schlick’s distinction between work and play collapses once we notice that even playful activity involves skill and implicit goals; one on whether his apparent privileging of youth as the age of play is a descriptive observation or a normative ideal that would stigmatize productive adulthood; and one on a worry I have not seen addressed—if play derives its value partly through contrast with necessary labor, then a post-scarcity society that eliminates obligatory work might not liberate meaning universally but erode the very background against which play becomes meaningful. For each, include a follow-up tailored to the defense I anticipate: that instrumental structure need not dominate motivation, that mature play integrates rather than abandons skill, and that the relevant contrast is internal and psychological rather than social and economic. The aim is not to summarize Schlick but to locate exactly where his view stands or falls on these three pressure points.
