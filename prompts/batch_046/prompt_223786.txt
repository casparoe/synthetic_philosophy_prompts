I'm preparing a short essay for an advanced moral philosophy seminar that attempts to refine the concept of supererogation by introducing what I term a "stewardship relation." Rather than treating supererogatory acts as merely good actions that lie beyond the boundary of duty, I argue that we must distinguish between moral maintenance—obligatory actions that arise when an agent uniquely occupies an epistemic or practical position relative to a potential harm or benefit—and moral gifts, which are genuinely supererogatory. A stewardship relation holds when an agent is not just accidentally well-placed to help, but is specifically positioned by knowledge, skill, or role such that others in the relevant community could not reasonably be expected to intervene in their absence. To test this distinction, I construct a hypothetical case: an independent cryptographer discovers a severe, previously unknown vulnerability in a widely adopted open-source encryption protocol. She spends several hundred unpaid hours developing and distributing a patch, declines institutional support, and insists that her work not be attributed to any organization. I contend that if she is genuinely the only individual with the technical expertise to understand and remedy the flaw, her labor constitutes obligatory moral maintenance rather than supererogation; however, if qualified peers exist who could have acted but did not, and she nevertheless assumes the full burden, her choice acquires a genuinely supererogatory character because she steps into a gap that was not uniquely hers to fill. I further suggest that this framework complicates popular narratives about "moral heroes," since many celebrated sacrifices may actually reflect hidden stewardship obligations rather than acts that go beyond the call of duty.

I would appreciate your comments on the coherence of this distinction, particularly whether the notion of a stewardship relation collapses into familiar special obligations or remains conceptually independent. I am also interested in whether the cryptographer example successfully isolates the relevant moral difference, and whether the argument risks making ordinary acts of professional responsibility—such as a physician treating a patient—incorrectly appear supererogatory when they are clearly not. Please focus on conceptual accuracy and internal consistency rather than on sourcing or further reading; I am working without access to external references and want to know whether the reasoning holds together on its own terms.
