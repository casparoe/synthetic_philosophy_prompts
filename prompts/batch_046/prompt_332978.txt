I am revising the syllabus for our graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and in preparing the session on the principle of alternative possibilities I have been cataloguing the verdicts my colleague has delivered across a sequence of Frankfurt-style cases discussed in our meetings. Her rulings to date are these: in the canonical scenario in which an agent decides to vote Democrat while a hidden observer stands ready to compel the same choice but never intervenes, she assigns full moral responsibility despite the absence of any robust alternative; in a hard-wired variant in which the observer's mechanism is constructed so that no prior behavioural flicker could possibly have alerted it—thereby eliminating even the most minimal flicker of freedom—she nevertheless maintains full responsibility; in a case where the agent acts upon a subliminally induced preference that he never notices or endorses, with no observer present at all, she denies responsibility; and in a case where the agent herself has designed and implanted a cognitive stabiliser that continuously suppresses any nascent counter-desire before it reaches conscious awareness, leaving her with a seamless and wholeheartedly endorsed volition, she again affirms responsibility.

The new case I wish to place before you involves what I shall term the Reflective Loop. Here, an external system—engineered by others and unknown to the agent—monitors her reasoning in real time and subtly reinforces the logical pathways that lead to a specific decision by feeding back her own higher-order commitments, thereby rendering deviation cognitively unstable. The agent experiences this process entirely as ordinary, autonomous reflection, detects no interference, and fully endorses both the reasoning and its outcome; there is no suppression of an existing desire, but rather an external scaffolding of the architecture of deliberation itself.

If we seek consistency with her established track record, should she judge the agent responsible in the Reflective Loop? Which prior ruling—the canonical inactive-intervener case or the self-implanted stabiliser case—provides the closest analogue, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, what principle would she be adopting with each available choice? Please identify the two most plausible implicit rules capable of generating her first four verdicts, state what each predicts about the Reflective Loop, offer your best guess as to which rule she is actually running together with your reasons, and propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two rules. I should be grateful if you could present your analysis in American English, using formal and polished prose.
