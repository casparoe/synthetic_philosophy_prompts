I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for literature graduate students with no background in political philosophy. I want them to understand Rousseau's argument that public theater corrupts republican civic virtue, specifically through a mechanism of spectatorial passivity—where emotional identification with fictional suffering substitutes for active moral judgment—rather than through the depiction of immoral content. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why (for instance, why the representation-reality distinction must precede the civic argument, and why the Geneva context should come before the generalization), the one concrete scenario that should carry the idea and what it must possess and lack (it should not be a modern media analogy that erases historical specificity), the predictable misconceptions—such as that Rousseau is merely prudish, that the objection is religious, or that he claims art causes crime—along with the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off, a brief end-of-session check that would reveal whether students grasp the structural mechanism rather than only the conclusion, and what I should openly decline to cover—such as the full theory of popular sovereignty—and how to frame that omission so it does not appear to be an evasion.
