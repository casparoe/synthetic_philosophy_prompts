i sit on our uni's research ethics board. a social-psych proposal needs to tell participants it's about "reading comprehension" when its actually measuring how they assign blame, since knowing the true topic would change answers; debrief happens afterwords. half the board thinks this is routine methodological deception, half thinks it fails informed consent outright because it's lying. draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons researchers could actually respond to. but do it through Augustine's view on lying: take seriously his claim that lying is always wrong no matter the good outcome, and apply his taxonomy of lies (those told in teaching, in jest, to avoid harm, etc.). also inject this twist: call it "institutional assent." if we vote yes knowing the protocol involves deception, is our board itself committing a lie by saying this study is ethically sound when we privately think it involves a moral wrong? treat the strongest objection — that deception is necessary for valid data and debrief fixes harm — honestly rather than dismissing it. take a definite stance; don't hedge between options. and please don't include bibliographies, citations, or a reading list.
