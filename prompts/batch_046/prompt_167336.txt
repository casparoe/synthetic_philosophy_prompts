I'm a philosophy of science PhD student working on determinism and scientific explanation. I want to work through a conceptual knot that standard chaos-theoretic discussions tend to gloss over. We know that deterministic chaos undermines long-range predictability through sensitive dependence on initial conditions, while determinism itself is usually framed as a metaphysical thesis about the uniqueness of world-lines given fixed laws. Now I want to inject two further conditions into the framing: first, that initial conditions are not merely epistemically inaccessible but ontologically underdetermined by any physically realizable measurement protocol—a kind of structural finitude; and second, that the dynamics in question are computationally irreducible, meaning no compressed algorithm can shortcut the temporal evolution. Given these additions, please explain precisely how determinism, predictability, and explanatory power come apart. In particular, distinguish determinism as a modal claim about trajectory uniqueness, predictability as an information-theoretic capacity bounded by finite precision and measurement resolution, and what we might call explanatory determinism—the idea that deterministic laws can still ground causal explanations even when forecasting is impossible. How does the introduction of computational irreducibility alter the standard chaos-theoretic framing? Does the failure of predictability undermine determinism's explanatory role, or does the structural underdetermination of initial conditions shift the burden from epistemology to ontology in a way that preserves determinism but empties it of predictive content? I am looking for a conceptually rigorous but intuitive account—no technical proofs required—that keeps these distinctions sharp and addresses whether the injected conditions change the terms of the debate in a substantive way.
