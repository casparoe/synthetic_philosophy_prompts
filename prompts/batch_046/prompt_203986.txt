As a novelist, I have become preoccupied with a question that arises constantly in fiction yet rarely receives rigorous philosophical treatment: whether the first-person perspective—the "I"—can genuinely be distributed across more than one brain. I am drawn to the kind of view Douglas Hofstadter has advanced, that a self is not a substance but a pattern, and that such a pattern might persist in others who come to embody its structure. Still, my intuitions about particular cases are uneven, and I wish to know whether they conceal a coherent principle or merely a set of confusions.

Here are my verdicts. First, consider a long collaboration between two writers: after one dies, the survivor reports that the deceased partner's judgments, emotional reflexes, and even sensory preferences continue to operate within their own deliberation, not as recoverable memories but as living dispositions. I feel that part of the dead writer's "I" genuinely persists in the survivor's brain—distributed, diminished, but real.

Second, imagine a scenario in which a person's cognitive architecture is gradually reconstructed in two separate hosts through sustained, intimate interaction. Each host acquires half the autobiographical memories and all the characteristic dispositions, yet neither feels like the original in isolation; together they feel like one distributed "I." I accept this as genuine distribution.

Third, take a split-brain patient in whom each hemisphere controls a separate body but both share a single continuous autobiographical thread that neither can fully articulate without the other. I judge that there is one "I" distributed across both hemispheres.

Fourth, consider a case of pathological assimilation: a brain-injured patient mirrors the personality of a caregiver so precisely that family members say the patient "became" the caregiver. Despite the structural resemblance to the collaboration case, I reject this: there is no distribution of the caregiver's "I," only an unconscious impersonation.

Fifth, suppose two strangers suffer identical injuries and subsequently share the same first-person narrative, values, and emotional responses, having never met and shared no causal history. I say these are two separate "I"s that happen to match; identical content does not entail distribution.

Sixth, imagine an artificial system trained on every writing, preference, and decision of a deceased person, producing work indistinguishable in voice and moral outlook. I feel there is no "I" present—only a static echo, not a distributed self.

These verdicts appear coherent, yet they harbor a tension I cannot resolve. The collaboration case and the assimilation case both involve one brain coming to house another's pattern, yet I accept distribution in the first and deny it in the second. Meanwhile, the split-brain and two-host cases involve physical separation, which I accept, while the strangers case involves an identical separation without prior causal link, which I reject.

So I ask: is there a precise principle that captures all six verdicts? If so, state it clearly, identify which clause explains which case, and tell me what it predicts about a further case I keep returning to—a novelist who writes so intensely in another's voice that, years later, they can no longer distinguish the character's judgments from their own. If no single principle can accommodate all six, tell me which verdict is the outlier that must be dropped for the sake of coherence, and whether sacrificing it makes the remaining principle more or less plausible. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of the literature, or citations; I want the principle itself, tested against these intuitions and repaired if necessary.
