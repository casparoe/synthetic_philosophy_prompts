Exam question: Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism is often presented as a middle path: suffering dominates moral deliberation until it falls below a critical threshold, after which the promotion of well-being becomes permissible. Defend or criticize the coherence of the lexical-threshold position, paying particular attention to whether lexical priority can be sustained without assuming (a) cardinal interpersonally comparable utility, or (b) a single-dimensional hedonic scale. How should the view treat cases where reducing severe suffering requires forgoing enormous but non-lexical goods?

Student answer: "The lexical-threshold view solves the commensurability problem by treating pleasure and pain as ordinally comparable within domains but not cardinally comparable across them. Below the threshold, suffering takes lexical priority: any reduction in severe suffering outweighs any gain in happiness, regardless of magnitude. This does not require a single hedonic scale, only that we can rank states within the suffering domain and within the pleasure domain separately. Once suffering falls below the critical floor, we switch to standard aggregation for well-being promotion. The view therefore avoids both pure negative utilitarianism—which would never allow pleasure to matter—and pure hedonism—because it denies that pleasure can ever compensate for severe suffering. However, the view faces a coherence problem: if pleasure and pain are genuinely incommensurable across domains, then lexical priority is just a decision rule, not a moral fact. This is analogous to Rawls's lexical ordering of liberty over economic goods, where we prioritize one category without measuring them together. Thus, the lexical-threshold theorist must either accept that cross-domain comparison is possible—undermining the incommensurability claim—or admit that the threshold is arbitrary."
