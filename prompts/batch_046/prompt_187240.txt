During a recent meeting of my epistemology reading group, two advanced doctoral students—whom I will call M and N—fell into a sustained disagreement about the interaction between Nozick’s tracking conditions and the principle of epistemic closure. M opened with the familiar skeptical challenge: if tracking requires that in the nearest worlds where p is false I do not believe p, then I clearly do not track the denial of brain-in-a-vat hypotheses, since in the nearest BIV-worlds I still believe I am embodied; yet if I genuinely know that I have hands and also know that having hands entails not being a BIV, closure should transmit that knowledge to the conclusion that I am not a BIV. Therefore, M concluded, tracking and closure are incompatible, and closure must be abandoned. N objected that M treats closure as an unrestricted transmission rule across any logical entailment, whereas Nozick’s conditions apply specifically to the modal profile of individual belief-states; closure, N argued, should be interpreted as holding only within a unified epistemic method, so that inferring q from known p via entailment does not automatically subject q to the same tracking conditions that govern p, without this constituting a failure of closure itself.

M responded that N’s method-relative restriction saves closure only by draining it of content. If we are permitted to block transmission whenever the conclusion fails to track, then closure never generates a problematic result in the first place; it becomes a descriptive claim about method-internal coherence rather than a normative principle governing the expansion of knowledge through deduction. At that point, M added, closure loses its traditional role in anti-skeptical argumentation, since it can no longer bridge ordinary knowledge to the rejection of radical skeptical scenarios. N then proposed a refinement she called inferential-path tracking: a conclusion reached by deduction can still satisfy a modified tracking condition if the inferential pathway itself is modally sensitive—that is, if in nearby worlds where the conclusion is false, the subject would not have executed that particular inference—rather than requiring direct world-to-belief sensitivity for the conclusion considered in isolation. She maintained that this allows closure to remain substantive: the path from having hands to not being a BIV is not path-sensitive, because in nearby BIV-worlds the premises remain subjectively identical and the inference still occurs, so closure correctly fails here, yet other deductive paths might preserve knowledge transmission where the reasoning step itself varies across worlds.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of M’s first two moves and each of N’s two replies, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Clarify who is correct about what—particularly whether N’s path-sensitive formulation genuinely rescues closure or merely renames the restriction M identified, and whether M is right that any such rescue empties closure of anti-skeptical force. Finally, say what both interlocutors appear to be missing, such as whether tracking conditions should apply differently to beliefs retained versus beliefs acquired, or whether there is a principled distinction between logical entailment and evidential support that neither party has exploited. Indicate, if you can, which side holds the stronger overall position, without simply declaring a winner by fiat.
