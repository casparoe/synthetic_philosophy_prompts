I've been preping a unit on virute ethics for my intro course and I keep getting stuck on Franklin's moral perfection scheme — you know, the thirteen virtues, the little book where he tracks his faults like some kind of 18th-century spreadsheet nerd. I want to know what actually distinguishes real moral improvement from just performative self-management, and my gut reactions to cases are kind of a mess.

Here's my verdicts. Franklin deciding he'll practice temperance by eating only one kind of food at each meal and sticking to it for decades: that's genuine progress, even though it's just a behavioral rule. A person who never lies on their taxes because they're terrified of the IRS: not real honesty, just risk aversion. Someone who masters "industry" by working ninety-hour weeks and burning out everyone around them: I want to say that's vice disguised as virtue, not perfected industry at all. But then Franklin himself admits he never achieved perfection and kept finding new faults in his ledger for his whole life — is he a failure, or is the perpetual striving itself the perfected state? And here's the case that really bugs me: imagine someone uses an AI assistant that listens to everything they say, flags every minor deception in real time, and feeds them corrections until they literally never lie. They feel no internal tug of temptation — the machine handles it. Is that perfected sincerity, or is it outsourced virtue with no character change?

There's also a tension I can't resolve. Franklin treats the virtues like separate experiments — perfect one, then move to the next. But I also think real virtue has to be integrated; you can't be genuinely temperate if you're cruel, right? So is his modular approach just wrong? Is there one principle that gets all my verdicts — the food-rule case, the tax-cheater case, the burnout workaholic, Franklin's lifelong imperfection, and the AI-corrected liar — or do I have to drop one? If you can save them all, what's the principle? If not, which verdict should I abandon, and why? And what would your account say about a case I haven't mentioned: someone who practices humility by deliberately putting themselves in humiliating situations? Is that Franklin-style perfection or just masochism in a virtue costume?
