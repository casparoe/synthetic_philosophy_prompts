I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates who have completed an introductory course in epistemology but have not yet studied second-order reasoning. The topic is epistemic akrasia arising from higher-order evidence: the structural situation in which an agent's first-order evaluation of the available evidence strongly supports belief that P, yet she simultaneously possesses robust evidence that this very evaluation is unreliable, leaving her torn between trusting her reasoning and distrusting it. Rather than explaining the concept to me, plan the teaching session. First, lay out the dependency chain: identify which conceptual component must be secured before the audience can recognize the puzzle as genuinely puzzling rather than as a simple mistake, and specify the exact order in which the following must be introduced—first-order evidence, the transparency thesis about reasoning, the object-level versus meta-level distinction, and the analogy to practical akrasia—justifying each dependency. Second, specify the carrying example. It must satisfy three constraints: the first-order case for the belief is genuinely compelling and not obviously erroneous; the higher-order defeat is equally compelling and cannot be reduced to new first-order counterevidence; and the agent experiences a structural conflict rather than mere confusion. Do not use the standard exhausted-mathematician or cognitive-bias-drug scenarios; propose a concrete scenario meeting these conditions and explain why it carries the idea. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order students will form them: I anticipate they will treat higher-order defeat as just another first-order reason against P, assume that rationality unproblematically demands immediate belief revision, and conflate epistemic akrasia with practical weakness of will. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and with what conceptual move. Fourth, design one check-for-understanding question that distinguishes someone who grasps the structural tension from someone who has only memorized the terminology. Finally, state what must be deliberately omitted at this level—such as formal frameworks in epistemic logic, specific historical attributions, or debates about whether rational response is unique—and justify each omission. The deliverable is the teaching architecture, not the lecture itself.
