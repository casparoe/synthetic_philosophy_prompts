I have been discussing the Epicurean argument—that death is not harmful to the one who dies, since harm requires a subject to experience it—with a colleague in philosophy of mind. She has offered consistent verdicts on several cases: she judges that the sudden death of a healthy adult with rich future-oriented projects is genuinely bad; that the death of a newborn infant is not bad; and that the death of a patient in an irreversible vegetative state with no prospect of recovery is not bad. Now consider an engineered scenario: a synthetic consciousness is instantiated each morning from an identical blueprint. Every instance is fully conscious, holds the same long-term goals, and has loaded memories of prior instances, yet there is no causal or psychological continuity linking one day’s instance to the next—only informational replication. One morning the system is not activated, and no further instances ever occur. Would she regard this permanent cessation as bad for the affected sequence? Please identify at least two distinct principles that could consistently account for her three earlier verdicts, state what each principle predicts about the blueprint case, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasoning, indicate which earlier case is the closest analog, say honestly whether her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, and propose one further hypothetical case—different from those already discussed—that would best discriminate between the two principles.
