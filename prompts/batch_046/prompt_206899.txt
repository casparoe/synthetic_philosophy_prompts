I serve on a regional health-equity board that must evaluate funding proposals affecting populations with different baseline welfare levels. We need a reusable decision procedure grounded in prioritarian reasoning—benefits to the worse off receive greater weight—but it must be robust against the leveling-down objection: it must never approve a proposal that reduces anyone's welfare solely to diminish disparity, and it must distinguish opportunity-cost trade-offs from direct harm to the better off. Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions my reviewers can run without cardinal welfare metrics: what must be established at each step, what answer permits continuation, where the procedure terminates in approval, where it terminates in rejection, and where it must escalate to the board because the conflict is evaluative rather than calculable. Include a concise weighting rule applicable with ordinal rankings and rough estimates, and specify what counts as sufficient evidence for each input. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a vaccination campaign that saves lives primarily in a high-baseline region while diverting resources from a lower-baseline region's nutrition program; (b) a redistribution funded by eliminating elective-care subsidies for a middle-baseline group, with no direct benefit to that group; (c) a mandate that caps all regional health interventions at the level available to the worst-off region, thereby reducing care quality everywhere to achieve equality. For each case, show the path through the procedure, identify the step that forces a judgment call rather than a procedural answer, and note whether the result conflicts with intuitive egalitarian demands. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle—for example, whether a small weighted gain for the worst off can justify a large absolute loss for others—and flag any built-in misfire where prioritarian weights and equality intuitions diverge irreducibly.
