I am preparing a fifty-minute lecture for first-year philosophy students on the thesis that conscious experience functions as a kind of user illusion — that phenomenal appearance is a constructed representational interface for practical navigation rather than a transparent window onto underlying neural reality. I want a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. What dependency structure should govern the sequence: must they first secure the appearance-reality distinction in ordinary perceptual cases before applying it analogously to introspection, or can the two be developed in parallel? Which single concrete analogy or case should carry the core idea — perhaps a navigational interface, perhaps the desktop metaphor — and what properties must it have and lack so that it does not collapse into either eliminativism about experience or a hidden-observer Cartesian picture; in particular, why should I avoid an analogy that invites students to posit a literal inner user behind the screen? Map the predictable misconceptions they will form — for instance, that calling consciousness an illusion entails it is unreal, or that there must be a deceived subject who receives the interface — and specify at exactly which point in the sequence each should be intercepted and with what brief corrective. Propose one diagnostic question for the close of the session that distinguishes genuine grasp of the representational structure from mere slogan repetition. Finally, say what should be deliberately left out at this level — such as the full machinery of predictive processing or global workspace models — and justify the omission given the audience and the time constraint.
