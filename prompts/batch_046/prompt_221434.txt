I have been working through the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda and its logical formulation in syādvāda, especially the saptabhaṅgī scheme that assigns one of seven conditional predicates—assertion, denial, inexpressibility, and their combinations—to any property of a substance from a given standpoint (naya). Applying this to metaphysics, I considered a diachronic identity case: from one naya a person persists through time by retaining an essential jīva, while from another naya the person is a momentary aggregation with no enduring substrate. Because syādvāda requires every claim to be prefixed with syāt (perhaps), it seems both descriptions must be affirmed simultaneously without restriction, which suggests that anekāntavāda treats temporal identity as both preserved and destroyed in exactly the same respect. That looks structurally identical to Graham Priest’s dialetheism about change, where contradictory states genuinely co-obtain. Yet Jain commentators consistently say that anekāntavāda avoids the extremism (ekānta) of unqualified affirmation and denial, not by embracing contradiction but by contextualizing it within standpoints. So my confusion is this: if the seven-fold formula allows both “it is” and “it is not” to apply to the same object without qualification, how is this different from saying the contradiction is literally true? And if the naya framework is doing the work of keeping the descriptions separate, why does syādvāda formulate them together in a single compound statement rather than isolating them in non-interacting perspectives?
