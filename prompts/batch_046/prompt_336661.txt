I serve on the advisory board of a national museum currently redesigning its permanent gallery on the late Roman West, and I write about science and public ideas for a living. The board must settle on an interpretive framework for the fifth-century Western Empire before our design deadline in six weeks, and I have been asked to circulate a formal decision memo for Thursday's meeting.

We are divided among four positions that have each acquired serious institutional advocates. One faction defends a Gibbon-style institutional-decay narrative centered on administrative corruption, military overextension, and the erosion of civic virtue. A second emphasizes external barbarian migration and military pressure as the primary driver of political fragmentation. A third points to climate stress and environmental toxicity—including lead contamination of water systems and aridification visible in Western Mediterranean proxy records—as decisive systemic stressors. A fourth rejects the "fall" framing altogether, arguing that what occurred was a gradual transformation in which Roman institutional forms persisted through reconfiguration rather than collapse.

Rather than ask for a summary of these views, I need the memo to recommend a concrete curatorial strategy: should the gallery present a single master narrative, a plural multi-causal account without hierarchical framing, or a specific hybrid? The recommendation must specify how the physical layout, artifact grouping, and captioning should embody that choice, and it must propose a review timeline—say, eighteen months after opening—to assess whether visitor comprehension matches our intent.

The museum's education director has also proposed integrating a comparative resilience-studies model that treats the empire not as a unitary state but as a network of coupled institutional subsystems—fiscal, military, civic, and ecological—that decoupled at different rates across regions. The memo should evaluate whether this framework genuinely clarifies the historical dynamics for a general audience or whether it imposes an anachronistic organizational vocabulary. It should recommend either full integration into the public-facing narrative, restriction to the scholarly companion volume, or omission.

Most importantly, the memo must include an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side it does not take. If the recommendation favors transformation over collapse, it must explain why rapid administrative discontinuity in parts of Gaul and Britain, and the disappearance of certain Roman material cultures, do not falsify that framing. If it favors a multi-causal collapse narrative, it must explain why such framing does not mislead visitors into imagining a uniform catastrophe across Italy, North Africa, and the Eastern provinces.

Please draft the memo addressed to the board. It should have a clear bottom-line recommendation with actionable reasoning, should avoid bibliographic references or citation lists since we need conceptual clarity rather than a literature survey, and should be written in a tone appropriate to an institutional decision document that a journalist might circulate among scholars and museum professionals alike.
