Ever since my roommate and I got into it over this playlist-generating algorithim we both messed with, I've been obsessed with figuring out what I actually think about owning ideas. Here are my gut calls, and I can't make them line up.

If two architects on opposite sides of the world design the same bridge without talking, neither stole anything — totally fine.

If a master carver teaches me a specific joint technique and I use it to build furniture I sell, I don't owe them royalties; but if I film myself teaching that exact technique in a paid class, I do owe them. That feels definately right to me.

But if a pharma company patents a plant remedy that a specific village has used for centuries, that's wrong, even if their chemists discovered the compound independantly in a lab.

Then there's this: I have an idea in a dream, write it down, and later a scientist explains it matches their unpublished theory — but I never heard them talk. I think it's fully mine. But if I did overhear them once at a party and forgot, it's partly theirs, even if the idea looks identical on paper.

I'm stuck on this one: my friend writes a poem and I change one word that completely shifts the meaning. Do we co-own it? I keep going back and forth. Some days I say yes because the new meaning is the valuable part; other days I say no because the structure is all theirs.

And there's this weirder case — if I internalize every chord progression I've ever heard and write a new song that's just a statistical blend of all of them, I haven't stolen any specific song, but it still feels sketchy. Is that closer to the bridge case or the carving-class case?

Is there a single principle behind all this, or am I just being inconsistent? And whatever principle you find, what does it predict about my poem co-ownership thing? I don't need named theories, I just want to know which of my verdicts I'd have to drop to get a clean rule.
