I am a philosophy professor developing a research agenda at the intersection of aesthetics, cognitive science, and the philosophy of design, and I would like you to assist me by drafting a short research proposal. Please adopt the role of a senior philosopher of art with particular expertise in conceptual categorization and morphological analysis. Before you write, please think carefully and begin with a brief internal brainstorming session: distinguish carefully between statistical clustering in feature-space, normative grammatical constraint, and the role of practical judgment in recognizing instances of a kind.

The proposal should be approximately 900 words, suitable for a one-semester graduate workshop, and should address the philosophical problem raised by the Letter Spirit framework: what makes every instance of the letterform 'a' recognizably an 'a' despite radical variation in stroke weight, proportion, curvature, and decorative style? Frame this not as a technical problem in feature extraction, but as an inquiry into whether the 'essence' of a letterform is best understood as a geometric invariant (a skeleton or grid), a family-resemblance cluster distributed across a high-dimensional style-space, or a normative standard governing acceptable deviation—something closer to a grammatical rule than to an average shape.

To prevent the inquiry from collapsing into familiar debates about natural kinds versus conventional categories, inject the following somewhat niche conceptual tension: introduce the idea of 'morphological humility,' by which I mean the perceiver's tacit acknowledgment that any particular instance of an 'a' might violate the usual geometric expectations—distorted proportions, broken strokes, asymmetrical bowls—yet remain fully legible because it respects deeper, non-quantifiable constraints of contrast, closure, and directional flow. Your proposal should ask whether this notion is compatible with any framework that cleanly separates 'style' from 'skeleton,' or whether it implies that the two are interdependent in ways that undermine the search for a context-independent essence. State the precise research question, explain why existing answers in aesthetics and cognitive science leave it genuinely open, describe the method (conceptual analysis supplemented by constructed visual examples rather than empirical data collection), specify what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure, and provide an honest assessment of whether the scope is realistic for a single semester. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; refer to positions and arguments by descriptive name only, and avoid attributing specific claims to named authors unless necessary for clarity.
