I have been working with digital archivists to develop a cataloging protocol for reconstructed historical texts, and in doing so I have noticed a recurring structural problem that I cannot name precisely. Imagine two digital files that are identical in every accessible respect—same text, same formatting, identical checksums—where one is a direct digitization of an original manuscript and the other is a later scholarly reconstruction in which illegible passages were inferred from context and seamlessly integrated. For any user interacting with the archive, the two files present exactly the same properties; the divergence lies solely in the causal provenance, which is either buried in inaccessible institutional records or permanently lost. Nevertheless, the archivists insist that treating them as the same object would obscure something important about what each file represents.

I am trying to determine whether philosophy offers a concept for this kind of ontological distinction—one that depends not on observable differences but on a causal history that is in principle unavailable to anyone who encounters the object. I am aware of debates surrounding the identity of indiscernibles, but those typically concern properties that could be observed under ideal conditions; here the inaccessibility is structural, built into the separation between the user-facing object and its hidden origin. Does this specific configuration have an established name, and if so, how is it distinguished from ordinary epistemic uncertainty about an object's history? If there is no precise term, what boundary conditions should govern a new one—would any unrecoverable causal divergence create this status, or only those where the functional identity is perfect?
