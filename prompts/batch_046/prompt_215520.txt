Consider a philosopher of social science—call her the advisor—who has ruled, case by case, on whether cultural values can operate as independent causes in historical explanation. Her verdicts to date: in the classic case of the Protestant ethic and the rise of capitalist rationalization, she accepts that Calvinist ascetic values were a genuine causal factor, not reducible to prior economic change; in the explanation of the French Revolution, she rejects value-causation, holding that revolutionary ideologies rationalized material class interests that fully determined the outcome; in Japan's Meiji restoration, she accepts that Confucian-trained elites' normative commitments causally shaped which Western institutions were adopted and which were rejected; in the 19th-century European demographic transition, she denies independent causal status to shifting family values, treating falling fertility as driven by urbanization and mortality with values tracking rather than directing change; in the abolition of foot-binding in late Qing and Republican China, she accepts that converging missionary, reformist, and nationalist value-commitments functioned as an autonomous mechanism, but only after reaching a critical density of institutional transmission through schools, print culture, and legal reform.

Now consider a proposed explanation of regional divergence during deindustrialization: in the Ruhr Valley during the 1980s, economic contraction and sectoral decline were comparable to those in other post-industrial regions that rapidly secularized and dissolved communal ties, yet local religious and communal practices persisted. The proposed explanation appeals not to economic interest but to a sustained local "craft ethos"—a normative commitment to manual skill as a source of personal identity, transmitted through apprenticeship networks and reinforced by non-economic social rituals—that selectively preserved practices incompatible with pure economic rationalization. The explanation treats this ethos as an autonomous causal factor shaping how economic shocks were interpreted and responded to, rather than as a cultural gloss on material adaptation.

Predict the advisor's verdict on this Ruhr case. Identify at least two plausible interpretive frameworks—such as an institutional-Weberian framework requiring values to achieve cross-stratum institutional density before they can drive macro-level change, versus a causal-gap interactionist framework allowing values to cause whenever material conditions underdetermine outcomes—each consistent with her prior five rulings. Explain which framework her track record best supports and why the other is not eliminated by her earlier verdicts. Then specify the single-feature variation of a further case that would definitively settle which framework she is running.
