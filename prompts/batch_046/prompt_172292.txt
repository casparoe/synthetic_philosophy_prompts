You are delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled "One Vocabulary, Many Failures," convened for an audience split evenly between machine-learning researchers who work on reward modeling, interpretability, and scalable oversight, and scholars from law, political theory, and philosophy of science who are drafting the regulatory frameworks within which those technical systems will operate. The organizers have asked you to set the intellectual frame for the proceedings without delivering a lecture, and they have warned you that several attendees on both sides already believe "the alignment problem" has been essentially defined—whether as a problem of optimizing reward functions or as a problem of democratic governance.

Your remarks should take as their central structural premise that "the alignment problem" is not a single problem but a cluster of at least three conceptually distinct challenges that are routinely conflated because they share the same vocabulary of intent, value, and control. You should distinguish them precisely as follows:

First, the specification problem: the difficulty of translating human normative commitments into formal objective functions without stripping away the contextual judgments that originally made those commitments meaningful.

Second, the delegation problem: the difficulty of transferring decision-making authority to an automated system while preserving human accountability structures—legal, moral, and institutional—that were designed for human agents.

Third, the stability problem: the difficulty of maintaining whatever alignment relation has been achieved as the system's capabilities, its operating environment, or its self-model change over time.

These three distinctions must do real work in the speech: they should explain why a technical breakthrough in one area does not automatically resolve concerns raised in another, and they should justify the workshop's parallel-track structure by showing that cross-track conversation is necessary precisely because progress in one domain can obscure failure in another. The framing should also constrain what you can and cannot say: you must not present yourself as having solved any of the three, you must not imply that alignment is purely a technical matter awaiting more compute, and you must not reduce the technical work to mere politics. The speech needs to hold both halves of the room in genuine respect.

Write the remarks in a formal but accessible tone—precise enough that the researchers recognize genuine conceptual distinctions rather than philosophical ornament, but free of jargon that assumes familiarity with specific architectures, loss functions, or named algorithms. The speech should run about five to six minutes when spoken, roughly 750 to 900 words. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not ask the audience to read specific papers.
