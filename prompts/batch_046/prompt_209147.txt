I am preparing a graduate seminar on medieval political philosophy and find myself dissatisfied with the standard framing of Aquinas's position on tyrannicide. Too often, the literature collapses into a binary—either Aquinas licenses assassination by private citizens or he categorically forbids all resistance—without first specifying what an adequate interpretation must preserve. Before I assign any secondary literature, I want a rigorous specification: what are the desiderata that any adequate theory of Aquinas's doctrine of resistance to tyranny must satisfy?

Please construct the requirements list. Each desideratum should be paired with the case or conceptual distinction that motivates it—for instance, the need to distinguish a ruler who lacks legitimate title altogether from one who abuses a valid office; the need to explain why private killing is generally forbidden even when public deposition might be permitted; and the need to remain consistent with Aquinas's broader metaphysics of law, in which an unjust command does not bind but rebellion still risks damaging the common good. I also want the list to include a less-discussed constraint: any adequate account must be able to address whether institutional or "distributed" tyranny—where corruption is structural rather than concentrated in a single person—falls within the scope of Aquinas's personalist framework, or whether applying the doctrine to such cases requires conceptual extension that the text does not authorize. For each requirement, indicate whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable for an interpretation that claims to be genuinely Thomistic rather than anachronistically liberal.

Then, identify which single desideratum does the most damage to the most common readings—particularly those that read Aquinas as endorsing a subjective right of revolution or, conversely, those that treat him as an absolute quietist. Briefly score the three main interpretive camps—the liberal-revolutionary reading, the strict obedience reading, and what we might call the constitutional-analogist reading—against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves: the conceptual work lies in separating genuine interpretive obligations from modern prejudices. If my request rests on a confused premise—for example, if separating interpretive from normative desiderata is impossible here, or if the distinction between structural and personal tyranny is a red herring in Thomistic terms—say so plainly rather than complying politely. I need the list to stand up under scrutiny, not to flatter my framing.
