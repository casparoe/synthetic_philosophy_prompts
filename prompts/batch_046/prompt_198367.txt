You are an expert philosopher of biology. Before answering, think step by step about the logical tensions between reproductive isolation, ecological niche, and phylogenetic continuity as criteria for specieshood, and identify where they conflict.

Here are my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases. Extract the principle that best unifies them, state it precisely, and tell me which verdict—if any—I must abandon to avoid an ad hoc patchwork. Then apply your principle to an unlisted case.

Case 1: Two frog populations separated by a highway can interbreed in captivity with fully fertile offspring, but in the wild they never meet and have diverged in mating call frequency. Verdict: one species.

Case 2: Two morphologically identical firefly populations occupy the same meadow but flash on different rhythms, so mating never occurs. Verdict: two species.

Case 3: A circular chain of salamander populations around a mountain range: each adjacent pair interbreeds, but the two ends of the ring do not. Verdict: the entire ring is one species.

Case 4: Bacteria in hot springs and cold streams exchange genes horizontally within each habitat but not between them, with no migration across the thermal boundary. Verdict: two ecological species.

Case 5: Two plant populations separated by a glacier for millennia now interbreed freely with no loss of fertility after the ice retreated. Verdict: one species.

Case 6: Two bird populations interbreed in a narrow hybrid zone, but the hybrids suffer severely reduced lifetime reproductive success. Verdict: two species.

What is the simplest coherent principle behind these judgments? If one case breaks the pattern, name it explicitly and explain the revision. Finally, apply your principle to a case I have not listed: an asexual lineage splits into two genetically isolated clusters with different host preferences but identical morphology—does this yield one species or two, and why?
