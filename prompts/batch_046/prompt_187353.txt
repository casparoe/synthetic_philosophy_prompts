I have no formal training in philosophy or history, but I have been trying to work out for myself whether the Holocaust should be treated as incomparable to other atrocities or whether comparison helps us understand it, and I recently encountered the argument—associated with the sociologist Zygmunt Bauman—that modern bureaucracy and rational division of labor did not fail to stop the genocide but actually enabled it by creating moral distance between actions and outcomes. I would like to read Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List through exactly this tension. Does the film portray the killing as an eruption of irrational, personal evil—Amon Göth shooting prisoners from his balcony—or as the output of a modern, administrative system, with trains, lists, and factories? Is Schindler's rescue list itself, a bureaucratic document that saves lives, an illustration of Bauman's point that modern rationality is ambivalent rather than inherently humane? And does the film's intense focus on individual moral heroes and villains obscure the systemic, structural nature of the destruction that Bauman emphasizes? At the same time, does the film's visceral depiction of the Kraków ghetto liquidation and the camps insist on a singular horror that resists comparison with other historical events? I am preparing notes for a small community discussion group, and I want a reading that takes both the film's specific scenes and these ideas seriously: where the film dramatizes the philosophy, where it complicates it, and where an honest critic must say the work pulls in the opposite direction.
