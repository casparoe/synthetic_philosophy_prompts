After several months of caring for a family member through a prolonged and painful illness, I have found myself drawn to the view—sometimes called tranquilism—that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while all positive states are at best instrumentally good. Before I commit to this framework, I want the full specification of what any adequate theory of well-being must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: the specific lives or scenarios it must correctly rank, with the reasoning behind each—such as a life of continuous mild contentment with no suffering versus one of deep achievement interspersed with severe pain; a life in which all desires are satisfied versus one in which no strong desires ever form; and, crucially, the state of being free from craving after it has been actively eliminated versus never having developed the capacity for it in the first place. For each case, explain why it functions as a genuine test rather than a prejudice of one camp. Then specify the distinctions the theory must preserve—between the absence of suffering and the presence of goods, between the value of ending existing craving and preventing its formation, and between subjective satisfaction and objective flourishing—along with any results from biology or psychology it cannot contradict without serious cost. Identify which pairs of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, particularly whether any view can consistently deny intrinsic value to all positive states while still explaining why rational agents pursue them. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are mainly preferences of hedonist or objective-list theorists. Finally, score tranquilism, classical hedonism, and a desire-fulfillment account briefly against the list, noting for each contender the single failure that does the most damage.
