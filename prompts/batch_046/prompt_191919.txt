I sit on the Interdisciplinary Historical Sciences Funding Board, and we must reach a decision on Proposal 2024-H7, entitled 'Symmetrical Divergence Mapping: A Counterfactual Framework for the Great Divergence'. The applicants propose a three-year comparative study built around the question 'Why Britain and not China?', treating both societies as paired trajectories diverging from a shared institutional and ecological baseline around 1750. Their core method involves constructing 'counterfactual accessibility profiles' for each case — specifying which combinations of conditions would have needed to differ, and in what minimal form, for an industrial breakthrough to have occurred in the alternative society.

Our board is divided. One faction maintains that this framing captures the classic Needham question precisely, and that treating the cases symmetrically forces explicit causal claims rather than allowing narrative exceptionalism to go unexamined. The opposing faction, led by our methodological adviser, argues that the proposal commits a 'modal scope error': it conflates metaphysical possibility with historical accessibility, treating path-dependent institutional processes as if they were interchangeable variables in a controlled comparison. They further claim that selecting Britain as the realised outcome and then retrospectively identifying differentiating conditions introduces a 'counterfactual selection bias', risking circular attribution.

We require a written opinion for circulation before our meeting on 14 June, recommending approval, approval with conditions, or rejection. If approval with conditions is advised, the conditions must specify concrete revisions to the applicants' protocol that they could actually implement — for instance, restrictions on how counterfactual scenarios are generated or validated. The reasoning must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with genuine seriousness: specifically, whether symmetrical divergence mapping can avoid treating contingent pathways as interchangeable without collapsing into an unfalsifiable narrative, and whether the concept of 'historical accessibility' can be operationalised in a way that distinguishes it from mere metaphysical possibility. The opinion should assume only general familiarity with comparative historical method and elementary modal reasoning; it must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Please write in British English.
