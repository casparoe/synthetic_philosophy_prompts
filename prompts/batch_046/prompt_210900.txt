I'm trying to determine what I actually think about phenomenal judgments if epiphenomenalism is true, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind my intuitions. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, when I sincerely judge "I am now experiencing a sharp pain," I treat that judgment as correct, even though I assume the pain itself plays no causal role in producing it. Second, if a physically identical zombie twin makes the identical report, I treat its judgment as false or empty—there is nothing it is like for the twin, so the judgment lacks an object. Third, a blindsight patient who sincerely reports "I have no conscious visual experience in my left visual field" strikes me as correct, and this correct judgment is produced entirely without phenomenal involvement. Fourth, a philosopher who infers from epiphenomenalist premises "my belief that I am conscious is not caused by my consciousness" strikes me as having reached a true conclusion; yet I also feel that this conclusion undermines the reliability of all her phenomenal judgments, including the very judgment she just made. Fifth, if a pharmacological intervention suppresses all phenomenal experience but leaves linguistic reporting mechanisms intact, and the subject reports "the experience of blue is vivid right now," I regard that report as false rather than merely misleading. Sixth, consider a hypothetical "phenomenal echo" substrate: a non-biological system replicates the exact neural dynamics of a conscious state and generates first-person reports about "what it is like," but by design any phenomenal properties present remain outside every causal loop, including those that produce the reports. I find myself genuinely unsure whether to treat its reports as calibrated analogues of mine or as sophisticated empty mechanisms.

The pair that keeps pulling me in opposite directions is the first and second: if physical processes are identical and phenomenal properties are causally inert, I seem to need a reason why my judgment tracks truth while the zombie's does not, yet any reason that appeals to causal efficacy contradicts the premise. Meanwhile, the third case suggests that correct phenomenal judgments need no phenomenal cause, which weakens the demand for tracking; but the fifth case suggests that intact physical mechanisms are not sufficient for correctness, which revives some normative constraint.

Is there a principle, or a small family of candidates, that captures all six verdicts? Please state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and specify its predictions for two further cases I have not listed: a split-brain scenario in which the left hemisphere reports absence of experience in a visual field where the right hemisphere might harbor isolated phenomenal content, and a caregiver who judges "my patient is in pain" based solely on behavioral and contextual cues without any direct phenomenal access. Most importantly, please state honestly which of my verdicts must be abandoned if I want to avoid an ad hoc principle, and say whether the "phenomenal echo" case should be grouped with the first case or the second.
