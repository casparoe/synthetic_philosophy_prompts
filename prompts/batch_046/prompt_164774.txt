For decades, interpreters of al-Ghazālī have been divided over whether his occasionalism—especially in his critique of the philosophers and associated writings—undermines the possibility of natural science or merely removes a metaphysical overlay that empirical practice never required. Both sides agree on the core textual claims: he denies that created things possess causal powers, asserts that God directly produces every particular event, and accepts that regularities occur according to divine habit. They also agree that occasionalism is logically consistent and that predictive success does not, by itself, entail necessary connections. Yet the debate has stalled: one camp argues that without necessary causation induction becomes rationally groundless, since expectation of the future loses any modal justification; the other camp responds that stable correlation is sufficient for science and that al-Ghazālī explicitly preserves such correlation. My working hypothesis is that this impasse is not mainly a historical disagreement about al-Ghazālī's intent, nor a straightforward metaphysical dispute about causation, but rather a hidden disagreement about what I will call explanatory inertia—the degree to which a scientific explanation must carry modal force across instances to count as rationally justified rather than merely descriptively adequate. Those who defend science's compatibility with occasionalism appear to treat inertia as dispensable; those who deny compatibility treat it as indispensable. Is this diagnosis correct? Or is the stall better explained by the sides answering different questions under one name—one group asking whether occasionalism coheres with empirical practice, the other asking whether it can supply a normative foundation for inductive reasoning—so that they are not actually contradicting each other? Might there also be a concealed formal issue, such as whether occasionalism can support the counterfactual dependencies that inductive inference seems to require? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest answer about whether any distinction—perhaps an explicit account of explanatory inertia, a formal analysis of induction without necessary connection, or a reconceptualization of the original question—could move the debate, or whether it rests on bedrock intuitions about rational justification that neither side can independently argue for.
