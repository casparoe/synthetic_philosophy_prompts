I'm in my second year of a psych phd and our lab just watched a social-priming study collapse on direct replication — same n, same protocol, p went from .03 to .42. My PI wants us to switch everything to Bayesian methods but I'm not convinced that's anything more than changing the dashboard lights. I want a decision tree for our journal club that sorts people by what they actually think caused the crisis and what stats should do about it, not by whether they call themselves a frequentist.

Start from that concrete case: you run the protocol, get p = .42, and have to interpret the failure. First branch: do replication failures mostly show the original effect was false, or that our measurements are too noisy to detect small true effects? Second: is the core problem p-hacking and garden-of-forking-paths, or is it that null-hypothesis testing forces false binary thinking? Third: is the right response procedural (pre-regestration, larger N, blinding) or statistical (Bayes factors, shrinkage, severity testing)? Fourth: if you go statistical, do you think Bayes actually fixes the epistemology or just relocates the arbitrariness into the prior?

The leaves should include: (A) pure frequentist with bigger samples and stricter alpha; (B) proceduralist who keeps NHST but locks the protocol; (C) Bayesian reformist who updates priors and reports Bayes factors; (D) likelihoodist who rejects both p-values and Bayes in favor of pure likelihood ratios; (E) sociology-of-science view that stats are a red herring and publication incentives are the real disease; (F) my weird hybrid — frequentist pre-registration plus Bayesian sensitivity analysis used only as a robustness check, with zero ontological commitment to priors; and (G) the 'construct-validity-first' view that says no statistical framework can save a study that never measured what it claimed to measure. For each leaf, name it (or mark 'unnamed'), give one sentence describing its stance on the failed replication, and state thier most awkward commitment. Then map out the full tree: which combinations of answers are occupied, which are empty, and for each empty slot say whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Every branch question should be answerable without knowing the labels — just ask what someone thinks about the failed study. Don't include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; half my cohort is off-campus this term and can't access the library anyway. Don't lean on obscure philosophers of science unless the position genuinely needs them.

Present the tree as nested bullet points with the split-claim at each node. At the very end, put exactly one line that reads Verdict: [the label of the empty or incoherent branch-combination you find most interesting, or 'none'].
