Consider the following paraphrase of the argument traditionally associated with Aristotle’s treatment of the sea-battle problem in his discussion of future contingents. The text asks whether it is necessary that a naval battle will occur tomorrow, or necessary that it will not occur. It observes that if every affirmation and denial must be determinately true or false in advance—so that if it is now true that there will be a battle, then its occurrence is already fixed—then contingency appears to vanish and all events happen of necessity. Against this, the passage notes that statements about the past and present seem necessarily true or false because the past is unchangeable, but it worries that applying the same principle of bivalence to future singular events forces us to treat them as equally predetermined. The argument ends obscurely: with future contingent matters, it is apparently not necessary that one part of the contradiction be true and the other false, though the text does not clearly say whether this means such statements lack truth-values altogether, or whether they possess them in some weaker, non-necessary mode.

I am trying to understand whether the reasoning here depends on an implicit asymmetry between past and future truth-bearers—specifically, whether the author holds that actualization in the past creates a metaphysical constraint on contradictory possibilities that does not apply to the future, not merely because of causal determination but because being actualized at a temporal index excludes the contradictory potential from being true at that same index. Some interpreters suggest this amounts to a kind of diachronic exclusion principle: once a potential is realized, its contradictory is not merely false but metaphysically incompatible with being true at that temporal location, whereas future potentials remain jointly compatible with their contradictories in the present. Others read the passage as simply failing to resolve a tension about bivalence across time.
