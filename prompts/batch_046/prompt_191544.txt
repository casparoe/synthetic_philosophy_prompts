I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of historiography, and we've been debating whether historians can ever fully suspend the normative and conceptual standards of their own era. In our readings, a recurring analogy treats the past as a foreign country—spatially distant cultures have customs that should be understood internally rather than graded by the visitor's home norms, and by parallel reasoning, historians should approach past societies as ethnographers rather than as moral auditors, bracketing modern categories like individual autonomy or universal human rights to avoid presentism. The analogy is usually invoked to license methodological humility: if temporal distance works like cultural distance, then applying contemporary frameworks to premodern arrangements is not just anachronistic but a kind of intellectual imperialism.

Could you audit this analogy systematically? First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the traveler, the homeland, the foreign customs, the language barrier, and the act of judgment or evaluation? Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the anti-presentist conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In particular, does the analogy quietly import a false symmetry between spatial separation and temporal separation? Does it treat moral norms as if they were local etiquette rather than claims with potential universal scope? And does it obscure the fact that historians are not voluntary tourists but citizens of the present with unavoidable stakes in how the past is narrated for current political and ethical life?

I'd also like you to consider a modified version that some of my classmates have floated: instead of a tourist, imagine the historian as a bilingual translator who must render past normative claims into modern conceptual vocabulary without endorsing them—what we might call moral bilingualism. Does this revised analogy avoid the relativist pitfalls of the foreign-country version, or does it merely relocate presentist bias into the unavoidable choices involved in translation? What corresponds to fluency, to untranslatable terms, and to the translator's loyalty?

Finally, once these disanalogies and the modified analogy are on the table, does the original conclusion—that historians should understand the past strictly "on its own terms"—actually survive? Or was the foreign-country analogy carrying the entire argument by making temporal distance feel like cultural distance, when in fact the two may demand entirely different methodological responses? If the analogy collapses, what would be a more apt comparison for describing the historian's unavoidable but disciplined engagement with present standards?
