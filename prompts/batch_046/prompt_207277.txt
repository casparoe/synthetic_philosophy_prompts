design a 90-minute workshop game for about 14 philosophy-of-science grad students that makes them feel the force of no-free-lunch theorems and hume's induction problem together, not just as "here's hume then here's wolpert." inject a framing concept: call it "inductive debt" — the hidden prior every learning algorithm smuggles in without ever paying for it with observation, and the way those debts cancel out exactly over a uniform world-space (NFL) while leaving you epistemically bankrupt if you try to discharge them.

specify: 4 teams of 3-4, roles (engineer, skeptic, accountant tracking "assumption credits"), timeline in minutes, rules and payoffs. the activity should have 6 rounds predicting hidden binary rules. rounds 1-3 they see training data, refine their "prediction engine," and earn points — they should feel genuinely clever. then reveal the generator draws uniformly from all 2^32 possible rules (show the physical card/deck shuffle so they dont think youre cheating). for rounds 4-6 they can spend up to 3 assumption credits (each costs 5 points) to restrict the space — e.g., "continuity" or "simplicity bias" — but they cant earn more credits through observation alone.

arrange incentives so they hit the wall themselves: without spent credits their round-1 engines perform at chance in the uniform space, while teams with different priors win and lose in ways that feel arbitrary until mapped to explicit assumptions. include the debrief that connects round-1 confidence to inductive debt, round-4 collapse to NFL over uniform space, credit-spending to explicit priors, and the predictable failure that some students will object "but the real world isnt uniform" — note how to turn that objection into the point. be honest about what the game cant reproduce: hume's circularity argument (justifying induction requires induction) and true mathematical uniformity over infinite spaces. also note the logistical risk that teams will think the deck is rigged, so the physical shuffle needs to be visible, and that the accountant role sometimes dominates discussion so rotate it.

make it casual, maybe a typo or two, and dont ask for bibliographies or reading lists.
