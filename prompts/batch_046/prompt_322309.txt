Construct a detailed but realistic scenario—no longer than two paragraphs—in which an ordinary, cognitively unimpaired adult voter reverses her opposition to a carbon-tax proposal after encountering what we will call structured option architecture: the policy is presented through a sequence of nested binary choices (for instance, immediate rebate versus deferred infrastructure investment, then local versus national implementation) arranged so that the framing exploits an asymmetry in how she weights present versus deferred costs, though every factual claim about the policy remains accurate and accessible to her. Engineer the case so that an autonomy-based account of manipulation, which holds that influence counts as manipulative when it bypasses or subverts the agent's rational evaluation rather than engaging it, classifies this framing as manipulation; meanwhile, a transparency-based account, which holds that influence is legitimate persuasion so long as it does not conceal its mechanism and remains open to the agent's reflective endorsement, classifies it as legitimate persuasion. The voter must reach the same conclusion—support for the carbon tax—under both descriptions, and there must be no deception about policy content, no coercion, and no appeal to emotional imagery or authority.

After presenting the scenario, provide the reasoning that yields each verdict, specifying exactly which feature of the structured architecture does the separating work. Then state what happens if that feature is removed—for example, if the nested framing is eliminated and the voter reaches the same conclusion through direct comparison of policy summaries—and whether the case then collapses into an uncontroversial instance of ordinary political advocacy. Finally, articulate the strongest reply available to a defender of the autonomy-based account against the transparency theorist's claim that reflective accessibility is sufficient for legitimacy, and identify whether that reply relies on an empirical claim about actual human reasoning or on a normative claim about what rational evaluation requires.
