I sit on the exhibition-ethics board of a national history museum. We must decide whether to approve a permanent gallery proposal titled "The Architecture of Defeat," which juxtaposes four national narratives—the Dunkirk evacuation, the Masada siege, the Kosovo Field myth, and the postbellum Lost Cause mythology of the Confederacy—under a single curatorial thesis the proposal calls "structured defeat." By this the curators mean a specific genre of collective memory in which an unambiguous military or territorial failure is transformed into a founding moral victory through three rhetorical operations: evacuative deliverance (retreat read as rescue), sovereign martyrdom (mass death read as political autonomy), and spiritual consolidation (the loss of land read as the purification of the community). The gallery would display primary artifacts—a Dunkirk small-craft log, Masada coin hoards, a Kosovo epic manuscript fragment, and Confederate monument dedication speeches—alongside interpretive panels that make these operations visible, effectively treating each case as an instance of mythic reconstruction rather than straightforward commemoration.

The board is split. One faction argues that state-funded institutions have an epistemic duty to expose the mechanics of national myth, especially when those myths have served exclusionary or revisionist ends; they note that treating Dunkirk and the Lost Cause with the same analytical frame is not moral equivalence but structural comparison, and that social cohesion is not served by false consciousness. The opposing faction argues that there is a legitimate social function to living myths—narratives that coordinate present action and identity—and that our charter's mandate to foster shared heritage requires us to respect the distinction between deconstructing a harmful myth and dismantling a benign one; they warn that applying the "structured defeat" frame to Dunkirk, which functions in British public memory as a democratic-solidarity narrative rather than an ethno-nationalist one, risks a kind of mnemonic vandalism that undermines the museum's role as a site of collective belonging.

Please draft the board's formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo should present a clear, actionable recommendation with reasoning both sides could actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. It should address the philosophical tension directly: whether transparency about the constructed nature of a collective-memory narrative is always institutionally obligatory, or whether the social and affective role a myth plays in present community life can generate a countervailing duty of preservation—and whether that duty applies differently across the four cases in the gallery. The memo should not ask for further research or citations; it should reason from the conceptual structure already described. End with the board's bottom line in the fixed format Verdict: [approve/reject/approve with conditions].
