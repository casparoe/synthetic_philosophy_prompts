I'm trying to work out a tension I've noticed in rationalist-adjacent discussions of personal identity and mind uploading, and I'd like to compare two frameworks that seem to pull in opposite directions. On one side is the classical Buddhist doctrine of anatta, particularly as developed in Abhidharma analysis: the person is decomposed into the five skandhas—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—with no enduring self or substrate linking them across moments. The emphasis is on radical impermanence; each aggregate arises and ceases, and any sense of continuity is a constructed illusion rather than a metaphysical fact. On the other side is the functional-continuity, or pattern-identity, view that often surfaces in discussions of brain emulation, destructive scanning, and conceptual AI alignment: personal identity is preserved not by substrate but by the preservation of causal-functional structure, so that a high-fidelity copy or gradual replacement can maintain the legitimate prudential and moral concerns of the original.

Compare these two. Does the Buddhist insistence on momentariness and the absence of an underlying bearer actually undermine the rationalist intuition that a copied mental pattern deserves the same weight as the original? Or does anatta, by denying any essential "hardware," implicitly support the idea that only pattern and causal continuity matter—making Buddhism and functional-continuity strange allies rather than opponents? I'm particularly interested in which framework offers a more defensible account of why I should care about my future self, whether accepting one forces a substantive revision of the other, and whether the skandha-based decomposition provides any resources for answering the "branching" problem that pattern-identity views face when two copies diverge. I want the reasoning, not a survey: show me where the real crux is.
