I chair the accreditation committee for a large public university's world-civilizations survey-course approvals. Every semester I evaluate proposals for semester-long courses that promise a unified narrative arc from deep human history to the present, usually pitched to general-education students as 'the human story.' The committee is mixed—historians, anthropologists, biologists, and education faculty—and I need a reusable evaluation procedure we can run in about forty-five minutes per proposal, yielding one of four sign-off recommendations: approve, approve-with-caveats, reject, or refer-to-external-specialist. The procedure must be grounded in the actual critical framework developed against grand narratives—the failure modes visible in critiques of Harari's Sapiens, Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, and Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything—but it must not require specialist knowledge of every region or period.

Please turn that critical framework into an ordered decision procedure. Specify the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact points where the procedure stops with a reject, and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the decision must be handed to a person (for example, because the issue is pedagogical purpose rather than historical accuracy). Include hard cases the procedure must handle: proposals that use non-linear or oscillating models with no clear causal spine; proposals that depend heavily on ethnographic analogy for pre-literate societies; and proposals that frame modern liberal-democratic industrial society as the implicit telos. Inject a specific operational tool—call it a silent counter-example inventory—that requires the proposer to list three documented historical trajectories that deviate from the spine and classify each as exception, branch, or falsifier. Also include a counterfactual stress-test applied to the two most consequential causal links in the spine.

Then apply the procedure to two proposals. First, a course built on a three-stage spine—cognitive revolution, agricultural revolution, scientific revolution—that treats each stage as unlocking the next and treats modernity as the natural culmination. This should be the case where the procedure delivers a verdict you think is too harsh; defend that verdict anyway, and note exactly which steps drive it. Second, a course using an oscillating model of hierarchical and egalitarian social experimentation with no single endpoint, drawn broadly from Graeber and Wengrow's framing. This should pass the factual checks but force a value-laden judgment call about whether a survey course requires a causal spine for pedagogical coherence.

Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of disagreement it will miss, where it risks false positives or false negatives, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedural checks. And tell me what would change your mind about the procedure's adequacy—which argument about macro-historical explanation, or which empirical result in comparative archaeology or genetics, would cause you to drop or revise a step—and flag the single point where your reasoning is most exposed.
