Anyway, my thesis is that Nietzsche's master/slave distinction in the Genealogy doesn't actually map onto modern therapy culture's obsession with 'boundries' the way people usually think. In master morality, good and bad are just strength versus weakness, right? But slave morality is reactive: it invents 'evil' to describe the strong, and turns weakness into moral virtue. I think the modern injunction to 'set healthy boundaries' sounds like masterful self-assertion—like saying 'I won't be used'—but structurally it's slave morality in disguise. It defines the self negatively against other people's demands, and it moralizes being overwhelmed (a kind of weakness) as something that deserves protection and social recognition. So the boundary-setter is kind of like Nietzsche's priest: they transform their own vulnerability into a moral high ground, except now the weakness is emotional bandwidth rather than physical strength, and the 'evil other' is anyone who makes demands on you.

I also want to say this isn't just a cute analogy. Nietzsche's framework predicats that any value system born of resentment eventually produces a new priestly class that mediates between the resentful and the world. In our case, maybe that's the wellness industry—coaches, therapists, influencers—who certify which boundries are 'healthy' and which people are 'toxic.' I know I'm stretching the historical specificity here, since Nietzsche was clearly targeting Christian asceticism and not neoliberal self-care, but I think the structral dynamics still apply.
