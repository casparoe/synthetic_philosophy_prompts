I am preparing a seminar paper on Roman Stoicism and have been working closely with Cicero's De Senectute, particularly his claim that old age should be welcomed rather than feared because it liberates one from the passions and allows for sustained intellectual and civic activity. In our discussions, a classmate summarized Cicero's position as essentially a form of "productive aging" — the idea that the elderly retain social value precisely insofar as they continue to contribute labor and wisdom to the state, and that any decline in physical capacity is acceptable only because mental and moral faculties remain intact. This summary strikes me as somewhat anachronistic, importing modern economic categories of productivity into a Roman framework centered on virtue and natural completion. Still, the core tension feels real: Cicero does insist that withdrawal from public life is a kind of death, and he treats physical decay as largely irrelevant to human flourishing.

Given this framework, I want to test how far it extends into genuinely post-classical territory. How would Cicero likely respond to three developments he could not have imagined: first, modern biomedical efforts to radically extend human lifespan through senolytic interventions and regenerative medicine, which promise to decouple chronological age from biological decline; second, institutional policies of mandatory retirement that remove older professionals from positions of authority regardless of their mental competence; and third, the growing legal and ethical acceptance of assisted suicide for patients suffering from progressive dementia, where the very rational selfhood Cicero considers the seat of virtue is gradually dissolved? In each case, please ground your answer in the specific arguments of De Senectute — for instance, his comparison of old age to the final act of a play, his dismissal of the four charges against aging, and his Stoic conviction that death is not an evil — rather than in generic Stoic commonplaces. I am especially interested in whether his acceptance of natural limits would make him hostile to life-extension technologies, or whether he would welcome them as tools for prolonging the philosophical life; and whether his emphasis on civic engagement would lead him to oppose mandatory retirement on principle, even if he shared Roman concerns about generational succession. Where his premises genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so explicitly, and flag any modern assumptions (such as individual autonomy or economic utility) that risk distorting the projection.
