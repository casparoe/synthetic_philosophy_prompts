Exam question: In a recent essay, a historian claims that counterfactual reasoning is only legitimate if we reject what she calls the 'strong pipeline thesis'—the idea that history moves through necessary stages like a pipeline. She argues that without rejecting this thesis, asking 'what if Napoleon had won at Waterloo' is like asking 'what if 2+2=5.' Is she right? Discuss, making sure to distinguish between determinism, inevitability, and the kind of lawlike stability counterfactuals actually need.

Student answer:
The historian is half right but she overreaches. The strong pipeline thesis—basically Hegel's idea that history has to pass through fixed stages like feudalism then capitalism—isn't the same thing as determinism. Determinism means every event is fixed by prior causes, but inevitability just means big outcomes are bound to happen eventually, even if the path changes. Counterfactuals don't need determinism to be false; they only need local lawlike stability, meaning background conditions stay mostly the same except at the divergence point. So asking about Waterloo is fine as long as we backtrack to change Napoleon's orders or the weather, not suspend physics.

That said, miracle counterfactuals—where you let a law of nature break—are automatically incoherent once you accept local stability. Local stability means we keep all the laws intact everywhere, so letting physics break violates the whole framework. Also, the pipeline thesis is really Marx's stage theory, not Hegel's—Hegel was about dialectical spirit unfolding, not economic stages. So the historian's comparison to 2+2=5 is too strong, but she correctly sees that if history is a rigid pipeline then altering one link breaks the whole chain.
