ok so i was talking with my roommate about criminal justice reform and she mentioned that in alot of states judges now use these risk-assessment algorithms for bail and sometimes sentencing. the algorithms score you on employment history, neighborhood, family ties, not on whether you actually feel remorse or on how badly the victim was hurt emotionally. but then in the exact same courtroom, victims get to give these impact statements describing trauma, and judges are legally required to weigh that in the sentence. so one half of the system is pure statistical consequentialism—predict future harm based on risk—while the other half is all about moral desert and the specific wrong done to this person.

what philosophical commitments does that split practice actually presuppose? is it just incoherent, or is there a hidden theory that says risk-management belongs to the pretrial phase and retributive punishment belongs only after conviction? and does the victim-impact part strictly presuppose retributivism, or could a smart consequentialist defend it as a signaling device that strengthens deterrence? also there's this weird asymmetry: if the algorithm flags you as high-risk for a minor theft, you sit in jail for months, but if you commit assault and score low-risk, you walk out early. does that mean the practice quietly treats predicted risk as more fundamental than the act's moral gravity? i'm trying to understand whether these two institutional parts could be unified under one view, or whether they necessarily pull in opposite directions—like, if we genuinely adopted a control-based view of responsibility, wouldn't we have to reject both the algorithm (since it ignores what you actually controlled) and the victim statement (since emotional harm isn't within the offender's control either)? or is there some middle-ground theory about institutional roles or legitimacy that licenses both?
