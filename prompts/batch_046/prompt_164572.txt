Construct a realistic, non-science-fictional thought experiment—no longer than a paragraph, with no demons, perfect predictors, or artificial devices—that isolates a multipolar trap distinct from both the prisoner's dilemma and the tragedy of the commons. The scenario should involve three or more independent agents who share common knowledge that a binding collective-restraint agreement would leave every agent strictly better off, and who face no existential or market-competitive pressure to defect. The destructive equilibrium should emerge solely from a stability cascade: each agent, observing the prior agent's moderate but non-binding commitment, rationally opts for a marginally more aggressive course because the private gain is fully internalized while the collective cost is distributed; crucially, once the final agent escalates, all prior agents retroactively prefer having escalated first, yet no past action can be revised. After presenting the case, demonstrate that it meets each specification: map every agent's decision point; show why unilateral deviation is irrational at every step despite shared knowledge of the worse outcome; explain why the mechanism is not reducible to a two-player defection logic or to open-access resource depletion; and identify exactly which structural feature—the pairing of irreversible actions with reversibly updated regret—does the work. Finish with an honest note on what happens if that feature is removed, and on how a defender of cooperative rationality would attack the case, specifically by challenging whether the marginal calculus survives once the cascade structure itself is common knowledge.
