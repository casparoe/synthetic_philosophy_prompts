After a career in statistical physics, where I routinely treated cross-covariance terms as negligible absent an explicit coupling mechanism—a habit that now strikes me as an application of the so-called presumption of independence—I have recently studied attempts to formalize this heuristic through the framework of heuristic estimators. I understand that the program demands coherence conditions such as linearity and path independence, and that no existing candidate, including the cumulant-propagation scheme sketched in an appendix, satisfies all of them at once; indeed, the central open problem asks whether any estimator can license the intuitively valid uses of the heuristic without also accepting spurious arguments. From this I have concluded that the presumption is not merely informally applied but fundamentally incoherent in principle, which would imply that my lifelong scientific reliance on independence assumptions was rationally unjustified no matter how empirically successful it proved. I am further troubled by the observation that Bayesian updating with diffuse priors reproduces approximately the same independence-based predictions in large-sample limits: if the underlying heuristic is incoherent, should not such convergence be impossible or systematically misleading? So I ask whether my practice was indeed philosophically bankrupt, and whether the failure of formalization genuinely undermines the reasoning or only marks a gap between defeasible judgment and axiomatic representation. Specifically, please state where this line of argument is most likely to fail, and tell me precisely what result or consideration would change your verdict—whether, for example, a proof that cumulant propagation violates path independence in every non-trivial domain, or a rigorous demonstration that large-sample Bayesian convergence is strictly irrelevant to the coherence question, would overturn your assessment.
