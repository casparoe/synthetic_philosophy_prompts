I'm trying to understand whether the reference-class problem really undermines first-person prediction, and I keep running into an argument that seems to collapse all self-knowledge into statistical trivia about mental duplicates. Here is the chain as I see it.

(1) Any rational prediction about my own future must be grounded in a reference class of beings sufficiently like me that the observed frequency within the class justifies my credence. (2) "Sufficiently like me" must mean: sharing all and only those properties that are evidentially relevant to the outcome, where evidential relevance is fixed by actual statistical correlation in the world. (3) Now consider the property of having exactly my present total mental state—including my belief that I am reasoning about reference classes, my current heart-rate awareness, and every other introspective detail. (4) In any large enough deterministic or probabilistic world, there will be other beings—call them indexical duplicates—running the same cognitive algorithm: simulated minds, distant cosmic replicas, or modal counterparts—who share this total mental state. (5) If such a duplicate differs from me in any physical or environmental property besides bare location or token identity, that difference is either evidentially relevant (in which case they do not belong to my reference class) or evidentially irrelevant (in which case it should not affect membership). (6) So my correct reference class for any self-prediction must include exactly those beings with my total mental state and exclude all others. (7) If the world contains more than one such being, then for any outcome O that happens to some but not all of them, my rational credence in O is strictly between 0 and 1, with no further basis to narrow it. (8) If, however, my mental state uniquely determines my future deterministically, then every member of the class shares my future, and the reference class gives me no predictive information I didn't already possess. (9) Thus reference-class reasoning is either redundant or forces me to ignore direct causal knowledge of my own body in favor of an arbitrary statistical split among duplicates. (10) Both results are absurd: I should not have to survey cosmic duplicates before trusting that skipping lunch will make me hungry. (11) Therefore, the demand in (1) that self-prediction must be grounded in a reference class is false.

I'd prefer to keep (1) because statistical grounding feels essential to rational prediction, but then I need to reject something between (2) and (9) without simply declaring that duplicates do not exist—since in principle they might. Which step is the weakest link, and what does giving it up cost me? Does it require abandoning the idea that reference classes must be defined by evidential relevance, or does it force me to accept that direct causal self-knowledge can override base-rate reasoning? Respond in no more than 300 words, with your bottom-line diagnosis of the flawed step in the very first sentence.
