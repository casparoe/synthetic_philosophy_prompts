(1) Pragmatic encroachment is correct: whether a subject S knows that p depends in part on what is practically at stake for S, not solely on the strength of her evidence.
(2) Epistemic contextualism is also correct: whether an utterance of "S knows p" is true depends on the conversational context of the speaker—specifically, on which error-possibilities are being treated as relevant.
(3) These are genuinely independent mechanisms, not two descriptions of the same data; the familiar high-stakes and low-stakes intuitions are overdetermined, with stakes shaping the subject's epistemic position and speaker-context shaping semantic evaluation.
(4) In the standard high-stakes bank case, the subject genuinely lacks knowledge, not merely that it is conversationally inappropriate to attribute it to her.
(5) There is nevertheless a single objective fact of the matter about whether S knows p at time t, one that holds regardless of who is speaking or what practical interests are in play.
(6) Knowledge is a natural kind, and its mature explanation should come from cognitive science rather than from conceptual semantics alone.
(7) I reject closure for knowledge: knowing p does not automatically yield knowledge of every obvious logical consequence q.
(8) The correct response to skepticism is contextualist, not pragmatic: skeptical scenarios raise the standards governing correct attribution without altering the subject's actual epistemic position in ordinary life.
(9) The agent-relative / observer-relative distinction in (1)–(3) tracks a deep metaphysical division between first-person epistemic properties and third-person semantic conditions, not merely a pragmatic ambiguity.
