Please describe the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox—treating it as a St. Petersburg-style game in which the payoff sequence is arranged so that the expectation is undefined (for instance, conditionally convergent or sign-alternating) rather than diverging cleanly to infinity—for normative decision theory. In particular, address how a rational agent should evaluate three structurally different offers: playing the game once, entering an indefinitely repeated sequence of independent plays, and purchasing a single bundled contract that resolves all plays simultaneously. Given that every finite truncation of the game possesses a well-defined expected value but the untruncated limit lacks one, what does this case reveal about the coherence of expected-utility maximization when expectation fails to exist? Furthermore, discuss whether dynamic consistency requires an agent to hold stable preferences across these three framing conditions, or whether the absence of a unique expectation legitimately permits preference reversals between playing now, deferring, and accepting a truncation boundary that was not specified ex ante.
