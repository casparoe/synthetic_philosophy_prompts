I've been chewing on those coherence arguements for expected utility—Dutch books, money pumps, the represenation theorems—where if your preferences violate the axioms then you're vulnerble to being exploited. I think I've basically bought into the stronger position: it's not just that inconsistencies are impractical, but that coherence genuinely establishes EU maximization as rationally mandatory once you have beliefs and desires. But I'm not clear on what it would actually take to knock this down. I know people bring up ambiguity aversion or that real life doesn't come with complete betting markets, but those feel like surface-level complaints. I'm more interested in whether there's a fatal circularity—like, do the arguments presuppose you're already embedded in a full probabilty space with well-defined acts and outcomes, so they can't really *pull* you into that framework but only police you once you're inside? And are there cases, maybe with small state spaces or non-separable preferences, where the represenation theorems fail in ways that coherence defenders themselves admit are awkward?

Could you rank the defeaters from fatal to merely annoying, and say which ones the coherence crowd usually bites the bullet on versus which ones they'd concede actually damage the view? I'm also wondering about the diachronic angle—if coherence applies across time, does that commit me to locking in my future preferences now, or is that just a misreading of how the argument works? I'd like the answer to distinguish the stuff that genuinely threatens the position from the objections that are really just about practical implementation, and to be honest about whether the coherence view ends up being well-insulated or actually quite exposed once you dig past the standard textbook objections.
