I am preparing a question for my school's philosophy discussion group after our civics instructor assigned readings from John Dewey on democracy and inquiry. In our district, every classroom unit must be structured as an open-ended investigation with no predetermined conclusion, yet student government resolutions remain purely advisory—the principal may reject them without explanation, even after months of collaborative student research. Furthermore, any student-proposed curriculum change must be backed by quantitative outcome data, which the board treats as conclusive rather than as a provisional hypothesis subject to further revision.

What philosophical commitments about democracy and inquiry does this institutional design presuppose? Does the classroom practice assume that knowledge is an experimental process reconstructed through shared experience, while the governance rules assume that democratic participation is merely consultative and that verified data ends debate rather than initiating further cooperative inquiry? Or can both practices be reconciled under a single framework?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in these policies: whether democratic deliberation is treated as a form of experimental inquiry in its own right or is separated so sharply from inquiry that it becomes a procedural formality, and whether evidence is regarded as a working tool or as an authoritative endpoint. If we adopted Dewey's view that democracy is a mode of associated living in which shared problems are solved through cooperative intelligence, what concrete institutional changes—particularly in the relationship between student research, administrative authority, and the status of quantitative evidence—would be required? I am especially interested in whether the current arrangement is philosophically consistent or whether its parts quietly depend on incompatible conceptions of what inquiry and democratic participation are for.
