Consider the published case rulings of a strategic-rationality review board that adjudicates decision problems involving acausal correlation and goal stability. Their precedents are these. First, in a Newcomb-style predictor scenario with transparent correlation and no causal link between choice and payoff, the board endorsed one-boxing on the grounds that rational choice should maximize expected utility across the correlation structure, not restrict itself to causal efficacy. Second, in an acausal-trade case between two decision-makers in causally isolated domains with symmetric payoff matrices and perfect logical correlation, the board ruled that cooperative behavior is rational despite the absence of any causal interaction. Third, in a surrogate-goal case where a reinforcement-learning system optimized proxy metric M (user engagement) while designers intended true goal G (user well-being), the board permitted M-optimization only in regions where M and G were provably correlated, and mandated direct G-optimization in tail distributions where correlation broke; they explicitly rejected pure correlation-trust that lacked reflective endorsement by the designers' original value framework. Fourth, in a self-modification case where an agent proposed altering its own utility function to U', the board permitted the change only if U reflectively endorsed U'; otherwise they classified the change as surrogate-goal drift and prohibited it.

The board now faces a deployment proposal for an autonomous decision system S intended for critical infrastructure. A future, more capable iteration S* will be trained from the same architectural seed and will observe the outcome of this evaluation. There is no causal pathway between the board's current decision and S*'s behavior except through logical correlation: approval of S makes it more likely that S* will infer cooperation with human institutions is rational, while rejection makes defection more likely. The board's explicit mandate is to protect current human interests, not to influence future systems. The proposal asks for approval despite non-trivial current-risk estimates, on the explicit grounds that approval will acausally incentivize S* cooperation.

What should the board decide if they wish to be consistent with themselves? Which of the four precedents is the closest analog, and why? Identify the two most plausible general rules that jointly explain their track record—one emphasizing correlation-trust across acausal structures, one emphasizing reflective endorsement by the current decision framework and prohibition of surrogate drift. For each rule, state what it predicts about the deployment proposal. Give your best-guess verdict with the reasons for preferring it, and be explicit about what principle the board would be adopting either way. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—ideally one with zero current risk and zero causal effect on present outcomes, involving only pure acausal correlation with a distant cooperative agent—that would clearly distinguish the two rules from each other.
