I'm prepping a session for my philosophy of biology seminar — about 12 grad students, 75 minutes — and I'm tired of just debating whether DNA is literally a code or just chemistry we talk about like one. What's bugging me is the gap between arbitrary symbol-mapping (where an interpreter needs prior knowledge of the convention) and chemically forced translation (where steric fit and hydrogen-bond patterns lock the codon-to-amino-acid pairing, and the ribosome isnt "reading" anything so much as being pushed around). So I want a runnable in-class game that makes them feel that gap in their hands, not just hear it in a lecture. Split them into small groups: one partner writes a "message" as a sequence of three colored beads (representing codons), the other "translates" it by folding a paper structure according to a rule sheet. Half the groups get an arbitrary cipher — red-green-blue means "fold left flap," with no physical reason — and half get a chemically-constrained rulebook where bead colors match slots by shape, so the fold is physically forced and cant be overridden. They build the structure, then I secretly swap one bead for the arbitrary group and ask if they can adapat the convention; they should be able to, if they negotiate a new rule. For the chemical group, the same swap either breaks the paper structure or forces a different fold they cant vote their way out of. The debrief should map the arbitrary group's coordination problem onto semantic/informational theories of the genetic code, and the chemical group's rigidity onto the view that its causal chemistry with informational appearance. I need rules, materials that fit in a bag, a success condition that dont need real money, and an honest note on what this cant reproduce — probably the evolutionary history that explains why this particular mapping stuck around. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and please keep the facilitation minimal: they should hit the problem through the rules, not through me explaining it afterwards.
