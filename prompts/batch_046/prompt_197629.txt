I am drafting a novel about a woman who, following a localized neurological alteration, retains flawless sensory discrimination of injury, inflammation, and temperature extremes—she can describe a burn's precise topography and a fracture's mechanical properties—yet insists that none of these bodily events possess even the faintest unpleasantness or aversive urgency. She now cares for an aging parent who suffers conventional distress from parallel conditions. Drawing on her own experience, she withholds analgesics and argues that the parent's anguish is not an intrinsic property of the physiological signals but a culturally acquired interpretive layer—one she herself dissolved through sustained contemplative practice that allows her to encounter comparable sensations as aesthetically neutral or even subtly interesting. She therefore presents her refusal not as neglect but as philosophical liberation: she is freeing the parent from an unnecessary affective overlay. I feel that this reasoning conceals a deep error, but I cannot determine whether it collapses a distinction between sensory representation and evaluative content, commits an illegitimate inference from first-person phenomenology to third-person obligation, or denies something constitutive about pain's nature. Please identify the central philosophical failure in her position and advise what it implies for whether pain's badness belongs to the sensation itself or is separable from it, so that I may shape her moral trajectory with conceptual rigor rather than narrative instinct alone.
