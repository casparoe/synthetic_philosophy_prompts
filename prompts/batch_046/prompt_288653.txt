Write a six-month fellowship proposal, about four pages, on Whig history and the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a precise plan. The standard historiography of the late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century psychologism debate in logic and philosophy of mathematics treats Frege's and Husserl's anti-psychologism as an inevitable conceptual correction, framing preceding psychologistic positions as naive confusions that had to be cleared away for modern formal semantics to emerge. I suspect this is a Whig narrative that suppresses a genuinely coherent intermediate position—call it normative contextualism—which held that logical laws are neither descriptions of mental processes nor purely abstract structures, but rather constitutive norms for reasoning that acquire their force only within specific disciplinary contexts. The proposal should state the precise question: was the defeat of psychologism conceptually inevitable, or did a viable third option disappear mainly because later historians wrote the past as progress toward present formalism? Explain why existing treatments leave this open: philosophers tend to treat the debate as settled on conceptual grounds without examining the historiographical framing, while historians of logic acknowledge some complexity but still organize narratives around the emergence of modern formal semantics. Set out the approach—conceptual reconstruction of the intermediate position drawn from published texts in the neo-Kantian and early phenomenological traditions, paired with a comparative analysis of how those texts are characterized in standard histories versus in the original arguments—rather than an empirical archival project, since the question is whether a coherent view was available, not whether it was widely held, and conceptual analysis can settle that with published materials alone. Say what a result would look like, either a reconstructed framework that can be articulated without anachronism, or a demonstration that the position collapses into one of the two standard sides once its context is stripped away, and say what would count as failure, such as finding that the intermediate texts are too fragmentary to support a coherent view or that the reconstruction requires smuggling in modern distinctions. Give an honest assessment of scope: what can be done in six months of conceptual work without archival research in multiple languages, and where the limits lie. Include a brief argument for why a philosophy foundation should fund conceptual historiography rather than empirical history of science here. The response should be written in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if in a letter. It should also say explicitly what argument or discovery would change its assessment of whether the Whig narrative is genuinely misleading here, and identify the point in the reasoning where it is most vulnerable to collapsing into either a defense of the standard narrative or an overcharitable reconstruction of lost views.
