I'm leading a second-year philosophy seminar. The students have just read Benjamin on mechanical reproduction and have settled on "aura"—presence in time and space—as the uniform explanation for why originals matter and copies or forgeries fall short. They now apply it indiscriminately to paintings, music, and literature. I want to teach them that the value of an original is not one thing: in autographic arts the history of production is constitutive of the work's identity, while in allographic arts only compliance with a notational scheme matters, so a perfect copy can be the work itself. But I want them to see this through a deliberately hybrid case, not through standard classroom pairs.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, state the one conceptual dependency that must be in place before the question "why do originals matter?" can even split into different answers for different arts. Second, choose the single carrying example or tightly paired contrast that should bear the whole distinction—I'm weighing a perfect AI-rendered Vermeer reproducing every brushstroke without human touch against a robot arm in Rembrandt's studio applying his pigments with his techniques but lacking artistic intention—and say which should carry the session, what each must possess and what each must lack, and why the standard painting-versus-symphony comparison should be deferred. Third, map the misconceptions students will form in the order they will arise—likely: indistinguishability entails identity; all value is either aesthetic or market value; Benjamin's aura applies identically to music and literature; and if the causal chain is correct, authorship is irrelevant—and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Fourth, design one end-of-session check-for-understanding using an unseen case—perhaps a 3D-printed marble replica of Michelangelo's David carved from the same Carrara quarry using the original chisel marks—to reveal whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than memorized labels. Fifth, say what should be openly left out at this level—legal definitions of forgery, the ontology of musical performance, economic scarcity—and explain why excluding each protects the conceptual point rather than simplifying it.
