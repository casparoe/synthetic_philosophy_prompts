I spent my career in theoretical physics, and since retiring I have been working through Kurt Gödel’s philosophical theology—especially his conviction, apparently held on rationalist grounds, that personal consciousness survives bodily death. To clarify my own thinking I have sketched two competing reconstructions of that conviction, and I want your comparative assessment. The first, which I call the modal-persistence view, holds that Gödel treated the self as an abstract structure—analogous to a mathematical object—whose identity conditions depend only on internal relational properties; since abstract structures are not subject to temporal destruction, the self necessarily persists, and Gödel’s modal-logic work serves mainly to establish that such structures are realized across all possible worlds, including those accessible after physical death. The second, which I call the axiological-completion view, holds that Gödel’s belief was not ontological in that technical sense, but normative-rationalist: he thought that if consciousness can grasp eternal, mind-independent truths, then its total annihilation would constitute an irrational waste of cosmic value, and therefore a rationally ordered universe must provide for its continuation in some form.

Please compare these reconstructions with respect to fidelity to Gödel’s known Platonism, his actual use of modal logic, and the general tenor of his reported remarks on survival. More importantly, I want a committed verdict, not a balanced survey: take a definite position on whether either reconstruction is philosophically coherent on its own terms, or whether both collapse—one into a category mistake that treats consciousness as an abstract object, the other into wishful thinking disguised as rational inference. Explicitly choose which reconstruction, if either, a consistent rationalist should prefer, defend that choice with reasoning, and state which of Gödel’s actual commitments each reconstruction forces us to abandon if we reject it.
