I'm drafting an essay on Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis and the long-standing problem of whether cultural values can be genuine causes rather than post-hoc descriptions. The standard defense is that Weber offers an interpretive causality: religious meanings shape action through the subjective orientations of actors, without reducing to psychology or material conditions. I want to press a different difficulty—what I call the activation gap. Even if we grant that ascetic Protestantism possessed the meanings Weber attributes to it, we still need an account of why that particular value-complex became causally efficacious in early modern Europe rather than remaining inert, as comparable belief-systems did elsewhere. Weber appeals to elective affinity between religious ideas and emerging economic interests, but elective affinity seems to describe a structural resonance, not a mechanism that selectively activates one set of values over another. If we specify that mechanism in psychological terms—say, salvation anxiety generating compulsive labor—we fall into the psychologism Weber explicitly rejects. If we specify it in institutional terms—property law, market structures, status-group closure—then values appear to do no independent explanatory work, and culture collapses into its material conditions. So Weber's thesis appears to require a third kind of mechanism, which I tentatively call a normative transmission structure: a social arrangement that translates shared meanings into selective motivational force without being reducible to either individual psychology or institutional constraint. Yet Weber never systematically supplies such a structure, and constructing one risks reintroducing exactly the reduction he sought to avoid.

I'd like comments on three specific points. First, is my characterization of elective affinity as non-mechanistic accurate to Weber's methodology, or am I illicitly imposing a causal-mechanistic framework that his interpretive sociology is designed to reject? Second, does the activation gap genuinely threaten his argument, or does it merely restate the familiar problem of multicausality in historical explanation—namely, that values are always one factor among many? Third, is the proposed notion of a normative transmission structure coherent on its own terms, or does it secretly depend on psychological or institutional explanations I claim to avoid? I am especially interested in whether reading Weber's discussions of charismatic authority or status-group dynamics could implicitly fill the gap, and if so, whether that resolution preserves the autonomy of cultural explanation or dissolves it into sociology and politics.
