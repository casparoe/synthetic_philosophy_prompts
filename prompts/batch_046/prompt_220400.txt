I'm presenting a dissertation chapter to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind seminar whose members include analytic philosophers of mind and cognitive neuroscientists. My thesis is that dreams are neither experiences in the sense Norman Malcolm defends—where reportability upon waking functions as the criterion for having had an experiential state—nor are they mere post-hoc confabulations in the manner of Daniel Dennett's cassette-model view, on which the only real event is the waking narrative reconstruction. Instead, I claim that dreams are temporally unbound predictive simulations running during sleep that generate proto-phenomenal content but fail to satisfy the integrative temporal-binding conditions necessary for full experiential status. Consequently, the question "Are dreams experiences?" is malformed unless one specifies which binding conditions are being invoked, since sleep-state simulations lack wake-state integration without thereby being fictional reconstructions.

The audience is precisely this mix:
- Two Wittgensteinian philosophers who treat Malcolm's reportability criterion as decisive and defend the autonomy of dream-language games against neuroreductionism.
- One eliminativist about dream phenomenology who endorses Dennett's cassette model and knows the false-memory consolidation literature linking REM replay to waking reconstruction.
- Two predictive-processing neuroscientists who accept that sleep involves generative-model updating but deny that phenomenological categories apply to subcortical dynamics; they will press me on empirical tractability.
- One embodied-cognition phenomenologist committed to graded, distributed temporal binding, who will accuse me of wake-state chauvinism.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they are likely to come, phrased as each faction would actually phrase them. For each objection, state what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firmly, and the weakest point in my reply. Rank them by combined likelihood and damage. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises: specifically, the charge that my binding-condition criterion cannot distinguish deep non-REM sleep or general anesthesia from dreaming without circularly appealing back to reportability, thereby collapsing my position into Malcolm's. Add a brief strategic note on how to respond if the neuroscientists demand a concrete, empirically distinguishable prediction that separates my view from both Malcolm's and Dennett's.

Finish with a one-sentence summary of the core strategic recommendation as the very last line, prefixed with "Summary: ".
