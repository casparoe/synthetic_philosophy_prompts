I spent four decades in experimental physics before retiring into philosophy, and I am struck by an organizational feature of the AI safety industry that seems philosophically loaded yet rarely examined. In the laboratories I have come to know, evaluation of capabilities and evaluation of alignment are treated as distinct institutional functions: separate teams, separate benchmarks, separate publication norms, and separate failure metrics, as though one could fully characterize a system's behavior without asking what ends that behavior serves. This division is presented as pragmatic, but its persistence and institutional rigidity suggest a deeper commitment. I want to understand what philosophical view of the alignment problem makes that separation intelligible. Does it presuppose that alignment is an external constraint—like a containment vessel applied to a completed reactor—rather than a property that emerges through the same dynamics that generate capability? Or does it presuppose that the problem is actually several problems—specification failure, robustness failure, emergent misbehavior—that happen to be administratively grouped under one label? Reconstruct the implicit theory: distinguish what the organizational practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, identify which rival conceptions of alignment it quietly rules out, and tell me what parts of the practice—hiring structures, benchmark design, peer review—would have to dissolve if we adopted a view according to which alignment and capability are coupled in the way turbulence and flow are coupled, not layered like safety equipment on machinery.
