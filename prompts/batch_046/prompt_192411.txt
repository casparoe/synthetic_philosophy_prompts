I've been arguing with my roommate about whether comparing Mencius and Xunzi to Rousseau and Hobbes actually does any work, or if it's just a lazy shortcut profs use in intro courses. The analogy is that Mencius is the optimistic Rousseau-type — people are born with good sprouts that get corrupted by bad social structures — and Xunzi is the Hobbesian — people are born selfish and need strict ritual and law to be beaten into shape. People use it to argue that Chinese philosophy just recapitulates the same old Western nature-vs-nurture fight, which feels wrong but I can't pin down why.

Can you audit how far the mapping actually holds? I want a correspondance table — what plays the role of the state of nature, the social contract, the corrupting institution, the educational mechanism — then the places it breaks. I'm especially stuck on whether it smuggles in a Western individualism that doesn't fit either thinker, and whether the conclusion that they just repeat the same old debate survives once you take seriously that Mencius's sprouts are innate but not fully formed, and that Xunzi thinks ritual is accumulated like a craft skill rather than imposed like a sovereign's decree. Is there a better analogy that captures the actual disagreement without importing all that baggage?
