hey—I'm a philosophy prof and I moonlight editing copy for this small investigative mag, and I'm writing this for my junior editors who keep fighting over whether a source "lied" or just "misled." I need a reusable decision procedure they can run on any claim in about five minutes, grounded in the philosophy of lying vs misleading—asserting falsehood with deceptive intent versus saying something technically true but manipulating implicature, framing, or omission. Don't make them quote Grice back at me.

Fix the role: junior copy editor, checks belief reports, quoted dialogue, and headlines every issue, stakes are reputation and occasional legal exposure, they have maybe five minutes per claim and must escalate to me if they hit a values call. The procedure should ask ordered questions, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, stop with a "no/flag/escalate" decision, and explicitly mark where judgment can't be eliminated. Handle hard cases: technically true statements that rely on hidden institutional structure, headlines that summarize weak studies without false sentences, and omissions in quoted dialogue where the speaker's claim is true but the context makes the false belief unavoidable.

Then run it on three cases. First: a source says "the senator received no donations from the energy sector"—true, because the money went to a PAC he controls, not his campaign. Second: a headline reads "New Study Links Supplement to Memory Loss"—the study found a correlation in a forty-person sample with no control, the body is technically accurate but buries the weakness, and the false belief comes mostly from the headline's framing. Third—this one should give them an answer they won't like: a quote in a belief report reads "I never approved that contract"—he never signed it, but he verbally okayed it in a meeting, and the article includes the quote in a paragraph about his "consistent denials" without mentioning the verbal approval. The procedure should probably flag this as structurally misleading rather than a lie, and they should hate that.

Also inject this niche wrinkle I'm calling "structural implication"—where no individual sentence is false, but the paragraph layout or headline placement makes the false belief unavoidable for a reasonable reader. The procedure needs a separate branch for that.

Finally, say clearly what the procedure can't do—probably anything that turns on whether the audience "should have known better"—and don't ask for bibliographies or citations. Keep it casual, maybe a typo or two.
