The Hart-Fuller debate over whether extremely immoral statutes—such as those enacted under National Socialism—can still count as valid law has persisted for decades without either side conceding ground. Hart maintains that legal validity is a matter of satisfying institutional criteria of recognition and that morality operates as an external check on obedience, while Fuller holds that law possesses an internal morality of aspiration and that systematic violations of procedural and substantive fairness cause a rule to fail as law altogether. Despite this apparent clarity, the disagreement has stalled: each side treats the other's central examples as misunderstandings, and later literature largely repeats the original moves rather than resolving them. My hypothesis is that the impasse is not genuinely about the ontology of legal rules, but about two competing conceptions of normative guidance—one that requires that a valid directive be capable of rational endorsement by the subject, and one that treats guidance as a functional feature of authoritative sources regardless of content. Is that diagnosis accurate, or does the stall conceal a hidden empirical question about how judges and officials actually reason with norms, or a verbal ambiguity in the term validity that allows both positions to seem consistent? I want a diagnosis grounded in what Hart and Fuller actually say about the relation between legal form and moral content, not a survey of subsequent scholarship. And I want an honest answer about whether any conceptual distinction—perhaps drawn from the philosophy of action or the logic of norms—could dissolve the disagreement, or whether the dispute is ultimately rooted in irreconcilable starting intuitions about the point of law, such that no further argument should be expected to move it.
