You serve as the senior technical auditor for a national transportation safety board that reviews expedited software-patch requests from autonomous-vehicle manufacturers after near-miss incidents. Manufacturers submit explanatory reports proposing a causal account of the failure and arguing that an update eliminates it. Your board must decide within five working days whether the explanation is adequate to approve the patch without requiring a full-scale replication study, but you have no direct access to proprietary source code and only limited statistical staff.

Translate the philosophical framework of inference to the best explanation into an operational decision procedure you can apply repeatedly. Convert the standard criteria for explanatory goodness—scope, simplicity, coherence with established engineering knowledge, predictive novelty, and comparative advantage over rival hypotheses—into a sequenced set of auditable steps with explicit stopping rules. For each step, specify what counts as sufficient evidence, where the procedure terminates with a rejection, and where it must defer to a human decision-maker because the issue is risk tolerance or ethical weighting rather than explanatory quality. Be explicit about which conceptual distinctions must survive simplification—especially the gap between an explanation being the best available and being good enough to license public-safety action—and which can be collapsed for practical use. Identify the specific conditions under which the procedure will misfire, overreach, or produce false confidence.

Then apply the procedure to three submitted reports: (i) a sensor-fusion error attributed to a single calibration drift in one LiDAR module, supported by predictive tests already run on identical hardware; (ii) a multi-vehicle coordination failure attributed to an emergent protocol conflict in the manufacturer’s distributed decision architecture, supported only by post-hoc simulation and analogical reasoning from unrelated fleet logs; and (iii) a case in which the proposed explanation is genuinely the best available—rival hypotheses have been systematically eliminated and the account correctly predicts a novel, subsequently confirmed failure mode—but the residual uncertainty about system-level interaction still exceeds the board’s threshold for expedited approval, so the procedure must recommend denial despite the explanatory success.

Conclude by stating precisely what this procedure cannot determine, which judgments remain non-delegable to the auditor, and where the framework of inference to the best explanation reaches its limit as a basis for regulatory action.
