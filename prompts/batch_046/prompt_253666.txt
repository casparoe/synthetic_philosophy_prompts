I am a sixth-form student preparing to facilitate a forty-five-minute seminar for a small study group of peers who are taking both economics and philosophy at A-level. We have recently examined rational-choice models in economics, and several classmates appear to assume that a rational agent necessarily maximises expected utility; I wish to introduce Herbert Simon’s concepts of satisficing and bounded rationality, but I want to avoid the conventional examples—flat-hunting, job-searching, or restaurant menus—that dominate every introductory text. Instead, I intend to anchor the session in a deliberately constructed scenario: a municipal library acquisitions committee that must select exactly fifty titles from over two thousand unsolicited submissions during a single three-hour meeting, equipped only with a loose checklist of thematic criteria and no apparatus for comparative ranking or full information retrieval. Could you design a teaching plan for this session? Specifically, I need the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced—whether bounded rationality ought to be established as an exogenous cognitive and structural constraint before satisficing is presented as the adaptive procedural response, or the reverse—and an explicit justification of that dependency.

In addition, please advise how to deploy the library-committee case as the carrying example: what properties it must possess (severe time pressure, information overload, absence of a cardinal utility function) and what it must deliberately lack (any feasible path to global optimisation, hidden commercial incentives, or reliance on expert tacit knowledge) so that it clarifies rather than obscures the idea. I also need a map of the misconceptions this audience is likely to form—for instance, that satisficing is merely intellectual laziness, or that bounded rationality denotes irrationality rather than a systematic structural limitation—together with the exact point in the explanatory sequence at which each should be preempted. Furthermore, please provide one diagnostic question I can ask at the close to distinguish authentic comprehension from polite assent, and a clear statement of what ought to be omitted at this level—such as formal treatments of computational intractability or Simon’s later administrative-behaviour research—along with a brief rationale for each exclusion. Please present your response in British English, and do not include any bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I shall have no access to external sources during the seminar.
