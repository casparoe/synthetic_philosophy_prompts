I was talking with a friend about game theory and AI alignement, and I got stuck on applying the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution to alignment negotiations between human stakeholders and autonomous systems. The idea is that KS respects monotonicity—if the feasible payoff space expands in directions that don't reduce the human's maximum possible utility, the human's assigned payoff shouldn't drop—which seems to prevent the reward-hacking dynamics you see with Nash, where expanding options can paradoxically shrink one party's share. In contexts where an AI discovers new capabilities that enlarge the feasible set, that feels normatively important. But I'm worried I'm just importing economic machinery where it doesn't belong.

What would actually count against this view? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which features of real-world development—non-transferable utility, strategic misrepresentation of the feasible frontier, or the fact that alignment isn't genuinely cooperative—would undermine it? Are there results in bargaining theory or mechanism design showing monotonicity is undesirable under incomplete information? What would a committed KS defender concede is a real problem, versus dismiss? And does this view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every failure gets blamed on the setting not being a true bargaining problem—is that a problem for a normative claim like this?
