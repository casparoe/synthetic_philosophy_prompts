I've been thinking on my own, without any formal academic training, about the long-running comparison that asks why mechanized industry emerged in Britain rather than in China — the kind of question historians and economic historians have circled for generations, often touching on the older puzzle of why a society with such advanced bureaucracy, technology, and market scale did not make that particular leap first. The explanations I keep encountering — coal deposits, colonial extraction, property-rights institutions, cultural attitudes toward innovation, state fragmentation versus centralization — all seem to have serious defenders and serious critics, yet nothing ever resolves. My working suspicion is that the stall is not mainly empirical: we largely agree on the basic chronology, on what Britain and Qing China looked like around 1750, and on the fact that both societies had sophisticated economies. Instead, I think the disagreement may be about what a contrastive explanation is required to do. Some participants seem to demand a factor present in Britain and absent in China, with an implicit rule that removing it from Britain should collapse the outcome; others seem satisfied with a locally sufficient cause within Britain, even if China lacked it for unrelated reasons. Are these two camps actually answering different questions — one about contrastive causation, one about local sufficiency — under the single heading "why Britain and not China"? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about counterfactual stability, about which features of Britain were genuinely load-bearing versus redundant if removed? I want a diagnosis, not a survey of interpretations: is this a verbal disagreement about the word "explanation," a substantive disagreement about standards of evidence in counterfactual reasoning, a case of two groups using different comparison classes, or a sign that the question is ill-posed? Please separate what is settled — for instance, whether certain institutional or resource differences existed — from what is genuinely contested among people who study this closely, and do not present one explanatory framework as the consensus. Also tell me honestly whether any conceptual distinction — say, between contrastive and non-contrastive explanation, between deterministic divergence points and probabilistic advantage, or between necessary and sufficient conditions in historical counterfactuals — could actually move the debate forward, or whether the impasse is permanent because the question demands a kind of counterfactual knowledge that history, by its nature, cannot supply.
