Since receiving a diagnosis that was only recently formalized within mainstream psychiatric nosology—specifically a prolonged grief-related category—and since beginning to work with digital phenotyping datasets in which patient-reported symptoms are continuously fed back into algorithmic screening protocols, I have become personally invested in a philosophical question that now feels urgent rather than abstract. The question concerns the status of psychiatric classifications in light of looping effects: do diagnostic categories describe pre-existing conditions, or do they partly constitute the phenomena they claim to classify?

On one side stand philosophers working in the tradition of Hacking, who treat psychiatric kinds as interactive kinds. They argue that being classified changes self-understanding and behavior, that prevalence and symptom profiles shift in response to the category itself, and that this feedback undermines any straightforward claim that the kind is a natural kind discovered rather than shaped. On the other side stand clinicians and philosophers of medicine who accept that looping occurs but treat it as epistemically manageable. They maintain that predictive validity, treatment response, and underlying pathophysiology provide stable anchors, so that classification can be both interactive and genuinely explanatory without collapsing into pure construction.

Despite decades of exchange, the debate appears stalled. Each side has standard rebuttals—the loopers point to shifting prevalence curves, the stabilizers point to consistent biomarkers—and neither moves. My own suspicion is that the impasse is not mainly empirical, since both sides grant that classification influences identity. Rather, I suspect a deeper disagreement about the criteria for successful psychiatric explanation: whether therapeutic utility and predictive power can be separated from metaphysical commitments about kind stability, or whether asking for separation already begs the question.

But I am also struck by a development neither tradition seems to have fully absorbed. Real-time digital monitoring and algorithmic screening have introduced what might be called second-order looping: the data generated by classified individuals does not merely feed back into clinical understanding over years, but reshapes prevalence estimates and symptom thresholds within months, altering the very algorithms that determine who receives the diagnosis. This accelerated feedback may not fit Hacking’s original framework, which was developed for slower, institutionally mediated loops. I want to know whether this changes the diagnosis of the debate itself.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal—centered on competing uses of terms like natural kind, objectivity, or explanation? Is it substantive but empirically underdetermined, with both sides interpreting the same clinical and epidemiological data through incompatible frameworks? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about metaphysics, one about clinical utility—under a single heading? In your answer, address specifically whether algorithmically mediated looping collapses the distinction between discovery and construction in a way that makes the traditional debate obsolete, or whether it merely intensifies a loop that was already present.

Defend your diagnosis with reference to what each side actually claims, not with a survey of the literature. Then explain what would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, a formal model of feedback dynamics that generates testable predictions, an empirical design that could break the symmetry between the positions, or an argument that the framing should be abandoned in favor of a different question altogether. I ask this because I need to decide whether to commit my research over the coming years to this problem or to navigate around it, and I want a reasoned judgment about whether the disagreement is tractable, ill-posed, or simply not worth the investment.
