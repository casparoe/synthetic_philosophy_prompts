Write a short story set in a near-future archival institution where all personal records must be translated into a belief-free vocabulary: no attributions of desire, intention, or propositional attitude are permitted, only behavioral predictions, neural-state descriptions, and environmental correlations. The protagonist is a senior translator assigned to convert a cache of historical love letters saturated with mentalistic language—phrases such as “I believe you care for me” and “I want to spend my life with you.” As she works, she notices that the predictive models she generates capture every behavioral regularity mentioned in the letters, yet she feels they miss something structurally important about the relationships described; however, she cannot articulate the gap without using the very vocabulary her profession has eliminated. Through her conversations with a colleague who insists the translations are not merely adequate but ontologically superior—since beliefs and desires never existed to begin with—explore whether eliminative materialism is best understood as a scientific discovery that folk psychology was always false, or as a linguistic reform that preserves all observable phenomena while rendering certain human experiences permanently inexpressible. Show, rather than explain, how intimacy might appear from within a framework that denies anyone ever genuinely believed or wanted anything.
