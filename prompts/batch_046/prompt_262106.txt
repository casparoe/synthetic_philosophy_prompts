and here's the student answer (copied from my notes, might have a typo):

"In the Symposium, alcohol is present but Socrates drinks with everyone yet never gets drunk, showing that true wisdom isn't affected by bodily states. The symposiarch in that dialogue regulates the drinking so the conversation doesn't collapse into chaos. In the Laws, the Athenian Stranger proposes that older citizens drink together under supervision so they can speak truthfully about legislation. This is basically the same mechanism: alcohol lowers inhibitions, but reason — embodied in the leader — keeps it constructive. So Plato's view is consistent across both texts. He thinks wine reveals your true self, but only if there's a rational framework around it. The Symposium shows this working in a private philosophical setting, while the Laws shows it scaled up for civic life, which is basically structured vulnerability since they're being exposed to risk in a safe environment. There's no contradiction about reason coexisting with wine; Plato just thinks most people need external control, while Socrates has internal control. Some scholars say the Laws is more pessimistic about human nature, but i think that's wrong — it's just practical politics adapting the same idea."
