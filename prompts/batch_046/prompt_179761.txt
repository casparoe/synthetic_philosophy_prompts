I'm in this seminar on philosophy of life and we've been stuckk for weeks on whether absurdism is actually a theory of meaning or just a kind of mood you live with. One camp — mostly people who take Camus seriously — says that revolt and embracing the absurd counts as a genuine account of meaning, not mere coping, because meaning doesn't need cosmic justification or an objective value list to be real. The other camp says that's not meaning at all, just a psychological coping mechanisn, and that real meaning needs narrative coherence or active engagement with values that exist independently of your attitude. We've looped through all the usual moves but nobody budges: the absurdists accuse the coherence side of smuggling in a transcendent need that human life can't satisfy, and the coherence camp accuses the absurdists of redefining "meaning" to avoid admitting life lacks it.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn't really about meaning but about what kind of explanation has to do the work — like, one side wants a descriptive account of how people actually sustain purpose under cosmic insignificance, and the other wants a normative account of what makes a life genuinely good, and they're using the same word for both jobs. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical question about human psychology, or a verbal confusion between "meaning," "value," and "purpose"? I want an actual diagnosis with reasons, not another survey of positions, and an honest answer about whether anything — a conceptual split, an empirical finding, a change in what we're asking — could actually break the deadlock.
