I serve as the student representative on my high school’s Joint Ethics and Oversight Committee, which reviews every research and technology proposal involving living organisms or adaptive autonomous systems used on campus. Next Thursday we must vote on a revision to our current binary policy—under which only vertebrate animals receive full ethical review—brought jointly by the robotics club, the biology department, and one physics faculty member. I was discussing the case with my friends after AP Philosophy, where we had been considering whether moral status might come in degrees rather than all-or-nothing, and I now see that the board is effectively being asked to institutionalize a scalar framework with concrete boundaries.

The proposal assigns entities to four tiers. Tier 1 covers vertebrates with centralized nociceptive integration. Tier 2 covers invertebrates—specifically the crickets used in our behavioral neuroscience unit—that possess distributed nociceptive pathways but lack a unified neural architecture; the biology faculty want Tier 2 experiments approved with only abbreviated review. Tier 3 applies to adaptive algorithmic systems with sustained homeostatic feedback loops, which the robotics club argues describes our building’s climate-management AI and requests recognition so the system can be included in our annual ethical-technology showcase. Tier 4 includes simple reactive devices such as conventional thermostats, and a minority faculty report insists these must fall under the same logic if homeostasis is the operative criterion, since they too regulate temperature toward a set point; the majority finds this conclusion absurd but has not shown why it can be excluded without abandoning the scalar principle altogether.

Please draft the formal opinion memo I should circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action—adopt the scalar framework with precise tier boundaries, reject it and preserve the binary rule, or adopt a modified version with conditions—together with a clear implementation timeline, such as a pilot semester or an immediate vote with a mandatory review date. The memo must treat the strongest opposing consideration seriously: if you recommend keeping the binary threshold, you must engage the robotics club’s analogy between homeostatic AI and low-grade biological sentience rather than ignoring it; if you recommend the scalar model, you must address the minority report’s thermostat objection without treating it as a mere reductio ad absurdum. The reasoning should remain accessible to committee members who are not specialists in philosophy, yet precise enough that faculty reviewers can see exactly where the line is drawn and why that location is defensible.
