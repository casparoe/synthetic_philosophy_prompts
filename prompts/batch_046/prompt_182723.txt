Consider a cultural critic applying Nietzsche's framework of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. Her prior judgments are these: she approves a civic ritual that commemorates a revolutionary victory to inspire present reformers; she conditionally approves a family preserving ancestral letters purely for continuity, warning that preservation without present purpose risks paralysis; she approves demolishing monuments to a discredited tyrant as necessary for collective liberation; she rejects an encyclopedic chronicle that records every event without evaluative selection, on the grounds that it serves neither action, preservation, nor liberation; and she rejects applying destructive critical methods to ancient texts that pose no present oppressive burden, arguing that critical history is misdirected when there is nothing to liberate from. Now consider a proposed "adaptive heritage system": an algorithm continuously rewrites a city's public historical displays, amplifying heroic narratives when civic engagement is low, immutably archiving every superseded version in a distributed ledger, and suppressing accounts tied to documented injustices whenever users report feeling burdened by them. The system selects its mode—monumental, antiquarian, or critical—not by the intrinsic nature of the historical material, but by real-time feedback about the community's present psychological state. Predict whether the critic should approve, reject, or conditionally approve this system for consistency with her track record; identify the closest prior analog; lay out at least two coherent rules that jointly explain all five earlier verdicts but diverge on this case; state which rule she is likely running, what verdict it yields, and what principle the opposite verdict would commit her to; and specify the further case that would best distinguish the two rules.
