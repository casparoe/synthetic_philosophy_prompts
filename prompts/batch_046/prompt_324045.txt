I sit on the clinical ethics committee at a regional academic medical center, and we are divided over Case 2024-09, which raises a philosophical issue that the clinical staff have been unable to resolve with standard protocols alone. The patient is a 38-year-old adult who, following a targeted neurosurgical intervention for an unrelated condition, now exhibits a neurological profile closely matching descriptions of pain asymbolia found in the clinical-neuroscience literature: she reports nociceptive sensations—sharp, burning, localized—with accurate intensity scaling, yet she exhibits no behavioral withdrawal, does not request analgesics, and describes these states as "interesting" or "neutral" rather than unpleasant. Concurrently, her physicians document progressive tissue damage that she does not avoid, precisely because the sensations carry no aversive valence. Her family has petitioned the committee to override her refusal of both analgesic therapy and protective restraint, arguing that the absence of suffering is itself a deficit in her evaluative apparatus: without pain's normal badness, they claim, she cannot autonomously weigh risks, and the hospital has a duty to restore the protective function that her affective system has lost.

Please draft the formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action—approve her refusal, reject it and impose protective measures, or approve with strict conditions and a defined review timeline—and it must include reasoning that both sides of the committee can recognize as fair. Specifically, I ask you to treat the conceptual dispute about the nature of pain's moral significance with some precision: distinguish the thesis that pain is bad primarily because of its affective, suffering-laden quality (which this patient lacks) from the thesis that its urgency derives from its informational, protective role in guiding behavior (which remains intact but is behaviorally ignored). Your memo should not simply invoke the legal definition of decision-making capacity; it should explain whether and how the absence of affective pain undermines autonomy in this specific case, and whether the hospital's duty of non-maleficence requires intervening to prevent damage even when the patient reports no suffering. The document should be actionable: specify who monitors the patient, what triggers a reassessment, and how long the current authorization lasts. Finally, address the strongest argument for the opposing view—that either autonomy must be respected despite the missing affect, or that protection must override autonomy because the affective deficit is a form of impaired judgment—without caricaturing it.
