People defending lost-cause myths -- the Confederacy's 'Lost Cause', Masada in Israel, Kosovo Polje for Serbs, even Dunkirk spun into a moral victory -- often reach for the analogy that national memory is like a family photo album. You don't burn the album just becuase some photos show your relatives being awful; tearing pages out is self-mutilation, and the album is what makes you 'you'. They use this to claim that revising or 'canceling' these narratives destroys collective identity.

Lately I've also run into this idea called 'mnemonic labor' -- remembering isn't just storage but active work that shapes what's politically possible right now. I don't fully grasp it but it feels relevant.

Can you audit the photo-album analogy? Map the correspondences (album, photos, tearing pages, the family), show where it breaks, identify what it quietly imports about whether identity requires moral continuity with the past, and say whether it can actually carry the conclusion that these myths should be preserved. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the argument from the ones that don't matter. Also, is there a sharper analogy -- maybe a myth is like a load-bearing wall, or like a dead-pathogen vaccine? Which one does the work better?
