As a novelist accustomed to constructing characters whose fates hinge on the caprice of others, I have come to hold a strong version of republican liberty—freedom as non-domination—as the only adequate account of unfreedom. Negative liberty, I think, mistakes the absence of actual interference for freedom, thereby classifying the slave of a kindly master as free; positive liberty, by identifying freedom with self-mastery or collective autonomy, risks licensing paternalistic authorship over another's life. But I want to extend this framework with what I call narrative sovereignty: the condition in which one agent retains an unaccountable, unilateral capacity to determine the conditions of another's agency, regardless of whether that capacity is ever exercised. On this view, domination is not merely a political relation but a structural feature of any relationship where one party can arbitrarily rewrite the narrative conditions under which another acts. This amendment seems to me to expose the inadequacy of rival conceptions more sharply than standard republican arguments do. Still, I want an honest map of what would count against it—not the familiar charge that republican liberty collapses into negative liberty once we specify interference ranges, or that arbitrariness is irreducibly vague, but the genuinely damaging defeaters: which conceptual distinctions, which counterexamples from the philosophy of autonomy or social power, which theoretical results about agency and persistence, and which intuitive cases—perhaps involving benevolent guardianship, collective self-binding, or willingly assumed subordination—stand in fatal tension with the view? I ask that you rank these from decisive to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of this expanded republican position, including the narrative-sovereignty clause, would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss as misdescription. I also want to know whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability—whether every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by redefining domination or sovereignty—and whether such insulation is a theoretical vice for a framework meant to guide judgment about real relations of power.
