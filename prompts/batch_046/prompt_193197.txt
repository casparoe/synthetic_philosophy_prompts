They were reviewing a two-stage hiring system—an automated resume screener outputs risk scores, and then human reviewers make final hiring decisions. They discovered that if the screener satisfies within-group calibration—so that a score of 0.8 implies the same predicted success rate regardless of gender—then demographic parity in advancement rates is violated, given that observed base rates of success differ across groups. They recognized this as an impossibility tension but disagreed sharply on what to do about it and whether the architecture of the pipeline changes the problem.

Below is their exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each step, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly correct, or clearly right, with brief justification; then say who is more right overall, what important point both sides miss, and whether there is any coherent way to hold both of their core concerns together.

Move 1 (Maya): We should drop within-group calibration and enforce demographic parity at the screening stage. The primary legal and ethical obligation is equal opportunity to reach the interview stage; statistical consistency of a score is secondary to that.

Move 2 (Leo): That ignores what the score is actually for. If 0.8 means an 80 percent likelihood of success for one group but only a 60 percent likelihood for another, the human reviewers in stage two are operating with a mislabeled input. Calibration is not a hidden moral commitment; it is a precondition for the score to be interpretable by anyone using it.

Move 3 (Maya): Calibration encodes historical bias. If one group was previously given worse assignments or less mentorship, their observed success rates are depressed for reasons that have nothing to do with merit. Insisting on calibration therefore locks past discrimination into the present model.

Move 4 (Leo): You are conflating causal fairness with predictive fairness. The impossibility result does not claim that calibration is morally correct; it shows only that calibration, demographic parity, and unequal base rates cannot all hold simultaneously. But if we abandon calibration, we are not repairing the underlying injustice—we are merely obscuring it behind a uniform threshold that misleads decision-makers about what the numbers mean.

Move 5 (Maya): We can route around the impossibility by distributing the metrics across stages. Enforce demographic parity at stage one so that equal proportions advance, and then require calibration at stage two, where human reviewers apply group-aware contextual adjustments. The impossibility applies to a single decision rule, not to a sequential pipeline with different decision-makers at each step.

Move 6 (Leo): That is a category error. The impossibility concerns the joint distribution of predictions, protected attributes, and outcomes across the system as a whole. Splitting metrics across stages does not dissolve the tension; it relocates it. If stage one selects populations with different base-rate compositions into stage two, then stage two's calibration is computed on a distorted sample, which means neither metric is genuinely satisfied globally.
