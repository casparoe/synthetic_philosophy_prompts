I'm defending a thesis I call "recursive sphexishness" at a philosophy of action workshop next Thursday. The thesis is that free will is not the opposite of mechanical behavioral rigidity—the kind exhibited by the digger wasp Sphex, which repeats its burrow-checking routine invariantly when its prey is moved—but rather a specific hierarchical form of it: human agency consists in embedding rigid first-order subroutines within flexible-looking meta-routines, so that our "freedom" is literally a second-order rigidity about which first-order rigidities to activate. In other words, flexibility is not the absence of sphexish loops but their strategic nesting, and the feeling of open choice arises from introspective blindness to the governing meta-loop rather than from any break in deterministic causation.

The audience is a philosophy of action reading group: three compatibilists who think free-will talk should be replaced entirely by reasons-responsiveness and who will see my view as needlessly mechanistic; two libertarian-leaning PhD students who will argue that nesting rigidity merely pushes the problem up one level without explaining genuine origination; and one cognitive scientist who studies habit automation and will ask whether my hierarchy is empirically distinguishable from standard dual-process models.

Here is the sketch of my argument they should target: first-order sphexishness (invariant repetition of a behavioral loop) is ubiquitous in nature; second-order sphexishness—the rigid, context-governed switching between first-order loops—is uniquely developed in humans and is misidentified as "flexibility"; because the switch itself follows deterministic rules, agency remains fully naturalistic, but we must accept that "choosing" is structurally loop-like at every level. The payoff is an account of agency without non-physical causation; the cost is that our intuitive sense of being able to do otherwise is explained away as an epistemic limitation rather than a metaphysical capacity.
