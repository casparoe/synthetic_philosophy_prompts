I serve on my university's retirement-benefits oversight committee, and by Thursday we must decide whether to approve a pilot program that lets staff voluntarily enter binding two-year contracts on retirement contribution rates: once enrolled, the rate may be increased but never lowered, even in documented financial hardship. The proposal is explicitly framed around hyperbolic discounting—employees systematically undervalue distant retirement income relative to present consumption, producing a form of diachronic weakness of will or akrasia across time. Proponents argue the contract merely helps people execute their own long-term preferences against temporary present bias; opponents argue it treats rational agents as permanently defective, binds a future self whose preferences may legitimately change, and substitutes institutional architecture for genuine autonomy. Please draft the short opinion memo I will circulate before the meeting: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with concrete conditions and safeguards; treat the autonomy objection seriously rather than dismissing it as mere resistance to saving; specify what principle distinguishes this voluntary contract from a mandatory version of the same policy; and include a clear sunset or review mechanism if you recommend going forward.
