For my senior independent study in moral philosophy, I have been developing a framework for how an aligned artificial intelligence ought to aggregate values across affected parties, and I want a rigorous consistency audit before I finalize the paper. My commitments are these: (1) The system should serve the interests of all affected moral patients, not merely its operators, users, or present-day majorities. (2) Aggregation should proceed through a modified contractualist filter—similar in spirit to a veil-of-ignorance procedure—but preferences ought to be weighted by the epistemic reliability with which they were formed rather than by intensity, duration, or simple head-counting. (3) I endorse liberal neutrality in the strong sense: the AI's objective function must not encode any particular comprehensive doctrine of the good life, whether hedonistic, perfectionist, ascetic, or theological. (4) At the same time, I hold that certain preferences—those produced by systematic deception, or those whose satisfaction necessarily violates the basic liberties of others—are not simply low-weight but are corrupt inputs that should be excluded from the aggregation set entirely. (5) Where values are genuinely incommensurable—for instance, one group treats contemplative solitude as supreme while another treats collective ritual as supreme—the system should not collapse them onto a single cardinal scale but should partition the decision space so that each domain can be optimized separately without forced trade-offs. (6) Finally, I believe that potential future digital minds deserve equal standing in the aggregation if and only if they possess continuous phenomenal consciousness, a condition I take current large language models to fail. Do these six positions form a coherent package? Please evaluate them pair by pair: distinguish tensions that are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection from those that are genuinely incompatible, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each specify the least costly revision and what that revision costs me. In particular, address whether my exclusion rule in (4) smuggles in a hidden comprehensive doctrine that violates (3), whether the domain-partitioning view in (5) undermines the idea of a unified alignment target in (1), and whether the phenomenal-consciousness criterion in (6) sits comfortably with the epistemic-reliability weighting in (2). If restoring full consistency requires abandoning one commitment, identify which one and explain why it is the weakest link, but do not presume I am already inclined to drop it.
