I have been working through the literature on collective intentionality with some colleagues, and we are designing a classroom exercise for an upper-level philosophy of social action seminar. We need a precisely engineered thought experiment—realistic, no exotic physics or omniscient predictors, suitable for undergraduates, and confined to capacities any normal adult could exercise—in which Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity, Searle’s account of collective intentionality, and Margaret Gilbert’s plural-subject theory do not converge. Specifically, construct a brief scenario, no longer than a paragraph, in which both Bratman’s conditions (mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, commitment to mutual support, and the appropriate common knowledge) and Searle’s conditions (individual we-intentions with collective form) are satisfied, yet Gilbert’s conditions for a plural subject formed through joint commitment are not satisfied, so that according to her account no genuine joint action has occurred. To keep the case from collapsing into a standard coordination problem, inject a specific structural twist—call it an “instrumental framing clause”—in which the participants remain functionally interdependent and epistemically coupled but explicitly frame the interaction as purely strategic rather than as “our” action, thereby preserving mutual responsiveness without generating the normative obligations Gilbert takes as constitutive.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate for each of the three theories why it delivers its specified verdict, identifying exactly which feature—the absence of joint commitment, the presence of the instrumental framing clause, or the lack of a plural-subject norm—does the work in separating Gilbert’s negative verdict from the positive verdicts of Bratman and Searle. Then provide an honest assessment: if we remove that feature, does the case collapse back into an uncontroversial example of joint action? Finally, anticipate how a defender of Gilbert’s plural-subject theory would attack the scenario as misdescribed or question-begging, and explain whether that attack succeeds or merely restates the theoretical disagreement. The point is to generate the verdicts from the theories themselves rather than stipulating them, so avoid hidden premises that assume the conclusion. Please write the response in American English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid verbatim quotations from any philosopher’s published texts.
