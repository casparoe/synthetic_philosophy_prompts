I'm facilitating a 75-minute workshop on collective intentionality and joint action for fourteen philosophy graduate students. I want them to experience—before anyone names the concept—the particular fragility that distinguishes a genuinely shared cooperative activity from a mere coordination of individual intentions: specifically, the moment when one participant unilaterally reinterprets what "we" are doing together, without anyone technically withdrawing or defecting, and the collective action either collapses or continues under a description that half the group no longer endorses. Design a structured, low-materials activity (no screens, minimal props) that engineers this breakdown. Include hidden role instructions distributed privately, a phased timeline (formation, execution, crisis, resolution or dissolution), rules that prohibit explicit renegotiation of the shared goal once execution begins, and a symbolic or social consequence structure—rather than monetary payoffs—that makes the rupture genuinely felt. The participants should not hear terms like "joint commitment," "plural subject," or "we-mode" until the debrief.

Then provide the debrief: map each phase onto the conceptual distinction between summed individual intentions and an irreducible joint commitment, pinpoint exactly what happens in the crisis phase when one party exercises unilateral redescriptive authority over the collective end, and be explicit about which feature of joint action—say, the normative binding that holds plural subjects together, or the phenomenological "we-feeling" of acting together—this simulation can reproduce and which it necessarily cannot, along with how you would supply the missing feature through discussion rather than rules. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely a premature collapse, an accidental resolution through politeness, or participants treating the hidden instructions as deception—and how to handle that failure without undermining the philosophical point. Keep the setup formal enough that the idea emerges from the incentives and roles rather than from anything you tell them afterward.
