After a long argument with a friend about the surprise exam paradox, I want to know whether my judgments about its variants rest on a coherent principle or are merely ad hoc. Case 1: The classic announcement—"a surprise exam will occur on one day next week"—followed by backward induction that eliminates Friday, then Thursday, and so on. I think the induction fails because it treats the professor's contingent announcement as an immutable logical premise; consequently, giving the exam on Friday genuinely surprises the students, and the paradox dissolves without denying any premise except an implausible assumption that the announcement itself is immune to being falsified by the event it predicts. Case 2: The professor seals the exam date in an envelope opened by a quantum-random process unknown to the students; they understand the physics but not the outcome. Here I think there is never a paradox—no day can be ruled out—yet every day remains surprising, which feels structurally different from Case 1 rather than a mere resolution of it. Case 3: The professor promises "you will be able to derive the day with certainty, yet you will lack subjective confidence in that derivation until the exam is before you." I think this separates epistemic surprise from logical predictability and dissolves the tension, though my friend dismisses it as verbal sleight-of-hand. Case 4: The date is fixed by a deterministic but computationally intractable algorithm; students could not finish the calculation in time. I think a "theoretical surprise" is undermined even if practical shock remains, so the paradox partially applies. Case 5: The professor adds a post-exam certification—"I will confirm afterward whether it was genuinely surprising"—which seems to feed back into the students' reasoning in a self-referential loop that makes the original announcement incoherent, unlike Cases 1–4. Is there one principle—perhaps tracking whether the surprise-condition is stable under the specific kind of epistemic access available to the students—that captures all five verdicts? State it precisely, show what it predicts for a variant where an AI scheduler predicts student beliefs and deliberately avoids the day they expect, and tell me honestly which verdict, if any, I must abandon to keep the principle simple.
