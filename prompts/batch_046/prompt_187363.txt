I have been trying to work out on my own whether there are genuinely unavoidable moral conflicts—situations in which every course of action violates some real duty, rather than merely disappointing a preference. It strikes me that if such dilemmas are real, they cannot be isolated incidents; each one must leave behind an unresolved obligation to the option not taken. Over a lifetime, these residual obligations ought to accumulate like unpaid debts, eventually overwhelming any person's moral standing. From this I conclude that if even one authentic moral dilemma occurs, perfect moral integrity becomes structurally impossible, since the agent can never fully discharge all concurrent duties. I have even begun to wonder whether societies implicitly recognize this problem through practices of collective acknowledgment or ritual forgiveness that function as a kind of moral bankruptcy proceeding—canceling accumulated debt rather than resolving each claim individually. So my question is: does the existence of genuine moral dilemmas necessarily entail that every morally serious person must eventually become irredeemably indebted, or is there a coherent account of how these lingering obligations are genuinely dissolved rather than simply ignored?
