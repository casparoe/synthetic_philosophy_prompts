Consider three institutional mechanisms that are rarely compared in detail: the holistic-review affirmative-action framework used in selective U.S. university admissions, where race may serve as a plus factor but never as a mechanical quota; the certificate-based caste reservation system in Indian public-sector employment and higher education, where eligibility is determined by administratively verified hereditary category and seats are set aside as hard percentages; and Norway's boardroom gender-quota law for public limited companies, which mandates at least 40 percent representation of each sex and carries severe enforcement penalties.

These regimes differ in legal form, the social categories they invoke, and their stated justifications—educational mission and diversity in the U.S. case, historical redress and representational justice in the Indian case, democratic parity and economic efficiency in the Norwegian case. Yet all three treat group-level statistical disparity as a direct trigger for state-mandated distributive correction, rather than treating disparity solely as evidence of hidden individual-level discrimination to be remedied case by case.

What philosophical commitments about the ontology of social groups, the temporal structure of injustice, and the metric of fair distribution does this shared pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—distinguishing what the practices strictly require from what merely fits them well—paying attention to whether they presuppose (a) that group membership carries non-derivative moral weight, (b) that present individuals can stand in compensatory relations to past institutional wrongs they did not personally suffer, or (c) that proportional presence is an intrinsic good rather than an instrumental one. Are the three regimes philosophically coherent with one another, or do they quietly depend on incompatible views about whether identity is essential, constructed, or administratively assigned?

Then consider a counterfactual design: suppose a jurisdiction replaced all group-based criteria with a pathway-based rule that reserved positions solely for applicants whose parents lacked tertiary education or professional credentials, regardless of race, caste, or gender, calibrated to reproduce the same statistical distribution of outcomes. Would the philosophical justification of the existing practices survive this substitution intact, or does it reveal that the practices presuppose something specifically about the moral salience of race, caste, and gender that pathway-dependence cannot replicate? And if the underlying commitment is not to any particular group but to breaking intergenerational transmission of advantage, what exactly in the current institutional architecture—verification mechanisms, legal categories, sunset provisions—would have to be rebuilt?
