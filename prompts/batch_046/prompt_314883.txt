In Europe, commercial surrogacy is banned in most jurisdictions: compensation is limited to reimbursement of direct expenses, and several countries extend that prohibition to citizens who travel abroad to enter commercial arrangements, with the legal and policy rationale typically invoking human dignity, the non-commodification of the body, and the protection of both the gestational carrier and the child from being treated as market goods or mere means. In the United States, by contrast, commercial surrogacy operates as a contract-based market in many states, with agencies screening and matching gestational carriers and egg donors, compensation structures that scale with prior experience, successful prior births, and whether the pregnancy involves multiples, and legal mechanisms—such as pre-birth parentage orders—that treat the intended parents as the legal parents from conception and frame the carrier’s role as a contracted reproductive service. What philosophical commitments about personhood, bodily integrity, commodification, and the moral status of reproductive labor does each regime presuppose? Does the European prohibition strictly require a dignity-based or Kantian view that treats gestation as inherently non-alienable, or could a consequentialist framework concerned with coercion, exploitation, and asymmetrical risk justify the same ban? Conversely, does the American market necessarily presuppose a libertarian or contractarian conception of the body as alienable property, or could it be defended on autonomy grounds that treat compensated gestation as labor like any other?
