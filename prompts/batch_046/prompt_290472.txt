As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy with any discipline, I find myself leading an informal seminar for other retired scientists—engineers, chemists, and mathematicians—who are intellectually sharp but possess no formal training in moral philosophy. For our next session I wish to introduce Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection, particularly the thirteen virtues and the iterative tracking method he describes in his autobiographical writings. I do not want a biographical lecture; I want a rigorous teaching plan tailored to this audience. First, specify the precise dependency order in which the conceptual components must be introduced—beginning with whatever must be in place before the notion of “moral perfection” can sound like a serious philosophical project rather than a naïve self-improvement scheme—so that each layer structurally supports the next. Second, identify the single carrying example or analog, preferably drawn from scientific practice such as instrument calibration, feedback-loop refinement, or error-correction protocols, that can illuminate Franklin’s weekly rotation and self-examination method; explain exactly what this analog must capture and what it must avoid distorting. Third, map the misconceptions this particular audience is prone to form—perhaps interpreting the thirteen virtues as a quantitative optimization problem, conflating his iterative tracking with modern behaviorist conditioning, or assuming he claimed actually to have achieved perfection—and indicate for each the exact point in the sequence where it should be preempted. Fourth, provide one concise diagnostic check—whether a targeted question, a brief counterfactual exercise, or a structured discussion prompt—that would reveal by the end whether a participant has grasped the structural logic of Franklin’s project rather than merely retained an anecdote about diligence. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and openly acknowledged as such—for instance, the relation of his project to Puritan theology or to contemporary virtue ethics—so that the session remains focused and does not overreach. The plan should assume no prior philosophical background and should not depend on assigned readings from secondary scholarship.
