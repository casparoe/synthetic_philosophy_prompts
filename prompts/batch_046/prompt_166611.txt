I cover philosophy of science and bioethics for a long-form publication, and I am preparing a feature on the conceptual architecture of valid consent under conditions of algorithmic mediation. Rather than treating competence, absence of deception, and absence of coercion as independent gates, I am exploring the heterodox view that they constitute interdependent dimensions of a single underlying capacity—call it epistemic sovereignty—where deception does not merely conceal information but actively degrades competence by distorting the cognitive architecture through which a person evaluates options, and where coercion is not only an external pressure but a structural collapse of the alternative-space that renders competence assessments moot. If this integrated framework is correct, what are the most important implications for evaluating consent in emerging contexts—such as predictive neurotechnology that calibrates preferences before they reach conscious formation, or algorithmic recommendation systems that structure the option space itself—where information and choice are not merely hidden or forced, but actively shaped?
