I am writing this for a philosophy of science seminar that has been working through the demarcation problem and falsificationism. The debate I see in the literature runs in a loop: Popper offers falsifiability as a logical criterion separating science from pseudoscience; critics show that astrology, Freudian theory, or even physics can be immunized against refutation through auxiliary hypotheses; defenders reply that demarcation should apply to research programs rather than isolated statements; critics then ask whether that move saves science only by abandoning any sharp logical boundary. Nothing seems to resolve it. My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not about the historical cases—everyone agrees that astronomy and astrology behave differently—but about a hidden disagreement over what kind of thing demarcation applies to: individual propositions, methodological rules governing a community, or institutional structures that enforce error correction. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement in disguise—perhaps a formal one about whether Popper's verification-falsification asymmetry actually holds for statistical hypotheses, where null results are always ambiguous? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of interpretations: do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Are they answering different questions under the name 'demarcation'? Is the question ill-posed, or just hard? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—could actually move it forward: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about statistical testing, a change of question, or should we conclude that no progress should be expected and why?
