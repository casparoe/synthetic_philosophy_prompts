I am a student in an upper-division seminar on genocide as a legal and moral category, and we have been working through Lemkin’s original broad conception—coordinated destruction of a group’s political, social, cultural, economic, and biological foundations—contrasted with the UN Convention’s narrower enumerated acts. Our professor applies a framework that distinguishes four modalities we have developed in the course: executive genocide (direct killing), biological genocide (measures intended to prevent births or cause lethal biological harm), cultural genocide (destruction of language, religion, and institutions without physical targeting), and infrastructural genocide (deliberate dismantling of the legal, archival, and spatial infrastructure that sustains collective existence, where lethal effects are instrumental rather than biologically targeted).

Over the term she has ruled consistently on the following cases. First, a documented state campaign of mass killing against a protected group: she called it unambiguous executive genocide and insisted that political reluctance to apply the label is moral evasion. Second, a historical program of forced assimilation through residential schooling, language suppression, and family separation with no systematic killing but elevated mortality from neglect: she classified it as cultural and infrastructural destruction, serious and possibly crimes against humanity, but not genocide under the Convention because the specific intent was assimilation rather than physical or biological destruction, though she noted Lemkin’s broader framework might capture parts of it differently. Third, a hypothetical state-mandated sterilization program targeting a religious minority with no killings: she ruled it biological genocide within Article II(d), warned against diluting the category by calling it only a reproductive rights violation, and emphasized that absence of killing does not diminish the genocidal character. Fourth, a scenario in which a regime destroys all archives and census records of an ethnic group, bans public language use, disperses populations into majority regions, and imposes economic conditions producing modest excess mortality from deprivation—while internal memos speak of solving the problem permanently through integration but never mention killing: she said this was primarily infrastructural genocide, that the lethal deprivation was an instrumental effect rather than a biologically targeted act, and that naming it full genocide risks conceptual inflation unless one can demonstrate that the economic conditions were deliberately calibrated to destroy the group physically. Fifth, a case of purely symbolic destruction—burning religious texts and monuments—where the group retains full citizenship, economic access, and physical security, and eventually recovers its practices: she called it cultural vandalism and a serious human rights abuse, but said applying the genocide label would be analytically wrong and politically counterproductive because the group’s collective existence was not threatened.

Now consider this new case, which introduces what our course terms registry dissolution as administrative absorption. A government implements a comprehensive Identity Integration Program for a specific ethnic minority. The program digitizes and then deletes all civil registry records identifying members of the group, replacing them with generic citizenship IDs that erase ethnic markers. It mandates dispersed relocation with legal prohibitions on ethnically homogeneous neighborhoods. It criminalizes the group’s language in all educational settings but not in private homes. State-provided healthcare and food remain technically available, but access requires navigating the new registry system, which is chronically malfunctioning for those lacking legacy documentation. Internal planning documents refer to the final administrative resolution of the minority question through total absorption, with no mention of killing or biological interference. Mortality has risen significantly—though not catastrophically—due to bureaucratic barriers to care and economic exclusion, and the group’s ability to organize collectively has been severely weakened by the loss of documentary identity and spatial dispersal.

Predict my professor’s verdict on whether this is genocide, and if so which modality or modalities apply. Identify the implicit rule or combination of rules she is running—whether strict Convention anchoring that requires specific intent for enumerated acts, an expanded-modalities intentionalism that treats infrastructural destruction as genocide when coupled with lethal effects, a politics-of-naming pragmatism that weighs conceptual inflation against victim recognition, or some other framework—and say which other rules fit her prior verdicts equally well. Name the further case that would best distinguish those competing rules. Then identify the precise feature of this scenario that is doing the normative work, explain what happens to her analysis if that feature is removed, and describe how a defender of the theory that comes out poorly here would attack the case. Keep the answer accessible to someone familiar with the basic Convention framework but not with our course’s specific taxonomy.
