Make a conceptual argument for the claim that presentist moral framing is not merely unavoidable in historiography but an indispensable explanatory resource—specifically in the study of institutionalized harm. Defend a thesis of double-vision historiography: the historian should simultaneously reconstruct past agents' internal normative grammars and apply present-day ethical categories that those grammars could not accommodate. Argue that the friction between these two frameworks—far from being a distorting anachronism—reveals structural mechanisms (for instance, how an institution distributed benefits and burdens in ways its own vocabulary systematically obscured) that neither a purely internalist nor a purely presentist account could expose. Address the standard anti-presentist objection that imposing modern values undermines historical understanding, and show how double-vision avoids this charge by treating present norms not as verdicts on past actors but as diagnostic instruments that make visible the gap between what a society's practices actually did and what its own concepts could recognize. Do not rely on bibliographic references or obscure theoretical labels; build the case conceptually, and illustrate it with a concrete hypothetical institution whose moral vocabulary lacked any equivalent to the modern concept of structural coercion.
