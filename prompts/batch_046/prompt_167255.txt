I am a philosophy professor preparing the opening address for an interdisciplinary workshop on reproductive identity. The audience is split evenly between philosophers of personal identity and clinical genetic counselors who guide families through donor conception, surrogacy, and embryo adoption but have no background in metaphysics. I want the question of whether one could have come from a different sperm and egg to do genuine structural work in the speech, not decorative work: it must determine exactly what I can and cannot claim about metaphysical continuity across these technologies, and this constraint should shape the welcome rather than appear as a digression. In particular, I need to navigate between strong gametic origin essentialism and a weaker trajectory-dependent view—where identity is tied not to the specific gametes but to the specific embryonic-developmental path, including uterine environment and early epigenetic marking—so that I do not offer the clinicians false metaphysical comfort nor bore the philosophers with oversimplification. The address should run about five minutes spoken, land for both halves without unexplained jargon, and honor origin stories without imposing false continuity claims. Please begin immediately with the speech text itself—no preface, no commentary, no “Here is the address”—with the first sentence stating the bottom-line claim about origin and identity that governs everything that follows, then developing the reasoning and its practical stakes for both groups.
