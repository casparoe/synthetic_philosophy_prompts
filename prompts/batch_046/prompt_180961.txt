After a long argument last night with a close friend — she maintained that blaming anyone for outcomes outside their control is always irrational, and I found myself defending the intuition that we do and should distinguish blameworthy agents even when luck dominates the result — I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session for a small interdisciplinary group (two environmental lawyers and an economist, none with philosophy training) on the problem of moral luck. I do not want the session to collapse into a debate about determinism, nor do I want them to memorize the standard taxonomy; I want them to see why the problem survives intact even for agents with full control over their intentions. I need a teaching plan — the order of ideas, the carrying case, the misconception map, and the check for understanding — rather than the lecture itself.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, identify the idea that must be firmly in place before moral luck can appear as a distinct philosophical problem rather than a confusion about determinism or statistical risk. Then specify the dependency-respecting sequence in which the three layers of control should be introduced: control over one's action, control over the circumstances in which one chooses, and control over the moral constitution one brings to the choice. Explain why this sequence must be cumulative rather than merely taxonomic — that is, why understanding the second layer requires having secured the first.

Next, choose one concrete carrying example that can bear all three layers. It should be drawn from a context the lawyers and economist will recognize — for instance, professional risk-allocation or contractual design — but it must avoid the overused drunk-driver comparison. The example must feature the same agent performing the same intentional action in two parallel conditions that differ only in an outcome-determining factor outside the agent's control, and it must allow you to vary outcomes without varying intentions or efforts.

Then map the misconceptions. I anticipate at least three: that moral luck is just determinism restated; that it can be dissolved by expected-utility or risk-management reasoning (the economist's likely move); and that accepting moral luck requires abandoning all distinctions between agents (a skeptical overreaction). For each, specify the exact point in the dependency sequence where it should be intercepted and defused, and what brief response or distinction neutralizes it without derailing the arc.

Include a final check — a single scenario or question — that reveals whether the group has grasped the conceptual tension rather than adopted a slogan like "blame is unfair whenever luck is involved." The check should be answerable in two ways, one that misses the point and one that demonstrates understanding.

Finally, state explicitly what standard part of the literature or taxonomy you are deliberately leaving out at this level, and why that omission protects the arc rather than simplifying it away.
