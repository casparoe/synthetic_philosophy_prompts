Write a joke that turns on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. In the setup, a decision-maker applies the principle of indifference to select a random policy argument spanning between two fixed opposing positions, treating every argument as a chord inside a circle representing the space of disagreement. Depending on whether she parameterizes the possibilities by picking two random endpoints on the boundary, picking a random midpoint inside the circle, or picking a random radial line and constructing the perpendicular chord, she computes three different probabilities that the argument is long enough—exceeding the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle—to bridge the gap. The punchline should hinge on the fact that her state of ignorance is not method-neutral: the same indifference principle, applied with equal justification to the identical set of arguments, yields contradictory probabilities solely because the continuous parameterization changed. Make the joke coherent to someone who understands that Bertrand's paradox undermines a naive geometric application of indifference, and ensure the humor arises from the agent treating a mathematical artifact of description as if it were a substantive discovery about which policies actually connect.
