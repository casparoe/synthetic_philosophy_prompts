Act like an expert in Nyāya epistemology and a senior referee for a cognitive-science journal. Before you answer, do a quick brainstorm of how each pramāṇa gets twisted when neuroscientists deal with first-person reports.

I need a reusable decision procedure — like a flowchart in words — that my editorial board can run on submissions where authors claim direct inner knowledge (say, "pre-reflective anxiety" or hidden suffering metrics) that clashes with behavioral or imaging data. Base it on the four pramāṇas: pratyakṣa, anumāna, śabda, upamāna.

Structure it as ordered quetions:
1. Perceptual check — what counts as valid pratyakṣa here, and when does a brain scan override or complement it?
2. Inference check — does the author's reasoning meet anumāna rules, or is there a hidden hetu error?
3. Testimony check — is the subject's self-report functioning as valid śabda, or is it circular?
4. Analogy check — are upamāna analogies to other minds or animal models being stretched?

At each stage, say where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it hits a value judgment about whose epistemic authority counts and must be handed to the board. Include a failure-mode note on where this will wrongly reject good papers or accept bad ones. Then run it on two submissions: (A) subjects report anxiety that fMRI misses, and (B) a study using animal pain analogies to score unobservable human suffering. Make sure one yields a recommendation the board won't like, and flag exactly where the checklist has to hand off to human values rather than rules. Also state clearly what the procedure can't do — e.g., resolve metaphysical disputes about other minds.

Keep it practical — board members have 20 minutes, no Sanskrit background, and can't look stuff up. Don't ask for reading lists or citations.
