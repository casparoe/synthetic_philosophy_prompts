Write a poem presented as exactly five numbered stanzas, each consisting of two sentences in blank verse, with no title, epigraph, or lines outside the numbered sequence. The speaker should be a museum curator debating whether to deaccession a nineteenth-century astrolabe whose calibration marks no current staff can read. Treat Chesterton’s fence not as nostalgia but as an epistemic check: the instrument might preserve a lost error-correction, a forgotten conceptual distinction, or a methodological purpose that modern practice has collapsed without noticing. Structure the stanzas to trace a Burkean arc—stanza 1, the practical impulse to clear storage space; stanza 2, the recognition that removal presupposes knowledge of why the object was kept; stanza 3, the admission that its purpose may be embedded in an obsolete framework now invisible to present users; stanza 4, the understanding that preservation is a suspension of judgment rather than an endorsement of the old science; stanza 5, a resolution that leaves the instrument in place but transforms the speaker’s posture toward institutional memory. Let the philosophical reasoning do structural work in the verse, so that the turn from action to restraint is audible in the meter and the progression of the stanzas.
