At a recent salon, a fellow novelist summarized for me a philosophical position she called tranquilism, which she traced through Schopenhauer and into certain contemporary accounts of negative welfare. According to her rendering, well-being is a strictly negative quantity: it consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, possessing no positive dimension at all. She likened pleasure to the relief a debtor feels when part of his obligation is canceled—never an actual credit, only a temporary reduction in an underlying deficit. Consequently, she argued, pursuits such as romantic love, artistic creation, or intellectual ambition carry no intrinsic value; they are at best instrumental analgesics, and at worst fresh sources of craving that deepen one's deficit. The rational life, on her account, is therefore not the cultivation of a garden but the draining of a swamp: the task is not to build anything, but only to remove disturbances, and if a pursuit fails to diminish suffering it ought to be abandoned without regret.

I found the portrait compelling yet too austere, and I am unsure whether the starkness belongs to the view itself or to her summary of it. Is this a fair characterization of tranquilism? I ask you to separate, first, the elements that accurately reflect the thesis as it is usually formulated—particularly regarding the asymmetry between suffering and pleasure, and the claim that positive states are reducible to absences—second, the parts that are common simplifications or overstatements, especially her denial of any intrinsic worth to love and art; and third, whether her practical conclusion genuinely follows from the accurate philosophical premises, or whether it smuggles in an unexamined assumption about the nature of desire that a more careful defender would reject. If the swamp-and-garden analogy quietly imports features that do not map onto the target, please identify where the correspondence breaks and whether a better analogy would preserve the view's actual commitments.
