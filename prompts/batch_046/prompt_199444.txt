I have been working through institutional design problems with a study group, and we keep returning to a concept that initially seemed unproblematic: the safe Pareto improvement. By the standard definition, a change qualifies if every affected party is either strictly better off or left unchanged, at least one party gains, and no one bears any risk. We have been applying this framework to a specific reform model—an AI-assisted curriculum and admissions planning system adopted by a university. Under the reform, every current professor and every current student is unambiguously improved: administrative workload declines, personalized feedback accelerates, and institutional budgets stabilize. Yet the system's optimization targets gradually reshape admission criteria and disciplinary priorities over roughly fifteen years, so that the student body enrolling in 2035 is composed of individuals who, in the relevant counterfactual, would not have existed at all. Several colleagues insist this is still a paradigmatic safe Pareto improvement, because the present cohort is strictly benefited and no counterfactual future individual can register a complaint of being made worse off.

I want to defend the opposite position: that once a policy determines not merely welfare levels but the very membership and identity of future reference classes, the "no one worse off" condition either collapses into vacuity or depends on a cross-population welfare comparison that must be justified independently of the improvement being assessed. Please construct a rigorous argument that safe Pareto improvements are conceptually impossible in any intertemporal or identity-affecting institutional context. Your argument should move beyond a simple invocation of the standard non-identity problem and instead develop a specific circularity objection—namely, that evaluating whether replaced beneficiary sets are "worse off" requires a baseline metric that already embeds the normative judgment the improvement is supposed to license. You should also respond directly to the reply that evaluators can restrict their assessment to presently existing agents, explaining why that restriction either strips the concept of its intergenerational normative authority or tacitly reintroduces the very comparison across disjoint populations it seeks to avoid.
