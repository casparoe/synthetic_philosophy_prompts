I retired a couple years back after running a neurobiology lab for decades, and now I'm trying to get my head around philospophy of action — specifically this thing people call the absent-minded driver problem, where someone means to turn left but just drives right past the turn because they're on autopilot. In my old work we'd model this as a glitch in the feedback loop between intention and motor execution, but philosophically it seems to splinter into a bunch of directions: whether the driver still counts as having the relevant intention, whether he's morally responsible if something goes wrong, and how much skilled behavior can run without any conscious monitoring at all.

What I'm really trying to sort out is which of these open questions actually matter for the bigger picture, and which are kind of dead ends or overated. There's talk about whether absent-mindedness breaks moral responsibility in a special way, separate from regular negligence; there's also this line connecting it to predictive-processing ideas about the brain running constant error-correction loops; and then there's a question about whether intentions are more like 'set and forget' programs rather than continuously updated mental states. I'm also wondering if people are underrating a fourth angle — whether this problem tells us anything about distributed or collective agency, like when a team of people is running on habit and no single person is tracking the goal.

If I only had a few years to think about this seriously, which line should I treat as upstream? Which questions depend on resolving others first? And if one of these turned out to be settled, which settlement would actually change how we design artificial agents, or at least how philosophers talk about responsibility? I want a defended ordering, not just a survey — what's tractable, what's conceptually central, and what's probably a distraction?
