Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the Pasadena paradox and the rational status of undefined expected values. The two camps are: (1) the Constraint Resolutionists, who hold that undefined expectations in Pasadena-style games are always artifacts of unrealistic modeling assumptions—specifically, unbounded utility functions paired with physically impossible payoff structures—and that once empirically grounded bounds on resources, risk aversion, and cognitive limits are imposed, standard expected-utility maximization applies without theoretical revision; and (2) the Structural Revisionists, who hold that the divergence is not an artifact but reveals a structural limitation in aggregating infinite outcome spaces via expectation, and that rational decision-making in such spaces requires abandoning or supplementing expectation with a different aggregation rule, such as quantile-based or robustness-based criteria.

The document must contain, in this order: a numbered section of agreed claims; a disagreements table; the cruxes with predictive tests; and a committed concluding judgment. In the agreed-claims section, list precise claims both sides can sign about the formal structure of the Pasadena game—its payoff schedule, the conditions under which the expectation is undefined, the distinction between divergence to +∞ and oscillation of partial sums, and what standard decision theory prescribes when expectation is undefined—together with claims about actual human economic and cognitive constraints and about which features of the case are agreed to be unrealistic. In the disagreements table, phrase each camp's view in wording its own members would accept. Include at least: (a) whether undefined expectation ever arises in decision problems not already vitiated by unrealistic assumptions; (b) whether imposing bounds is an ad hoc rescue or a principled correction; (c) whether a rational agent should suspend judgment, select the safe option, or apply an alternative aggregation rule when expectation fails. The cruxes must include at least one concerning a non-Archimedean extension: if payoffs are permitted to take hyperreal values, does this dissolve the divergence or merely relocate it? For each crux, specify a concrete formal result, argument, or hypothetical empirical finding—such as observed choice patterns in high-stakes bounded analogues, or an integrability proof—that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's explicit prediction of what that evidence will show. End with a definitive, non-hedged judgment—supported by reasoning—on which parts of the dispute are genuinely substantive and which are verbal or terminological; if you identify a verbal component, say exactly which formulations are equivalent and why; if you judge the dispute fully substantive, identify the irreducible cruxes. Write in a formal, polished style suitable for a philosophy-of-science or decision-theory venue. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to obscure named theories; rely only on concepts a well-trained generalist should command. Take definite positions and defend them rather than presenting options for the reader to choose.
