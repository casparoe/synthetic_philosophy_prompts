I was observing a discussion circle that compares folk moral orientations across cultures without relying on academic framing. At the last meeting, two members — Lena and Derek — had a sustained disagreement about whether German folk morality genuinely has the better of it over American folk morality regarding rule-following and order versus individual conscience.

Lena began by arguing that the German folk orientation treats social rules as shared coordination infrastructure: people follow them not primarily from fear of punishment but because unilateral violation collapses the predictability others depend on. She claimed this is morally superior to what she sees as the American tendency to treat rules as provisional suggestions that can be overridden by personal conscience whenever someone feels strongly. She said the American model sounds noble but actually licenses selfishness: anyone can invoke their own conscience to justify cutting in line, ignoring collective obligations, or refusing shared burdens. Derek replied that Lena was romanticizing German order and ignoring its pathologies. He said that when rules become sacred infrastructure, people follow them even when they cause obvious harm — he mentioned the familiar bureaucratic defense of having only followed orders, but applied to everyday life, such as refusing aid to a stranger because it violates local protocol. He argued that American folk morality's emphasis on individual conscience is a necessary corrective that prevents the system from running over people. He added that Lena's infrastructure analogy fails because moral rules lack clear engineering specifications, so treating them as fixed just freezes contingent habits into false necessities.

Lena answered that Derek was conflating extreme bureaucratic failure with ordinary folk practice. She said ordinary Germans do not follow rules blindly; they treat rules as a default that can be overridden in genuine emergencies, but the default matters because it prevents constant social negotiation. She claimed the American model, by making conscience the primary authority, actually increases anxiety: everyone must constantly judge whether this is an exceptional case, which produces paralysis or arbitrary self-exemption. So the German system does not suppress conscience but distributes cognitive load — rules handle routine coordination, conscience handles the exception. Derek countered that this neat distribution hides a power dynamic. He said who declares an exception is never neutral: in practice, those with social capital invoke conscience to bend rules, while the powerless are told to follow them. He argued that the American emphasis on conscience, for all its messiness, at least makes this authority visible and contestable, whereas the German model masks it behind a facade of neutral order. He concluded that if one must choose which folk morality has the better of it, the American orientation is superior not because it is more orderly but because it is more honest about where moral authority actually lies — with individuals who must answer for their choices, not with an abstract system behind which they can hide.

They asked me to referee and I genuinely could not determine who was carrying the argument.
