I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of science and technology course on how the precautionary principle functions in cases of radical uncertainty, and I want to use Jurassic Park as a philosophical case study rather than a generic hubris narrative. I am specifically working with the distinction between a weak precautionary principle—where absence of full scientific certainty should not delay cost-effective preventive measures against serious or irreversible harm—and a strong version that places the burden of proof on proponents of an action and treats plausible catastrophic outcomes as prohibitive regardless of precise probability estimates. I also want to bring in Hans Jonas's argument that responsibility toward future generations requires reasoning from the fear of worst-case scenarios rather than from optimistic forecasting, though I am not asking for an exegesis of his work.

Read the film through this lens. Does John Hammond's project fail because he violates the weak principle—ignoring concrete evidence of chaos in reproductive control and ecological containment—or does it demonstrate that even a strong precautionary stance would have failed, since the risks involved genuinely unquantifiable emergent behavior? Focus on the control-room debate over containment protocols, the tour of the embryo laboratory, the T. rex paddock sequence, and the final evacuation, asking in each case whether the failure is one of risk assessment or of precautionary reasoning itself. How should we interpret Ian Malcolm's chaos-theory interventions: do they provide the epistemic humility that precaution demands, or do they undermine precaution by suggesting that predictive confidence is always illusory? Does the film take a position on whether precaution is a rational decision rule or an emotional posture? Finally, I want to know what the film contributes to this debate that a purely argumentative analysis misses—perhaps about the narrative conditions, visual spectacle, or institutional incentives that make precaution either compelling or dismissible in practice.
