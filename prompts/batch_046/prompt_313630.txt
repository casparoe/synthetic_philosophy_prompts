I have spent thirty years running an experimental structural-biology laboratory, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy of language and aesthetics with a lingering preference for formal clarity and explicit constraint satisfaction. I am now organizing an adversarial collaboration on the problem of translating poetry whose form is tightly coupled—specifically the classic case discussed under the heading Le Ton beau de Marot, in which a short, formally dense poem by the Renaissance poet Clément Marot must be rendered into another language without dissolving its rhyme architecture, syllabic count, and metrical pacing. One camp, which I call the Isomorphists, holds that a translation succeeds only if it maps the original’s coupled formal constraints—meter interacting with rhyme scheme, line length conditioning syntactic break—into an isomorphic structure in the target language, so that the reader experiences the same pattern of formal resistance and release. The opposing camp, the Functionalists, holds that these surface constraints are language-specific vehicles for a deeper functional trajectory—emotional pacing, cognitive surprise, pragmatic address to the reader—and that translation succeeds when it substitutes a different but functionally equivalent architecture, even if every measurable surface feature is altered.
