For the final unit of my seminar on urban moral philosophy, I need a conceptual taxonomy—specifically a decision tree—that sorts normative positions on zoning, exclusion, and the right to the city. Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a roll call of ideologies, though the following five stances must appear as leaves: (i) strong property-rights localism, which treats zoning entitlements as extensions of private ownership and grants existing residents a moral veto over densification; (ii) a right-to-the-city position that regards exclusionary zoning as a denial of collective urban citizenship, treating spatial access as prior to market allocation; (iii) a market-facilitation stance that regards zoning itself as the primary injustice, holding that regulatory barriers to supply should be minimized regardless of local democratic preference; (iv) a procedural-democratic view that accepts local control over land use but rejects homeowner veto in favor of inclusive deliberation that weights the interests of non-resident potential occupants; and (v) what we might call generational zoning justice—the view that current residents hold no special moral claim against future residents who do not yet live in the jurisdiction, so that intergenerational equity trumps preservationist demands.

Begin with a concrete case: a municipality proposes upzoning a transit-adjacent single-family district to permit multi-family construction, and incumbent homeowners object on grounds of neighborhood character, property-value stability, and democratic self-determination. From this starting commitment, construct a branching tree of yes-or-no diagnostic questions. Each node should present a claim or case on which the five positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. For example, ask whether the homeowners' objection is best understood as an exercise of ownership rights, a democratic claim about collective self-governance, or an unjust exclusion of outsiders.

The tree should map the logical combinations of answers, then locate the five named positions within that space. Note any unoccupied combinations—branches that yield no coherent stance—and briefly diagnose whether the gap reflects incoherence, normative unattractiveness, or mere theoretical neglect. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining which shared commitment or ambiguous middle case causes the overlap.

Please write the response in American English, in a formal register appropriate for a philosophy seminar. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the taxonomy should stand on its own conceptual architecture. Ensure that every branch is labeled by the separating question rather than by an -ism, and that the opening case is specific enough to ground the abstract distinctions.
