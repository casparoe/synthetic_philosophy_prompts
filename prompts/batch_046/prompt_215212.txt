I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of archaeology and the epistemology of material culture, and after last week's discussion about how radically material traces underdetermine the social institutions that produced them, I found myself wondering whether students can actually feel that inferential gap rather than just treating it as an abstract thesis. I'm hoping you can help me design what I'm calling an "inverse excavation"—a structured classroom activity for about fourteen advanced undergraduate and graduate students, running roughly 75 minutes in a seminar room with movable tables. The premise should be that participants work in small groups, each receiving identical sets of artifact cards describing a hypothetical settlement layer: ceramic fragment distributions, spatial layout sketches, wear patterns, and deposition contexts. Crucially, each group is also assigned a different minimal "stratigraphic prior"—not a named scholarly theory, but a bare background assumption about how decisions were organized in this society, such as distributed through kin networks versus centralized through ritual authority. Their task is to reconstruct, using only the cards and their assigned prior, the specific governing or deliberative institution that must have existed to generate and maintain these traces, then defend that reconstruction in a brief "publication" round where another group can challenge them using only the shared card set.

Please provide concrete rules, assigned roles within each group (for example, one student must serve as an internal skeptic who blocks any inference not strictly entailed by the card text), a phased timeline with approximate minutes, and a payoff or stake structure that doesn't involve real money but creates genuine social pressure—perhaps a vote in which groups award "interpretive confidence points" and must justify any deduction that crosses from object to institution. After the activity, include a debrief that maps specific moments—such as the moment two groups realize they built incompatible institutions from identical shards—onto the philosophical problem of underdetermination in archaeological inference, and be explicit about which feature of this problem the simulation cannot reproduce, such as the irreversibility of published interpretation or the temporal distance that prevents direct testing, along with how I should supply that limitation verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Write the response in American English, and do not ask participants to cite specific authors or texts during the exercise; the point is to experience the force of the inference gap firsthand.
