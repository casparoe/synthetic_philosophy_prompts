A philosopher of rational choice I have been interviewing holds four positions. On Buridan's ass: the agent should not starve—perfect symmetry permits choosing either bale without further reason. On investment: faced with two strategies of identical risk and return, one should divide capital evenly rather than commit to one arbitrarily. On employment: given two job offers identical in every relevant respect, one should decide promptly rather than delay, though without invoking a random device. On politics: when two candidates share indistinguishable platforms, abstaining is rational, not a failure analogous to starvation.

Consider now a composer resolving a symphony's final passage with one of two chord progressions that are structurally equivalent—same harmonic function, emotional weight, and formal role—yet only one can be inscribed in the score. The composer lacks any aesthetic preference, and the selection is constitutive: whichever progression she chooses will retroactively determine the work's identity, with no possibility of division or delegation.

Predict her verdict: arbitrary choice, randomization, indefinite delay, or the claim that preference is generated by the act of choosing? Identify the competing principles—such as bare will, symmetry preservation, or domain-sensitive urgency—that accommodate her earlier four rulings, state what each predicts here, and defend your best interpretation of her implicit rule. Then specify the single further case that would best distinguish these principles. Finally, indicate what consideration would lead you to revise your prediction and where your analysis is most likely to fail.
