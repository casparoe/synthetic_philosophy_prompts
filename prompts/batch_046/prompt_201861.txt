I'm preparing a draft essay for a graduate seminar in moral psychology that asks us to examine whether the wrong kind of reasons problem takes on a different structure inside institutional roles. The standard formulation—associated with Kant and later developed in discussions of moral motivation—worries that if an agent performs the right action for the wrong motivation, such as self-interest or fear of punishment, then the action lacks full moral worth even if it conforms to duty. I want to argue that this framework needs revision for cases of what I am calling delegated moral agency, where an individual operates within a professional or bureaucratic role—say, a clinical ethics consultant or a public defender—such that her immediate reasons are procedural rather than directly moral. Rather than asking whether her heart is in the right place, I propose that we evaluate whether the institutional design satisfies what I term integrity conditions: structural features that align procedural compliance with moral outcomes without requiring the agent to possess direct moral motivation.

In my draft, I make two moves that I suspect may be problematic. First, I draw on Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons to suggest that institutional roles generate second-order internal reasons—reasons that are genuinely the agent's own, but only in virtue of her acceptance of the role—which then interact with the Kantian distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty. I use this to claim that in delegated contexts, an agent can act with the right kind of reason even when her first-order psychology is purely procedural, because the reason-structure is distributed across the role itself. Second, I argue that when these integrity conditions are met, the wrong kind of reasons objection should be redirected away from the individual agent and toward the institutional framework, though I do not fully specify how to adjudicate conflicts between sound institutional design and malicious compliance by the agent.

Could you review these notes as if you were the seminar instructor evaluating an early draft? I am specifically interested in whether my use of Williams's internal and external reason framework to reinterpret the Kantian duty distinction represents a serious philosophical confusion or a genuinely productive reframing. I also want to know whether the concepts of delegated moral agency and integrity conditions are coherent, or whether they simply collapse into a familiar form of rule-based consequentialism that I have failed to distinguish from my own view. Finally, please point out any subtle mistakes in reasoning—conflations, unsupported steps, or overlooked objections from the literature on moral motivation and role morality—that a hasty reader might miss but that undermine the argument. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need to assess the argument using only the conceptual resources already in play.
