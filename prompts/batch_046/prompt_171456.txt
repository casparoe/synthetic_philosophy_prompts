After a long argument with a friend about Schrödinger's later philosophical essays—particularly his Vedanta-inspired claim that individual consciousnesses are not separate substances but localized perspectives of a single, undivided awareness—I want to work through a conceptual knot that kept surfacing. My friend insisted that if consciousness is genuinely unitary in this way, then the indexical "I-here-now" cannot be merely a cognitive construct but must be an ontological illusion: not just that the ego is built by the brain, but that there is literally nothing in reality corresponding to the boundary between one mind and another. I tried to defend a more nuanced reading, suggesting that even within monism there might be a "focal structure"—a way the single consciousness organizes itself around particular nervous systems without being literally divided into parts. But we kept circling whether this focal structure is only an epistemic veil (we cannot access other perspectives, so they feel absent) or an ontological feature (the one consciousness genuinely differentiates itself into these perspectives). Can you explain the precise conceptual distinction between these two readings—the epistemic-veil reading and the ontological-differentiation reading—of Schrödinger's unity thesis, and map how each handles boundary phenomena? Specifically, consider split-brain cases where one body seems to host two distinct experiential streams, and the converse hypothetical of two people linked by a direct neural bridge where the boundary between "I" and "you" gradually blurs. I am not asking for a biography of Schrödinger or a list of texts; I want a rigorous conceptual analysis of whether there is a consistent middle position—perhaps one that treats focal structure as a "mode of presentation" that is neither pure subjective error nor a full objective division—and what that middle position commits us to regarding personal identity across time and across possible neural configurations.
