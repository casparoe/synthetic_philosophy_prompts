I'm working through a course module on the evolution of cooperation and have been thinking about a pattern I keep encountering in both simulated and conceptual discussions of the iterated prisoner's dilemma, though I lack the vocabulary to place it precisely. In spatial or networked populations, I observe dynamics that do not match the classic narratives of gradual erosion or global equilibrium shifts. Specifically, a strategy sometimes arises that conditions cooperation on a partner's recent behavior—cooperating if the partner has cooperated in the preceding two interactions—but, crucially, applies punishment partner-by-partner rather than globally: after a single defection from a particular neighbor, it defects permanently against that neighbor alone, while continuing to cooperate with others. When such strategies are present, the population does not homogeneously decline into defection. Instead, cooperation fragments into discrete clusters separated by narrow boundary zones of irreversible mutual defection. These clusters can remain stable for extended periods; however, if random drift or invasion causes one cluster to shrink beneath a critical size, it collapses discontinuously rather than gradually, leaving behind a dead zone of sustained defection that resists recolonization by cooperators, since the local density is too low to satisfy the initial two-round cooperation condition. Notably, the strategies that maintain these boundaries are rarely dominant globally; they persist at the edges, sustaining the dead zones without outcompeting cooperators within the clusters.

This configuration seems to sit near several familiar ideas—spatial reciprocity, grim-trigger punishment, and partner-selection mechanisms—but I want to understand whether it corresponds to an established concept with its own literature, or whether it should be treated as a distinct composite phenomenon. The partner-specific nature of the punishment distinguishes it from global grim trigger; the discontinuous, threshold-dependent collapse of clusters distinguishes it from smooth spatial erosion; and the ecological role of the boundary-maintaining strategies—stabilizing dead zones without achieving global dominance—seems different from standard competitive selection. Is there an established term for this exact combination: partner-specific irreversible defection, cluster fragmentation with sharp collapse thresholds, and non-dominant edge strategies that maintain defection zones? If not, how should one conceptually disentangle the partner-level punishment mechanism from the macro-level cluster dynamics, and is there a recognized framework for thinking about discontinuous cooperation collapse driven by local density thresholds?
