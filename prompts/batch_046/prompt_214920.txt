I am drafting a dissertation chapter on mnemonic sovereignty versus archival justice, focusing on what I term "stratigraphic silence"—the idea that certain communal agreements are structurally designed to leave no material trace, so their absence from sedimentary and documentary records is not disconfirmation but a form of structural confirmation. To explore this, I want a dialogue that stages the conflict between collective memory and the historical record without reducing either side to caricature.

Write a dialogue in exactly five exchanges between two equally sharp interlocutors: a proponent of mnemonic pluralism who defends an indigenous river-community's oral tradition of a "silent treaty" governing rotational fishing rights, arguing that the treaty's purpose was precisely to operate outside written and sedimentary archives; and a materialist historian who reads continuous exploitation patterns in sediment cores and colonial ledgers as proof that the treaty is a post-hoc ritual legitimation serving present political needs rather than recording past fact. Each speaker must respond directly to the specific claim made in the previous turn—no talking past one another, no strawmen, and no concession of the central thesis—while the pluralist wields real conceptual resources such as the distinction between embodied practice and documentary presence, and the historian grants only minor methodological points (for instance, that archives have blind spots) without abandoning the claim that this memory functions as ideological repair. Present the dialogue as continuous prose with no introductory framing and no concluding summary outside the five exchanges; do not include citations, bibliographies, or requests for further reading, since the argument should stand solely on its internal logic.
