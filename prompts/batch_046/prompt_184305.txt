I have recently begun, together with my partner, to navigate the expanded options at our fertility clinic, where preimplantation screening now extends well beyond severe monogenic disorders. The laboratory offers polygenic risk profiles for traits that fall entirely within the normal human range—cognitive development trajectories, emotional resilience, metabolic efficiency—without any clinical indication of disease in either of us. The clinic's consulting ethicist frames this as an exercise in reproductive autonomy: we are free to incorporate or ignore the data, and in a liberal framework the state should not restrict such choices so long as access remains equitable. Yet I find myself caught between two convictions that both seem compelling and incompatible. On one side, if I genuinely possess the capacity to influence which potential child comes into existence, and if one genetic profile promises a substantially better life course than another, it strikes me as negligent—perhaps even a failure of parental duty—to disregard that information and leave the outcome to pure chance. On the other side, treating embryo selection as an optimization problem feels as though it transforms begetting into manufacturing, imposing on parents a perfectionist burden that no previous generation faced, and eroding the unconditional acceptance I take to be central to the moral meaning of parenthood. I am not seeking medical guidance or a defense of reproductive rights. Rather, I want you to identify the precise philosophical structure of this tension—particularly the conflict between an obligation to confer the best possible life on a future person and the commitment to treating reproductive technology as a domain of private, non-coercive choice rather than public moral duty—and to tell me whether these two commitments can be reconciled without either reducing parenthood to a form of genetic consumerism or abandoning the intuition that parents owe their children the maximization of welfare.
