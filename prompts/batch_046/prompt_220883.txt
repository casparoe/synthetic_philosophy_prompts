I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher of law who defends a dynamic-systems resolution to paradoxes of legal self-reference. She argues that self-amending rules—whether in constitutional clauses or in Peter Suber's game Nomic—are best modeled not as static logical hierarchies but as trajectories within a stability basin: an emergent region of rule-space in which recursive amendments converge to fixed points rather than producing contradiction. She coins the phrase entrenchment entropy for the resistance of an amendment mechanism to recursive destabilization, claiming that real legal systems persist not because they avoid self-reference but because their entropy remains inside a viable band. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond restating the view and that press precisely where the literature on Ross's paradox and self-amending constitutions applies pressure. At least one question should target her handling of Ross's classic loop—where an amendment rule authorizes changing a second rule that can then change the first—with a follow-up keyed to her expected reply that the loop resolves to an attractor inside the basin. At least one question should address Suber's Nomic directly: anticipate her move that Nomic proves practical consistency despite logical self-reference, then follow up by asking whether the game's dependence on a human referee to resolve ambiguity undermines her claim that coherence is an emergent feature of the rule-system itself rather than an imported interpretation. Include a question applying the framework to institutional design, comparing Article V's relatively high procedural barriers with constitutions that permit rapid amendment, asking whether frequent constitutional replacement signals high entrenchment entropy or impending basin exit; the follow-up should ask what empirical trajectory—gradual drift versus abrupt constitutional replacement—would genuinely falsify the model rather than merely shift the system's location within the basin. Another question should tackle the normative crux: if democratic sovereignty is the power to revise even the rules of revision, and the stability basin constrains which revisions are dynamically reachable, does her view reduce popular sovereignty to navigation within a pre-structured attractor? Anticipate her likely defense that sovereignty operates within, not outside, the basin, and follow up by pressing whether this collapses her position into a form of implicit constitutionalism. End with a direct crux question: what specific institutional feature or historical episode would she accept as counting against the stability-basin account, and why does the long history of frequent Indian constitutional amendments not already serve that disconfirming role?
