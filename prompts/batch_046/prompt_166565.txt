In my current novel, the protagonist is trapped in a two-day loop with induced amnesia: each morning she is awakened and must determine whether a hidden coin landed heads on the first day, knowing that if it landed tails she will be awakened twice—once on each day—while if it landed heads she is awakened only once. The scenario is structurally identical to the Sleeping Beauty problem. I understand the thirder view: upon awakening she ought to assign one-third credence to heads, since her centered possibilities are Monday-heads, Monday-tails, and Tuesday-tails, all subjectively indistinguishable. But I worry this introduces an incoherence into first-person narration itself. If her belief about the coin's fairness shifts from one-half to one-third not through any new observation of the external world but purely through her being located at an unspecified temporal index, then Bayesian conditioning seems to depend on self-location rather than on empirical evidence. Does this mean that any narrator who lacks knowledge of her own position in the narrative timeline is reasoning from an epistemically defective stance? Or is the confusion in treating indexical self-location as something that must be filtered out rather than incorporated into rational belief?
