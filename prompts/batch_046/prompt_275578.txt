Consider a rigorous version of eliminative materialism that does not merely reject folk-psychological beliefs and desires, but also mandates replacing them in both clinical practice and jurisprudence with a tripartite schema: behavioral profiles, neural-state descriptions, and contextual dispositions. What are the most important conceptual implications of this specific replacement strategy? Address in particular: (1) how legal responsibility should be assigned when an agent’s behavioral profile is stable but the corresponding neural-state descriptions remain ambiguous; (2) how psychiatric therapeutic goals should be reconceived if patients can no longer be characterized as desiring recovery; and (3) what epistemic weight, if any, should be granted to first-person mental reports under this framework. In your answer, avoid generic recapitulations of standard eliminativist arguments; concentrate instead on the pressure points that arise specifically from substituting these three categories for the traditional belief-desire framework.
