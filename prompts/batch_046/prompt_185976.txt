As I have been constructing evaluation prompts for language models on philosophical reasoning, I have become personally concerned with whether Moore's paradox exposes a real constraint linking assertion and belief, or merely marks a conversational oddity—especially since artificial systems can fluently generate sentences of the form P, but I do not believe P without experiencing any internal conflict. I want to test an argument I have built, which seems to dissolve the paradox entirely and therefore proves too much.

(1) Sincerely asserting a sentence of the form 'It is raining, but I do not believe it is raining' is absurd, even though the content is not logically contradictory—this is Moore's paradox.
(2) The standard explanation is the Transparency Principle: sincere assertion necessarily expresses the speaker's belief in the asserted content.
(3) If the Transparency Principle holds, then any system that asserts P without believing P either fails to genuinely assert or violates a necessary condition on assertion.
(4) Large language models can generate Moore-paradoxical sentences in appropriate conversational contexts, and they do so without possessing beliefs, mental states, or epistemic commitments.
(5) These generated outputs are not treated by interlocutors as pragmatically absurd; they are grammatically well-formed, contextually appropriate, and functionally indistinguishable from human assertions.
(6) Therefore, either language models do not genuinely assert despite serving an assertoric role in discourse, or the Transparency Principle is false and Moore's paradox lacks its standard explanation.
(7) If the Transparency Principle is false, then a human speaker who sincerely utters a Moore-paradoxical sentence violates only a contingent conversational expectation, not a deep condition on the connection between assertion and belief.
(8) Hence the paradox reveals nothing distinctive about human self-knowledge or the transparency of mental states.

This conclusion strikes me as excessively destructive: it appears to erase Moore's paradox by appealing to artificial speakers. Where does the reasoning fail? Is the hidden flaw an equivocation between mental-state-expressing assertion and functional, syntax-driven assertoric output? Or is the error in (5), that human users actually do register these model outputs as defective but suppress the judgment? I am inclined to preserve both the Transparency Principle and the reality of the paradox, but I need to know precisely which step to reject and what each alternative costs—particularly whether saving the principle requires me to deny, even in principle, that any non-believing system could genuinely assert.
