ok so I retired from physics a few years back and have been poking around philosphy of science, mostly for fun. Last week at a coffee meetup with some old lab freinds and a couple grad students, two of them got into it over whether philosophy actually helps science or is just noise. I was just listening and want an outside take.

The first guy, Mark, said basically Weinberg, Hawking and Feynman were right: philosophers haven't discovered anything about nature since maybe Newton's day. He said science fixes its own conceptual problems — like Bell sorting out locality — and that philosophers just show up later to rename what physicists already did. He added that if philosophy were useful, you'd see philosophers in actual labs, not just writing about them.

The second person, Elena, pushed back. She said that's a bad sampling error — we notice the failed philosophy, not the quiet scaffolding. She pointed out that deciding what counts as an "emergent" property in renormalization-group physics isn't just math; it involves judgments about parts versus wholes that physicists make implicitly but don't justify. She said philosophy makes those explicit, and that hidden metaphysical assumptions actually slowed recognition of topological phases because people assumed symmetry breaking needed a local causal mechanism, which was a philosophical bias about causation, not an empirical result.

Mark said that sounds like branding: when a physicist corrects an assumption it's physics, not philosophy. He said Weinberg's chess analogy holds — philosophers discuss the rules while scientists play. He also said Hawking's point wasn't that philosophers are dumb, just that they don't keep up with the formalism, so they talk past us.

Elena replied that formalism isn't self-interpreting. She said the multiverse debate in cosmology is a perfect example: you have empirically equivalent theories, and picking between them requires criteria that aren't in the data — simplicity, explanatory power, etc. Those are philosophical judgments, and pretending they're "just science" hides the reasoning.

Mark countered that those criteria are pragmatic, not philosophical. Scientists pick the simpler model because it works, not because of some metaphysical commitment. He said if philosophy is just "making implicit reasoning explicit," it's a labeling exercise that doesn't change outcomes.

Elena then said that sometimes labeling changes outcomes: when people realized thier "symmetry breaking needs a mechanism" assumption was metaphysical rather than empirical, they started looking for topological order without local order parameters, which led to actual new experiments. So explicitness wasn't decorative; it removed a hidden constraint.

Then say who's more right overall and what both are missing. Please don't just split the difference; take a side if you think one is stronger.
