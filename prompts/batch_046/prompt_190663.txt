I am a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue set in a provincial university town, and in order to write a scene in which two characters reach an honest philosophical impasse rather than a mere shouting match, I need to understand why the debate over G.E. Moore's proof of an external world has remained structurally frozen for so long. On one side, defenders treat Moore's gesture—presenting his two hands and reasoning from their presence to the existence of objects outside the mind—as a legitimate, if deliberately modest, epistemic achievement: it starts from premises that are more certain than any skeptical premise that might deny them, thereby exposing the skeptic's demand for prior justification as self-undermining. On the other side, critics maintain that the proof is dialectically inert, not because its premises are false, but because it either begs the question by assuming the very reliability of perceptual judgment that the skeptic puts in doubt, or because it confuses a psychological report of conviction with a philosophical justification capable of withstanding all modal error possibilities. Despite decades of commentary, neither camp has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about empirical facts or formal logic, but about competing norms of epistemic entitlement—specifically, whether basic perceptual beliefs carry a default justification that requires no further defense, leaving them epistemically exposed only if one already accepts skeptical framing, or whether they remain dialectically insulated only after all relevant error possibilities have been explicitly neutralized. I also suspect that the sides may be answering different questions under one heading: one about the structure of justification, the other about the limits of philosophical argument against someone who withholds assent. Could you diagnose the disagreement's actual architecture—whether it is verbal, substantive, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about certainty and naturalness—supported by what each side actually says? And given that diagnosis, is there any conceptual distinction, formal result, or reframing of the skeptical challenge that could count as progress, or should we expect the impasse to persist as an irreducible divergence in starting points?
