I serve on the Acquisition and Preservation Committee of my university’s special collections library. Until recently I had never thought about archival philosophy; then I inherited my grandmother’s effects, including a fragmentary 1932 diary kept by a coworker at a millinery cooperative, and I realized how easily objects that never entered official records can vanish. Our committee must now allocate a fixed preservation budget between two competing proposals. Proposal A requests full digitization of a complete, well-catalogued set of regional state labor-inspection records from 1925 to 1940: bureaucratically comprehensive, structurally complete, and indispensable for macro-level legal and economic history. Proposal B requests funding to acquire, stabilize, and digitize a scattered, incomplete set of artifacts from that same cooperative—part of the worker’s diary, a damaged union ledger, a box of fabric scraps, and a few unlabelled photographs—with almost no internal cross-reference and little overlap with the official archive. The philosophical tension is whether historical significance derives primarily from causal connection to state power and structural coverage (the elite/state archive) or whether it can reside in the irreducible particularity of lived experience that official discourse never captured (history from below and microhistory).

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s vote. It should recommend either Proposal A or Proposal B, or an approved hybrid with explicit conditions and a funding split, and it must specify a concrete course of action with a timeline—e.g., immediate stabilization, digitization phases, access restrictions—so the committee can act on it without further philosophical debate. The reasoning should stand on its own; do not include a bibliography or reading list. Crucially, you must treat the strongest consideration for the proposal you reject with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it: if you choose the state records, explain precisely why the cooperative fragments’ incompleteness and risk of preservation failure outweigh their particularity; if you choose the fragments, explain why their lack of structural coverage and representativeness does not render them trivial beside bureaucratic completeness.

In addition, address a subtle methodological problem that has become personally urgent for me: microhistorians often treat a single recovered case as a “representative anecdote,” yet the criteria by which one worker’s diary represents broader labor conditions are rarely made explicit. Apply that concern directly to Proposal B—would preserving these artifacts implicitly commit our archive to an unstated generalization about working-class experience that the fragments themselves do not support? And if so, does that weaken the case for Proposal B, or can the archive accommodate such particularity without claiming representativeness? Write in a formal register appropriate to an institutional memorandum, with a clear bottom line and honest acknowledgment of what the committee will be giving up regardless of the decision.
