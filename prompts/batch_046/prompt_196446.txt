Design a one-paragraph thought experiment in material constitution that isolates whether an artifact's survival conditions depend on form, matter, or enduring substance. The scenario must involve a physically realistic, reversible transformation of a homogeneous metal object—say, a bronze figure—through a thermal phase in which its macroscopic shape is lost and the material temporarily distributes into a different geometric configuration, then returns exactly to the original form. Engineer the case so that: (i) a constitution theorist, who distinguishes the statue from the clay that constitutes it, judges the original artifact survives, but only because its form-capacity is preserved in the material's potential; (ii) an endurance theorist, who takes objects to be three-dimensional and wholly present at each time, also judges survival, but on the ground that one enduring substance persists through the change; and (iii) a mereological essentialist, who identifies the artifact with the exact sum of its parts arranged just so, denies survival and instead posits a numerically distinct intermediate object identical to the rearranged matter. After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning for each verdict, identify the feature that forces the divergence—specifically, whether continuity is tracked by formal potential, substantial endurance, or part-arrangement—and explain how removing that feature collapses the case into a standard statue-and-clay problem. Finally, indicate how a mereological essentialist could attack the case by arguing that the intermediate state retains the original form at a microstructural level.
