As a retired biologist who spent decades in the laboratory and only recently began reading philosophy of science, I have become preoccupied with a conceptual question my former colleagues tend to dismiss with a shrug: Is DNA literally a code in a sense that requires genuine semantic content, or is "genetic code" an irreducible metaphor that ultimately collapses into chemistry once pressed hard enough? I want your actual credence—expressed as a number between 0 and 1—that DNA instantiates a genuine semantic code rather than a causally structured correlation system, with the reasoning decomposed into at least three sub-questions: first, the probability that any biological system can carry semantic information without an interpreter possessing intentionality; second, the probability that the triplet-to-amino-acid mapping displays the kind of arbitrariness, in the Saussurean sense, that distinguishes codes from natural laws; and third, the probability that the concept of "information" in molecular biology is eliminable in favor of pure thermodynamic and kinetic descriptions without explanatory loss. Provide a number and a brief justification for each term, show how they combine into your overall credence, identify which term your final number is most sensitive to, and specify what finding or argument would shift that term most. Before answering, please separate what is settled—such as the mapping rules themselves and the historical origin of the terminology—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of biology and theoretical biologists, and do not present any contested interpretation, particularly regarding teleosemantics or the necessity of arbitrariness, as if it were the consensus view.
