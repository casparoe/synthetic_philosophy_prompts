I've been working through the platonism versus nominalism debate in philosophy of mathematics, partly for a course I'm taking, and I realize my commitments shift with the framing of the case in a way that feels unsystematic. I'd like to know whether there's actually a principle behind my judgments, or whether I'm just inconsistently attached to different metaphors. Here are my verdicts. If physicists invoke a particular infinite cardinal purely to block a paradox in quantum gravity, and that cardinal has no internal mathematical motivation, I think we should treat it as a calculational convenience and withhold ontological commitment—so nominalist. But if set theorists find that every coherent extension of ZFC that resolves the continuum problem requires accepting the existence of a specific kind of large cardinal, I feel that counts as genuine discovery of an abstract object—so platonist. When a computer program autonomously proves a theorem that no human mathematician had anticipated, I want to say the theorem was always true and the program discovered it, yet I'm unsure whether that commits me to the existence of the specific structures the theorem describes, or only to the rules of derivation. In a work of fiction, a mathematician proves a statement that is false within the story's own axiomatic framework; I think there's no corresponding false mathematical object, only an error in reasoning, yet when physicists model electromagnetism using complex numbers, I treat the complex plane as genuinely part of the ontology of the model, not as a mere notational device. I also have an intuition about what I might call orphan structures: formal systems that mathematicians study rigorously for decades despite having no interpretation in physics, computation, or any other applied domain. If the community sustains sustained, fruitful work on such a system, I lean platonist about its objects; if the system is abandoned after a single unpublished preprint, I think it was never more than notation.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on explanatory indispensability, on the social-epistemic stability of a practice, or on some combination—that captures all of these? Please state it precisely, indicate which clause governs each of my cases, predict its verdict on a case I haven't considered—specifically, a mathematical object defined entirely within a virtual-reality simulation that has no causal or representational link to our physical world—and tell me honestly which of my judgments the principle must reject if it is to remain simple rather than ad hoc.
