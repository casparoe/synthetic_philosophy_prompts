You are a professional philosopher of physics with deep familiarity in the interpretive debates around general relativity—especially the hole argument, active versus passive diffeomorphisms, and the substantivalism–relationalism dispute—together with a practiced eye for narrative structure in science-fiction film. Before you begin, please take a few minutes to brainstorm the precise logical steps of Einstein’s hole argument, the threat of physical indeterminism it raises, and the standard responses, so that your analysis remains technically sharp rather than metaphorical.

The question has become personally urgent for me: I recently entered a graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and after rewatching the film Interstellar I have been unable to shake a specific ontological puzzle about the tesseract sequence. In that scene, Cooper moves through a five-dimensional structure in which different regions correspond to specific moments in his daughter Murph’s bedroom, allowing him to push books and manipulate gravity at particular times. This narrative choice seems to presuppose that points within the tesseract carry some intrinsic identity—what philosophers call haecceity—independent of the relational field structure defined by gravity, books, and Murph’s location.

Read the tesseract scene through the hole argument. Does the film implicitly commit to a substantivalist ontology of spacetime points, and if so, does the argument show that such points are physically empty? Or can the scene be reconstructed relationally: Cooper navigates not by the bare identity of points but by recognizing patterns of relation—this shelf, that gravitational pull, this emotional memory—without needing “this very point” to exist? Identify precisely where the philosophical lens illuminates a directorial or narrative choice—perhaps in the visual repetition of identical rooms across different eras—and where the film actively resists the reading, perhaps because dramatic coherence requires Cooper to intend “that specific shelf at that specific Tuesday,” not merely “the shelf standing in these relations.” Be honest about the tension: if diffeomorphism invariance undermines the idea of an intrinsic “here” inside the tesseract, what does it mean for a father to choose one moment over another?

Conclude by saying what the film contributes to the debate that formal argument alone cannot capture. Does dramatizing point-identity through parental love expose an intuitive, perhaps irreducibly human pull toward haecceitist thinking that physics can dismiss but meaning cannot? I want a reading that takes both the physics and the cinema seriously—neither reducing Interstellar to an illustration of the hole argument nor distorting the argument to fit the plot—and that ends by stating what the work adds that pure philosophy misses.
