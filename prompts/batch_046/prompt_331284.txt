In retentionist legal systems, capital defendants typically receive multi-layered appellate and collateral review, DNA testing, and specialized defense resources, yet once an execution is completed there is generally no statutory mechanism for posthumous exoneration, no state reparations to survivors for a later-proven wrongful death, and no institutional obligation to reopen the record—whereas a living exonerated prisoner often receives compensation and a formal innocence declaration. What implicit theory of state justice, the moral status of the dead, and the balance between retributive finality and epistemic humility does that institutional asymmetry presuppose? Can it be reconciled with deterrence rationales that treat execution as a revocable policy instrument, or does it reveal that retribution is doing the silent work? Reconstruct the view, examine whether it is internally consistent with the legal system's simultaneous insistence on near-certainty of guilt, and specify what procedural and reparative obligations would have to be adopted if the system genuinely treated an erroneous execution and an erroneous life sentence as morally equivalent harms.
