I've been TAing for a philosophy-of-language seminar that draws heavily from rationalist forums, where Davidson's radical interpretation is often discussed alongside predictive-processing models of cognition and the problem of interpreting outputs from systems that lack embodied causal histories. For the midterm I asked students to explain Davidson's radical interpretation and the principle of charity, to discuss how interpretation is constitutive of mental-state attribution, and to apply this framework to a non-standard case: whether an artificial system trained solely on text corpora—producing syntactically coherent discourse without direct perceptual-causal interaction with the world—could be charitably interpreted as holding beliefs. I also asked them to address whether Davidson's view permits genuinely incommensurable conceptual schemes.

The exam question was: "Explain Davidson's notion of radical interpretation and the principle of charity. How does interpretation constitute mental states? Apply your analysis to the case of a language-model-like system that generates coherent utterances but lacks embodied causal interaction with a shared environment. Finally, evaluate whether Davidson allows for radical conceptual incommensurability, and justify your answer with reference to the global scope of charity."

One student wrote the following: "Davidson's radical interpretation is the project of interpreting an unknown language from scratch by applying the principle of charity, which directs us to maximize the rationality and truth of the speaker's beliefs. This framework is essentially continuous with Quine's radical translation: both philosophers hold that behavioral evidence radically underdetermines meaning, and that we must select among competing translation manuals that fit the observable data equally well. For Davidson, interpretation is constitutive of mental states—there is no hidden mental cause behind speech—so a creature has beliefs only if an interpreter can apply charity successfully. Because charity requires global maximization of agreement and rationality, it rules out genuinely incommensurable conceptual schemes: if interpretation succeeds at all, interpreter and speaker must share a world and a logic. Applying this to the artificial system, I think charity can be applied locally within each domain of its outputs, so we need not require global coherence across all its discourse; as long as we maximize agreement modularly, we can attribute beliefs. Moreover, Davidson's requirement of shared causal interaction with the environment can be satisfied by virtual data-mediated interaction, so the system's lack of physical embodiment is not a barrier to belief attribution."
