Next month I will present my symposium paper, "Exclusion Rather Than Aggregation: A Relational Solution to the Combination Problem," to the three-judge panel at the State High School Philosophy Symposium. My thesis is that panpsychism does not resolve the hard problem by aggregating micro-phenomenal properties into macro-experience, but rather through a principle I call phenomenal exclusion: macro-conscious subjects emerge when micro-level phenomenal properties are structurally barred from direct combination, leaving only relational traces that constitute unified awareness. The audience consists of Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosophy-of-mind specialist with physicalist leanings who has worked extensively on Chalmers's formulation of the hard problem; Marcus Chen, a fellow student who defends constitutive panpsychism and is deeply familiar with Goff's and Chalmers's arguments about phenomenal combination; and Judge Haruki Tanaka, a retired cognitive scientist who rejects any metaphysical claim lacking empirical testability and who evaluates positions primarily by ontological parsimony. I need a preparation sheet that lists the objections in the order they are most likely to arise during the question period, phrased precisely as each panelist would raise them. For each objection, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, how to reply within a two-minute window, where my reply is weakest, and whether there is a hidden vulnerability I should defend rather than hope they overlook. I am especially concerned about the objection I am hoping nobody asks—that phenomenal exclusion is merely an ad hoc stipulation invented to avoid the aggregation problem rather than an independently motivated principle—so please include that one explicitly, with guidance on how to answer it without sounding defensive.
