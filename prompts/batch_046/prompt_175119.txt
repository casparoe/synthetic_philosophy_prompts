I have adopted a unified structural account of the procreation asymmetry that I take to be more fundamental than the standard person-affecting explanation. The core claim is that moral obligations are necessarily directed at actual subjects: because a merely possible happy person is not an actual subject, there can be no obligation to bring her into existence, whereas bringing a suffering subject into existence creates an actual bearer of harm who is wronged. I apply this consistently across domains—reproductive choice, obligations to future generations under climate change, and the ethics of breeding animals for welfare—arguing that in each case the absence of actual subjects removes any positive duty to create, without generating a corresponding wrong in omission. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position is. Which arguments or intuitions in population ethics or moral metaphysics would genuinely count against it? Must a successful critique demonstrate that obligations can be directed at merely potential beings, or is there a more damaging path—such as showing that the view collapses into a broader anti-natalism, or that it implicitly relies on a contested theory of personal identity to distinguish actual from potential subjects? Please distinguish between defeaters that a committed defender should concede and those that can be dismissed as resting on rival metaphysical commitments. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and address whether extending the asymmetry to climate and animal cases renders the view unfalsifiable, or whether that extension is precisely what gives it explanatory power.
