I'm currently drafting a chapter for my thesis in political philosophy on whether distributive justice permits stronger obligations to compatriots than to distant strangers, and I keep encountering the same analogical argument: a nation is like a family, so just as parents and siblings have special claims on one's care that strangers do not, citizens owe priority to fellow nationals. Proponents use this to defend a bounded nationalism—compatriots come first, but basic human rights set a floor—while critics say it smuggles in emotional bonds and hierarchical dependencies that do not exist between citizens. More recently, I have come across a refined version in discussions of associative obligations: instead of birth-family membership, national belonging is compared to entering a voluntary cooperative scheme, like co-authors of a joint work, where obligations arise from reciprocal contribution and shared institutional rules rather than blood or affection. I want to audit both analogies—the familial one and the cooperative-scheme variant—to see whether either can actually carry the normative weight being placed on it.

Could you lay out the correspondence tables explicitly: for the family analogy, what plays the role of parent, child, sibling, and unconditional duty; for the cooperative-scheme analogy, what corresponds to the contract, contribution, exit option, and shared product? Then identify the precise points of disanalogy—where the source domain imports features (non-voluntary membership, emotional attachment, hierarchical authority, or instrumental coordination) that the target lacks or transforms. I also want to know which conclusion each version actually supports: does the family analogy license priority for compatriots only if we accept that national membership is as non-volitional as kinship, and does the cooperative analogy collapse that priority into a conditional, contractual partiality that cosmopolitans could accept? Finally, if neither analogy survives without carrying the conclusion, is there a more defensible analogy for patriotic partiality—perhaps drawn from shared democratic authorship or collective historical narrative—that avoids both the emotional baggage of family and the voluntarism of contract?
