I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and next month I am giving a seminar presentation at an interdisciplinary workshop on language, cognition, and artificial intelligence. I want to defend the claim that Kripke’s skeptical argument about rule-following—the idea that there may be no fact of the matter distinguishing genuinely following a rule from merely being disposed to behave in accordance with it—creates a conceptual problem for current AI alignment evaluation. Specifically, I argue that frameworks such as RLHF and constitutional AI implicitly presuppose a robust normative distinction between “following an instruction” and “producing outputs that happen to match an instruction,” but if the skeptical challenge holds, that distinction collapses even for artificial systems. In place of normative alignment talk, I propose a purely behavioral construct I call “dispositional coherence across context shifts”: a system exhibits coherence not because it grasps an internal normative constraint, but because its response distribution remains stably correlated with the instruction profile even under perturbations that would break a merely accidental correlation.

The audience will be divided into three subgroups, and I need to be ready for all of them. There will be analytic philosophers of language who are deeply familiar with the Kripkean literature and will treat the skeptical paradox as a live problem rather than a puzzle to be dissolved. There will be AI safety researchers who regard philosophical arguments about rule-following as irrelevant to engineering practice and will ask why this should change how they train or evaluate models. And there will be cognitive scientists who study representation and learning but are skeptical that debates originally framed around human mental states apply at all to statistical language models. For each group, I want to know: what is the objection they will actually raise, in their own vocabulary? Which objections have genuine force, and what should I concede in my opening remarks rather than defend? For each reply, where is its weakest point? I also need to know the one reply that absolutely has to land if the proposal is not to be dismissed as philosophy theater, and I want you to flag the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—perhaps that my “dispositional coherence” metric is itself vulnerable to the same skeptical challenge, or that I am smuggling intentional vocabulary back into a framework that claims to eliminate it. Please order everything by likelihood and potential damage, keep the tone appropriate for a speaker who can discuss gradient updates and loss surfaces fluently but is not professionally trained in metaphysics or philosophy of mind, and avoid asking me for bibliographies or specific textual citations I could not verify without access to a library.
