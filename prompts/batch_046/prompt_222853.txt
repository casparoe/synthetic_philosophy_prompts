Exam question: Consider the distinction between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) in anthropic reasoning, and the problem known as the presumptuous philosopher’s inference about future observer populations. First, explain how SSA and SIA differ in their treatment of predicted observer counts across competing theories. Second, apply both assumptions to a calibrated reference class defined by an epistemic-access threshold—counting only observers capable of formulating anthropic arguments themselves—and determine whether this calibration strengthens, weakens, or leaves unchanged the presumptuous philosopher’s conclusion that humanity will likely expand to produce enormous numbers of future observers. Third, identify one reason—independent of outright rejection of SIA—why one might resist that conclusion.

Student answer: SSA requires that an observer reason as though randomly drawn from all members of her reference class, which means the total number of observers predicted by a cosmological theory does not by itself update the probability of that theory; only the fact that the observer exists within some permissible class does. SIA treats the observer’s existence as direct evidential support: worlds that contain more observers are a priori more probable homes for this particular observer. The presumptuous philosopher—who argues that humanity probably expands to produce on the order of 10^58 future observers—applies SIA, since he takes his present existence to be far more likely in a densely populated future. If we calibrate the reference class by an epistemic-access threshold, counting only beings capable of formulating anthropic reasoning, SSA becomes more restrictive: it directs us to sample only from that cognitive elite, which amplifies the philosopher’s conclusion because future technologically advanced populations will dominate the class. Nevertheless, we can resist the inference without rejecting SIA altogether, by observing that Carter’s Doomsday argument uses the very same sampling logic—SSA applied to birth rank—to reach pessimistic predictions about small populations. Thus the presumptuous philosopher is essentially running Carter’s SSA-based reasoning backward, and the contradiction between the two shows that anthropic reasoning is unreliable.
