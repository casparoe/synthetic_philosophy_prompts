I'm trying to clarify my own judgments about doing versus allowing harm, specifically killing versus letting die, and I've noticed that physical action and inaction don't track my verdicts. Here are six cases.

A. A physician, at a competent patient's request, withdraws a ventilator and the patient dies. Verdict: allowing death, not killing, despite the physical act of switching the machine off.

B. A physician refuses, on personal religious grounds, to prescribe an antibiotic that would save a consenting patient's life; the patient dies. Verdict: allowing death.

C. A hospital that has long monopolized the supply of a maintenance drug terminates its contract with the sole manufacturer for budgetary reasons; within weeks the chronic patients who depend on it die. Verdict: closer to killing than to allowing, even though no one administers poison.

D. A healthy adult walks past a shallow pond in which a child is drowning and does nothing, though rescue would only wet her clothes. Verdict: allowing death, though the moral gravity feels almost as severe as killing.

E. An airline pilot who survives a crash neglects to pull an emergency evacuation slide that is within arm's reach; one hundred passengers who could have escaped die. Verdict: killing, not allowing, despite being an omission.

F. A legislator votes to repeal universal coverage for a specific life-extending therapy, knowing thousands of current patients will lose access and die. Verdict: allowing death, despite the active vote and the predictable, large-scale deaths.

The tension I can't resolve is between C and F: in both an institution removes a sustaining condition it previously maintained, yet C strikes me as killing-like while F strikes me as allowing-like. Meanwhile D and E are both omissions with opposite verdicts, and A involves a direct physical intervention but is allowing.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving causal maintenance, dependency relations, or operational duty—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of C, E, or F should I overturn for the sake of simplicity? And what does the best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: a factory owner stops producing an antidote that only her factory can synthesize, knowing existing stockpiles will run out in a month and poison victims will then die?
