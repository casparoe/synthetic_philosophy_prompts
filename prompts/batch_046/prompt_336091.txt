I just finished a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about whether the necessary a posteriori can arise for terms that seem to have “missed” their target entirely. We were working through standard examples in our heads—water and H₂O, Hesperus and Phosphorus—but she kept pushing a hypothetical case that I think exposes a hidden tension in the usual Kripke-style account, and we ended up talking past each other on exactly where the baptismal event succeeds or fails. I want an adjudication that takes both of our positions seriously rather than simply declaring one of us “the rigid-designator view.”

Here is what actually happened, move by move. (1) I said that rigid designation presupposes a successful baptism: if the descriptions used to introduce a term are completely unsatisfied—there is no object or kind that plays the relevant causal role—then there is no baptismal event, hence no rigidity, and certainly no necessary a posteriori identity waiting to be discovered later. The term is just a failed definite description, not a name. (2) She replied that this ignores hybrid causal-descriptive reference-fixing: we can stipulate that a term denotes whatever actually plays the causal role tracked by our descriptions, even if those descriptions are mostly false. She invented a hypothetical case: in 1923 chemists postulate “Heptium,” defined descriptively as the element producing spectral line L and expected to fill gap 137; decades later it turns out the lines come from an isotope of neodymium. On her view, “Heptium is that neodymium isotope” is then necessary a posteriori, discovered empirically—parallel to water/H₂O—because the term was always rigidly anchored to whatever caused the anomaly. (3) I countered that there is a critical asymmetry: with water, actual samples exist and causally ground our usage, so the descriptions are approximately satisfied; with Heptium, nothing plays the role—there are no samples, no causal chain linking our tokenings to a real kind—so we are not fixing reference, we are merely hoping. Even if the neodymium isotope turns out to be the cause, that does not retroactively make “Heptium” rigid; it makes it a lucky misdescription, not a revealed identity. (4) She said I am smuggling in an existence condition that the standard account never requires: a rigid designator can be empty in some worlds, perhaps even actually, and still be rigid. What is required is only that in worlds where it denotes, it denotes the same thing. The empirical discovery reveals the referent of a term that was always rigid, just as Hesperus/Phosphorus reveals Venus’s identity without altering Venus. The only difference is that in the Heptium case we also discover our causal chain was tracking the neodymium isotope all along—an empirical finding about reference-fixing, not a failure of rigidity.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and—crucially—identify what both of us are missing. In particular, is there a principled way to distinguish a “lucky misdescription” from a “revealed rigid reference” without already assuming the answer? And does the necessary a posteriori require that the referent actually exists in the world of evaluation, or only that the identity holds in worlds where both terms denote? Work through the logic of our specific disagreement rather than offering a generic summary.
