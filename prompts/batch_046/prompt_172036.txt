Consider the following examination prompt and student response from an advanced seminar in moral psychology. You are acting as the instructor or exam grader; do not indicate your own agreement or disagreement with the student’s conclusions.

Question: Contemporary virtue ethics faces a persistent challenge from situationist social psychology, which reports low cross-situational consistency in behavior—correlation coefficients often near 0.30—suggesting that global character traits have weak predictive power. One proposed reconciliation is to reconceive virtues not as globally consistent dispositions but as “threshold-activated” tendencies: they manifest reliably only when situational pressure remains within a specific band, and they may be “domain-fragmented,” operating with different thresholds and internal structures across physical, social, and intellectual contexts. Assess whether this reconciliation saves virtue ethics or collapses it into a sophisticated situationism. Your answer should address three things: (a) whether the empirical finding of low cross-situational correlation undermines global trait attribution; (b) whether threshold-activation and domain-fragmentation preserve a meaningful normative role for character in practical reasoning; and (c) whether the unity of virtue—traditionally understood as a single practical wisdom integrating all virtues—can survive such fragmentation.

Student answer:

The situationist critique, developed by social psychologists and later adopted by philosophers of character, demonstrates that people behave inconsistently across situations. With correlations near 0.30, global traits appear to explain only about nine percent of variance, which seems devastating for virtue ethics.

The threshold-activation model responds by treating a virtue like courage not as a stable global property but as a disposition that activates only when perceived risk falls between a lower and an upper bound. Outside that band—whether in trivial or in extreme circumstances—the disposition remains dormant. Domain-fragmentation adds that physical courage, moral courage, and intellectual courage each possess independent thresholds and need not co-occur. This explains observed inconsistencies: an agent can face bodily danger bravely yet fail in social confrontation, because the domains are genuinely separate.

This model preserves virtue ethics in a modified form. We can still assess agents for their domain-specific dispositions, and practical reasoning remains possible because an agent can identify which domain is active and apply the relevant local virtue. However, there is an important cost. Traditional virtue ethics requires that practical wisdom unify all virtues into one coherent character. Once domain-fragmentation is accepted, that unity dissolves: there is no reason to expect that someone with well-developed physical courage will also possess moral courage. In fact, because agents rarely possess precise knowledge of the situational intensity they face, they cannot determine in advance whether they lie within their own activation threshold. Consequently, practical reasoning under this model cannot genuinely guide action, since one never knows whether one’s virtue is currently available to be exercised. Thus the reconciliation ultimately concedes the situationist point: behavior is still driven by local conditions and domain-specific mechanisms rather than by an integrated agent. The vocabulary of virtue is retained, but the explanatory and normative work is done entirely by situation and fragmentation.
