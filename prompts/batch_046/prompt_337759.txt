In a philosophy discussion group I attend, two members—Leila and Derek—got into a sustained disagreement about Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic and whether a modified version of the ring of Gyges makes the case for moral skepticism stronger or weaker. I report their exchange below as a neutral bystander; I do not know which side is correct.

Leila began by arguing that the standard ring-of-Gyges scenario is not the strongest form of the challenge, because physical invisibility still leaves traces—footprints, altered locks, witnesses who notice absence. She proposed a stronger hypothetical: a ring that does not hide the wearer physically, but instead erases every trace of an action from the minds and records of everyone except the agent. She called this mnemonic invisibility. With this version, she said, the common reply that one might still be caught disappears entirely, and the social-contract defense of morality collapses more cleanly. She then introduced a distinction between transactional harm, which is brief and experienced only in the moment, and narrative harm, which persists in a victim's life story. Under mnemonic invisibility, she argued, only transactional harm remains; if the wrong is quick, the victim's subsequent life is untouched. Therefore, the defender of intrinsic moral value must explain why a fleeting, unremembered injury should permanently constrain the agent's character.

Derek replied that Leila had changed the subject rather than strengthened the challenge. The original point, he said, is to separate justice from reputation and punishment, not to eliminate every epistemic trace. Even with mnemonic erasure, he argued, reasons to be just remain: internal harmony, the agent's own knowledge of the deed, and the fact that the victim still suffered in the moment regardless of later memory. He accused Leila of implicitly assuming that morality is justified only by lasting consequences—the very view Glaucon challenges rather than assumes.

Leila countered that Derek was begging the question. If the moralist appeals to internal harmony, she asked, why should a rational agent care about harmony unless it produces some good—peace of mind, self-respect, or coherence? Once those goods are available through self-deception or compartmentalization, the appeal looks like a disguised consequentialist argument. She pressed the transactional and narrative harm distinction further: if the victim forgets instantly and lives a fully flourishing life thereafter, then the only damage is a brief unpleasant state. She asked Derek to explain, without circular appeal to being the kind of person who does not do such things, why that brief state should limit the agent's choices when no other person is affected.

Derek responded that Leila's distinction was artificial. Harm, he said, is not defined by storage in memory; a wrong that is immediately forgotten still violates the victim's autonomy in the moment it occurs. More importantly, he argued that her mnemonic-ring version actually undermines her own position. The social-contract view holds that people are just because they fear detection and punishment. By making detection literally impossible, Leila removes the mechanism that view requires. But this does not help the skeptic; it shows that if morality were only fear of detection, it would indeed be rational to do wrong under the ring. The moralist can reply that this proves morality was never about detection in the first place. So, Derek concluded, Leila had not strengthened Glaucon's challenge; she had constructed a scenario so extreme that it exposes the social-contract view as a strawman, leaving the intrinsic-value position intact.

Leila's final move was to say that Derek had misidentified her target. She was not defending the social-contract view, she said, but testing whether any non-consequentialist reply can survive once all external and memorial sanctions are removed. If the only remaining reason is that the act is wrong in itself, she argued, then the moralist owes an account of what makes an unremembered, undetected wrong wrong—without appealing to consequences, reputation, or the agent's future self-image. Until that account is given, she said, the mnemonic ring shows that intrinsic-value morality rests on an unexplained intuition.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Leila's four positions and Derek's four responses, rate the move as clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall about what the ring scenario demonstrates, and identify at least one significant point that both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether mnemonic invisibility genuinely strengthens Glaucon's challenge or instead transforms it into a different argument altogether.
