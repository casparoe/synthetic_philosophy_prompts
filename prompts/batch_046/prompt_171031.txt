I have been working through the problem of dirty hands in politics, and I find myself stuck on a conceptual distinction that the standard literature tends to collapse. Typically, dirty hands are described as cases in which a political actor does what is morally wrong in order to achieve a necessary political good; the difficulty is then whether the actor retains some form of moral residue—guilt, taint, or an obligation to acknowledge the wrong—despite being all-things-considered justified. But there seems to be a competing structural interpretation: the dirt may belong not to the individual agent but to the political office itself, which carries permissions or obligations to transgress ordinary moral limits that are unavailable to private persons. On this view, the conceptual puzzle is not psychological but institutional—how can a social role generate moral permissions that are not reducible to simple consequentialist override?

To test these interpretations against each other, consider a form of what might be called fragmented authorization. Imagine a wrong act—say, the authorization of a harmful but strategically necessary operation—is decomposed across a bureaucratic chain: one official designs the policy framework, another approves the specific execution, a third manages the concealment of its costs, and no single individual performs or authorizes the complete moral wrong in one act. The collective outcome is identical to a classic dirty-hands scenario, yet there is no unitary agent who clearly bears the moral cost. Is this fragmentation a dissolution of the dirty-hands problem, because no one person's hands are dirty? Or is it an intensification, because the wrong becomes institutionalized in a manner that makes acknowledgment and moral accounting structurally impossible?

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake here. In particular, clarify whether the notion of moral residue requires an identifiable, unitary bearer, or whether it can be distributed across a collective without losing its normative force. Does the structural interpretation of dirty hands survive this fragmentation, and what does the answer imply for the familiar claim that a leader with dirty hands owes the public some form of confession or moral penalty? I am especially interested in whether the problem is ultimately about individual moral psychology or about the moral ontology of collective political action, and where the boundary between the two should be drawn.
