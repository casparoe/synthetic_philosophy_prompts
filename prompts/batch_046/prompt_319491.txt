I work as a distributed-systems engineer, and my day-to-day conceptual vocabulary is shaped by state-machine replication, leader election, network partitions, split-brain failures, eventual consistency, health-check probes, and the distinction between a client session and the nodes that serve it. I have no formal background in philosophy of mind. I want you to take Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment—in which a thinker's brain is extracted and sustained in a vat of nutrients, linked to an original body through computer-mediated signals, and then duplicated so that a second brain in a second vat drives a second body with identical psychological continuity, leaving both bodies with an equal claim to being the original person—and translate it entirely into my engineering framework. Be precise and honest: map the extracted brain, the computer mediator, the original and duplicate bodies, and the competing first-person claims onto specific distributed-system concepts such as primary nodes, replicas, consensus protocols, partition healing, and session state, without smuggling philosophical assumptions into the technical terms.

Once the translation is complete, provide a residue report. For each central element of Dennett's puzzle—the continuity of consciousness across extraction, the problem of two simultaneous claimants with identical histories, the first-person sense that one is located here rather than there, and the intuition that perfect duplication undermines uniqueness—indicate whether it maps cleanly onto a replication or consistency scenario, whether it can only be expressed by distorting an engineering concept beyond recognition, or whether it has no counterpart at all in distributed-systems practice. Then address the interpretive question: does whatever fails to translate reveal a real feature of subjectivity that my vocabulary cannot capture, or does it show that Dennett's framing imports assumptions—perhaps about an indivisible owner of experience—that dissolve once identity is treated as a property of consistently maintained state rather than of a singular substrate? I am especially interested in whether treating the puzzle as a split-brain consistency problem misses something philosophically essential, or whether the philosophical puzzle is largely an artifact of treating first-person location as a primitive that rigorous engineering design has no reason to preserve.
