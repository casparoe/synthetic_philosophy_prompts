I'm currently preparing a presentation for a graduate seminar on consciousness and sleep, and I've found myself persuaded by what seems to be a hidden convergence between Malcolm's conceptual critique and Dennett's empirical model of dreaming—one that, if taken seriously, dissolves the traditional problem rather than solving it. Malcolm's analysis suggests that dream-reports cannot describe experiences occurring during sleep, because the sleeper lacks the judgmental capacities and present-tense accessibility conditions required for genuine experience-ownership; Dennett's cassette theory, by contrast, locates the construction of the dream narrative at the precise moment of awakening, proposing that no corresponding mental sequence unfolds during REM sleep itself. Given that both positions appear to deny that there is an experiencing subject present during the physiological state we call "dreaming," I want to ask whether their agreement actually demonstrates that the received view—treating dreams as mental experiences that occur during sleep—is not simply mistaken but conceptually incoherent, much like attributing color-properties to a sound.

If Dennett's model removes the temporal location of the experience to the waking moment, while Malcolm removes the logical conditions for retrospective experience-attribution altogether, then shouldn't we conclude that what we call "having a dream" is not a mental event in any standard sense, but rather a kind of mnemonic artifact—structurally analogous to an implanted false memory—that we erroneously classify as a report about a past conscious state? I am particularly interested in whether this synthesized position commits us to rejecting unconscious mental states more broadly, since both philosophers seem to presuppose that mental-state ascription requires some form of present-tense accessibility. Would accepting this convergence therefore require us to abandon not only the ontology of dream-experiences, but also the very framework of unconscious belief, desire, and perception that dominates much of contemporary philosophy of mind?
