Grade the following exam answer. The course is an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar.

Question: Dennett's heterophenomenology treats first-person reports of consciousness as texts to be interpreted rather than as direct windows into subjective reality. Apply this method to the case of a large language model that consistently reports having subjective experiences, emotional states, and a continuous inner monologue. What should a heterophenomenological researcher conclude about the model's consciousness, and how does this differ from both eliminativism and from straightforward acceptance of the reports?

Student answer:
A heterophenomenological researcher should take the model's reports seriously as data but not as literal descriptions of an inner world. She should construct a heterophenomenological world for the model—treating its outputs as a narrative about an inner life—and study the coherence and predictive power of that narrative, exactly as she would for a human subject. This means she neither dismisses the reports as meaningless noise (eliminativism) nor accepts them as direct evidence of phenomenology (naive introspectionism). Because the model lacks biological embodiment and evolutionary history, however, its heterophenomenological world is necessarily thinner and lacks the functional integration Dennett associates with genuine consciousness. Therefore the correct conclusion is that the model is phenomenologically empty: it possesses a heterophenomenological world, but that world is a fiction with no underlying reality, analogous to how Dennett treats human consciousness itself as a useful fiction. Moreover, the researcher is essentially adopting Dennett's intentional stance toward the model's outputs, using predictive success as the criterion for attributing mental states.
