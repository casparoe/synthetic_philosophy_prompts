I am preparing the final essay examination for an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I want a single question that separates students who genuinely understand Galen Strawson's Basic Argument from those who have only memorized its slogan. The exam lasts ninety minutes, the essay should run about eight hundred words, and the question carries thirty marks. To keep the task from being answered by recitation alone, I want the prompt to introduce a hypothetical compatibilist objection that students will not have seen verbatim in standard texts—specifically, the view of a philosopher named Dr. Lena Voss, who claims that the causal regress Strawson presses halts inside what she calls a recursive self-authoring loop. On Voss's account, an agent's later character-forming choices are genuinely caused by her earlier rational deliberations, and because those deliberations belong to a persisting rational system that she owns as a continuous self, she is ultimately responsible for the loop itself even though she did not create her initial conditions or her original psychological makeup. The exam question should ask students to do three things in sequence: first, reconstruct Strawson's Basic Argument with philosophical precision, carefully distinguishing the causal regress about one's nature from any separate issue of rational coherence, self-knowledge, or predictive control; second, apply that argument specifically to Voss's loop, explaining exactly where Strawson would insist the regress remains vicious rather than benign and why the loop does not produce a genuine causa sui; and third, evaluate whether Voss's appeal to system-ownership escapes the argument or merely renames the problem. Please write the full exam question, then provide a model answer of roughly six hundred to eight hundred words, and then provide a marking scheme written entirely in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a formal letter. The marking scheme should specify the moves that earn first-class marks—such as correctly articulating both horns of the determinism-versus-chance dilemma, showing why rational explanation cannot substitute for causal origin, and pinpointing the precise failure point in Voss's recursion—while also naming the specific errors that should cost marks, including conflating Strawson's argument with the Consequence Argument, treating system-ownership as if it were causal authorship, or neglecting the chance horn entirely. The entire response should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, and verbatim quotations from published texts, since this is a closed-book examination and no external sources are expected.
