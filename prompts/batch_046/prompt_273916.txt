ok so i'm trying to salvage a Devlin-style argument for a seminar and i think i've talked myself into a corner. here's the loop:

(1) Hart's harm principle says law shouldn't ban private conduct just because it's immoral, only when it harms others.
(2) Devlin replies that some immoral conduct damages society's "moral ecology" — the shared normative framework that makes coopertion possible — even without direct victims.
(3) Let's refine that: law can protect "keystone practices" that are actually load-bearing for social trust, not just whatever the majority likes.
(4) Now take "symbolic inversion" — public rituals where people perform the opposite of a keystone norm (say, ceremonial promise-breaking) without actually harming anyone.
(5) This erodes the symbolic environment sustaining the real practice.
(6) So the state can ban symbolic inversion to preserve the ecology.
(7) But then satire, blasphemy, and avant-garde art that invert norms symbolically are also banishable.
(8) That proves too much: we've licensed suppressing harmless expression.
(9) So either reject (3) — keystone practices can't be protected this way — or reject (6) — symbolic erosion never justifies prohibition.
(10) But giving up (6) seems to gut Devlin's whole reply to Hart, since without symbolic damage there's no harm to prevent.

where's the exit? i really want to keep (3) and (5) if i can. is there a hidden slide between (5) and (6) about causation, or is the real problem that (3) was already too broad? what does each rejection cost?
