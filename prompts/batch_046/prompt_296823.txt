I have been reflecting on the ethics of biographical dramatization—particularly the licence taken in biopics and historical fiction where a deceased figure’s documented motivations or moral choices are reshaped for narrative effect. In a recent discussion with colleagues, I proposed that there might be a direct, non-instrumental duty owed to the dead themselves: specifically, an obligation not to narratively bury a person under a misrepresented moral profile that contradicts well-attested historical records, especially when that misrepresentation renders them more vicious or foolish than the evidence suggests. One interlocutor objected that, because the dead lack consciousness and interests, any wrong here must be purely derivative—either harm to living relatives, epistemic corruption of the audience, or an offence against general truthfulness—rather than a direct violation of the deceased’s dignity. I want to examine whether this objection succeeds.

Please provide your credence, expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1, that such a direct, dignity-based obligation exists and genuinely constrains dramatists, independent of any harm to the living. Decompose your reasoning into the following terms: first, the probability that moral status or dignity can extend posthumously in a way that generates direct obligations (rather than serving as a mere rhetorical figure); second, the probability that misrepresenting a documented moral character in fiction constitutes harm to that posthumous status even in the absence of experience or current interests; third, the probability that artistic licence is not automatically overriding when the alteration concerns core moral identity rather than incidental biographical detail; and fourth, any further factor you deem relevant—perhaps relating to cross-cultural practices of ancestor veneration or to the distinction between public and private figures—with its own rough probability and one-line justification. Combine these terms into an overall figure, indicate which component you are least confident about, and specify what kind of philosophical argument or comparative cultural evidence would most substantially revise that weakest term.
