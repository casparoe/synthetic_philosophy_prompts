I serve on a review board that audits mandatory "explanation artifacts" — the visual summaries that banks, agencies, and healthcare providers must display to non-expert users when an automated system denies a benefit, assigns a risk tier, or allocates a scarce resource. I want to adapt Otto Neurath's picture-language principles — standardized icons that replace metaphoric imagery, quantitative differences shown by repetition rather than scaling or perspective, flat non-hierarchical layout, color restricted to categorical distinction, and the elimination of decorative or persuasive embellishment — into a reusable compliance procedure my reviewers can run in about twenty minutes without philosophical training. Please construct that procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the specific questions to ask about an artifact, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure terminates in a clear reject, where it terminates in a clear pass, and where it must defer to human judgment because the issue is normative rather than formal. The procedure should handle hard cases: scaled graphics used for accessibility, minimal perspective to indicate temporal sequence, color gradients representing intensity, and narrative framing devices that embed the chart in a story. Then apply it to three artifacts: (a) a loan-denial display that uses progressively smaller human icons arranged along a descending perspective corridor to represent falling approval rates; (b) a vaccination-priority chart that repeats identical patient icons in flat rows with a single red outline marking the cutoff, but embeds the chart inside a pyramid that implicitly ranks diseases by social value; and (c) an artifact that uses a slight size gradient to represent statistical confidence intervals, which aids visually impaired readers but violates the repetition rule — a case where I expect the procedure to reject something I personally think should be permitted. Be explicit about which distinctions from Neurath's original program must be dropped for usability, where the procedure will predictably misfire, and what it cannot adjudicate — for instance, whether the underlying algorithm is fair. Finally, invite pushback plainly in your own voice: if my project is confused — say, if Neurath's anti-persuasive, protocol-style visual language is fundamentally at odds with the task of justifying a contested automated decision to an affected party — then say so and do not construct a cosmetic checklist that hides the tension.
