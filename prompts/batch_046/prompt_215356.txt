Longtime reader of rationalist blogs here. In discussions of forecasting ethics and belief formation, I often encounter a compressed version of the evidentialist stance—essentially, that to believe anything upon insufficient evidence is wrong, always, everywhere, and for anyone. The slogan gets used to dismiss arguments that certain working hypotheses, for example provisional confidence in an unverified architectural intuition for a long-horizon research program or a tentative credence assigned in a prediction market despite sparse data, might be permissible despite lacking full proof. I want to separate the precise claims hiding inside that one sentence. Please lay out the distinct readings as a menu: which are tautologies about rational consistency, which are substantive evidentialist theses, which are false because they ignore James-style cases where belief causally enables its own verification or serves vital practical interests, and which remain defensible. In particular, tell me which precise reading is being invoked when someone deploys the slogan to end an argument about whether it is ethically permissible to adopt a working acceptance of a low-probability, high-stakes hypothesis—treating a novel conceptual framework as a provisional guide to action—before the evidence is fully in hand. Finally, address whether any defensible version can accommodate doxastic partiality, the idea that practical identity or long-term commitment may license stronger belief in certain domains, or the distinction between propositional belief and decision-theoretic acceptance, or whether those distinctions inevitably collapse back into a strict evidentialist position.
