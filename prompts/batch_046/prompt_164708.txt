I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for doctoral students in democratic theory who already understand representation and aggregation but have never encountered the boundary problem in a non-territorial setting. Rather than using standard state-citizenship examples, I want the session to turn on a single constructed case: a transnational climate-adaptation cooperative that distributes relocation funds through quadratic voting, where the potential demos includes present coastal residents, trustee-represented future generations facing sea-level rise, and non-voting algorithmic risk-modeling agents whose predictions are binding on allocations. Plan the explanatory architecture, not the lecture itself. First, identify the one conceptual distinction that must be established before the boundary problem can even appear as a problem rather than an administrative membership list—why does it depend on that distinction? Second, specify exactly what the carrying case must include and exclude relative to a standard territorial demos so that the boundary question becomes genuinely open; in particular, address why the presence of the forecasting agents and the trustee structure should not be treated as mere complications but as stress-tests for any boundary principle. Third, map the misconceptions you expect in the order students will likely form them—such as conflating boundary-definition with stakeholder-management, treating future-generation trustees as straightforward proxies rather than as a category that breaks the presentist assumption of democratic equality, or assuming that binding algorithmic input resolves rather than intensifies the boundary question—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question to ask at the end that distinguishes whether a student understands the boundary as a constitutive feature of democratic authority or merely as a procedural filter. Finally, state explicitly which canonical formulations—e.g., the all-affected-interests principle or the all-subjected principle—you should withhold until after this diagnostic, and why introducing them earlier would allow students to substitute citation for reasoning.
