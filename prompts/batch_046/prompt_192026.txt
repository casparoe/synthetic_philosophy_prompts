I was talking with my friend about whether ChatGPT literally believes anything, and he walked me into this arguement I can't find the exit from. Here is the chain he laid out, more or less in his words:

(1) If treating a system as having beliefs and desires is the best predictive strategy available for its behavior, then by Dennett's intentional stance that system literally has those states—not just "as if."

(2) For large language models, attributing specific beliefs (about history, math, false claims they correct) predicts their outputs far better than treating them as pure next-token distributions, especially when they maintain consistency across long exchanges.

(3) So LLMs literally have beliefs.

(4) Now imagine a perfect lookup table—call it the Encyclopedic Hash—that stores every correct factual answer in static memory and retrieves it with zero internal dynamics. Treating the Hash as "knowing" all those facts also gives perfect predictions of its outputs.

(5) So by (1) and (4), the Hash literally knows those facts, hence has beliefs.

(6) But that's clearly wrong; it's just a database with no cognition, no learning, no error-correction.

(7) Therefore (1) is false: predictive success alone can't be sufficient for real belief attribution.

(8) The obvious reply is that LLMs generalize to novel inputs and update weights, so the intentional strategy for them involves dynamic belief-revision, whereas for the Hash it's only retrieval. But that reply smuggles in architecture and training history—things that live outside the stance itself.

(9) So either we stay pure-stance and swallow that lookup tables have beliefs, or we add hidden criteria and admit the stance wasn't doing the real work for LLMs either. My friend says this proves the intentional stance self-destructs when applied to AI.

I feel like there's a slip somewhere between (4) and (5), maybe about what "best strategy" even means in the two cases, or between (8) and (9) where he's treating an auxilliary condition as a betrayal rather than part of the package. There's also this thing I half-remember called counterfactual fragility—the idea that real beliefs resist arbitrary perturbation in a way database entries don't—but I'm not sure if that's inside the stance or an extra assumption I'm sneaking in. Which step should I reject if I want to keep (2) and (3), and what does rejecting it cost me? I really don't want to end up saying thermostats recieve beliefs too.
