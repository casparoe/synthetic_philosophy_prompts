I'm currently taking an upper-division course that combines Buddhist metaphysics with contemporary philosophy of personal identity, and I'm trying to work out whether anatta can be reconciled with any coherent account of what makes someone the same person over time. Rather than asking for a literature survey, I want to see whether my own intuitive verdicts on concrete cases point to a single principle, or whether consistency forces me to abandon some of them.

Here are my judgments, reported as honestly as I can. First, in the standard teletransportation case—my brain is scanned, my original body destroyed, and a psychologically continuous replica built elsewhere—I think the replica is me; psychological continuity seems sufficient for identity. Second, in a gradual neural-replacement scenario, every neuron is swapped for a functionally identical synthetic analog, all memories and cognitive patterns are preserved, but I consistently and sincerely report a total absence of any felt ownership or mineness regarding my experiences—there is no ego-grasping whatsoever. Despite the perfect functional and behavioral match, I think the resulting being is not me. Third, regarding a long-term meditation practitioner who genuinely realizes anatta and reports never having found a self in experience, I still treat him as the same conventional person who took monastic vows years ago, and I hold him responsible for past actions; so I accept conventional identity and moral accountability even in the absence of metaphysical selfhood or subjective ownership. Fourth, in a split-brain case where both hemispheres are separated into distinct bodies, each retaining full psychological continuity with the pre-surgery individual, I think the original person is gone and two new conventional persons have emerged.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a transmission scenario inspired by Yogacara accounts: only the store-consciousness (alayavijnana), carrying karmic seeds (bijas) and volitional formations (samskaras), is transmitted into a new embryo, with no autobiographical memory, no personality continuity, and no bodily link preserved. Sometimes I think this is merely causal influence, not personal identity; other times I think it is enough for the same mental stream (santana) to continue, though not necessarily for the same person in any ordinary sense.

Is there one principle—perhaps one that distinguishes conventional (samvriti) from ultimate (paramartha) identity, or one that assigns different weights to bodily continuity, psychological continuity, and the presence or absence of ego-grasping—that delivers all of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, with the specific case that motivates each clause. Give its verdict on the transmission case I described. Also tell me what it predicts for a further case I have not listed: suppose the teletransported replica is instantiated in a body that already contains a nascent, weak stream of consciousness (for instance, a developing fetus's alayavijnana), so that two streams might seem to inhabit one form. Finally, be explicit about which of my four listed verdicts must be overturned if the principle is to remain parsimonious, and defend that choice.
