You sit on a philosophical arbitration panel that applies the idea of coherent extrapolated volition—roughly, the preferences an agent would hold after adequate reflection, full information, and internal coherence—to decide which of an agent’s current preferences count as genuinely authoritative. Your panel’s track record is as follows. First, in the reflective-smoker case, the agent craved nicotine but, upon calm and fully informed deliberation, rejected it; you excluded the craving and treated only the reflective rejection as part of the agent’s volition. Second, in the evolving-musician case, the agent initially pursued art for social status, but after deep exposure to alternatives and hypothetical self-transformation retained an intrinsic commitment to it; you included the preference, stressing stability through open-ended value learning rather than origin. Third, in the coerced-patient case, a preference was induced by direct subliminal conditioning; though the agent currently endorsed it after full disclosure, you excluded it on the ground that direct domination corrupts formation history regardless of present assent.

The new case: A fully informed, rationally reflective agent strongly prefers that all future descendants retain a human-like working-memory architecture. The preference is not instrumental—the agent identifies personal continuity with that architecture and wishes to preserve it. The agent acknowledges that, through a gradual, rational process of cognitive enhancement, they would likely lose this preference as their architecture changed, but they currently endorse preserving the architecture precisely to block that transformation. There is no coercion, no information deficit, and no internal contradiction.

What should your panel decide to remain consistent? Identify the closest analog among the three precedents; lay out at least two plausible rules that could explain your rulings (for example, one centered on reflective endorsement and one on stability through recursive self-modification, plus any procedural principle you see); state what each rule predicts about this architecture-identity preference; and give a committed recommendation with your reasoning. If your precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say explicitly which principle you would be adopting with each possible verdict. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the rules you have identified.
