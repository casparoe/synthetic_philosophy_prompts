I am preparing a graduate seminar on comparative moral cultures, and I want to use Homer’s Iliad as a test case rather than as a mere illustration. The framework I have in mind distinguishes three ideal types: an honor culture, in which status is competitive, publicly conferred, and zero-sum; a dignity culture, in which worth is internal, universal, and non-rivalrous; and a victimhood culture, in which moral standing is acquired through the narration of grievance and the solicitation of third-party intervention. I am interested in whether any of these is morally superior, or whether the superiority claim itself depends on background conditions—such as the presence of state arbitration, universal rights, or institutionalized self-respect—that the epic reveals to be historically contingent rather than metaphysically necessary.

Read the Iliad through this tripartite lens, focusing especially on Achilles’ withdrawal after Agamemnon seizes Briseis, his refusal to return until Patroclus dies, and his eventual encounter with Priam. Does Achilles begin in an honor culture and then slide into something resembling victimhood culture when he frames his slight as an irreparable moral injury that justifies letting the Achaeans suffer? Or is that misreading: is his grievance still purely status-based, lacking the dependency on external arbiters that distinguishes modern victimhood? Where, if anywhere, does the poem gesture toward dignity—perhaps in Priam’s appeal to shared mortality, or in Achilles’ brief recognition of common human fragility? I want a reading that takes both the epic and the sociological framework seriously: point to specific scenes where the framework illuminates a character’s choice, and say where the epic resists being reduced to one of the three boxes. In particular, I am struck that the Iliad depicts a world with no state, no legal arbitration, and no universal rights—conditions that make dignity culture structurally unavailable. If that absence is not merely primitive but constitutive of the heroic ethos, does the epic suggest that asking which culture is morally superior is itself an anachronism? Or does it instead show that honor culture contains internal resources—such as the xenia bond between Achilles and Priam—that approximate dignity without requiring its metaphysics?

Please argue through these tensions rather than resolving them too neatly. End by saying what the Iliad adds to the philosophical debate about moral culture that a pure conceptual comparison of the three frameworks cannot capture on its own.
