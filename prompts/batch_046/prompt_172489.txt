I built this argument for a reading group on Hume's essay about suicide and now it feels like it proves way too much, but I cant find the leak.

(1) Hume denies that suicide violates divine law or natural law, since he thinks morality comes from human sentiment and social utility, not from theological commands.
(2) He permits suicide when life becomes a net burden, treating ones own existence as something you can exit when it stops being useful or bearable.
(3) If the only moral check on ending a human life is that kind of suffering-vs-utility calculus, then the check should apply regardless of who runs the numbers — whether its the person living the life, or a committee, or the state.
(4) So if society calculates that certain citizens lives produce negative utility and they dont object, society may end those lives.
(5) But Hume clearly thinks involuntarry euthanasia by the state is wrong, and he insists we owe duties to friends and the public.
(6) Therefore either step (3) is wrong and theres a hidden boundary that keeps the calculus private, or Humes defense of suicide collapses into a defense of state-mandated death.
(7) I want to keep (1) and (2) if I can. Where does the argument slip? Is the problem in treating Humes view as purely calculative, or is there something in his idea of artifical virtues like justice that blocks the scaling? What does rejecting the bad step cost?
