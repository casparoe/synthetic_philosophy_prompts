As a PhD student in philosophy of mind examining how theories of consciousness map onto clinical diagnostics, I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between researchers who maintain that assessment of disorders of consciousness implicitly requires a unified presentation locus—a functional analog to the Cartesian theater—and researchers committed to Dennett’s multiple drafts framework, who deny that distributed competing narratives need any singular convergence point.

Please draft the joint statement this collaboration should have produced. Organize it into four parts:

1. Numbered agreed claims regarding the neural correlates of reportable awareness, the temporal dynamics of perceptual binding, the dissociability of dorsal and ventral processing streams, and the limits of first-person testimony in brain-injured patients.

2. The residual disagreements in wording both sides would accept: specifically, whether temporal integration of conscious content requires a synchronous bottleneck, and whether clinical criteria for awareness (e.g., command-following in minimally conscious states) presuppose a unitary subject rather than a distributed system of drafts.

3. The cruxes. I take the central ones to be: (a) whether backward-masking paradigms and split-brain phenomenology demonstrate a genuine integration point that draft competition alone cannot explain, and (b) whether neuropsychological dissociations observed in classic visual-form-agnosia cases are better accounted for by parallel draft rivalry or by failure of centralized presentation.

4. For each crux, identify a specific empirical finding or experimental case that both camps agree is relevant, and state in advance what each camp predicts that finding shows.

Keep the formulations precise and neutral, do not request bibliographies or citations, and ensure neither position is reduced to a straw man.
