I've come to hold what I call a dual-track view of path dependence in institutional history, and I want an honest assessment of how exposed it actually is. On the first track, institutional genealogy is genuinely locked in: the specific chain of contingent events, personal alliances, accidental precedents, and local compromises that produce a particular legal doctrine or technological standard is historically necessary in its details, so that interrupting any link in the chain would yield a different internal structure, different personnel, and different symbolic associations. On the second track, functional equilibrium is not locked in at all: the coordination problems, efficiency pressures, or social needs that the institution ultimately addresses converge on a bounded range of substitutable solutions, so that if one genealogical route had been blocked, another would have arrived at a recognizably equivalent functional outcome within a bounded time horizon. This is not the standard strong-path-dependence versus convergence framing, because the two tracks are meant to coexist rather than compete; institutional forms are historically unique in their genealogy but substitutable in their social function.

The implication for counterfactual reasoning is that we should reject both "without X, nothing like Y could have existed" and "Y was historically inevitable." Instead, the proper counterfactual concerns which specific institutional variant emerges, not whether an institution serving that functional role emerges at all. I take this to be a substantive claim about the structure of historical explanation, not merely a semantic distinction, but I am unsure whether it risks being qualified into unfalsifiability.

What would have to be true for this dual-track view to be wrong? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness. Would a rigorously documented case of true functional divergence—where blocking one institutional path led not to a substitute within the equilibrium range, but to the permanent absence of any functional equivalent, or to a radically different social outcome—count as fatal, or could the view absorb it by expanding the bounds of the range post hoc? Conversely, what kind of evidence would show that institutional genealogy is far less contingent than I claim—perhaps systematic convergence on identical internal structures across independent historical origins? I also want to know what defenders of pure path dependence and pure convergence would themselves concede as counting against my hybrid view, not only what they would cite against each other. Finally, is there a risk that my distinction between genealogy and function collapses upon inspection—say, if the internal structure of an institution is itself part of its functional profile—such that separating the two tracks is conceptually unstable? If the view turns out to be well insulated against all of these, I want to know whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a sign that the position has been rendered too flexible to do meaningful work.
