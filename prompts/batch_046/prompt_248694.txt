I'm a journalist who writes about institutional design and the philosophy of social science, and I've been tracking a configuration in modern knowledge workplaces that feels like a cousin to familiar Foucauldian ideas without being identical to any of them. I want to know whether philosophers, sociologists, or science-and-technology scholars have already given it a precise name, and—if not—I'd like a rigorous coinage with its boundary conditions drawn clearly.

The pattern runs like this. In several organizations I've reported on, authority does not operate mainly through top-down surveillance or direct prohibition. Instead, the institution supplies an elaborate taxonomy—categories of risk, productivity, emotional state, collaboration quality, or ethical posture—and then requires workers to apply that taxonomy continuously to themselves. The employee becomes the principal archivist of her own normalization: she logs her failures, scores her own teamwork, narrates her stress using the institution's exact lexicon, and produces granular self-reports that feed back into organizational planning. The institution rarely issues explicit commands; it refines the categories based on aggregate self-data, then feeds the refined taxonomy back to the workers, who apply it with even greater precision. Two concrete instances: a biomedical research lab where postdocs maintain mandatory "negative-result journals" not for peer review but as internal data streams that reshape funding priorities; and a corporate wellness program in which staff generate weekly "resilience narratives" using a company-defined vocabulary, which human resources then uses to restructure team assignments. In both cases, power functions not by suppressing autonomy but by delegating the labor of categorization downward, so that self-knowledge becomes indistinguishable from institutional bookkeeping.

This is adjacent to Foucault's panopticism—there is normalization and visibility—but it differs because the gaze is largely self-administered and the mechanism is not fear of being seen but the productive obligation to manufacture one's own classificatory data. It also touches governmentality and technologies of the self, yet those frameworks usually emphasize how subjects internalize rationalities of state, market, or medicine. Here the distinctive twist is institutionalized auto-archiving: the subject is not merely self-governing but is actively generating the raw material—the refined distinctions, the failure categories, the emotional metrics—that allows the institution to adjust its norms without ever having to observe directly.

Is there an established concept—within Foucault scholarship, organizational sociology, or science and technology studies—for this exact arrangement, in which disciplinary power operates through delegated self-taxonomization and feedback loops of self-generated data?
