I have been working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right, specifically his claim that war contains an ethical moment — das sittliche Moment — by suspending private particularity and affirming the state's universal will, together with his rejection of Kant's ideal of perpetual peace as a stagnating condition that allows corruption to fester. From these ideas I built the following argument, but I now worry it proves far more than Hegel intended — perhaps that a well-ordered state must constitutionally engineer conflict. Here are the steps:

(1) For Hegel, war is not mere evil but serves an irreplaceable ethical function: it prevents the corruption and stagnation that arise when civil society's private interests dominate without the unifying sacrifice demanded by the universal.
(2) If a practice serves an irreplaceable ethical function in preserving the state's vitality, and the state is the highest actualization of ethical life — the Wirklichkeit der sittlichen Idee — then the state has a positive obligation to secure the conditions for that practice.
(3) Prolonged peace is precisely the condition in which private particularity accumulates and stagnation threatens; therefore, in prolonged peace the conditions for war's ethical function are absent.
(4) So, whenever peace persists long enough to threaten corruption, the state is ethically obligated to initiate or at least not prevent war.
(5) This entails that a rational Hegelian constitution should mandate periodic conflicts or maintain institutional mechanisms that make war inevitable.
(6) But a constitutional duty to initiate war is morally monstrous and incompatible with international law.
(7) Therefore, either Hegel's view is false, or one of the premises above is wrong.

I am inclined to keep (1) — Hegel does think war has this ethical moment — but I want to know what I am paying if I reject something else instead. Where exactly does the argument slide from a descriptive claim about war's ethical significance to the normative conclusion in (4) and (5)? Is there an equivocation between the state's right to defend itself and a duty to manufacture conflict? Or is the error in (2), treating an ethical function as generating a positive obligation? Explain which step is the weakest link, why it looked plausible, and what each of the main alternatives — giving up (1), (2), or the move to (4) — costs in terms of fidelity to Hegel's text and the coherence of his political philosophy. Lead with your bottom-line diagnosis, then unpack the reasoning. Keep the whole response to no more than 350 words, in American English.
