A philosophy-of-science lecture I attended recently summarized Einstein's hole argument in the following way. Einstein noticed that in general relativity, if the field equations are generally covariant, then within any matter-free region of spacetime — a "hole" — one can apply a smooth coordinate transformation that is the identity everywhere outside the region but reshuffles the points inside. This produces two metric fields that agree outside but differ inside, yet both solve the field equations, so the theory apparently fails to determine a unique physical state wherever matter is absent. Einstein resolved the puzzle by arguing that spacetime points have no intrinsic physical identity of their own; only the coincidence of field values with material world-lines is observable, which means the two descriptions represent exactly the same physical world. The lecturer then interpreted this resolution as an early form of structural realism about spacetime, and went on to claim that the argument carries over directly to modern loop quantum gravity: because spin-network states in that framework are invariant under diffeomorphisms, the hole argument proves that no quantum-gravitational ontology can assign determinate properties to genuinely empty space. I suspect the basic logic of the argument is captured fairly but that the lecture overreaches in its conclusions. Could you audit this summary claim by claim? For each part, say whether a historian of general relativity or a foundations specialist would accept it, whether any subtle misrepresentation or anachronism is present — for instance, conflating Einstein's initial resistance to general covariance with his eventual resolution, importing a modern metaphysical label where a more modest relational claim was intended, or treating a feature of the classical theory as automatically constraining quantum-gravitational ontologies — and whether the conclusion about loop quantum gravity survives once the summary is corrected.
