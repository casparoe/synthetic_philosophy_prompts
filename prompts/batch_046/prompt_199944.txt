I'm designing a single seventy-five-minute seminar for first-year politics students on the phrase "Western values." The goal is not descriptive cataloguing but conceptual analysis: whether the category is coherent, what it includes and excludes, and how it operates as an argumentative move rather than a neutral geographic label. The students will arrive with one of two default positions — that the phrase is an obvious self-description of Europe and North America, or that it is pure propaganda with no content whatsoever.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, lay out the dependency structure: what conceptual distinction must be firmly in place before the phrase can be interrogated rather than simply affirmed or rejected. I have in mind the separation between a descriptive cultural cluster and a normative argumentative frame, but specify the exact order in which the pieces should come and why each depends on the prior one. Second, choose and refine the one carrying example that should run through the session: I want to build around a comparative regulatory scenario — specifically the tension between German data-protection norms, American openness doctrines, and Chinese social-credit architectures — but you should shape this into a single concrete case study, stating precisely what features it must include (to expose coherence problems) and what distracting elements it must exclude (to prevent the discussion from collapsing into technology policy). Explain why this particular case carries the conceptual idea.

Third, map the misconceptions they will bring or form in sequence: that coherence requires universal adherence across all Western societies; that "Western" functions as a purely geographic rather than genealogical or institutional category; that recognizing the phrase's rhetorical use is identical to denying any shared normative content; and that the phrase must be either sincerely descriptive or purely cynical with no intermediate possibility. For each, specify exactly where in the explanatory sequence it should be anticipated and headed off, and how the carrying example can absorb it.

Fourth, design one diagnostic question to ask at the end that will reveal whether a student has grasped the conceptual structure — that the category can be partially coherent, partly rhetorical, geographically overinclusive, and normatively contested all at once — rather than merely adopting a partisan stance for or against the phrase.

Finally, state explicitly what should be deliberately left out at this level and openly refused: for instance, a detailed historiography of the phrase's Cold War instrumentalization, deep comparative philosophy of East Asian value traditions, or any assigned bibliography, citation, or reading list. The session must work for a student who has no access to external sources beyond the discussion itself, so do not include references to specific texts, authors, or further research they should consult.
