I'm constructing a decision tree for my high school metaphysics seminar that sorts respondents by their actual commitments on Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, using the classic two-sphere universe—two perfectly identical spheres suspended in an otherwise empty, isotropic space—as the sole diagnostic case. I need the tree to be built from logical space rather than from a list of labels: each internal node should pose a concrete question about the scenario that a peer can answer without knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to, and only after all answers are given should the path map onto named views. The tree should contain about six terminal leaves, which must include at least the following: a bundle-theoretic or strong-Leibnizian denial that the scenario is metaphysically possible; a Newtonian substantivalist who saves the principle by treating absolute spatial points as real, intrinsic differentiators; a relationalist denial that appeals to the idea that spatial distance cannot be instantiated without a richer relational structure; a haecceitist who accepts the scenario, rejects the principle, and appeals to primitive individual essences; and a brute-difference view that also accepts the scenario and rejects the principle but denies any further explanatory ground for the distinction. In addition, I want one less commonly mapped leaf that distinguishes the spheres by their modal or counterfactual profiles rather than by spatial location—for instance, that each sphere has a distinct possible history of surviving the annihilation of the other. Wherever the branching logic permits answer-combinations that correspond to no named or defensible position, mark those combinations as unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. At the close of the tree, explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your questions genuinely fail to separate. The instrument should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and it should be presentable as a formal, polished taxonomy for an independent-study presentation. Write the tree in clear, formal prose with each node phrased as a direct question.
