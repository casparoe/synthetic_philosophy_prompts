As I am drafting a novel about a coastal town that must decide the fate of its destroyed archive after a flood, I have become absorbed by the philosophical tension between an ethics of remembrance and an ethics of forgetting—the kind of dispute mapped by Avishai Margalit’s reflections on collective moral memory and David Rieff’s arguments for the legitimacy of letting traumatic pasts dissolve. Rather than asking for scholarly apparatus, I need you to construct for me a precise conceptual instrument: a thought experiment engineered to exact specifications, set in an ordinary human institution.

Please design a realistic scenario—no longer than a single paragraph—located in a concrete institutional setting such as a municipal memorial board, a school district’s curriculum committee, or a public archive’s restoration council. In this scenario, a preservationist framework that treats collective remembrance of catastrophe as a non-derogable moral duty (owed especially to the dead and to future generations who must not repeat the past) and a therapeutic framework that treats strategic forgetting, narrative dissolution, or the refusal of fixed monuments as necessary conditions for collective autonomy and psychological recovery must both actively endorse the identical institutional policy. The convergence must not be trivial: it cannot be mutual inaction or a compromise that splits the difference. Instead, both sides must support the same structural intervention, but for reasons that are genuinely diametrically opposed—one interpreting the policy as the deepest fulfillment of remembrance, the other as the successful accomplishment of forgetting. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies these conditions: state the shared policy explicitly; trace the opposing reasoning of each framework as applied to the case; isolate the precise structural feature of the institution, the trauma, or the policy design that forces the two theories into alignment; show what happens to their verdicts if that feature is removed or substantially altered; and indicate how a committed defender of either view might attempt to break the convergence by disputing that very feature. Keep the setting firmly human-scale—no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no supernatural mechanisms—and do not provide bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further texts; I want to see whether the conceptual machinery runs entirely on its own.
