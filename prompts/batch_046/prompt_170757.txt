As a philosophy professor serving on the editorial-ethics board of the university archive that holds a recently deceased moral philosopher's private papers, I must circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting. A producer proposes an audio drama based on her unpublished notebooks that stages internal monologues attributing to her settled convictions drawn from tentative, later-abandoned sketches, translated into modern conceptual vocabulary she never used. Draft the board's formal written opinion—exactly five numbered paragraphs, each comprising one or two sentences, with no separate introduction or conclusion—recommending either conditional approval with binding structural safeguards and a specific compliance deadline, revision-required deferral, or rejection. The opinion must take seriously the producer's objection that sanitizing contradiction violates the audience's epistemic autonomy, concede honestly the strongest consideration favoring artistic license, and center the distinction between representational harm to the philosopher's posthumous reputation and hermeneutic harm to the conceptual ecology preserved in the archive, stating precisely what must change by the deadline if approval is granted.
