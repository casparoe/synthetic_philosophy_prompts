You are a political philosopher with expertise in distributive justice and welfare-state ethics. Before answering, take a moment to clarify the conceptual framework you will use: combine a contractualist view of reciprocity—where social benefits are conditional on some form of contribution or compliance—with the notion of “moral visibility,” the idea that workfare rules function not merely as economic screens but as expressive rituals that render a recipient’s moral membership visible to the taxpayer-citizenry. Now apply this framework to the 1996 U.S. Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act and its replacement of AFDC with TANF, concentrating on work requirements, five-year time limits, and block-grant devolution. How does the framework map onto these design choices—who counts as the relevant player, what counts as contribution, and what exactly is being made visible? What does it explain well about the political coalition that supported the reform and about the lived experience of recipients? Where does it misdescribe or fall silent—does it overlook the racialized and gendered dynamics of sanctioning, the bureaucratic gap between legislative intent and caseworker discretion, or the fiscal motives behind block grants? Finally, treat the episode as a test of the theory: does PRWORA support the claim that conditionality is primarily about reciprocity and moral visibility, or does it show that labor-market discipline, symbolic punishment, or cost-shifting dominate? Be disciplined about the mapping, and say explicitly where the theory offers no purchase rather than forcing it to speak.
