I am trying to sharpen my thinking about political ethics and have constructed an argument that seems to dissolve the dirty-hands problem by distributing it too widely, yet I cannot locate the precise misstep. The standard formulation holds that political leaders occasionally must violate ordinary moral prohibitions—through deception, coercion, or betrayal of trust—to secure overwhelming public goods, and that this leaves a distinctive moral residue or stain on the leader that citizens, who do not directly perform the act, do not share. Here is the argument that troubles me: (1) A dirty-handed act is defined as a morally wrong action that is all-things-considered justified by a sufficiently weighty public purpose, leaving a non-eliminable moral residue on the agent who performs it. (2) In a representative democracy, citizens do not merely predict that leaders will face such dilemmas; through authorization, they institutionally empower leaders to act within a governance framework where such violations are systematically required. (3) This authorization is active normative endorsement, not passive forecasting: by sustaining the office and its powers, the citizen structurally endorses the conditions under which the wrong act becomes necessary. (4) If an agent actively endorses the conditions that make a specific wrong act necessary, possesses the ongoing power to revoke that endorsement, and does not revoke it, then that agent acquires a share of the moral residue—unless there exists an isolating mechanism that blocks transmission. (5) Democratic theory provides no credible isolating mechanism: the ballot is precisely the conduit of authorization, not a firewall that quarantines the citizen from the leader's moral choices. (6) Therefore, democratic citizens bear the same moral residue as the leaders they authorize. (7) But if citizens already carry dirty hands, the special distinction between the stained leader and the clean citizen collapses, and with it collapses the very problem that makes dirty hands philosophically interesting—there is no unique burden of leadership to explain. (8) Consequently, either democratic citizenship is inherently and universally corrupting in a way that renders ordinary moral life impossible, or one of steps (2) through (5) smuggles in an equivocation—perhaps between authorizing an institutional role and authorizing particular wrong acts, or between foreseeing and intending.

I feel the reasoning is valid but the conclusion is intolerable, and I would most like to preserve both the definition in (1) and the claim in (7) that the dirty-hands distinction retains philosophical point. Where should I apply the pressure? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost—particularly for our understanding of democratic responsibility and the possibility of moral separation between ruler and ruled?
