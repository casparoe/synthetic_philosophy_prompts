I just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether Dutch-book coherence should apply not only to descriptive beliefs but to normative commitments, and she reconstructed a chain that I now find suspicious because the conclusion seems to eliminate reasonable moral uncertainty altogether. Here is the argument, laid out more carefully than we managed in conversation.

(1) A standard Dutch book demonstrates that if your credences over outcomes violate the probability calculus, you can be forced into a set of bets with guaranteed net loss; this is widely taken to show that rational belief requires probabilistic coherence.

(2) Moral decision-making can be modeled analogously: you assign moral weights to outcomes—for example, preventing harm H, respecting autonomy A—and choose actions that maximize expected moral value given those weights.

(3) If these moral weights violate finite additivity across a partition of outcomes—say, you are willing to pay $50 to prevent harm H, $50 to prevent harm H′, but only $70 to prevent both together—then a “moral bookmaker” can structure a sequence of obligations (donations, time commitments, institutional reforms) that you will accept individually but that jointly guarantee you fail to prevent a harm you weight highly, at a net moral cost.

(4) Therefore, rational moral agents must assign weights that satisfy the probability axioms over the space of outcomes.

(5) Extend this upward to theory-level uncertainty: if you assign 0.6 confidence to consequentialism and 0.6 confidence to deontology, treating them as independent normative frameworks, you can be made to accept trade-offs in specific cases (for instance, sacrificing one to save five in a context where deontology forbids it) that neither theory, applied consistently, permits, and you pay the “moral loss” of violating your own deepest commitments.

(6) Hence, maintaining high confidence in multiple, incompatible moral theories without integrating them into a single probabilistically coherent framework is not merely untidy but rationally indefensible—it exposes you to a second-order Dutch book against normative fragmentation.

(7) But this conclusion proves far too much: it implies that anyone with genuine moral pluralism, or who suspends judgment between competing frameworks without collapsing them into one, is irrational in exactly the same way as someone who assigns P(A) = 0.6 and P(not-A) = 0.6. That feels wrong; moral uncertainty is usually regarded as permissible, even epistemically responsible.

(8) So somewhere in the chain a step must be rejected, but I am unsure which one and what the price is. Which premise or inference should I give up? Does rejecting it require abandoning the idea that moral reasoning is subject to coherence constraints altogether, or only denying that theory-level uncertainty behaves like outcome-level uncertainty? And if I want to keep (4)—that moral weights over outcomes must be coherent—what do I have to pay to block the extension to (6)?

Please lay out which step is weakest, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives. In particular, distinguish whether the error is in applying Dutch-book reasoning across normative domains, in treating inter-theoretic weights as probabilities over worlds, or in assuming that coherence at the outcome level must propagate upward to the theory level without an intermediate aggregation rule.
