Hey — I’ve been poking at this for my seminar on self-deception and I think I built an argument that eats its own tail. Can you tell me where the gears strip?

(1) If self-deception is real, then one mind must simultaneously host both the belief that p and the belief that not-p.
(2) But beliefs are dispostions to act, assert, and reason, so housing contradictory beliefs requires separate sub-personal modules — call it modular doxastic isolation (MDI).
(3) Once you allow MDI for self-deception, consistency forces you to allow it everywhere we see compartmentalized behavoir: strategic ignorance in firms, denial in addiction, even split testimony in courts.
(4) But then attributing belief to a whole person becomes impossible without first mapping every module, which we never do in ordinary life.
(5) So either MDI is false — killing self-deception — or our everyday belief-attribution practices are systematically empty.
(6) Since the second horn is crazy, MDI must be false.
(7) But if MDI is false, then not only is self-deception out; so is any localized compartmentalization, including the kind that lets a scientist hold a working hypothesis she knows is probably wrong. That feels like it proves way too much.

Which step should I reject? I’d like to keep that people can genuinely deceive themselves, but I also don’t want to trash ordinary belief talk. Is there a middle ground where MDI is real but tightly localised, or is the mistake in (3) — assuming that allowing it once means allowing it everywhere? If I have to choose, I’d rather save self-deception than save the universality of belief attribution, but I want to know what I’m paying.
