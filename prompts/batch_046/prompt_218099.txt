hey, i'm a phil phd student working mostly on distributive justice (luck egalitarian stuff, though i also read some speech act theory on the side, don't ask). at a workshop coffee break last week two grad students got stuck on this thing they called 'nested option luck' — the idea that your brute luck (say, cognitive profile or geographic origin) can reshape your option-set in ways that aren't just resource deficits.

Maya said: imagine two people with identical initial resource bundles but different innate cognitive styles. The labor market rewards certain styles, so one person's 'choice' to develop marketable skills is genuinely available, while the other faces an option-set full of poor fits. The second person's disadvantage comes from the interaction of brute luck (cognitive profile) with option luck (available choices), and luck egalitarianism should compensate for this interaction, not just for the resource bundle.

Jonas pushed back: that's just brute luck about conversion abilities, which resource egalitarianism handles by giving more resources to those with worse conversion factors. Calling it 'nested option luck' is redundant — you've just redescribed the standard problem with extra jargon. He said if we treat every constrained choice as compensable brute luck, we dissolve the choice/brute distinction that makes luck egalitarianism coherent.

Maya replied: but resource equality doesn't solve it, because you can't just 'give more resources' to fix a cognitive-market mismatch without knowing what resources would convert properly. The problem isn't the size of the bundle, it's the structure of the option-set it generates. So there's genuinely a distinct moral category here — compound luck — that sits between pure brute and pure option luck.

Jonas said: then you've left luck egalitarianism and entered capability theory. Sen and Nussbaum already handle this. The point of Dworkin's framework is precisely to avoid tracking every interaction effect; if you start compensating for option-set structures, you're doing capability equality, not luck equality.

Maya came back: I'm not abandoning the distinction, I'm refining it for cases where brute luck operates on the option-set itself. Without this refinement, luck egalitarianism can't explain why two equally-resourced people with different profiles face different moral claims.

Jonas: but without a principled cutoff for which interactions count, 'compound luck' swallows every disadvantage, including ones people clearly choose.
