I've been mulling over this standng-to-blame business ever since I left the lab and started dabbling in philosophy books. Here are my gut verdicts on a few situations, and I'm wondering whether there's one principel behind them or whether I'm just being muddled.

One: my old colleague Dr Patel knew our post-doc was faking data but stayed quiet because she needed the grant. Years later she retired and then publicly blasted a junior researcher for p-hacking. I think she hasn't got standing to blame him — she's a hypocrit. But two: my neighbour smoked forty a day for decades, and now lectures teenagers about vaping. She never lied about it, just kept on. I feel she actually does have standing, because she knows the damage first-hand. So hypocrisy can't just mean 'you did something similar once'?

Three: a climate scientist who flies everywhere for conferences tells a student off for using plastic straws. The student says 'you've no standing.' I think the scientist still has standing on climate change, but maybe not specifically on straws? Or is standing always about the exact same wrong?

Four: someone blames you for a mistake they themselves made last week, but they've genuinely changed — maybe therapy, maybe just matured. Do they regain standing, or is it lost forever?

Five: in my old department everyone used the same dodgy statistical package. Now Professor Jones, who used it for decades, blames a new PhD student for using it. I think Jones lacks standing. But what if Jones was the first person in the department to switch to the better method? Does being an early reformer restore it?

Can you find a coherent account — maybe about clean hands, or moral equality, or issue-specific rights to blame — that saves most of these? Which verdict of mine does it have to throw overboard? And what does it predict for the case where the blamer is still doing the wrong, but at a tiny scale — like that flying scientist who now takes only one flight a year?
