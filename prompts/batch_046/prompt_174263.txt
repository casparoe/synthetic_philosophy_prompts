Draft the joint statement that a narrative-sovereignty camp and a behavioural-opacity camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the nature and value of privacy.

The narrative-sovereignty camp holds that privacy’s core value is the protection of an agent’s authority over the temporal integration and public narration of their own biography. They maintain that algorithmic data aggregation constructs a predictive double that preempts self-authorship, and that privacy harms should be assessed by whether they fracture this narrative authority rather than by whether any specific fact has been disclosed.

The behavioural-opacity camp holds that traditional information-control frameworks are obsolete under conditions of algorithmic inference. They maintain that privacy’s value lies in preserving actional slack—the gap between an action and its real-time predictive interpretation—and that the central harm is the collapse of that slack into anticipatory governance, regardless of whether anyone learns a particular fact about the agent.

Produce a document that both sides could endorse, structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims about what actually occurs in algorithmic societies, about the documented limitations of consent-based governance, and about which empirical phenomena are well-established. Within this section, clearly separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who work on the topic, and do not present any contested normative or empirical claim as the consensus view.

Second, a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is phrased in neutral wording that its own side would accept as accurate. The table should cover at least: (i) whether narrative authority is a distinct moral interest or derivative of a broader autonomy principle; (ii) whether actional slack can exist without some underlying informational boundary; and (iii) whether the predictive-double problem is better addressed by restricting data flows or by restructuring how predictions are used.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is whether there are privacy harms that reduce neither to narrative fracture nor to slack collapse, and another is whether collective or ecological privacy (for example, of groups or environments) can be derived from individual-level principles without remainder.

Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete case study, policy design, or piece of evidence that both camps agree bears on it, together with each side’s explicit prior prediction about what that case or evidence will show.

Fifth, diagnose whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—for instance, if the camps use privacy with different extensions yet share underlying normative commitments—and if so identify precisely which part and explain why.

Write in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not use verbatim quotations from real texts; paraphrase without quotation marks if reference to an existing concept is necessary. Ensure the answer relies on conceptual reasoning that does not require web access, avoiding dependence on highly specific empirical findings or obscure theoretical labels that a small model could not reliably recall.
