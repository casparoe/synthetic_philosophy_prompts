My study group has an argument that seems to show judicial review is either democratically illegitimate or practically useless, and that the same reasoning collapses contract interpretation and literary criticism. Here are the steps:

(1) Judicial review is justified only if courts apply constitutional meaning that is genuinely independent of the present legislature’s will.
(2) The only source of such independence is the fixed intentions of the original ratifiers.
(3) For modern issues the ratifiers never contemplated—say, predictive policing algorithms or biometric data retention—we have no reliable epistemic access to what they specifically intended.
(4) So in these cases a judge either applies her own preference (undemocratic fabrication) or admits she has no basis to overrule the legislature (making review redundant).
(5) But judicial review is exercised precisely in these unanticipated domains.
(6) Therefore judicial review, as actually practiced, is either illegitimate or redundant.
(7) The same structure holds for any authoritative dead text: interpreting a deceased contractor’s intent on an unforeseen technology, or fixing an author’s meaning on a context she never knew.
(8) Yet contract law and literary interpretation are legitimate and do not collapse into fabrication or refusal.
(9) Hence the reasoning that undermines judicial review proves too much, generating an absurd general skepticism about institutional interpretation of dead texts.

We want to keep both that judicial review can be legitimate and that contract interpretation is not absurd. Which premise or step should we reject—(2)’s exclusivity claim, (3)’s skeptical epistemic premise, (4)’s dichotomy, or (6)–(7)’s analogy? Explain why that step looked plausible and what giving it up costs. Then price the alternatives: what must originalism pay to reject (2) or (3), what must a living-constitution or common-law constitutionalist view pay to reject (4), and what must a hermeneutic theorist pay to reject (6)–(7)?
