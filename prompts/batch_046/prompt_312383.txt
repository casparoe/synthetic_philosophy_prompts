As a novelist whose current project traces a single family from the post-war economic boom through the algorithmic present, I am constantly confronted with an editorial demand that strikes me as philosophically dubious: my characters must be readable as Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Gen Z, with each cohort bearing an almost essentialist cluster of values, anxieties, and technological relationships. This framing treats generational labels as if they named natural kinds—entities with shared causal powers and modal properties—rather than administrative partitions of birth years. Write an essay on whether generations are real kinds in any ontologically serious sense. Examine whether shared exposure to historical events and technological regimes can generate collective dispositions strong enough to justify treating cohort membership as genuinely explanatory, or whether intra-cohort variance and the arbitrariness of boundary-drawing reduce these categories to marketing fictions and census artifacts. You should also consider a middle position—what we might call structural cohort realism—according to which generations are not natural kinds but are socially instituted kinds that acquire weak causal efficacy through institutional reinforcement and narrative expectation, making them real enough for a novelist’s craft but not metaphysically deep.

Reach and justify a definitive conclusion: defend either that generations possess sufficient unity to warrant realist treatment in both sociology and fiction, or that they are purely performative constructs whose predictive power derives entirely from self-fulfilling prophecy, or precisely articulate and defend the middle path of structural cohort realism. Your argument should be rigorous enough to guide a writer in deciding whether to let cohort membership determine a character’s psychology or to treat it as incidental background, and it should avoid treating the labels as self-evidently meaningful without first establishing what kind of reality, if any, they actually possess.
