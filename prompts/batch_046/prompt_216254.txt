I am exploring whether Frege's late private political writings—composed in the final years of his life and expressing authoritarian, nationalist, and anti-Semitic sentiments—have any bearing on the interpretation of his logic and philosophy of language. The standard view treats these writings as biographically regrettable but philosophically inert, insisting that Frege's commitment to the objectivity of thoughts (Gedanken) and the universality of logical law makes his technical work entirely separable from his private prejudices. Please argue for the contrary position: that the diary is not an aberration but reveals a normative architecture embedded in his philosophy itself. Specifically, argue that Frege's exclusion of all psychological, ethnic, and subjective particularity from the realm of objective thought was intended to secure a form of epistemic authority that could only be fully exercised within a homogeneous, hierarchically ordered cultural community of thinkers. On this reading, his hostility to psychologism and his insistence that logical laws are not descriptive generalizations take on a political valence: they define scientific objectivity precisely against those groups he regarded as incapable of grasping universal reason. Make the case that this continuity thesis is not only coherent but actually explains certain features of Frege's philosophy—such as his suspicion of empirical psychology in logic and his tendency to treat logical error as a kind of cognitive failure rather than mere disagreement—better than the standard separation thesis does.
