I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of technology working on ontology and ethics in persistent virtual worlds. During a recent graduate seminar on digital harm, a discussant repeatedly shut down arguments about avatar injury and sustained psychological distress tied to virtual object loss—what some researchers call haptic grief—with the maxim "if there's no blood, there's no harm." I am convinced the slogan is doing substantive philosophical work, but I cannot determine whether it expresses an ontological claim about the causal impotence of virtual objects, an epistemic or verificationist constraint on attributing suffering, a biological-naturalist criterion for moral status, a normative principle about risk thresholds, or something about the absence of biological substrate altogether. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that this one-liner could encode? For each reading, please specify whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or clearly false; and indicate which versions actually entail that persistent virtual worlds—where users form long-term attachments, where virtual economies generate real-world financial dependency, and where platform architectures enable systematic exclusion—generate no ethical obligations whatsoever. I want the answer structured as a menu of interpretations mapped to their commitments, not as an argument for one reading, and I need it without any bibliographic references, reading lists, or citation requests, since I am working without access to external databases.
