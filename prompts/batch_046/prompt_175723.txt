As a journalist covering science and public policy, I keep encountering a particular defense of continuing over-budget or low-yield projects—whether a multi-decade physics collaboration with null results or an infrastructure build whose costs have tripled. The defenders rarely claim that remaining benefits now exceed remaining costs; instead, they insist that stopping would render all prior investment, labor, and institutional commitment “meaningless” or “wasted.” The sunk expenditure is treated not as an input to a forward-looking calculation, but as a kind of moral debt that can only be settled by completion—an obligation to the past itself rather than to future welfare. It is not exactly the sunk-cost fallacy, since the reasoning is not “we must recover what we spent,” but rather “abandoning this now would dishonor what was already sacrificed.” I have seen this in debates over space missions, clinical trials, and public works, and I want to know whether philosophy, economics, or organizational theory already has a precise name for this redemptive, honor-bound commitment to finishing precisely because so much has already been lost. How is it different from escalation of commitment, from narrative institutional identity, and from ordinary path dependency? Where are its edges—when is it a coherent form of loyalty, and when does it collapse into irrationality? If no established term captures it exactly, please define the phenomenon sharply, including boundary cases. Keep your response to no more than 250 words, in one or two tight paragraphs.
