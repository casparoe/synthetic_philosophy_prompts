I'm trying to determine my actual commitments on material constitution—specifically, the relation between an artifact like a statue and the matter that constitutes it—by testing them against concrete cases rather than by aligning with a named theory. Here are my verdicts; they feel mostly right, but I am aware that at least one pair is hard to reconcile without either complicating the principle or overturning a judgment.

Cases and verdicts:

1. A sculptor shapes a lump of wet clay into a horse. Once the shape is complete and stable, there are two objects in exactly the same place: the clay, which could be squashed back into a lump, and the statue, which could survive being cast in bronze but not being squashed. They are not identical.

2. That clay horse is fired into ceramic; its chemical properties change radically, but its shape and artistic properties remain. Verdict: it is the same statue, now constituted by ceramic rather than clay. Material kind is not essential.

3. The ceramic horse is ground into dust, then the dust is collected and molded back into an identical horse. Verdict: this is a different statue. The original was destroyed in the grinding; the new one is a replica, even though made of the same atoms arranged identically.

4. A living oak tree is carved into the shape of a bear while it remains alive; the tree continues to grow and metabolize inside the carved form. Verdict: there is not a separate bear-statue coincident with the tree. There is only the tree, which now has an unusual shape. Shaping living tissue does not generate a new artifactual object.

5. The same tree dies, is treated with preservative, and is displayed in a museum labeled "Wooden Bear." Verdict: now there is a statue—a wooden bear—and the tree is gone. There is no longer a living tree coincident with an artifact.

6. Two sculptors begin with identical marble blocks. One chips away material until a horse emerges; the other chips randomly until only rubble remains. Verdict: in the first case, a statue comes into existence that was not there before; in the second, no new object comes into existence—there is only the destruction of the block.

7. Over decades, every part of the marble horse is replaced with synthetically identical material (same density, grain, appearance) through gradual repair. Verdict: the statue survives; it is the same object, now partly or wholly synthetic.

The tension I keep returning to is between (1) and (4)-(5). In (1), shaping inert matter seems to create a coincident artifact; in (4), shaping living matter seems not to. In (5), death and preservation seem to convert the tree into a statue, yet in (4) the very same carving did not produce a statue. I also go back and forth on whether (3) is correct: if every atom is recovered and arranged identically, my intuition that it is a different statue seems to depend on a hidden commitment to continuity of existence rather than mere structural duplication.

What I want:

State one precise principle—or a small, competing set of candidates—that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible. For each principle, specify:
- The clause or condition that explains each of (1)-(7) that it accommodates;
- Its prediction for the following unlisted case: the clay horse from (1) is never fired but is coated in a permanent preservative that keeps it soft and malleable; it retains its horse-shape indefinitely but can still be squashed back into a lump with moderate pressure. Is there still a statue coincident with the clay, or does the lack of rigidity dissolve the artifact?
- Which of my verdicts (especially the pair involving (1), (4), and (5)) it is forced to overturn, and why that overturning is or is not costly.
- Whether the principle implies that in (6) the statue's coming-to-be is a real ontological change or merely a change in our description.

Please do not offer bibliographic references, reading lists, or named-theory labels as substitutes for the analysis; I am looking for the principle itself, articulated clearly enough that I could apply it to new cases without knowing the literature.
