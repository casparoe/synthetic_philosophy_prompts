I am a novelist applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that supports conceptual work at the boundary of literary craft and cultural heritage. My current manuscript concerns the afterlives of objects taken in imperial and colonial contexts, and I want to use the fellowship period to develop a rigorous conceptual framework—not a novel draft—on museum restitution.

Please write a polished, two-page research proposal comparing the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles through an angle that is deliberately not the standard legal, ethical, or provenance-based one. My half-formed idea is this: restitution debates for these two collections are usually framed in terms of ownership, cultural patrimony, or harm to source communities. But I suspect there is an underlying narrative architecture that remains almost entirely unexamined. The Benin Bronzes are typically narrated as living heritage returning to a sovereign culture that never stopped claiming them; the Parthenon Marbles are narrated as architectural fragments returning to a unified monument. These may represent two distinct genres of restitution—what I am calling, provisionally, biographical return versus architectural reintegration.

The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the conceptual question—specifically, whether these two narrative genres generate different moral conditions for restitution, and whether we can distinguish between a return that completes a story of injury and one that merely relocates an object between competing narratives; an explanation of why existing legal, cosmopolitan museum-ethics, and postcolonial positions leave this particular question open, treating the object as property or symbol rather than as a narrative agent with an interruptible biography; the comparative, conceptual method (not empirical archival research or field interviews) and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives; a realistic six-month timeline divided into phases of comparative mapping, framework construction, and writing; what a successful result would look like—namely, a framework distinguishing narrative closure from narrative rupture that carries normative implications for curatorial practice—and what would count as failure, such as the distinction collapsing between the two cases or the framework turning out to be purely descriptive with no practical bite; and an honest assessment of scope risks, particularly whether comparing only two limit cases is sufficient to support any broader claim, and what would make the project too large for a single semester-equivalent fellowship.

The audience is a foundation board that funds humanities projects but is not composed of specialists in museum law. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations; I will supply references myself. Maintain a formal, precise tone, but allow the novelist’s concern with story, continuity, and closure to shape the framing throughout.
