I've been working through Kierkegaard's critique of the modern press and the anonymous collective he calls the public—especially in his review-essay on modernity, commonly referred to as The Present Age—where he argues that the press manufactures a phantom audience that is numerically enormous yet qualitatively hollow, producing a leveling effect in which individuals hide behind anonymity, substitute detached reflection for decisive action, and evade the passionate commitment that makes a person an individual. I want to apply this framework to a well-known contemporary episode: the 2022 Depp v. Heard defamation trial and, more specifically, the global social-media ecosystem that formed around its live-streamed proceedings. The trial itself was decided by a seated jury bound by oath to reach a verdict, but running parallel to it was a vast distributed audience—TikTok creators, YouTube commentators, Twitter accounts—who consumed clipped testimony, rendered instantaneous moral judgments, and participated in meme culture that collapsed complex allegations of domestic violence into binary hashtags.

Analyze this episode through Kierkegaard's lens, and run the analysis in both directions. First, map the actors with precision: who occupies the role of the responsible individual (the jurors? the litigants?), who occupies the role of the anonymous public, and what functions as the press in this environment—traditional news outlets, the algorithmic recommendation feed, or the participatory audience itself? Second, say what Kierkegaard's theory predicts about judgment and commitment in such a setting: does live-streaming function as a modern version of the press that creates distance without responsibility? Does algorithmic amplification of emotional clips constitute an accelerated, technologically mediated form of leveling? Third, identify what the theory explains well—perhaps the performative nature of public sympathy, the substitution of reflective consumption for decisive action, or the dissolution of personal accountability through anonymity—and also where it misdescribes or remains silent, especially regarding the fact that modern spectators are not merely passive readers but active content producers whose engagement generates economic and political feedback loops that Kierkegaard never anticipated. Finally, say what the episode shows about the theory itself: is it largely confirmed, does it reveal an anomaly (for instance, that the public was intensely passionate yet still without commitment), or does it require us to revise the concept of leveling to include algorithmic curation and user-generated spectacle? Be disciplined about the mapping: do not force Kierkegaard to speak where he offers no guidance, but do not ignore the structural parallels either, and reach a clear verdict on whether the trial's social-media dimension is best understood as a confirmation of his diagnosis or as a case that outstrips it.
