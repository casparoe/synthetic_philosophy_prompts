I'm putting together a pitch for a small summer fellowship in philosophy of religion, and I'm tired of proposals that just recycle Schellenberg's hiddenness argument without doing anything weird with it. My half-formed idea: treat divine hiddenness not as a basic evidence problem—God hasn't left enough footprints—but as a recognition problem, where the complaint is that available testimony fails to register because of some kind of credibility gap, structurally closer to testimonial injustice than to simple absence. Most answers split between free-will defenses and evidentialist critiques, and neither camp really asks whether hiddenness is best modeled as missing data or as unrecognized presence, so there's an open space there. I need a one-page proposal that states this question precisely, explains why reframing hiddenness as recognition rather than absence changes the moral and epistemic stakes, and lays out an approach that builds the comparison to testimonial injustice frameworks carefully—without just importing social epistemology wholesale—plus says what success looks like (maybe a working distinction between being hidden and being unrecognized, with conditions under which the hiddenness objection survives) and what failure looks like (probably collapsing back into "God hides for mysterious reasons" without illuminating the recognition problem). Realistic scope: six weeks, deliverable is a 5000-word working paper, not a book, and I'll track down all there citations myself so please don't include a bibliography. There's probably a typo in here somewhere, but write it like I'm pitching a foundation that funds conceptual work and doesn't want another literature review.
