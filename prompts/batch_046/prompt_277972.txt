Consider the consent architecture employed by large-scale longitudinal health repositories and linked digital-health platforms. At enrollment, participants sign one baseline agreement granting broad, open-ended permission for future research uses of biological samples and linked health data. They may later log into a portal to revoke or narrow permissions; the interface defaults every category to "shared" and requires active, granular navigation to restrict uses. By contrast, the same institutions require separate, witnessed, in-person consent—often with explicit risk disclosure—for every clinical drug trial, even when that trial draws on samples or data already covered by the baseline agreement.

What theory of valid consent does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does the digital, revocable layer treat consent as a property-like waiver that needs only initial competence and absence of coercion, regardless of how little participants understand future uses? Does the in-person clinical layer, with its demand for specific disclosure and witnessed deliberation, presuppose that consent requires actual comprehension of particular risks and that deception by omission undermines validity? Are these two layers guided by a single unified conception—perhaps that data consent is low-risk and therefore needs less protection—or do they silently combine incompatible theories, such as autonomy-as-ongoing-control for data and autonomy-as-rational-deliberation for interventions?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely accommodates. Could the same architecture be justified by a relational or trust-based theory of consent, or does it depend specifically on treating biological data and bodily intervention as belonging to different moral categories? Finally, say what would have to change institutionally if we replaced the implicit theory with a unified standard—one on which consent is valid only when it is simultaneously specific about intended uses, fully informed about deception risks, continuously revocable without default bias, and assessed for competence in context.
