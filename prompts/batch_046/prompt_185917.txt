I hold that the appropriate equilibrium between citizen and state in information governance is neither the American model of sectoral openness coupled with private aggregation, nor the Chinese model of integrated visibility through social-credit architectures, but a corrected German-style regime of data minimization and purpose limitation—one reinforced by what I will call an epistemic firewall. On this view, the state may retain data for narrowly defined administrative ends, but is constitutionally prohibited from synthesizing cross-domain inferences (combining fiscal, biometric, and mobility records into a unified behavioral profile, for instance), even when technically feasible. The claim is that this structured opacity protects democratic agency not merely by hiding discrete facts, but by preserving interpretive gaps that prevent the state from knowing citizens in the totalizing fashion that undermines autonomous self-definition.

What would have to be true for this position to fail? I want the defeaters sorted by severity—from fatal to merely awkward—and distinguished according to whether a committed defender of the epistemic-firewall view would concede them or dismiss them. Which empirical developments in actual data-protection regimes (German or comparable) would count? Which conceptual arguments strike at the core—for instance, that cross-domain inference is indistinguishable from legitimate policy evaluation, that opacity merely displaces surveillance power to private intermediaries rather than constraining state power, or that democratic agency requires transparency rather than protected ignorance? Does the view generate predictions that rival models do not, and if those predictions fail, can the position be qualified away without cost? Finally, give an honest assessment of whether the framework risks unfalsifiability: if every practical failure is attributed to imperfect implementation rather than to the principle itself, is that insulation a defect for a normative framework of this kind?
