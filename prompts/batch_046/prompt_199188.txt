I am a philosophy student writing for a course on philosophy of mind and rational choice, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative slide in discussions of transformative experience. The pattern works like this: someone begins by noting the epistemic problem—that a person who has never been a parent, for instance, cannot phenomenally grasp what parenthood is like. From there, they quickly shift to claiming that the current self therefore has no rational authority over the decision, on the grounds that the post-transformation self will be so different that it is effectively a different person with different values. The move collapses the gap between not knowing what an experience feels like and not being the same decider after it happens. I have seen this in seminar discussions and in popular essays, but I do not know whether philosophers have named this specific conflation.

Please act as an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of personal identity. Before giving your answer, take a moment to brainstorm the separate conceptual spaces involved—epistemic access, personal identity over time, and rational authority across temporal stages. Then tell me: is there an established term for this exact slide from phenomenal ignorance to identity-based disenfranchisement of the present chooser? If there is, identify it and explain how it differs from the pattern I have described. If there is not, propose a precise name and definition, and clearly mark its boundaries: show how it differs from L.A. Paul’s epistemic transformation problem on the one hand, and from standard psychological-continuity or Parfit-style objections to future-directed choice on the other. I am not looking for a reading list, but for a conceptual carving that I can use in my paper.
