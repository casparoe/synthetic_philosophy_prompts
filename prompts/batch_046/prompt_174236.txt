You are advising a small research group with three years of funding that wants to improve long-range forecasting for large-scale governance institutions—specifically, how long structures resembling historical empires, federations, or supranational unions persist before fundamental transformation. Their work draws on reference-class forecasting, historical base rates of institutional duration, and critiques of inside-view overconfidence. You want them to focus on open questions that actually change forecast reliability rather than producing another catalog of imperial collapses.

Within this area, there is an underdeveloped methodological tension. Most existing historical datasets treat empire as a uniform functional category—multi-ethnic territorial hegemony—without disaggregating structural features such as fiscal federalism versus extractive centralization, or distinguishing decline driven by external conquest from decline driven by internal legitimacy collapse. At the same time, a newer but still niche proposal—reference-class contamination analysis—argues that standard empire-duration base rates are unreliable because the cases are not independent: they share hidden common causes, including diffusion of administrative practices, shared military technologies, and transregional climatic shocks. If true, this undermines the statistical foundations of naive outside-view reasoning.

Given this framing, identify the two or three most important open questions or lines of work for this forecasting program. Rank them by how much resolving each would improve the reliability of institutional-persistence forecasts, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability for a small team, how many downstream forecasting problems depend on the answer, and whether current practice is actively misleading in the absence of resolution. In particular, weigh whether disaggregating reference classes by institutional structure deserves more urgency than addressing reference-class contamination, and whether either problem renders base-rate reasoning inapplicable to modern governance institutions that may fall outside the historical reference class altogether. Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
