In his essay "Criteria of Negro Art," W. E. B. Du Bois is often read as reducing aesthetic value entirely to political efficacy on the grounds that all art is propaganda and ever must be—implying that beauty matters only as a vehicle for racial uplift and that a masterpiece wholly silent on injustice would be artistically worthless by his lights. But Du Bois also celebrates the spirituals and Sorrow Songs for a transcendent beauty that seems to operate independently of any explicit political message. Given this tension, does Du Bois actually treat beauty as a necessary but insufficient condition for art, with propaganda supplying the missing sufficient condition? Or does he offer a more integrated account in which beauty and propaganda are co-constitutive rather than competing criteria? Applying this to a hypothetical case—an abstract painting that achieves a genuinely sublime aesthetic effect yet remains entirely silent on race, injustice, and social struggle—would Du Bois deny it the status of art altogether, deem it incomplete, or judge it deficient in some other specific way? What principle best captures his actual position, and which of the apparent tensions must be resolved by overturning one of our initial intuitions about his view?
