I have recently been forced to confront the structure of my own practical failures in a way that makes the standard vocabulary of weakness of will feel inadequate. What I experience is not a single lapse of judgment but a recurring intertemporal negotiation in which my present self consistently outbids my future self, not through ignorance of the better option but through a temporal proximity that systematically devalues it — a pattern closer to Aristotelian akrasia reconceived through hyperbolic discounting than to any simple failure of resolve. I want a poem that enacts this structure rather than describing it.

Write a villanelle of nineteen lines, with two refrains that must rotate according to the form and appear together in the final couplet. The first refrain should be voiced by the present-choosing self and should express the immediate valuation that dominates at short delay; the second should be voiced by the future-regretting self and should express the delayed valuation that was authoritative before proximity collapsed it. Between them, the poem’s middle stanzas should trace the reversal: the moment when the same reward, moved closer in time, shifts its rank relative to the alternative, and the corresponding dissolution of the agent’s authority over her own future.

The rhyme scheme and refrain rotation should do conceptual work. The fact that the refrains keep returning but never resolve into a single voice should mirror the absence of a unified self that could adjudicate between temporal perspectives; the final couplet should lock the two refrains together in rhyme so that they sound like agreement but mean an irreconcilable split. Do not name-check philosophers or cite specific texts. Instead, embed precise conceptual distinctions — the defeat of an all-things-considered judgment by a present-biased desire, the preference reversal that occurs without any change in the underlying values, the bargaining failure between successive temporal agents — in concrete imagery and direct address. The tone should be intimate and rigorous, suitable for a private reflection that carries philosophical weight. No bibliographies, reading lists, or citations are desired.
