Consider the institutional design of contemporary digital mental-health platforms that combine clinical-style intake with passive behavioral tracking. In the clinical module, users complete standardized self-report questionnaires assessing anxiety or depression, and their first-person ratings are treated as the authoritative baseline; the operating norm is that an external observer should not dismiss a user’s subjective report on the grounds that the user alone has direct access to their own experience. In a parallel tracking module, the same platform continuously harvests behavioral proxies—sleep regularity, keystroke dynamics, social-interaction frequency—and runs correlation models against the self-reported scores. When the model detects divergence, for instance a user reporting low anxiety alongside behavioral signatures that match high-arousal patterns, the system issues a discrepancy alert, prompting the user to revise their self-assessment or seek external evaluation. The institution therefore encodes two simultaneous stances toward introspection: one that treats first-person access as incorrigible, and another that treats it as a fallible data stream correctable by third-person behavioral correlation.

What philosophical view of introspection, or of self-knowledge more broadly, does this dual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it depend on a distinction between different kinds of mental states (for example, occurrent feelings versus dispositional traits), or on a contextualist view about when first-person authority applies? Is the practice philosophically coherent, or does it quietly rely on inconsistent commitments—perhaps treating introspection as transparent in one domain and inferential in another? Distinguish what the design strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, and identify rival theories of introspective reliability that the institutional logic rules out. Finally, describe what would have to change in the platform’s clinical workflow and algorithmic architecture if its implicit view were replaced by a uniformly probabilistic model, or by a model that granted first-person reports unconditional authority across both modules.
