Please compare two frameworks for accounting for large-scale historical outcomes: first, the traditional notion of contingency, often captured by the proverb that traces the loss of a kingdom back to a missing horseshoe nail; second, the importation of mathematical chaos theory—specifically sensitive dependence on initial conditions—into historiography. In particular, examine whether chaos theory supplies a distinct causal mechanism or merely restates the older intuition in the language of deterministic nonlinear dynamics.

From a contingency perspective, this is a branching point: a single decision alters the course of events. From a chaos perspective, it is the visible result of an amplification of microscopic differences that were already present. Which framework offers the more illuminating analysis, and where exactly do they overlap or diverge?

Please distinguish clearly between what is settled—such as the point that chaos theory requires deterministic equations and is not equivalent to randomness—and what is genuinely contested among philosophers of history and scientists, especially the question of whether human societies can be treated as nonlinear dynamical systems possessing meaningful Lyapunov exponents. Do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. I am not seeking a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I want conceptual precision that can stand without reference to external sources.
