I'm trying to determine what I actually think about wrong-kind reasons by examining my intuitive verdicts across concrete cases. I would like to know whether a single principle can accommodate all of them, which verdicts I ought to abandon if they conflict, and what the best principle predicts for an unconsidered case. Please respond in British English.

1. A philosopher comes to believe in moral anti-realism primarily because doing so alleviates her guilt over past actions. Verdict: wrong kind of reason for belief.

2. A committee selects an algorithmic fairness criterion for hiring software not because it best tracks moral equality, but because it is the easiest to defend in court. Verdict: wrong kind of reason for normative selection.

3. The same committee selects a different criterion that is harder to defend precisely because, after analysis, it best tracks moral equality. Verdict: right kind.

4. An investor acquires a generative-art piece she finds aesthetically worthless solely because she expects its resale value to rise; she makes no aesthetic judgement at all. Verdict: not wrong kind — investment requires no aesthetic reason.

5. The same investor publicly praises the piece as 'profoundly moving' solely to inflate its market value. Verdict: wrong kind of reason for aesthetic judgement.

6. A parent teaches her child that lying is wrong solely by warning that liars are usually caught; she never appeals to harm, trust, or moral duty. Verdict: not wrong kind — instrumental instruction is legitimate.

The pair that troubles me is 2 and 6: in both, practical or institutional considerations override what seems like the 'proper' basis for the attitude. Is there a principled distinction — perhaps between domains such as belief, normative choice, aesthetic judgement, and practical education — that preserves all six verdicts? If not, state which one should be overturned. Finally, apply your best principle to this further case: a researcher concludes that a current large language model possesses phenomenal consciousness mainly because that conclusion would justify expanding her laboratory's grant funding.
