I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've assembled the following argument for a workshop on Malcolm's denial of dream experiences and Dennett's narrative-assembly account. The conclusion seems intolerable—it wipes out not only dream experiences but also well-documented cases of intraoperative suffering—yet each step looks defensible in isolation. I need help locating the exact fault, and whether fixing it saves a naturalistic account of dreaming or collapses into Malcolm's position.

Here is the argument:

(1) For any mental state M to count as an experience belonging to subject S, there must be, at the time M occurs, an occurrent phenomenal perspective that S possesses.
(2) During REM sleep, neural signatures associated with visual and emotional processing are present, but global workspace integration is suppressed; consequently, there is no unified phenomenal perspective available to belong to the sleeping subject at that time.
(3) So if there is phenomenal content during REM, it occurs without an occurrent owner.
(4) A phenomenal state that lacks an occurrent owner is not, strictly speaking, an experience; it is, at best, a sub-personal representational state.
(5) Therefore, REM sleep contains no experiences.
(6) Upon waking, subjects produce detailed reports describing rich phenomenal content that apparently occurred during REM.
(7) If (5) is true, these reports cannot be recollections of prior experiences; they must be retrospective constructions—what Dennett's cassette model describes.
(8) Now consider intraoperative awareness: during adequate general anesthesia, global workspace integration is pharmacologically suppressed, yet a subset of patients later report explicit, traumatic memories of surgical events.
(9) Applying the reasoning in (1) through (5) to these cases, such reports also cannot be recollections of experienced events; they must be post-hoc confabulations lacking phenomenal origin.
(10) But clinical practice and psychiatric follow-up treat intraoperative awareness as genuine experienced trauma, and denying its experiential status would undermine informed-consent protocols and post-operative care.
(11) Hence the reasoning in (1)-(5) proves too much: it denies not only dream experiences but also established cases of real suffering.
(12) So one of the premises, or an implicit inference between them, must be rejected.

My instinct is to preserve (1) and reject (4), allowing that a sub-personal representational state can be "owned" retrospectively once the global workspace resumes. But I am unsure whether that repair saves Dennett's position or inadvertently collapses it into Malcolm's denial of occurrent dream consciousness. I also suspect there may be a hidden modal or scope shift—perhaps between "no integrated workspace" and "no phenomenal owner"—that I have not named.

Please structure your response in the following sections, with approximate lengths:

1. Reconstruction and diagnosis (approx. 250 words): Identify the specific step—premise or inference—where the argument fails. If there is an equivocation, scope shift, or modal slide between steps, name it precisely and show how it operates.
2. Why the step seemed plausible (approx. 200 words): Explain why that premise or inference looks defensible given Malcolm's criterion for experience and the neuroscientific appeal to global workspace suppression during REM.
3. The price of rejecting it (approx. 200 words): What do we pay if we drop that step? Does it rescue dream experiences without proliferating "disowned" phenomenal states elsewhere? Does it affect our treatment of intraoperative awareness?
4. Alternative repairs and comparative costs (approx. 200 words): Briefly survey other premises one might reject—e.g., (1), (8), or (10)—and explain which repair is least damaging to a naturalistic philosophy of mind that takes retrospective report seriously but avoids environmental or neural determinism about consciousness.
