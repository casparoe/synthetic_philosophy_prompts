I am drafting the philosophical backbone for a novel about welfare, dignity, and the state, and before I commit these ideas to dialogue I want to know whether my own position collapses under inspection. Here is where I stand:

(1) Every person has a claim to a baseline of material security that does not depend on employment, contribution, or moral merit—an unconditional floor that the state must guarantee regardless of behavior.

(2) At the same time, I believe the state may legitimately require documented job-search or participation in training as a condition of receiving benefits beyond a brief transitional period, because reciprocity and visible contribution sustain the social solidarity that makes the floor politically durable.

(3) I regard the rhetorical category of the "undeserving poor" as ideologically toxic and insist it should be expunged from policy discourse; nevertheless, I hold that sound policy design must still distinguish unemployment caused by structural economic forces from unemployment caused by a persistent, avoidable refusal of genuinely available work.

(4) I take workfare to be ethically preferable to unconditional cash transfers, not for reasons of economic efficiency, but because it preserves the recipient's standing as a contributor rather than a mere dependent, and I treat this standing as having intrinsic, not merely instrumental, value.

(5) I am convinced that ordinary moral intuitions about "laziness" or "refusal to work" are so thoroughly corrupted by class prejudice, racialized narratives, and status anxiety that they should carry almost no weight in either policy design or moral judgment; structural analysis and procedural fairness should do all the normative work.

(6) Finally, I hold that benefit fraud is not merely a legal violation but a genuine moral breach of the reciprocity that sustains solidarity; however, I think its wrongness is strictly proportional to the direct material harm it causes other claimants, not to any abstract offense against the state's authority.

Do these commitments hang together? I want a rigorous audit, ordered by severity: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict and dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere. I am particularly unsure whether my rejection of the "undeserving poor" in (3) can survive my endorsement of conditionality in (2) and my preference for workfare in (4), given that (5) undermines precisely the judgments that (2) and (3) seem to require in practice. Please also tell me what would change your mind—what specific argument or consideration would lead you to judge this package more consistent, or more broken, than you initially think, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
