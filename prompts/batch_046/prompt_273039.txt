I am a doctoral student in philosophy of physics writing on the ontology of spacetime, and I am trying to work through a specific interpretive problem in Newton’s bucket argument as it appears in the Scholium to the Definitions of the Principia. In the passage, Newton imagines a bucket of water suspended by a twisted cord and released. At first the bucket spins while the water remains at rest relative to the surrounding bodies, and the water’s surface stays flat; then, as the bucket communicates motion to the water, they rotate together, and only then does the surface become concave, climbing up the sides. Newton takes this sequence to show that true circular motion—motion relative to absolute space—produces real dynamical effects that mere relative motion to nearby bodies does not, since the concavity appears only when the water shares the bucket’s absolute rotation, not during the initial relative rotation.

What I am struggling to understand is the exact inferential architecture of the argument, particularly a tension that seems to arise between Newton’s definitional framework and his explanatory claim. He defines motion as change of absolute place, yet he also maintains that absolute places and motions are not observable. In the bucket case, he appeals to the concavity—a manifestly observable, relational phenomenon between parts of the water and the container—to license an inference to an unobservable absolute rotation. My question is whether he is doing more than correlating an observable effect with an unobservable cause. Specifically, I want to understand whether his reasoning already presupposes a dynamical account of inertia in which centrifugal tendencies are literally caused by motion with respect to absolute space, rather than being kinematically derived from relations among bodies. If so, then when he argues that the concavity cannot be explained relationally, is he relying on the very substantiality of space he aims to establish, rather than deriving substantiality from the failure of relational explanation? Or is there a non-circular reading on which the concavity functions as an empirical marker that distinguishes two conceptually distinct kinds of motion—true and relative—without already embedding a causal story about space?

I would also like help clarifying Newton’s claim that the effects of true circular motion are distinguishable from those of relative circular motion. Should this be read as a conceptual point about the definition of motion, as an empirical claim about the presence or absence of forces like tension in the cord, or as something in between? Any guidance on how to parse the step from observed concavity to absolute rotation, without either begging the question in favor of substantivalism or collapsing the argument into a trivial definitional stipulation, would be especially useful.
