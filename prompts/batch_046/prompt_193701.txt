I retired from marine biology about six years back and since then I've gotten weirdly obsessed with philosophy - started with philosophy of science, then Hobbes, and now I'm in over my head. At my little reading circle (mostly other retirees, one was an engineer) we were arguing about Hobbes's views on geometery and whether they actually cohere with his materialism.

Marcus argued that for Hobbes geometry is the gold standard of science because every object - points, lines, circles - is literally constructed by us defining motion. You start with a point as the path of a body, build up figures, and since we authored the definitions we can never be wrong about the consequences; geometric proof is just computation with our own terms. Elena said that sounds like formalism or intuitionism, but Hobbes wasn't doing that - for him motion is physical, bodies are real, so geometry is still describing actual stuff in the world, not mental constructions. Marcus countered that yes, bodies move, but the triangle or circle only exists as a geometric object because we define it through that motion, so the truth is definitional rather than observational. Elena pushed back: if it's purely definitional, why did Hobbes spend decades failing to square the circle? If it's all in the definitions, failure should be conceptually impossible - which suggests he believed there was a real spatial fact he was trying to discover. Marcus said that's actually the opposite: Hobbes failed precisely because he treated a constructed problem as if it contained a hidden physical answer, which shows he was confused about geometry being about our constructions rather than nature. Elena replied that he's failing because he applied physical intuition where pure deduction was needed, not because he was too physicalist - and if geometry were purely definitional, there'd be nothing to fail at, just like you can't fail at defining a triangle.

They asked me to referee since I used to build models. I couldn't decide. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right at each step, not just who wins? Also, are they both missing something about how Hobbes's geometric constructivism connects to his idea that all reasoning is just computation? And who is closer to being right overall?
