My colleague teaches a seminar on classical Daoist philosophy and has ruled on a series of cases involving dream skepticism and the butterfly passage in Zhuangzi. Her judgments so far are these. First, on the butterfly passage itself, she rejects both the skeptical-epistemic reading that Zhuangzi merely lacks a criterion for waking versus dreaming, and the psychological reading that he is expressing anxiety about personal identity; she holds that the passage demonstrates the inadequacy of applying fixed self-categories to a continuous ontological transformation in which conventional distinctions between human and butterfly dissolve. Second, on a modern false-awakening scenario in which a sleeper dreams of waking and then actually wakes, she says the ambiguity is purely epistemic and is resolved by the physical continuity of the body, which serves as a conventional but stable anchor absent in Zhuangzi's case. Third, on lucid dreaming in which a sleeper knows she is dreaming yet remains fully immersed, she argues that awareness provides no Archimedean point, since consciousness itself is part of the transforming field rather than an observer outside it. Fourth, on a hypothetical in which two strangers wake with identical dream narratives despite no prior communication, she says this challenges the privacy of mental content but does not bear on the butterfly problem, because both subjects remain intact and only share experience. Fifth, on a clinical case of anesthesia awareness in which a patient reports that during the gap there was no one present to experience anything, she says this comes closest to Zhuangzi, since the absence of a continuous subject undermines the assumption that identity must be preserved across states.

Next term she will consider a new case: a neural-interface technology allows a sleeper to receive, in real time, the full sensory and affective stream of a butterfly, and upon waking the subject reports that she cannot determine whether she was the butterfly undergoing transformation or the butterfly was a projection of her own transformed state, and that she feels no stronger identification with her pre-sleep human self than with the butterfly. Predict her verdict. Is she running an ontology of continuous transformation, an epistemology of missing criteria, or a conceptual critique of fixed self-categories? Identify the closest analog among her earlier rulings and state honestly whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, noting what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify one further case that would best distinguish these three interpretations.
