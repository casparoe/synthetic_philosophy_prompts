I hold that comparing historical atrocities is not only permissible but obligatory for any moral theory that claims universality, and that the common charge of obscenity confuses the emotional difficulty of weighing suffering with an epistemic prohibition against weighing it. To avoid reductiveness, I propose that comparisons should be multi-dimensional, assessing not only magnitude but also institutional novelty (whether the cruelty required inventing new bureaucratic or technological forms), reversibility of damage, and the extent to which victims' own conceptual and cultural resources were destroyed; a single-axis ranking by death toll is indeed obscenely reductive, but comparison itself is not. What would count against this composite position? Please sort the defeaters by severity: conceptual arguments showing that multi-axis comparison merely relocates rather than resolves the offense; empirical or historical cases where comparison caused harm that outweighed its analytical value; formal results in moral psychology or philosophy of language suggesting that ordering evils necessarily corrupts the moral response they demand; and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, if any failure of comparison is dismissed as employing the wrong axis. Which of these would a defender of comparison concede as genuinely damaging, and which are external criticisms they would reject? I am especially interested in whether a principled boundary exists between comparing atrocities for analytical clarity and comparing them for moral judgment, and whether that boundary survives scrutiny.
