After a long and somewhat frustrating argument tonight with a friend about whether Pascal’s fragments on justice and force in the Pensées represent a cynical acceptance that might makes right or a theologically disciplined recognition that fallen human justice must operate through force, I am designing a final exam for my upper-level course on early modern philosophy and I need a question that forces students to do more than summarize the familiar claim that justice is whatever is established. I want one essay prompt that can be answered in about seventy-five minutes, carrying thirty marks, aimed at students who have read the relevant fragments but who should not be able to succeed by reciting them. The question should ask them to evaluate a specific interpretive claim: that Pascal’s distinction between the three orders—flesh, reason, and charity—together with his definition of tyranny as the attempt to obtain through one order what belongs properly to another, entails that any state-enforced distributive justice pursued in the name of charity is necessarily tyrannical, and that therefore Pascal’s framework either condemns all positive law as illegitimate or collapses into the rationalist moralism he rejects. To answer well, students must explain what the three orders are, show how force belongs to the order of reason or flesh but never to charity, apply the tyranny concept precisely, address whether custom can serve as a legitimate bridge between orders or merely masks violence, and resolve whether Pascal allows for a non-tyrannical state without abandoning his theological commitments. Please write the exam question, then provide a model answer of roughly seven hundred words in continuous prose—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables—written as in a formal letter, followed immediately by a marking scheme that divides the thirty marks into five specific moves, each worth six marks, with a brief note on the typical mistake that costs a student those marks. The scheme should make clear what separates an excellent answer from a merely good one: for instance, that an A answer must not only name the three orders but explain why distributive justice belongs to charity and therefore cannot be enforced by state power without tyranny, whereas a B answer might list the orders correctly but fail to apply the tyranny concept to distributive justice specifically. I do not want any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations in the question or answer; the reasoning should stand entirely on conceptual analysis that a student could perform without access to a library or the internet. Please also avoid putting anything in quotation marks that comes from an actual text unless you have verified the wording from a source, and do not depend on specific fragment numbers, edition details, or publication dates that might be misremembered; paraphrase freely instead. Present the entire response as continuous prose only, the way one writes a long email or letter, because I intend to copy this directly into my exam design document without reformatting.
