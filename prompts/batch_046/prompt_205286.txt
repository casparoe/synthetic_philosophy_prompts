I've been arguing with a friend that pure expected-value maximization is a rational requirement, not just a heuristic: if one action has even a vanishingly small chance of an astronomically valuable outcome and the other is certain but modest, the first is obligatory, and refusing it requires abandoning coherence over lotteries. I apply this specifically to collective-harm cases—climate mitigation, consumption choices—where an individual's causal contribution is near-zero, but the expected value of abstaining might still dominate if the harm is vast enough and the pivotal probability is nonzero. I want an honest map of what actually counts against this view. Which defeaters would be decisive—formal results showing pure EV violates rational-choice axioms under ambiguity, empirical evidence about how reasoning actually works, or structural features of collective action that break the value calculation—versus merely awkward, like the counterintuitiveness of Pascal-style mugging? I also want to know whether the position risks unfalsifiability: if defenders always rescue it by adjusting probability assignments or value functions, is that a legitimate defense or a sign the view is empty? Rank the exposure from fatal to embarrassing, say which ones a committed EV fanatic should themselves concede as damaging rather than misdirected, and tell me whether being well-insulated is a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind.
