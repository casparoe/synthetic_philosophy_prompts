I have recently become preoccupied with the question of personal persistence after observing my grandmother’s gradual loss of autobiographical memory to Alzheimer’s disease, which led me to study speculative neuro-replacement therapies. I am asking not for a bibliography, reading list, or citation of authorities, but for a conceptual exercise built around a precisely defined hypothetical. Imagine two research groups that have conducted a year-long adversarial collaboration about whether a human subject could survive the gradual, in-vivo replacement of every cortical neuron with a biomimetic silicon analog that replicates not only spike trains but also local field potentials, glial calcium-wave modulation, and activity-dependent synaptic plasticity rules—performed slowly enough that at no point does synthetic tissue exceed 0.1% of the brain’s volume. One camp, call them the Functional Continuists, holds that if first-person narrative access, psychological continuity, and behavioral coherence are maintained across the entire procedure, then the post-substitution subject is numerically identical to the original. The other camp, the Biological Processists, holds that numerical identity requires an unbroken chain of living biological causation and embodied metabolic integration; on their view, the procedure eventually produces a perfect behavioral and experiential duplicate but terminates the original subject somewhere in the middle, regardless of what the resulting entity reports.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after good-faith collaboration. First, provide a numbered section of agreed claims: about the neuroscience of the hypothetical substitution, about the logical structure of personal-identity conditions (for example, that the dispute is not settled by behavioral indistinguishability alone), about exactly what the procedure involves, and about which familiar analogies—such as Ship-of-Theseus-style replacements—both sides regard as genuinely inconclusive for this case. Second, present a disagreements table in which each camp states its own position in wording it would accept: precisely what “numerical identity” requires for them, whether substrate continuity or functional continuity is necessary and sufficient, and at what point—if any—they believe the original subject is lost. Third, identify the cruxes, the specific sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux is whether first-person reports of uninterrupted consciousness carry evidential weight in gradual substitution scenarios or are systematically unreliable; another is whether metabolic and causal biological continuity is a necessary condition for identity or merely a contingent feature of our current biology. For each crux, describe a concrete thought-experiment or hypothetical empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case or finding would show. Finally, if any portion of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive—perhaps involving different uses of “the same person” or “survival”—specify exactly which portion and why, using neutral wording that both sides could endorse.
