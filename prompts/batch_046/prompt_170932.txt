I hold a functionalist resolution to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Rather than identifying a shared phenomenal quality—some uniform hedonic tone—across all pleasures, I claim that pleasures are unified by a structural feature in the agent's evaluative architecture: each is a state in which a modality-specific reward-prediction discrepancy collapses to near-zero and remains stable against perturbation for a characteristic duration, producing what I call evaluative closure. On this view, tasting wine and solving a proof share no introspective similarity, yet both exhibit the same information-theoretic profile, a temporary local minimum in expected free energy within the agent's value system. The common element is therefore functional and detectable in principle through cross-modal suppression patterns, not through phenomenological comparison. This keeps hedonism intact without committing to a qualitative commonality, but it ties pleasure to a specific predictive architecture that may not extend across all sentient beings. Which rival account—phenomenal, attitudinal, or representational—would gain ground if this structural story failed, and does that failure undermine hedonism generally or only this variant? Which objections should a defender concede are genuinely damaging, and which can be absorbed by refining the closure condition? Rank the defeaters from fatal to inconvenient.
