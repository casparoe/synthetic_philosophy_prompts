As a novelist planning a multi-book project on Black intellectual life between the wars, I am treating Du Bois's insistence that art must be propaganda for justice not as a settled slogan but as an unsettled craft problem. I see three competing lines of work that could determine how the novels are built, and I need to know where limited creative energy should go. First, does propaganda operate primarily through explicit thematic message and plot, or can it be structurally embedded—through narrative voice, temporal ordering, and the politics of attention—without collapsing fiction into tract? Second, does Du Bois's universalist claim that Black art reveals universal human truths conflict with his particularist demand for racial justice, and if these pull in opposite directions in practice, which should govern a novelist's priorities? Third, is there a live tension between the propaganda thesis and the autonomy of aesthetic form—irony, ambiguity, refusal of closure—and does resolving that tension matter more for political efficacy than resolving the universalism-particularism conflict? Rank these for a writer with roughly one career's span of books: which is upstream, which is tractable through drafting practice versus conceptual clarification, and what finished work—or demonstrable failure—would cause you to reorder them? I want a defended agenda with explicit criteria—importance to the project's integrity, tractability within novel-writing, and how many other craft choices depend on the answer—not a survey and not a reading list.
