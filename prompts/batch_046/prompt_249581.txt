I have recently begun volunteering with a digital humanities initiative that is constructing structured metadata for a collection of oral histories recorded in a small, poorly documented language, and to understand the theoretical stakes of our tagging decisions I have been studying Quine's discussion of radical translation and the gavagai problem. At a workshop last week, two of my colleagues—Nadia and Elias—entered into a sustained disagreement about whether Quine's argument actually undermines our practical work.

Nadia maintained that the indeterminacy of translation is not merely an epistemic limitation but an ontological obstacle: if a speaker assents to the term we gloss as gavagai in the presence of a running rabbit, the translation manual could consistently assign it to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal stages of rabbits, and no additional behavioral evidence can settle the matter because any discrepancy can be absorbed by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses about reference and individuation. She added that in our specific archive the difficulty is deepened by the language's lack of explicit morphological distinction between singular and plural reference, which means we cannot even stabilize whether a speaker is tracking one enduring entity or a scattered plurality, so any ontology we embed in our metadata is an arbitrary imposition rather than a recovered structure.

Elias responded that Nadia is misreading Quine as licensing global skepticism, when in fact Quine was a naturalist who held that we simply adopt whatever ontology best serves our overall theoretical economy. For archival tagging, he argued, we should select the ontological framework that maximizes the narrative coherence of the testimonies—if treating references as enduring objects yields smoother cross-referencing across recordings, then that is the correct translation not because we have discovered a unique metaphysical truth, but because pragmatic success is the only available criterion. Nadia countered that Elias's appeal to coherence already smuggles in an unexamined metaphysics of persistence and identity, since whether an interrupted appearance counts as the same thing returning or as a distinct stage depends precisely on the ontological choice that is supposed to be in question; therefore the pragmatic strategy does not resolve the indeterminacy but merely relocates it behind a preference for continuity. Elias then charged that Nadia is demanding a God's-eye standpoint that Quine explicitly rejects, and that insisting on such an impossible standard before acting amounts to the very philosophical paralysis that a naturalized epistemology ought to avoid.

They turned to me to referee, and I genuinely could not determine who had the stronger position.
