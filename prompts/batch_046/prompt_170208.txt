Consider the following case for analysis. Two senior editors at a small literary magazine—who have worked together for a decade and regard one another as equals in critical judgment—are deadlocked over whether to publish a controversial essay. Editor A is convinced the essay’s central argument relies on a misinterpretation of its primary source; Editor B is equally convinced that A’s reading is overly literal and misses the author’s ironic intent. They have shared every annotation, reviewed the source together, and agree that neither holds private evidence, yet they remain firmly opposed. Complicating matters, each privately suspects that the other is not reasoning differently but is instead allowing an unstated aesthetic preference—say, a taste for provocation versus a taste for scholarly caution—to drive the evaluation, though neither can point to any specific inference that exposes this bias. They ask you whether rationality requires them to split the difference and publish with a disclaimer, or whether one may justifiably stand firm and block publication. Analyze the structure of their disagreement: does the possibility that their divergence stems from a hidden values-conflict rather than an epistemic error affect whether conciliation or steadfastness is appropriate? And if they cannot determine whether they are facing peer disagreement or mere preference divergence, what should they do?
