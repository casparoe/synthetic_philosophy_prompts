You are grading a take-home exam for an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar. The exam asked:

"Frank Jackson's Mary argument is standardly met by the phenomenal concept strategy (PCS): Mary learns something upon leaving her monochrome room, but only acquires a new phenomenal concept, not knowledge of a non-physical fact. Evaluate whether this response succeeds. In particular, address whether accepting PCS forces the physicalist to treat phenomenal concepts as conceptually isolated from physical-functional concepts, and whether that isolation undermines the physicalist's broader explanatory ambitions—especially if one grants that other subjective domains (e.g., spatial or temporal indexicals) do not generate analogous isolation problems."

A student submitted the following answer (edited lightly for grammar):

"Jackson's Mary is a neuroscientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has lived only in black and white. When she sees a red tomato, she learns what it is like to see red. Jackson takes this to show physicalism is false, since a complete physical description should already include everything Mary knew.

The phenomenal concept strategy replies that Mary acquires a phenomenal concept of redness, not a new fact—much as someone might learn the concept 'water' without learning that water is H2O. The underlying reality stays physical; only the mode of presentation changes. So physicalism survives.

Still, I think PCS ultimately fails. First, it collapses into the ability hypothesis defended by Nemirow and Lewis: saying Mary gains a 'phenomenal concept' is just another way of saying she gains the practical ability to recognize and imagine red experiences. Once we see that PCS is really the ability hypothesis in different vocabulary, Jackson's core point returns—physical descriptions still leave out something essential about experience.

Second, Jackson's argument is circular because he assumes from the start that phenomenal properties are not physical; otherwise Mary could not learn anything genuinely new. The physicalist can reject that assumption outright. Therefore Mary's case does not establish dualism. It only shows that physical descriptions are incomplete in a way that was already presupposed."
