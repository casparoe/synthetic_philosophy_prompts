I am preparing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on the mechanics by which societies abandon practices they once treated as indispensable. I want to compare two canonical collapses: the disappearance of formal dueling among European elites during the long nineteenth century, and the abolition of Atlantic chattel slavery in roughly the same era.

My working framework includes three established explanations. First, there is Bernard Williams's thesis that moral revolutions are driven not primarily by rational moral argument but by shifts in the social distribution of honor and standing. Second, there is Kwame Anthony Appiah's account, in which moral revolutions occur when the social code of honor that sustained a practice is redirected, so that continuing the practice becomes a source of shame rather than prestige. Third, there is an institutional-interest account emphasizing state capacity, legal prohibition, and the alignment of elite economic incentives.

To this I want to add a fourth variable that philosophical discussions rarely isolate: normative inertia, by which I mean the structural resistance to abandoning a practice that is embedded in legal architecture, intergenerational status transmission, and ritual repetition, even after both honor and material interest have turned against it.

Please complete the following evaluations.

First, assign percentage weights totaling one hundred for the explanatory importance of the four factors—Williams-style honor shift, Appiah-style honor redirection, state-capacity and economic alignment, and normative inertia—in accounting for the rapid decline of dueling without a massive state enforcement campaign.

Second, assign percentage weights totaling one hundred for the same four factors in accounting for the long persistence of chattel slavery despite sustained moral criticism, followed by its relatively compressed collapse once abolition gained state backing.

Third, rate each factor on a scale of one to ten for its ability to explain the asymmetry between the two cases—specifically, why dueling faded as elite status codes changed, whereas slavery required sustained coercive restructuring to dismantle.

Fourth, place the honor-based cluster (combining the Williams and Appiah factors) and the institutional cluster (combining state-capacity and normative inertia) on a single tier list—S, A, B, C, or D—for general explanatory power across moral revolutions. Provide a brief justification that references the comparative results above.

Please avoid bibliographies, page references, or reading lists; the feature is intended for an audience without library access, and I need conceptual clarity rather than scholarly apparatus.
