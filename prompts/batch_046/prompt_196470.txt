Having spent a career in experimental physics before turning to philosophy, I find myself treating social norms as if they were boundary conditions in a coordination problem, and I have constructed the following argument about everyday conventions—tipping, queuing, observing local jaywalking norms—which now strikes me as proving far more than I intend.

(1) Where a practice P is a stable equilibrium of mutual expectation within a community C, an act that violates P predictably erodes the expectations sustaining P, thereby imposing a coordination cost on members of C.

(2) If an agent can avoid imposing a predictable coordination cost on others without comparable sacrifice, then failing to avoid it is pro tanto morally wrong.

(3) A visitor to C can comply with P—whether leaving a tip, joining a queue, or refraining from crossing against local custom—without comparable sacrifice.

(4) Therefore a visitor who violates P acts pro tanto wrongly.

(5) By parity of reasoning, if a local custom is arbitrary but equally stable—imagine a town where residents habitually exchange three brief bows on meeting, with no further functional purpose—then violating it also erodes mutual expectation and imposes coordination costs.

(6) Hence visitors are morally required to observe even arbitrary local customs, provided only that they are stable equilibria.

(7) That conclusion is clearly excessive: moral obligation does not extend to every stable local quirk.

(8) So at least one of (1) through (3) must be rejected.

I am inclined to preserve (1) and (3), but I want to know what repairing the chain costs. Is the flaw a hidden equivocation on “cost,” sliding between material harm and mere disappointed expectation? Or does the argument illicitly move from a negative duty not to disrupt an embedded practice to a positive duty to conform, ignoring what I have tentatively called a reciprocity gradient—the idea that the moral weight of a convention scales with the depth of reciprocal reliance it supports, not with stability alone? In other words, which step actually breaks, and what do I forfeit by fixing it there?
