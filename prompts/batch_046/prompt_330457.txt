In debates over American exceptionalism, I have watched a particular stall recur for years between two camps I will call the institutional comparativists and the civic-mythology hermeneuts. The comparativists treat exceptionalism as an empirical historical thesis: they argue that once you control for settler-colony resource endowments, weak aristocratic inheritance, and early universal white male suffrage, American institutional development tracks closely with other Anglo-settler societies, and they now press the point using synthetic-control methods that construct counterfactual stability paths for constitutional design. The hermeneuts respond that this misses the phenomenon entirely, because exceptionalism was never a claim about comparative sociology but about a reflexive constitutional practice—specifically, that American actors repeatedly redesigned institutions in explicit reference to a self-interpreted mission of liberal-republican uniqueness, a process they call operating under the reflexivity clause. The comparativists then charge that the reflexivity clause is unfalsifiable: any institutional outcome can be narrated post hoc as self-interpretation. The hermeneuts reply that falsifiability is the wrong standard for a hermeneutic object, and the cycle repeats with no movement. I am inclined to think this is not a hidden factual dispute but a disagreement about what counts as an explanation in political history—whether causal accounts must isolate material variables or may treat self-interpretive practices as primitive explanatory terms—or possibly that the two sides are answering different questions (one about institutional variance, one about meaning-formation) under the single heading “exceptionalism.” Diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually says: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of answering different questions under one name? If there is a hidden formal or empirical issue doing the work, identify it; if the stall reflects bedrock intuitions about naturalness or causation that neither side can argue for, say so. Finally, tell me what would count as genuine progress—an empirical result, a conceptual distinction, a change in explanatory standards, or a reframing of the question—and whether, given your diagnosis, any such progress should be expected.
