I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent research fellowship through a small public humanities foundation. I have no formal training in philosophy or sociology, but I have been thinking on my own about whether generational categories—Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z—are real in any interesting sense, or whether they are merely arbitrary marketing and demographic bins. I need the proposal to be rigorous and precise, not a culture-war opinion piece.

Here is my half-formed idea. The standard debate frames generations as either natural kinds, like biological species, or pure social constructions, like fashion trends. I think this misses a middle possibility that I am tentatively calling "synchronized expectation clusters." On this view, a generation is not defined by birth years alone, but by a cohort's shared exposure to a structural shock—for example, entering adulthood during a widespread technological shift or a severe financial crisis—at a life-stage where expectations about work, family, or consumption are being formed. The shock disrupts those expectations, and the institutions the cohort then enters adapt around them, creating a feedback loop in which the label reshapes the behavior it describes. I want to investigate whether this loop gives the cluster enough explanatory stability and non-arbitrary boundaries to count as a weak but genuine kind, or whether it merely makes a useful fiction self-fulfilling.

Please write the proposal. It should include: a sharply stated research question with two or three competing answers clearly distinguished; an explanation of why the natural-kind versus social-construction framing fails to resolve the issue; a month-by-month method for the six months that relies on conceptual analysis and publicly observable demographic patterns rather than specialized empirical fieldwork or literature reviews I cannot conduct; a description of what a successful result would look like—perhaps a taxonomy that distinguishes strong generational realism, weak institutional realism, and anti-realism—and what specific considerations would decide between them; an honest assessment of what could cause the project to fail or outgrow a six-month scope; and one genuinely novel conceptual angle or distinction—not drawn from well-known named theories—that moves the argument beyond the standard sociology. I will add citations myself, so please do not include a bibliography or reading list. The tone should be formal and polished, suitable for a foundation review panel that is not composed of professional philosophers.
