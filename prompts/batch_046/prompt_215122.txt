I'm leading a single fifty-minute graduate seminar on iterated elimination via dominance in mixed strategies, aimed at philosophy students who have seen pure-strategy dominance but treat mixing as a psychological fallback rather than as a strategic object in its own right. I need the explanatory arc, not the lecture itself. First, the dependency order: what conceptual layer must be in place before the students can see why dominance over beliefs about mixtures is irreducible to dominance over actions—specifically, why the elimination sequence operates on strategy sets rather than on outcomes, and where rationalizability enters as the fixed-point residue of common belief in rationality rather than as a convergence guarantee to uniqueness. Second, the carrying example: design one concrete game—more structured than matching pennies but simpler than a full auction—with a decoy strategy that survives first-round intuition yet collapses under second-order belief revision, and specify exactly what properties it must possess (observable payoffs, transparent mixing probabilities, a clearly non-singleton rationalizable set) and what it must lack (hidden information, continuous strategy spaces, evolutionary dynamics). Third, the misconception map: they will treat the elimination order as temporal reasoning about others learning in sequence; they will conflate a mixed strategy with epistemic uncertainty about one's own choice; and they will assume rationalizability always collapses to a unique prediction. For each, specify the precise point in the arc where it should be intercepted and how. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question at the end that distinguishes genuine understanding of the rationalizable set from mere recitation of the elimination algorithm. Finally, state explicitly which formal apparatus—say, the epistemic hierarchy of beliefs about beliefs or the measure-theoretic framing of strategy spaces—I should acknowledge exists but refuse to develop at this level, and justify that omission in pedagogical terms.
