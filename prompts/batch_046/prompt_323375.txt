I'm studying the role of common knowledge of rationality in sequential epistemic games—specifically whether the formal requirement that rationality be common knowledge at every node is a substantive constraint on reasoning or merely a modeling convention. On one side, formalists treat common knowledge as a structural precondition built into the game tree: if agents are rational, the tree itself encodes that each player knows the others won't choose dominated strategies, which is why backward induction holds in finite games like the centipede game. On the other side, processualists argue that common knowledge is an empirical achievement that fails in practice because real agents don't have transparent access to others' reasoning processes, so backward induction describes an idealized limit rather than actual strategic reasoning. Despite decades of agreement on the underlying mathematics, neither camp has moved: formalists say the processualists are confusing model with reality, and processualists reply that the formalists have made rationality unfalsifiable by definition.

I've started wondering whether this same structure appears in a newer context that might illuminate it: multi-agent systems composed of large language models, where each agent can observe outputs but not the internal reasoning weights of others. In such setups, second-order beliefs about rationality must be formed from behavioral traces rather than shared structural guarantees, which seems to make common knowledge genuinely fragile rather than conventionally assumed. I don't want to conflate the synthetic case with the human one, but I wonder if the analogy reveals that the original debate is not about human psychology at all, but about whether formal epistemology should treat transparency of reasoning as a default condition. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, empirical, methodological, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about what a model is for? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an empirical study of human reasoning transparency, a formal result showing that backward induction survives under partial opacity, a distinction that dissolves the disagreement, or something else? Please also say what argument or finding would change your diagnosis, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail—particularly if the multi-agent analogy turns out to be a category mistake rather than a useful probe.
