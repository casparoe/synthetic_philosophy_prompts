I've been reading a ton about the modern Stoic revival — all these apps and books framing Epictetus as basically ancient CBT. The analogy people keep leaning on is that the dichotomy of control is just the psychologial 'locus of control' idea: there's an internal circle (your judgments, intentions) and an external circle (other people, weather, your body), and therapy is essentially teaching you to stop trying to control the outside circle. Someone in my philosophy group said this mapping is almost perfect and that Stoicism is therefore redunant, just CBT with Latin names.

But then I found this older distinction in Epictetus that doesn't get talked about in the modern revival — he actually splits things into three zones, not two: fully in our power, fully outside it, and partly in our power (like health or reputation, where we have influence but not full command). Modern CBT doesn't really have a middle gear; it's either 'restructure the cognition' or 'accept and defuse.' Meanwhile Stoicism treats that middle zone as where virtue actually gets tested, not just a place to practice acceptance. So I'm wondering: if we map Stoicism onto CBT's internal/external locus, what exactly corresponds to what? Where does that middle zone go — does it vanish, does it get shoved into one bin or the other, and does that break the analogy's usefulness? Also, CBT's end-goal is reducing emotional distress, but Stoicism aims at something like perfecting your prohairesis or living in accordance with nature. Does the analogy quietly smuggle in a therapeutic, feel-better framing that Stoicism wouldn't recognize? And can it still support the claim that Stoicism is redundant, or do the disanalogies in the middle zone and the different end-goal mean the analogy carries weight it shouldn't?
