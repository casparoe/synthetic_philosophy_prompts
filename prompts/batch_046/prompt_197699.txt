I am drafting a short essay for a graduate seminar in metaethics and moral psychology, and I would welcome your critical comments on its structure, accuracy, and philosophical interest. The essay takes up the familiar body of work on cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgments—particularly the documented divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations in the moral-psychology literature—and asks what, if anything, this variation implies for moral objectivity. Rather than rehearsing the standard dialectic in which variation threatens realism and realism responds with error theory or relativism, I propose a different framing. I distinguish two senses in which moral judgment might claim objectivity. The first, which I call representational objectivity, holds that the truth of moral claims depends on mind-independent moral properties that cognition aims to track. The second, which I call stabilizing objectivity, holds that moral judgments are objective insofar as they converge under conditions of full non-moral information and mutual justification, regardless of whether there exist corresponding moral properties in the world. I then argue that the cross-cultural data undermine representational objectivity only if one assumes that moral cognition functions primarily as a tracking mechanism; but if moral cognition is instead understood as a mechanism for coordinating social expectations across variable ecological conditions, then the observed variation is not merely compatible with objectivity but is predicted by a stabilizing account. The niche claim I want to defend is that stabilizing objectivity does not collapse into mere intersubjective agreement, because it includes a counterfactual constraint: judgments must survive reflective equilibrium among culturally diverse interlocutors who share access to the same empirical and logical resources. I want the essay to avoid both the facile conclusion that WEIRD psychology proves moral relativism and the defensive move that all observed variation is superficial noise masking universal principles.

I would appreciate comments on three points in particular. First, is the distinction between representational and stabilizing objectivity genuinely coherent, or does the latter smuggle in the very correspondence relation it claims to reject? Second, have I mischaracterized the psychological literature—specifically, is it accurate to say that cross-cultural studies reveal divergence at the level of particular judgments (for instance, about harm, loyalty, or purity) without necessarily showing divergence in the underlying functional architecture of moral cognition? Third, what misconception should I anticipate and defuse in the essay itself? I am especially concerned that a reader might object that stabilizing objectivity is simply a relabeling of constructivism, or that it cannot account for the normative force we attach to moral judgments. If you think either objection is fatal, I would like to know where in the sequence of the argument—before the distinction is introduced, during its application to the data, or in the conclusion—it should be addressed, and what example or thought experiment might best expose the difference between my view and the constructivist alternative. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in whether the conceptual architecture holds together, not in citations I should add.
