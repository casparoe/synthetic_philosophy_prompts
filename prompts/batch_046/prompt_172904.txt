I serve on the allocations committee of a mid-sized humanitarian foundation that distributes approximately thirty million dollars annually across emergency relief and preventive infrastructure. We have adopted Peter Singer's principle that if we can prevent something very bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it, and we apply it specifically to life-saving interventions. Our board, however, has noticed a recurring distortion that is not quite the familiar psychological-distance objection. In our portfolio, proposals involving discrete, identifiable victims in acute crisis—flood rescues, surgical interventions for named individuals—receive overwhelming donor and committee support, even when the cost per statistical life saved is very high. Meanwhile, proposals addressing what I will call structural drowning—continuous, distributed harm in which no single moment or agent constitutes a rescue event, such as reinforcing sanitation infrastructure in a region where chronic contamination kills at a rate equivalent to a daily mass drowning—are rejected not on cost-effectiveness grounds but on the ground that they do not instantiate the moral structure of rescue at all. The harm is real, the preventability is real, yet the narrative form is absent.

Please construct an operational decision procedure my committee can run on every proposal, grounded in Singer's framework but explicitly handling the distinction between episodic rescue (a discrete event with an identifiable victim and a potential rescuer) and structural prevention (continuous harm with statistical victims and no singular rescue moment). The procedure should specify, in ordered steps: (1) how to verify that a proposal genuinely prevents serious harm without a comparable moral sacrifice; (2) how to classify a proposal along the episodic-structural spectrum; (3) whether Singer's principle applies with equal moral force across that spectrum, or whether the absence of a rescue narrative legitimately modifies the weight of the duty; (4) under what conditions donor narrative coherence may be permitted to influence allocation without violating the principle; and (5) the precise point at which the procedure must halt and defer to a value judgment rather than yielding a calculable answer.

Apply the procedure to three cases. Case A: funding airlifts for thirty identifiable children in an active conflict zone at a cost of two million dollars. Case B: funding a ten-year sanitation program that would statistically prevent an equivalent number of child deaths at one-tenth the cost, but with no identifiable beneficiaries and no discrete rescue event. Case C: a hybrid proposal—vaccinating a specific, named cohort of two hundred children in a region where the vaccine also eliminates a structural transmission vector—where the rescue narrative and structural prevention overlap but do not fully coincide. For each case, indicate where the procedure yields a clear directive, where it requires an explicit judgment call that must be recorded as such, and what the committee should document to remain consistent with Singer's underlying logic even if it departs from a pure cost-effectiveness ranking.
