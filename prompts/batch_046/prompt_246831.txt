As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest, I find myself with a set of intuitions about Maxwell’s demon and the physical status of information that I cannot reconcile into a single framework. I present five variant cases and my judgments on them, hoping that a coherent principle can be extracted, even if it forces me to revise one or two verdicts. First, in Maxwell’s original scenario, a being sorts fast and slow molecules using knowledge of their velocities; my judgment is that no second-law violation occurs only if we treat the storage of that velocity data as a physical process that itself generates entropy in the demon’s memory substrate. Second, consider a quantum variant in which the demon acquires velocity information via a non-demolition measurement that avoids direct thermal coupling to the gas; I still believe an entropic cost is unavoidable, but now it resides in the quantum correlation established between the demon’s state and the particles, not in any local heating of an apparatus. Third, apply the standard erasure argument: resetting a memory register to a standard state is thermodynamically costly; my intuition is that the cost attaches specifically to logical irreversibility—the destruction of prior distinguishable states—not to the act of writing a new bit. Fourth, imagine a demon implemented entirely on a reversible computing architecture that never performs a fixed erasure step, instead cycling information through a closed loop of logically invertible gates; here my intuition wavers, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the standard information-cost resolution fails or whether I am missing a hidden correlation that still carries the entropy. Fifth, suppose one proposes that the entropic cost of storing information should be weighted by its algorithmic compressibility, so that a highly regular velocity pattern costs less to record than random noise; I reject this, because I take thermodynamic cost to track physical degrees of freedom occupied, not the logical redundancy of the representation. Is there a precise principle—perhaps concerning the physical instantiation of logical distinctions, or the entropy of correlations between system and observer—that accommodates the first three judgments and clarifies the fourth? Which of my five verdicts must be abandoned for consistency, and in particular, what is the correct analysis of the fully reversible case in the fourth scenario?
