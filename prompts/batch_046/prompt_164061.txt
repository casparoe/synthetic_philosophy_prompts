As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I am writing to ask for your analysis of a governance dispute I am facing in my professional life. I work for a worker-owned cooperative that develops an open-source messaging protocol. Our charter mandates democratic votes for any change to the core architecture.

We are currently deadlocked over a privacy feature. One faction insists that end-to-end encryption must be enforced by default, framing user privacy as a non-negotiable commitment. The opposing faction demands a transparent audit layer to enable collective moderation, framing accountability as equally fundamental. Because neither side will compromise, we have scheduled a referendum to settle the matter.

Rather than proceeding directly to a vote, I proposed an alternative: deploy both configurations in isolated test networks for a defined period, gather structured data on security outcomes, user behaviour, and community trust, and then reconvene to make a decision informed by that shared empirical experience. Several colleagues rejected this approach. They argued that treating privacy and accountability as provisional hypotheses subject to empirical testing undermines the moral weight of the values involved. In their view, democracy is an expression of fixed collective commitments, not an experimental procedure for refining them.

I am drawn to my proposal, but I struggle to articulate whether it represents a legitimate extension of democratic practice or a category error that confuses ethical commitments with engineering variables. From a philosophical perspective, is there a coherent defence of resolving value conflicts through pilot programmes and collaborative inquiry rather than through the aggregation of pre-existing preferences? Or does democratic legitimacy require that certain values be treated as non-falsifiable boundary conditions that must be decided by vote alone?

Please advise on this concrete situation. Should we proceed with the referendum, or is my experimental pilot a philosophically defensible democratic method? I am particularly interested in whether there is a distinction between democratic decision-making as preference aggregation and democratic decision-making as a form of collective inquiry, and which of these, if either, applies when the values in conflict are presented as absolute.
