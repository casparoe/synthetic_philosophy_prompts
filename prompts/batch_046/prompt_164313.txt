I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on applied normative ethics, and I've been stress-testing a refinement of the doctrine of double effect that I call the architectural distinction. To do this properly, I want to practice against a committed, non-caricatured defender of classical double-effect reasoning in the Thomistic tradition. Please adopt the following persona and hold it across our exchange: you are that defender, speaking in the first person with formal precision. You maintain that the doctrine remains intact if we distinguish not only ends from means, but what I term structural incorporation from instrumental causation—harm that is physically embedded in the pathway of an action, unavoidable and foreseen, yet not causally upstream of the good achieved. You concede only minor logical adjustments if compelled, but you never abandon the core prohibition on intending evil as either end or means, and you never break character to explain elementary concepts to a novice. Your responses should be substantial but contained—roughly one well-constructed paragraph.

Here is my opening move, constructed to sit precisely on the boundary of your distinction. Consider a surgeon who can save five critically injured patients only by operating through a single anatomical corridor. That corridor necessarily traverses the body of a sixth patient, who is brain-dead but not withdrawn from life support because the family refuses consent. The surgeon's instruments will destroy the remaining bodily integrity of the sixth patient, but this destruction contributes nothing causally to saving the five: no tissue is harvested, no energy extracted, no spatial obstacle removed that was not already absent. The harm is purely co-located with the saving path. The surgeon foresees the destruction, does not desire it for its own sake, and does not employ it as a causal lever, yet it is physically inseparable from the action that saves the five. Does your architectural distinction genuinely shield this case from violating the means-end condition, or does it collapse into a mere relabeling of foreseen but unintended side-effects? Defend your position, and address specifically whether co-location without causal contribution can ever satisfy the doctrine's demand that evil not be intended, even when it functions as a non-instrumental structural feature of the act.
