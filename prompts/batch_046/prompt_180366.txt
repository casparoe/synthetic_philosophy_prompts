I spent three decades in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and for the past two years I have been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy—mostly philosophy of science and decision theory—to understand how formal models relate to the intuitions I developed in the laboratory. Last month a discussion of Maurice Allais’s paradox left me dissatisfied: the independence axiom was presented as an elegant formal constraint, but none of the students seemed to feel why violating it should be troubling, or why the Allais choices feel so natural despite that violation. I want to design a workshop that corrects that gap for a small group.

Could you design a one-hour, highly structured session for about twelve participants—mostly scientists or engineers without formal economics training, meeting in a seminar room with a whiteboard and envelopes of play-money—that makes them experience the pull of the independence axiom and then run into its violation themselves? I imagine a two-round game with real, small stakes: each participant begins with ten tokens exchangeable at the end for a modest gift card. In round one, each person privately selects between paired retirement-security contracts: one offering a guaranteed payout and one offering a riskier structure with higher upside, framed so that risk-averse judgment feels entirely reasonable. In round two, the same underlying probabilities appear with a common consequence removed, though participants must not be told the equivalence in advance. After both rounds, a collective tally reveals that the group’s aggregate choices display the classic Allais pattern. The rules should be arranged so that participants discover the contradiction through interaction—perhaps by defending their round-two choice in pairs and realizing the defense applies equally to round one, or by betting on group consistency—before the facilitator ever names the axiom.

Then I need a debrief of no more than fifteen minutes that maps each stage onto the formal structure: the common-consequence effect, the separation of probabilities, and the curvature of utility that might rationalize the shift. The debrief should be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, that it aggregates discrete individual choices rather than tracking sequential reasoning within a single agent over time, and that token stakes are not life-altering risks—so that I can supply that missing normative force verbally without lecturing. Please provide the exact payoff tables, the instructions given at each stage, predictions of how participants will try to outguess the consistency check once they suspect a hidden pattern, and guidance for handling that. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want a runnable protocol I can conduct next Tuesday.
