I have been reading, in my own time and without any formal academic background, about why ordinary people take part in mass violence—specifically Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen’s study of ordinary Germans during the Holocaust. The usual summary—“Browning blames the situation, Goldhagen blames the culture”—feels too neat to me. I keep returning to a more awkward possibility: that the bureaucratic machinery did not merely pressure neutral people, but actively selected for particular dispositions and then supplied the language, the incremental steps, and the institutional cover that turned those dispositions into lethal action. I want to test whether this “selection and amplification” idea actually holds up, rather than just feeling clever.

Please write a formal, upper-level undergraduate exam essay question—answerable in about ninety minutes and in roughly 800 words—that does not ask for a simple comparison of the two authors. Instead, it should force the student to examine exactly this integration problem. The question must require the student to:

1. Set out Browning’s explanation and Goldhagen’s explanation with enough precision to show how each accounts for evidence that the other struggles with;
2. Evaluate whether a “selection and amplification” framework genuinely resolves their disagreement, using one concrete piece of evidence from each author to support the evaluation;
3. Identify one serious objection that remains even if the integration is accepted;
4. Argue whether that remaining objection forces us to abandon one of the two frameworks, or whether it can be resolved by refining the other rather than discarding it.

Please provide a model answer of about 700 words, written in British English (using British spelling and terminology—e.g., behaviour, analyse, centre), that demonstrates what a first-class response would look like. In particular, the model answer should confront the “selection versus socialisation” ambiguity explicitly rather than glossing over it, and should show how each author handles cases of refusal or non-participation.

Then provide a marking scheme, also in British English, out of 20 marks, that separates an A-grade answer from a B-grade answer. The scheme should name five specific moves that a top answer must contain, assign marks to each, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong on each point. An A-grade answer must explicitly name the ambiguity and show how Browning and Goldhagen each treat refusal or non-participation; a B-grade answer should be defined as one that treats the two authors as flat contradictions without engaging the integration or the ambiguity.

Please do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not require the student to cite page numbers—the task is conceptual analysis, not source-hunting. Write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme entirely in British English.
