I'm trying to work out whether karma, taken seriously as a theory of moral desert rather than just causality, can survive being pushed past the usual Sunday-school examples. Here are my gut verdicts.

One: a dictator dies peacefully at 95, never tried, surrounded by family. I think he just got away with it — any theory that says he'll pay in the next rebirth feels like moving the goalposts, not serving desert.

Two: someone spends a decade quietly sabotaging coworkers, never caught, but lives with chronic self-loathing and insomnia. Here I actually think desert is partly satisfied, even though the suffering is self-inflicted and there's no external punishment.

Three: a genuinely kind person dies young in a freak accident, but thier community thrives because of the example they left. The suffering wasn't deserved, but there's this "moral surplus" that makes me hesitate to call the universe unjust.

Four: in some traditions you can burn off bad karma through ascetic practice without harming anyone or being harmed. If that's how it works, then desert isn't about bearing a cost — it's just metaphysical bookkeeping. I reject that; I think the wrongdoer has to actually feel the weight.

Five: a child is born with a horrific genetic condition and a guru says it's past-life debt. I reject this outright — desert requires agency, and retroactive karmic accounting on an infant is conceptually broken.

So is there one principle? Maybe something like "desert tracks proportional suffering causally tied to the wrong, unless it generates a greater moral surplus, except when it's internalized..." which is a mess. What's the cleanest principle that saves the most of these, and which verdict should I toss? And what does it predict for the case I keep going back and forth on — a corporation that poisons a river, pays a trivial fine, but then spends twenty years voluntarily cleaning it up at enormous cost?
