I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether contemporary bioethics has lost the plot on mortality. She kept insisting that Zhuangzi's well-known equanimity toward death—his treatment of life and death as continuous transformations like the turning of the seasons—would collapse the moment he encountered modern intensive care. I'm not convinced, but I need to work through the reasoning carefully. So: imagine Zhuangzi is alive now and fully informed about current clinical and legal practice. He learns that in most Western jurisdictions, legal death can be declared upon the irreversible cessation of all brainstem function, even while mechanical ventilation and circulation continue, primarily to preserve organs for transplantation under the dead donor rule. He also learns about cryonic preservation, where individuals are chemically preserved at cryogenic temperatures immediately upon legal death in the hope of future revival. Given his philosophical commitment to transformation rather than annihilation, his skepticism about rigid boundaries between self and world, and his narrative of encountering a skull and recognizing it as part of an endless cycle of change, how do you think he would evaluate these practices? Would he regard the maintenance of a brain-dead body for organ procurement as a natural extension of dissolution into other lives, or as a technological refusal to let transformation complete its course? And would he see cryonic suspension as a deeper form of the same delusion—an attempt to arrest the very flux he celebrated—or might he reinterpret it as merely another, more technologically elaborate stage in the cycle? I am especially interested in whether his view of personal identity as fluid and distributed would lead him to reject the very premise that the "same person" is being preserved or lost at all. I want careful reasoning, not a historical summary; and I want to know which of his commitments do the real work.
