Consider the near-universal editorial practice in modern critical editions of the received text of Gongsun Long: the opening thesis of the Bai Ma Lun is consistently punctuated with a caesura after the compound nominal bai ma, yielding the reading white horse, not horse, rather than white, horse is not horse or with ma treated as a verbal predicate. This convention is reproduced in standard philosophical anthologies, translations, and classroom editions, and it is usually accompanied by an implicit gloss that treats bai and ma as separable determinables that can be combined or withheld independently.

What theory of linguistic reference and semantic composition does this editorial convention presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of language built into the practice: does it commit the interpreter to the view that bai ma denotes a determinate kind with its own extension, distinct from the intersection of the extensions of bai and ma? Or can the same punctuation be sustained by a purely syntactic theory of compound formation that carries no ontological weight?

Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, for instance, with both a class-inclusion reading and a reference-fixing reading of the School of Names—or whether it strictly requires one of them. Then explain what would have to change in the standard presentation of Hui Shi's ten paradoxes, particularly the theses on similarity and difference (tong yi) and the hard and the white (jian bai), if editors adopted the alternative punctuation and treated bai ma as a predicative construction rather than a compound subject. Would the ten theses then cohere as a unified theory of predication, or would they fragment into unrelated sophisms?
