I've been trying to pin down what moral worth actually requires, and I've ended up with a view thats probably too specific but I want to stress-test it. I think an action only has full moral worth if the agent's motivating reason is not just correct, but structurally dominant in thier practical reasoning: remove the moral framing and leave the non-moral payoffs intact, and they still wouldn't do it, and this holds not just in the actual case but across nearby counterfactuals where self-interest pulls in different directions. Its not enough that morality happened to win this round; the reason has to be the kind that systematically wins even when the stakes shift.

What would actually count against this? I'm not asking for the usual stuff about people acting for mixed motives or accidentally doing good. I want the real defeaters: what results in moral psychology, what decision-theoretic formalisms, or what intuition pumps about moral identity and self-deception would genuinely undermine it? For instance, if moral licensing turns out to be so pervasive that agents with strong moral framing are actually less reliable in high-stakes cases, is that fatal? Or if neural imaging shows that so-called moral reasoning activates the same regions as strategic cost-benefit calculation, does that collapse structural dominance into just another form of instrumental reasoning? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether defenders can retreat to "this is just an ideal that real agents rarely meet" without making the view unfalsifiable. Also be honest: is there any evidence that has arguably already happened that should move me, or has this view been qualified into invulnerability?
