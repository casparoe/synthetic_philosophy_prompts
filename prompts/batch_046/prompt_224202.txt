Give an example of a conceptual issue in infinite ethics in which the standard distinction between obligatory and supererogatory beneficence breaks down, not through any aggregation paradox, but specifically because the population of moral patients is actually infinite and structurally unending. Construct a concrete scenario—such as a rescuer with strictly finite capacity confronting a spatially or temporally unbounded sequence of strangers, each in identical and non-diminishing peril—where saving one individual neither fulfills a determinate duty nor clearly counts as going beyond it, since every rescue leaves infinitely many others in precisely the same condition. Then pinpoint the exact conceptual tension: does the agent face a single infinite obligation that can never be satisfied, perform an endless series of individually optional goods, or does the framework of duty versus supererogation itself become inapplicable when need is both uniform and inexhaustible? Frame the problem without relying on total-versus-average utility comparisons, and avoid invoking well-known named paradoxes such as the St. Petersburg case.
