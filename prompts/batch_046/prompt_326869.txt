I have spent the better part of four decades studying evolutionary transitions in cognition among avian and cetacean lineages, and since my retirement I have been working through philosophy of mind in a small interdisciplinary seminar that meets monthly. We have been examining Dennett’s well-known taxonomy—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—and I find myself in a persistent minority against colleagues who treat the framework as a useful pedagogical ladder rather than an ontology of genuinely discontinuous natural kinds. I want your own credence, expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1, that the taxonomy in fact marks four real and non-overlapping kinds of mind, with Gregorian status defined specifically by the capacity for recursive meta-representational correction through cultural tools—that is, the ability not merely to store or transmit symbols socially, but to deploy an external symbolic system to revise one’s own inferential procedures—so that no extant non-human animal crosses the threshold, present large language models remain sophisticated Skinnerian systems despite their fluent linguistic output, and there are no stable intermediate “semi-Gregorian” kinds in nature or in principle.

Please decompose your credence into at least four terms: (i) the probability that evolutionary transitions in cognition produce discrete, non-overlapping kinds rather than continuous gradations; (ii) the probability that recursive meta-representational correction is the correct demarcation criterion for Gregorian status, as opposed to simpler measures such as symbolic transmission or collective computation; (iii) the probability that no non-human animal currently satisfies that criterion, given the behavioral and neuroanatomical evidence we actually possess; and (iv) the probability that current large language models, trained via gradient descent on predictive loss, lack the internal architecture for genuine meta-representational revision and therefore belong properly to the Skinnerian class. For each term, provide a rough numerical value together with a single line of justification, then combine them—whether multiplicatively, by weighted averaging, or by another aggregation you make explicit—into your overall figure. Finally, identify which of these four terms your final number is most sensitive to, and describe the specific empirical discovery or conceptual argument that would most substantially shift it.
