I have been debating the problem of universals at length with a close friend, and I have found myself drawn to a rather particular hybrid view that she thinks collapses under its own weight. Rather than accepting that universals are literally shared constituents of particulars, or reducing them to extensional sets of resembling things, I want to defend the claim that a universal—say, triangularity—is a modal invariant realised within each world solely as fully particularised tropes, none of which share any literally identical part with triangular tropes elsewhere. The cross-world stability of the universal is secured not by shared constituency or by resemblance, but by a primitive, irreducible modal relation that I call being-the-same-universal-type, which holds between world-bound tropes without requiring them to overlap in any concrete feature. My friend argues that this is either realism in disguise, because the primitive relation functions exactly like a shared universal, or else it is trope nominalism burdened with an obscure and ontologically costly modal apparatus. I want you to assess what would genuinely count against this specific position—not generic objections to realism or nominalism, but the particular vulnerabilities of this hybrid. Which formal or metaphysical commitments does it risk? Does the primitive modal relation collapse into literal identity of a shared constituent, and if so, is that fatal? Does the view require an implausible ontology of possible worlds, or an inflationary proliferation of tropes? Are there intuitions about particularity, about property-inheritance, or about causal powers that this account cannot accommodate without ad hoc qualification? I would like the potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest indication of which ones a committed defender of this view would concede as genuinely damaging and which they could plausibly dismiss. Finally, please consider whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counter-example can be absorbed by appealing to the primitive relation—and whether that insulation is itself a defect for a metaphysical theory of this kind.
