In a recent graduate seminar on moral psychology, a fellow student summarized what she called the "standard narrative" of the situationist critique and its implications for virtue ethics in the following terms. Situationist experiments—particularly the well-known bystander-intervention and obedience paradigms—show that behavioral consistency across contexts is negligible, which proves that global character traits such as honesty, courage, or compassion do not exist as genuine psychological realities. Because virtue ethics defines moral virtue as a stable, cross-situational disposition to act and feel in the right way, the empirical evidence does not merely complicate it but outright refutes it. The familiar virtue-ethical reply—that virtue is analogous to practical wisdom or to a skill—fails, she argued, because skills themselves are highly context-sensitive, so this response only reinforces the situationist point rather than answering it. From this she concluded that virtue ethics should be abandoned in favor of an agent-evaluation framework that replaces character-talk with an analysis of how agents design choice architectures, manage situational triggers, and adapt behaviorally to local cues.

This account strikes me as suspiciously tidy, especially its claim that virtue ethicists have "no answer" and its assumption that virtue requires near-perfect cross-situational uniformity. At the same time, I have encountered references to something called "moral ecology"—the idea that a genuinely virtuous agent is not a passive victim of circumstances but actively cultivates, selects, and modifies her environments to support virtuous action. Is my colleague's summary a fair representation of the situationist literature and of the virtue-ethical responses? Please separate what accurately captures the empirical findings (for instance, regarding the magnitude of personality-behavior correlations), what subtly overstates or mischaracterizes the situationist conclusion, and whether virtue ethics originally requires the kind of global consistency she assumes or whether that is itself a misreading of the Aristotelian conception of hexis. I am also unsure whether the "moral ecology" proposal genuinely addresses the consistency problem or merely relocates it: does it preserve the core virtue-ethical commitment to character, or does it collapse virtue into situational engineering?
