Question: Critics of interpersonal utility comparison argue that mental states are private and there is no common cardinal scale of well-being, so comparing how much two people value an outcome is meaningless. One proposed reply introduces a "behavioral calibration curve": for each person, plot the amount of standardized physical effort (measured in minutes of labor) they are willing to expend to secure identical objective outcomes, then compare the slopes. If person A spends 5 minutes to secure outcome X and person B spends 15 minutes for the same X, the reply claims we can rank and even cardinalize their valuations without accessing any inner experience. Does this reply solve the privacy problem? Can it deliver ordinal comparisons, cardinal ones, or neither? What is the standard objection involving differences in risk attitude or domain-specific preference?

Student answer: The calibration-curve reply solves the privacy problem completely because it replaces mental-state access with purely observable behavior. By using minutes of labor as a universal metric — labor time is the same for everyone — we create a common scale and can make cardinal comparisons: B values X one-third as much as A does, since B spends three times the effort. The method also delivers ordinal rankings automatically. The main objection is that people have different physical abilities, so B's 15 minutes may be easier than A's 5 minutes; but this can be corrected by normalizing for physical capacity, leaving the comparison intact. There is no serious problem with risk attitudes because the curve uses certain outcomes rather than lotteries, so risk preferences do not enter the measurement. Once physical differences are normalized, the reply successfully grounds interpersonal cardinal comparisons on entirely public data.
