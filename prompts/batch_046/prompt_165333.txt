ok so in my philosophy elective we spent like two days on Gettier cases — the stopped clock one, the barn facade with all the fake barns, all that. everyone was like knowledge isnt just justified true belief and then we argued about reliabilism vs virtue epistemology vs adding a no false lemma condition. but i was thinking about it on the bus and came up with my own take, which might be dumb but i wanna test it.

i think the real problem in Gettier cases isnt that theres a false premise hiding in the reasoning, its that the believer has what id call fragile ownership of the belief — theyd drop it or change how they justify it if they learned about the lucky part. so my position is that knowledge is true belief that you hold with what im calling resilient justification: you can still defend it using only the parts of your reasoning that are actually good, even if you dont know which parts those are yet. in the clock case, the guys justification is totally fragile cause if he knew the clock was broken hed stop citing it. but if he had actually checked the clock against his phone and then forgot the check, maybe thats resilient? idk.

anyway, what would count against this? im not asking for the standard objections like heres a case where belief is resilient but still not knowledge. i want to know what kind of intuition, formal result, or development in other areas — maybe about testimony, or about how kids learn stuff, or about AI knowing things — would actually break it. which defeaters would be fatal versus just awkward? and what would i, as someone who likes this view, have to admit is a real problem versus what i could just dismiss? also, is there a hidden commitment im making that collapses this into reliabilism or virtue epistemology without me noticing?
