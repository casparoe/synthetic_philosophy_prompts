I have been reporting on the philosophy of technology and recently found myself in a long discussion with colleagues about whether nineteenth-century liberalism can make sense of synthetic media and algorithmic curation. As I prepare a feature that takes John Stuart Mill’s harm principle as its conceptual backbone, I keep returning to a passage of reasoning in On Liberty that seems to me both clear and strangely incomplete. Mill distinguishes self-regarding actions from other-regarding ones and insists that opinion should remain free even when false, yet he also treats reputation, social boycott, and violations of definite obligation as legitimate domains of social control, which suggests that harm for him is not limited to direct physical injury. Here is where my confusion lies. If we take seriously the idea of informational externalities—distributed, probabilistic harms that emerge from the aggregate interaction of utterances within recommendation architectures rather than from any single speaker—does Mill’s framework require us to treat such systemic effects as other-regarding actions subject to regulation? Or does his defense of free expression presuppose that harm must be intentional, immediate, and traceable to an identifiable agent, a model of causation that networked communication precisely dissolves? I want to avoid forcing Mill to answer questions he never asked, but I am unsure whether the distinction he draws between harm and mere offence can survive once the causal chain between speech and injury is mediated by platforms and synthetic content generation.
