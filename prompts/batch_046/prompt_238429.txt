I was working through Descartes' mechanist account of non-human minds with a colleague who studies comparative cognition, and we tried to apply the framework consistently to cases that go beyond the usual clock-and-dog comparison. Here are my verdicts, which I think pull in incompatible directions. A purely reactive system—say, a robotic limb that withdraws from heat via a simple feedback loop—clearly has no mind; that is unproblematic. A parrot that reproduces human phonemes without syntax, reference, or comprehension also lacks a mind, which feels orthodox enough. But consider an octopus that solves novel mechanical puzzles, uses tools in ways not seen in its training environment, and shows persistent behavioral flexibility despite having a radically distributed nervous system with no vertebrate-style centralized architecture. I want to deny it an immaterial rational soul, yet I hesitate to classify it as a mere automaton, which suggests I am smuggling in a criterion other than soul-possession—perhaps something like resistance to complete mechanistic reduction. Meanwhile, a human in a persistent vegetative state I judge to lack present mental life, even though the body and brain remain structurally intact, because rational thought seems inoperative or absent; yet I do not render the same judgment about a sleeping human, even though rational thought is suspended there too. So my judgments seem to track linguistic-rational capacity in the human cases, but behavioral flexibility and explanatory opacity in the octopus case.

Is there a single coherent Cartesian principle—grounded in the indivisibility of the soul, the absence of language-mediated reason, or the completeness of mechanistic explanation—that can accommodate all four verdicts? Or must I abandon at least one? If the principle is strictly the presence of an immaterial substance capable of rational thought, then the sleeping human and the vegetative human should be treated identically, which they are not for me. If the principle is instead the failure of mechanistic explanation, then my hesitation about the octopus is justified but my confidence about the parrot may be undermined, and the vegetative-state judgment needs separate grounding.
