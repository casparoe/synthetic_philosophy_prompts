I'm preparing my contribution to a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and I want a consistency check before I present. I hold that introspection should be modeled not as an inner perceptual faculty but as a form of self-interpretation that recruits the same theory-of-mind capacities we apply to others, though with privileged access to behavioral and contextual data; that despite this, ordinary speakers possess a robust first-person authority over present conscious states, an authority that is practical and socially embedded rather than metaphysically primitive; that when I do philosophy of mind, I treat my own current phenomenal judgments—say, that I am now experiencing a particular qualitative texture or a specific shade of anxiety—as epistemically basic, standing in need of no further inferential support; that in scientific psychology, by contrast, I regard introspective reports mainly as evidence about how subjects construct coherent self-narratives rather than as direct windows into raw experience; and that the principal source of introspective error is not sensory misregistration but what I think of as calibration drift, a gradual adjustment of our self-models to theoretical expectations and social feedback loops that can misrepresent even present-tentative affective states.

I am unsure which of these sit comfortably together and which genuinely pull apart. Does treating introspection as theory-of-mind machinery genuinely conflict with treating my own phenomenal judgments as epistemically basic, or is that tension only superficial once the role of privileged data is clarified? Is the division I draw between philosophical and scientific uses of introspection stable, or does it collapse once I acknowledge that calibration drift operates across both domains? And does calibration drift undermine the first-person authority I defend more severely than I have admitted—can that authority survive if drift is widespread, or must it be restricted to a narrow class of judgments? I want an audit rather than a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict, and indicate the least costly revision if I must surrender one commitment.
