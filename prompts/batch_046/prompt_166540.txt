Question: Consider a museum installation titled Continuous Form consisting of a humanoid shape maintained by a bioreactor that replaces every cellular component within the form every seventy-two hours, preserving only the morphological pattern and the institutional label. Is the installation at the end of a year materially identical to the installation at the opening? Address whether the relation between the biological substrate and the artwork is one of identity, constitution, or something else, and discuss whether a sortal-essentialist view can accommodate gradual replacement without invoking a separate, non-material form.

Student’s answer: “The installation is not identical to its cellular substrate, since material constitution allows an object and its matter to have different persistence conditions—just as a clay statue can survive squashing that destroys the lump of clay. Here, the artwork is constituted by successive biological substrates, not identical to any of them. Because the form and institutional designation remain constant, the artwork survives total replacement, much as the ship of Theseus survives plank replacement. However, a sortal-essentialist like Wiggins would deny this: if ‘art installation’ is a functional kind with essential continuity conditions, then gradual replacement is permitted only if the kind’s criteria allow it; but Wiggins actually holds that no material object can survive the replacement of all its parts, since the parts are essential to the individual. Therefore, the essentialist must either reject the installation’s persistence or invoke a non-material form. Thus, the correct analysis is constitution without identity, with the artwork being a constituted object that is ontologically dependent on but not reducible to its substrate.”
