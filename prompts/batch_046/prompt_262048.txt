I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am constantly sent essays and expert briefings that argue: because scenario X is conceivable, it is genuinely possible, and therefore society must prepare for it. I need a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure — an ordered checklist with explicit stop-points — for evaluating whether such an argument holds, grounded specifically in the philosophical question of whether conceivability guides possibility and, if so, under what conditions. The procedure must distinguish descriptive coherence from modal independence, and must include what I will call a background-conditions lock: a check for whether the conceived scenario implicitly requires holding constant the very institutional, causal, or physical conditions that the scenario itself would destabilise. Specify, in order: the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; where the procedure halts with a clear negative; where it halts and defers to editorial judgement because the remaining issue is one of values or risk tolerance rather than modal logic; and how to flag cases that pass coherence but fail independence. Apply it to two cases: first, a proposed brain-computer interface permitting direct transmission of tactile sensation between users, where the procedure should endorse the move from conceivability to possibility; second, a proposed rapid global transition to a gift economy that eliminates market pricing without transitional institutional collapse, where the procedure should block the move because of the background-conditions lock. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do, including how an advocate might game the coherence filter by burying unstated premises. Write in British English. Begin with your bottom-line verdict on whether conceivability alone should ever license policy-relevant possibility claims in my reporting, then present the procedure, the two applications, and the limits. No preamble, no restatement of the question.
