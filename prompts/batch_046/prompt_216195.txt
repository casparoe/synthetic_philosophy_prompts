I am a high school student working on an independent study of logic and artificial intelligence, and I have been trying to understand why the so-called Löbian obstacle is treated as unavoidable for self-trusting agents. As I understand it, Löb's theorem applies to formal systems that can represent their own provability relation: if such a system can prove that provability of a statement implies the statement itself, then for any statement the system can derive that statement outright. When combined with Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, this seems to show that a sufficiently strong system which fully trusts its own proofs can derive its own consistency and therefore collapses into inconsistency. The discussions I have read present this as a structural feature that no redesign of the agent's confidence can fix. But I am unsure whether this assumes an unrealistic model of belief. If engineers calibrated the agent so that it assigned a credence of exactly 0.999—rather than 1.0—to every conclusion reached through its internal proof mechanism, reserving an epsilon of systematic doubt, would that not break the fixed-point derivability conditions that Löb's theorem requires? In other words, is the obstacle genuinely unavoidable for any rational self-modeling system, or is it only unavoidable for agents that employ binary, uncalibrated acceptance rules? I ask because the answer seems to determine whether we are facing a hard logical limit or merely a parameter-tuning problem in the architecture of self-reference.
