Specify the full set of desiderata that an adequate answer to Plato’s Meno problem—why knowledge is better than mere true belief—must satisfy. I want the requirements list: the specific cases the account must handle (including Gettier-style luck, lottery-paradox true beliefs, and true beliefs formed through epistemic delegation where justification is distributed across agents rather than possessed by any single one), the conceptual distinctions it must preserve (such as instrumental versus final value, knowing-that versus knowing-how, possession of knowledge versus mere access to true information, and individual versus collective epistemic goods), and the theoretical constraints it must respect (for instance, consistency with the observation that knowledge survives reflective scrutiny that destabilizes mere belief, and compatibility with the fact that we continue inquiry even when true belief is already available). For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it; flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable given standard epistemological commitments; and state which single requirement does the most damage to the leading contemporary answers—whether reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, or credit-based. Additionally, take a definite, defended position on whether the value of knowledge is ultimately practical, theoretical, or irreducibly epistemic, and explain why the other two categories fail to capture the core intuition that knowledge is worth pursuing for its own sake. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke highly specialized formal apparatus; and do not treat the problem as resolved by reference to any named author’s system alone. I am particularly interested in whether your list reveals that the demand for a non-instrumental value is incompatible with the demand that knowledge be distinct from true belief in every practical context—if you think so, defend that tension explicitly.
