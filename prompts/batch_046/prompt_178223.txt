I was discussing Wittgenstein's private language argument with a friend who works in cognitive science, and she proposed a defense I haven't seen handled well in the standard literature. For a philosophy of mind assignment, I need to compare Wittgenstein's claim that a genuinely private language—one whose terms refer to sensations accessible only to the speaker—is impossible, with what I'll call an "indexical calibration" defense of private reference. On this defense, a solitary speaker could establish a mental term's meaning not by appealing to public criteria, but by treating her own memory of past applications as an internal calibration standard: she checks her present use of the term against her remembered prior uses, correcting for drift much as one might recalibrate an instrument against its own history rather than an external benchmark. I want you to assess whether this calibration strategy genuinely escapes Wittgenstein's circularity objection—where, in the private case, seeming right is being right—or whether it merely relocates the same problem inside the speaker's psychology. Be precise about the roles of memory, error-correction, and the distinction between correct and seeming-correct application. Justify which position is stronger, and identify exactly where Wittgenstein's appeal to shared, public criteria does work that internal calibration cannot replicate, even in principle.
