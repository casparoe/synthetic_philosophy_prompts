As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of religion in earnest, I have been working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed and the surrounding scholarship on his treatment of animal sacrifice. In doing so I have noticed a recurring interpretive pattern for which I lack a name, and I should like to know whether it is already recognised.

The pattern concerns two explanations that Maimonides appears to offer for the institution of sacrificial worship. One is accommodative: the Israelites, long accustomed to the cultic practices of surrounding nations, could not be expected to abandon ritual slaughter altogether, so the law channels an existing habit towards the service of one God. The other is teleological: sacrifice possesses an intrinsic rational function, disciplining the imagination, weaning worshippers away from corporeal representations of the divine, and cultivating a specific form of reverence. These are not merely different emphases; they carry distinct philosophical commitments about whether divine law may prescribe practices that lack value in themselves.

What I observe in both academic discussion and informal debate is a kind of unacknowledged oscillation between these two frameworks. For example, in a recent seminar a speaker invoked the accommodative story to answer a historian who stressed the brutality of ancient Near Eastern cults; yet twenty minutes later, responding to a theologian who asked why an omniscient lawgiver would not simply abolish sacrifice, the same speaker appealed to its irreplaceable pedagogical purpose. The move is not a standard motte-and-bailey, because neither explanation functions as a defensible core with an indefensible fringe; both are textually supported and philosophically serious. Nor is it plain equivocation, since the speaker is not hiding a shift in meaning but genuinely drawing on two different lines of argument present in the source. The tension between them is left unresolved, and the interpreter travels between them according to the direction of the objection.

I have seen analogues in evolutionary accounts of morality and in certain readings of Kant's philosophy of religion, but I am after the precise structure: two substantial, textually grounded explanations of the same practice, held in reserve against different criticisms, with no synthetic resolution offered. Does this configuration have an established name in hermeneutics or in the philosophy of religion? If there is none, could you coin a term, define it precisely, and delimit its boundaries—distinguishing it clearly from motte-and-bailey, from ordinary ambiguity, and from the legitimate deployment of auxiliary hypotheses?

Please state what would change your mind: for instance, a demonstration that Maimonides explicitly reconciles the two explanations into a single coherent account that renders the oscillation illegitimate, or the identification of an established technical concept that already captures this dialectical structure together with its standard boundary conditions.
