Consider the admissions and talent-recruitment policies of a large public university system: applicants to academic programs are evaluated primarily on standardized measures that claim to assess general cognitive ability, while applicants to the athletic program are evaluated on highly domain-specific physical and strategic skills, with no expectation that basketball court awareness predicts swimming performance. At the same time, the university's disability-services office treats specific learning disorders as localized deficits that do not diminish general intelligence, but treats a generalized physical coordination deficit as disqualifying for the athletic program altogether rather than as a localized impairment. What conception of intelligence—general, modular, embodied, or something else—does this three-part pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the same conception can consistently underwrite both the academic and athletic evaluations, or whether the system is philosophically underdetermined between rival views.
