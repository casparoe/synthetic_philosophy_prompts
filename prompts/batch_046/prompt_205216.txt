I'm putting together a philosophy workshop for grad students — about 12 people, 90 minutes, pretty informal setting with chairs in a circle and no projector. We're talking about tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level, you know, the Escher Drawing Hands thing where the frame that's supposed to be outside ends up drawn by what's inside. I want an activitiy that makes them actually feel that collapse rather than just hear me explain Hofstadter's strange loop, so I need something with real roles and a hidden trap in the rules.

Split them into three groups — Floor, Gallery, and Balcony. Floor writes rules for a mock task (ranking ethical priorities for AI deployment, something concrete but low-stakes). Gallery writes the appeals process. Balcony writes the charter that governs Gallery; they're told they're "outside" the system and only convene if there's a constitutional crisis. They run the task, stage an appeal, and just when Balcony thinks they're safe up high, drop a hidden clause: Floor can invoke Article 7 to reconvene Balcony if they think Gallery overreached. But to decide if Balcony was overreached, they have to use Floor's original discussion rules — so Balcony, the supposed inviolate level, is suddenly sitting in Floor's framework, being drawn by the hands it was meant to draw. The deliverable should include the full rules/payoffs, the staged conflict, the debrief that maps each moment onto parts of the idea, what predictably goes wrong (Balcony refusing to sit down, Floor claiming it's all just politics), and an honest note on what this can't reproduce — probably the actual logical self-reference of Gödel numbering — so I know where I have to supply the theory myself.
