I'm preparing materials for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar centered on Dennett's brain-transplant scenario — in which Hamlet's brain is removed, duplicated as Yorick in the original skull, and Hamlet must ask whether he now inhabits the new body, the old one, both, or neither — and I want a classification instrument that sorts students not by the labels they prefer but by the concrete commitments they make about the case. Please construct a decision tree that begins with the question of whether there is a determinate fact about Hamlet's location immediately after the procedure, then branches through successive commitments: whether the original body retains any authoritative psychological claim; whether branching is metaphysically possible for a single self; whether duplication without division is coherent; whether psychological continuity alone fixes location regardless of bodily continuity; and whether the self is best understood as an informational pattern that can be instantiated in multiple substrates simultaneously. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete scenario or yes/no claim that a student can answer without knowing philosophical taxonomy — for example, 'If both bodies wake up with Hamlet's complete memory set and both sincerely claim to be Hamlet, is there one person or two?' — and each leaf should name an actual position (such as brain-based animalism, psychological-continuity-with-no-branching, the narrative-self view, the deflationary no-fact position, or a distributed-pattern account) or be marked 'unnamed,' accompanied by one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Because the logical space may contain unoccupied combinations, please include at least one such empty branch, diagnose briefly whether its emptiness stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and flag any pair of named leaves that this particular tree of questions cannot cleanly separate, explaining whether the failure is due to the views being genuinely indistinguishable on these cases or only underdetermined here. The audience is graduate students familiar with the basic setup but not necessarily with the literature, so avoid asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, and do not frame any node as 'do you accept animalism?'
