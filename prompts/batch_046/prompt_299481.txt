To the Institutional Bioethics and Clinical Policy Committee:

We are asked to formalize binding guidance for an increasingly common edge case in our neurological intensive care units: a patient exhibits irreversible, total destruction of cortical function—verified by sustained absence of cortical electrical activity on repeated EEG and by failed standardized clinical tests of awareness and intentionality—yet retains fully intact brainstem reflexes, spontaneous respiration, and unassisted cardiac circulation. The patient requires only basic nutritional and fluid maintenance and could, in principle, be sustained in this state for years. Some clinicians and family advocates propose recognizing this condition as a form of neurological death under a cortical or "higher-brain" criterion, which would permit discontinuation of custodial support and evaluation for modified organ-procurement protocols. Others insist that our institution should continue to recognize only two valid declarations of death—irreversible cessation of whole-brain function, or permanent circulatory arrest—meaning this patient remains alive, and any withdrawal of support must be justified on quality-of-life or surrogate-decision grounds rather than on a determination of death.

Draft a formal advisory memo recommending one definitive institutional position. Your memo should include: (1) the precise clinical and philosophical criteria that justify treating this state as either death or life, with explicit attention to how it differs from both whole-brain death and from persistent vegetative states with preserved cortical islands; (2) an honest and rigorous treatment of the strongest argument for the opposing position, particularly the concern that adopting a cortical-death criterion collapses the morally important distinction between killing a living patient and allowing a dead one to be taken off support; and (3) an explicit articulation of the implicit theory of personal identity, consciousness, and biological integration that your recommended policy presupposes, together with an assessment of whether that theory is consistent with our current practice of using brain-death criteria for neurological donation pathways and circulatory-death criteria for donation after cardiac death. The recommendation must be actionable—specifying documentation standards, family-communication protocols, and staff obligations—so that the committee can adopt it directly into clinical policy.
