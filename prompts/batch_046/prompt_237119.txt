I am taking an undergraduate course on the philosophy of death, and I have come to hold a qualified Epicurean view: death is not bad for the deceased because harm requires a subject, and this applies symmetrically to prenatal non-existence. To this I have added what I call a narrative boundary condition — that harm also requires a continuing practical identity capable of registering deprivation — which entails that posthumous damage to one's projects harms no one, though it may damage a narrative thread with which the living agent identified. I further treat the well-known asymmetry in our attitudes toward future and past non-existence as a cognitive bias rather than a metaphysical clue. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want a map of its exposure — which arguments (the deprivation account, the symmetry objection, results in the philosophy of personal identity about narrative persistence), which intuitions about grief or anticipatory dread, and which ethical developments about unassigned harm would count against it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. What does the view predict that its rivals do not, and does it risk anything at all, or has the narrative boundary condition insulated it into unfalsifiability? If it has been insulated, is that a problem for a claim of this kind? Please say what defenders of the qualified Epicurean view would themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics allege, and assess honestly whether the position is well protected.
