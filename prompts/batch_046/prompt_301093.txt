Analyze Oxford University's 1956 decision to confer an honorary doctorate upon Harry Truman, and G. E. M. Anscombe's protest articulated in her 1958 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree, as a case study in her doctrine of double effect and her absolute prohibition on intentionally killing innocent civilians. Apply her concepts rather than summarizing her biography. First, map the institutional actors and their available options: what intentions attach to a university vote honoring Truman, and how does her distinction between intended harm and merely foreseen harm apply when the institution itself causes no direct physical injury? Second, distinguish formal cooperation with murder from material cooperation, and ask whether conferring an honorific degree counts as endorsing Truman's choice to kill noncombatants at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, or whether it falls outside the scope of her absolutism because it lacks direct causal efficacy. Third, examine whether her framework can consistently condemn this award without also condemning other state honors for leaders whose policies foreseeably but unintentionally killed civilians, and identify whether the episode reveals an unanticipated gap in her theory—specifically, whether she needs a general account of moral contamination through association, or whether her existing action-theoretic apparatus already covers institutional honor. Finally, conclude whether this episode strengthens her moral absolutism, exposes a limit in applying it to symbolic acts, or forces her distinction between killing and honoring to collapse.
