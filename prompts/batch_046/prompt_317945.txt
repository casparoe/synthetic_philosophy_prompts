I'm coordinating an interdisciplinary seminar on the historiography of non-human causation—specifically how germs, long-term climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions are treated not as inert background conditions but as entities with genuine causal efficacy in human affairs, without sliding into environmental determinism. In my preliminary reading I keep circling four unresolved tensions, but I suspect they are not equally fundamental. The first is conceptual: can we attribute a form of "agency" or "historical efficacy" to non-intentional forces—say, a strain of Yersinia pestis or the aerosol forcing from Tambora—without either anthropomorphizing them or reducing them to mere "conditions"? The second is methodological and comparative: should germs, climate regimes, and volcanic events be analyzed under a single framework of exogenous shock, or does each require its own ontology—perhaps a "trigger versus load" distinction borrowed from seismology for volcanoes, but a co-constitutive model for pathogens that depend on human social density to become historically significant? The third concerns weighing and interaction: how should historians integrate these forces with institutional and political choices—do we model them as independent variables, as multipliers of existing vulnerability, or as events that only become decisive through specific human failures of adaptation? The fourth is normative and historiographical: does emphasizing non-human actors risk excusing human institutions from responsibility for the distribution of harm, and if so, is that a reason to deprioritize this research program altogether?

Assume the seminar has only one semester and no capacity for empirical data collection—only conceptual clarification, comparative case analysis, and argument evaluation. Which of these open questions (or an alternative you think I'm neglecting) should be settled first, in the sense that its resolution would most restructure the others? Provide a dependency-aware prioritization: identify which questions are upstream, which are tractable given current tools in philosophy of science and environmental history, and which, if answered, would change little downstream. Defend your ranking with explicit criteria—importance for avoiding determinism, tractability, and neglectedness—rather than treating all four as equally urgent. Crucially, state exactly what would change your mind: for instance, if empirical historians demonstrated that institutional responses consistently swamp environmental shocks in every documented case, or if philosophers of action produced a consensus account of non-intentional agency that made the conceptual problem dissolve. Also identify the weakest point in your reasoning—particularly whether my framing falsely opposes human and non-human causation, and whether that opposition itself is the problem to dismantle rather than resolve.
