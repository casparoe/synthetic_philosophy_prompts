Student answer:

The sphex wasp demonstrates pure behavioral rigidity: it executes a fixed sequence of digging, dragging, checking, and repeating without adapting when the environment changes, such as when the cricket is moved during the inspection phase. Hofstadter uses this case primarily to illustrate determinism—if every action is fully caused by prior physical states, the wasp lacks freedom. Extending this to humans, if our neural processes are deterministic, we are essentially complex sphexes with longer behavioral loops and more internal variables.

Flexibility, however, distinguishes genuinely free agents. A flexible system can suspend or alter its rules in response to novel conditions, whereas the sphex repeats its check endlessly. Therefore, flexibility is sufficient for free will: once an agent can choose whether to apply a rule, determinism no longer entails sphexishness.

Critics sometimes raise second-order sphexishness—the idea that even if an agent revises its rules, the mechanism governing that revision might itself be fixed. This objection fails. Any system that genuinely updates its own operating code has broken out of the deterministic chain; there is no remaining higher level that stays rigid if self-modification is authentic. The difference between deterministic and stochastic processes is largely irrelevant here, since introducing randomness into the wasp's algorithm—say, randomly varying burrow depth—would not confer freedom, whereas true self-modification does.

Consequently, the sphex analogy threatens free will only if we deny all flexibility to human cognition. Because our minds clearly permit rule revision, we are not sphexish in any meaningful sense, and determinism is therefore compatible with freedom.
