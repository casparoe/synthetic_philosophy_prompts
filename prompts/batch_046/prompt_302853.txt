I am a doctoral student in philosophy of welfare economics, and I am trying to determine whether my judgments about interpersonal utility comparisons cohere around a single principle or whether they require me to abandon at least one verdict. Here are my concrete cases.

First, two individuals report identical subjective well-being, but one arrived there through adaptive preference formation after prolonged deprivation while the other arrived through the fulfillment of complex, self-authored goals. I judge that they are not equally well-off: the adaptive case is normatively inferior despite matching experiential reports. This suggests that first-person testimony does not by itself fix comparative welfare.

Second, comparing the suffering of someone with congenital analgesia who experiences severe emotional distress with someone suffering intense chronic physical pain but emotional equanimity, I find direct cardinal comparison genuinely difficult. My inclination is that the emotional distress case is worse overall, yet I also feel that physical pain possesses an objective intensity that emotional distress lacks, which makes precise ranking suspect.

Third, faced with a forced choice between saving one hundred young children and saving one hundred one elderly people, I judge that we ought to save the children. This violates straightforward utilitarian aggregation, but the verdict feels firm to me.

Fourth, comparing welfare losses from an identical broken promise, I think the loss is greater for a psychologically fragile recipient than for a resilient one, holding all other conditions constant. Here I accept interpersonal comparison but insist on weighting by vulnerability.

Fifth, comparing the well-being of an ascetic monk who has renounced material goods and reports profound contentment with that of a moderately prosperous person with conventional attachments, I think direct comparison for policy allocation is illegitimate: the two lives are incommensurable on a single scale, though both may be genuinely flourishing.

These verdicts seem to pull in incompatible directions. The first and fourth accept normative correction and vulnerability-weighting, respectively, while the fifth denies that comparison is appropriate across radically different life structures. Meanwhile, the third introduces qualitative weighting by life stage that conflicts with any simple additive framework.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one distinguishing phenomenological commensurability, the normative authority of first-person testimony, and structural conditions under which comparison is legitimate—that can accommodate all five? If not, which verdict should I drop? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, flag where residual judgment is unavoidable, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot preserve.

Finally, apply the resulting principle to a case I have not listed: comparing the welfare of a person in sustained flow-state creative work with that of a person in tranquil contentment, when both report equal subjective satisfaction. What does your account predict, and does that prediction feel right?
