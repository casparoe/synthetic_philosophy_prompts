For a philosophy of psychiatry seminar handout, construct a taxonomy tree of positions on what Ian Hacking terms looping effects in psychiatric classification, but extend the map into distinctions that standard overviews rarely draw. Use this root question: when a diagnostic category becomes widely known outside clinical settings and people begin to self-identify using its criteria, does this awareness alter the properties of those classified? From there, split on whether the looping is uniform or 'asymmetrically distributed' — affecting some social groups far more than others because of differential exposure to the diagnostic narrative. For those who accept looping, split on its directional form: stabilising (homogenising symptoms), destabilising (blurring adjacent categories), or 'refractive' — meaning the loop generates socially real sub-types that lack prior clinical precedent rather than altering the original kind. Then split on the normative stance: is looping an epistemic corruption clinicians should resist, an ontological discovery to be embraced, or a practical inevitability that makes the category purely instrumental? At each leaf, name the position if it carries a recognised label (e.g. Hacking-style interactive-kind realism, biological essentialism, strong social constructionism, instrumentalist pragmatism) or mark it 'unnamed'; state its most awkward practical commitment in one sentence. Identify any logically possible combinations that remain unoccupied and briefly diagnose them (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — for instance, a biological realist who accepts hidden feedback but denies observable change versus a 'silent-loop' interactionist — and explain why the distinction collapses at the practical level.
