Two participants in a long-running forum thread on ontology in persistent virtual environments had the following exchange. The first designs economic mechanics for multiplayer worlds. The second studies institutional philosophy and participates in these worlds as a long-time user.

(1) The first argued that virtual objects lack real existence because they reduce to database states maintained by servers; consequently, what users call "theft" or "destruction" of such objects is merely an authorized change in access permissions, not a genuine harm but at most a breach of user agreement.

(2) The second countered that this reduction misses the normative layer: if a virtual object is embedded in a system of recognized rights, labor investment, and social practices that treat it as property, then damaging it is structurally analogous to damaging institutional property in the physical world—like destroying a shared library's catalog cards, where the physical substrate is trivial but the institutional function is not.

(3) The first then introduced a specific architecture: a virtual world running on a distributed ledger where deletion requires breaking cryptographic consensus, making restoration technically impossible without collective agreement—what he termed procedurally irreversible virtual damage. He claimed that even here the harm remains derivative: the real loss is the frustration of user expectations and the devaluation of prior labor-time, both psychological and economic facts about physical people, not metaphysical facts about virtual entities.

(4) The second granted that psychological and economic spillover exists, but accused the first of conflating the cause of harm with its locus. She proposed a distinction between substrate harm (damage to physical matter) and functional harm (disruption of an institutional role that participants have legitimate expectations about). She argued that virtual worlds can instantiate functional harm independently, just as canceling a legal contract harms institutional standing without damaging paper. She added that procedural irreversibility actually strengthens her case: if the architecture makes virtual destruction irreversible in practice, then the normative conditions for serious harm—irreversibility, non-substitutability, and recognized entitlement—are satisfied within the virtual domain itself. The first replied that functional harm applies only if the virtual world is a total institution—where participants have no meaningful exit and virtual goods constitute a significant portion of welfare—otherwise invoking serious harm is a category error borrowing emotional weight without meeting conditions. The second responded that this bar is arbitrarily high: physical-world harms occur outside total institutions—stealing a bike from a casual cyclist is still real theft—so requiring total embeddedness for virtual harm proves too much and undermines the first's own appeal to labor investment.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then identify what both participants miss—specifically, whether there is a principled way to distinguish virtual harms that remain purely internal to the world from those carrying genuine normative weight, without relying either on physical reduction or on an overly demanding total-institution condition.
