I've been thinking about Plato's idea that drunkenness doesn't so much corrupt a person as peel back the rational layer and reveal what's really driving them—the spirited and appetitive parts of the soul. He also seems to argue in the Laws that a wise legislator shouldn't ban wine outright but should permit it under careful supervision, treating the drinking gathering as a kind of civic exam to see who remains master of themselves. I want to apply this framework to American Prohibition (1920–1933). Rather than using alcohol as a regulated diagnostic tool, the Volstead Act tried to eliminate it entirely, which drove consumption underground into unsupervised speakeasies and fueled bootlegging and organized crime. Analyze Prohibition through Plato's lens: treat the ban as a legislator's refusal to administer his civic test, and treat the speakeasy culture as an accidental, unregulated version of it. What should the policymakers have done if they had taken Plato's diagnostic view seriously? Which features of the episode—enforcement failure, the rise of criminal networks, popular resistance—does his theory explain well, and which does it miss or misdescribe? Finally, does the collapse of total prohibition support Plato's claim that virtue is better revealed through regulated exposure than through prohibition, or does it show that his model depends on conditions—small scale, shared norms, a trusted overseer—that simply don't scale to a large modern nation?
