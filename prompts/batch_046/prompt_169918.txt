I'm currently revising a chapter of my dissertation on predictive modeling in behavioral economics, and a disagreement in my study group last Tuesday has left me unsure how to frame the status of unrealistic assumptions in agent-level forecasts. One participant—I'll call her A—defended a macro-level demand model that treats individuals as having perfect information, zero transaction costs, and lexicographically ordered preferences. When others objected that these premises are descriptively false of real agents, A invoked what she called Friedman's F-twist: in positive science, she argued, the realism of assumptions is irrelevant; only predictive accuracy at the aggregate level matters. She conceded that in physics unrealistic assumptions—point masses, frictionless planes—function as harmless approximations of underlying entities, but insisted that social-scientific idealizations differ only in degree, not in kind, from these physical approximations.

Another participant, B, countered with a reflexivity objection. He noted that physical entities do not alter their behavior upon being modeled, whereas in behavioral forecasting the model is often embedded in the institutional environment it describes: once policymakers design incentives around the assumption of rational optimization, agents adapt to those very incentives, so the unrealistic ontology becomes self-fulfilling. B therefore claimed that the F-twist fails in this domain—not because predictions are inaccurate, but because predictive success is achieved by reshaping reality to match the unrealistic premises. A responded that climate models face an analogous reflexivity—emission policies respond to model outputs—so B had not isolated a distinctive failure for agent-level theories. B then distinguished the two cases: in climate science the feedback loop is external to the ontology of the entities modeled (molecules, radiative forcing), whereas in behavioral models the unrealistic ontology—rational optimization—is internal to the mechanism generating the predictions. They reached an impasse there.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Is A correct that aggregate predictive success alone vindicates unrealistic agent-level assumptions? Is B correct that reflexivity undermines the F-twist specifically for models whose unrealistic premises are constitutive of the predicted mechanism, and does A's climate analogy defeat that claim? What are both missing—perhaps regarding whether the representational versus constitutive distinction actually holds, or whether predictive success can ever be cleanly separated from self-fulfillment in policy-relevant domains? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
