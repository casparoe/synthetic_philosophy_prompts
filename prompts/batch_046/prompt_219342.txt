I have been working through an extension of David Lewis's argument in "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers, 1997) and have constructed what I take to be a reductio that now strikes me as suspicious. The reasoning runs as follows.

(1) Lewis asks us to imagine that a burglar has been justly sentenced, and that a devoted, innocent friend volunteers to serve the prison term in his stead. Even if the authorities were to permit this, they would still have reason to apprehend and punish the burglar himself; the friend's suffering does not discharge the penal claim directed at the offender.

(2) The reason, on Lewis's view, is that penal liability is essentially agent-indexed: to be punished is for a specific moral subject to undergo deprivation in direct response to his own wrongdoing. Unless the deprivation is literally undergone by that indexed subject, the normative structure of retributive justice remains unsatisfied.

(3) Consequently, for any vicarious suffering to count as genuine penal substitution, there must obtain between substitute and original a relation that makes their suffering numerically identical — that is, metaphysical identity, or a union so complete that there is only one bearer of moral responsibility and experiential deprivation.

(4) Now apply this principle to what I shall call normative inheritance in collective agency. In international law, when a successor democratic regime pays reparations for atrocities committed by its authoritarian predecessor, the claim is treated as settled; victims and the international community do not regard themselves as retaining an unsettled grievance against the old regime's surviving members.

(5) Yet the successor regime is not metaphysically identical with its predecessor: it possesses a different constitution, different office-holders, and different normative powers of authorisation. By (3), its payments cannot discharge the predecessor's obligations, so the original penal-reparative claim should remain live.

(6) The same difficulty arises in corporate restructuring: a legally distinct successor corporation may assume the liabilities of its predecessor, and creditors treat the debt as discharged, despite the absence of any metaphysical fusion between the two corporate persons.

(7) Moreover, in ordinary moral practice we accept that a guarantor's payment settles a debtor's obligation, even though guarantor and debtor are plainly non-identical.

(8) Hence, if (3) is correct, then institutional reparations, corporate succession, and personal guarantorship are all systematically illusory: they leave the original obligations intact. But this conclusion is absurd.

(9) Therefore, one of the premises (1)–(3) must be abandoned.

My difficulty is that I should like to preserve Lewis's central conclusion — that ordinary penal substitution in criminal justice is incoherent — while avoiding the global scepticism about vicarious discharge that (3) seems to generate. Which step should I reject? In particular, is there a principled way to deny (3) without thereby licensing the very substitution in the burglar case that Lewis rightly rejects? Or is the error rather in (2), and if so, what does abandoning the agent-indexed conception of punishment cost us in explaining why the innocent friend's offer is indeed futile? I should be grateful for a precise diagnosis, together with an account of what each alternative forces us to pay.
