In my upper-division course on existentialist philosophy, a visiting lecturer offered a summary of Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom and bad faith that I want to check before I write my paper on it. She presented it roughly like this. Sartre holds that human consciousness is radically free because it is never identical with anything it is: there is no fixed human nature, no psychological determinism, and no essence that could constrain choice. Bad faith is essentially a form of self-deception in which we flee from this freedom by identifying with our social roles or past actions, treating our transcendence as if it were pure facticity; the classic illustration is the café waiter who plays at being nothing but a waiter. She further claimed that because freedom is absolute, any appeal to external constraints—whether institutional roles, physical coercion, or severe mental illness—must always be bad faith, since consciousness retains the power to reinterpret every situation. She also tied this to Sartre's analysis of being seen and objectified by others, arguing that bad faith is primarily a defensive reaction against the judgment of the other rather than a flight from one's own freedom. From these premises she concluded that Sartre's philosophy eliminates all moral excuses, and therefore renders moral praise and blame incoherent: if every action is an equally unconstrained choice of values, the distinction between free and compelled action collapses, leaving no ground for attributing merit or fault. I want to know whether this is a defensible reading. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? For each part, please indicate what Sartre would actually accept, what is a common pedagogical simplification that needs qualification, and what is a straightforward error—paying particular attention to how freedom relates to facticity, to whether bad faith is best understood through the phenomenon of the other, and to whether the conclusion about moral responsibility actually follows from Sartre's premises once they are corrected.
