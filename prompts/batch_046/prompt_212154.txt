I'm writing for a general-interest science and ideas publication and have been exploring whether the familiar demandingness objection to consequentialism relies on an implicit assumption that moral reasoning itself carries no moral weight—an assumption that collapses once we treat "deliberative overhead," the cognitive, emotional, and temporal cost of identifying and verifying the right action, as a genuine cost that any adequate theory must internalize rather than treat as an external excuse. Most classroom treatments compare the personal sacrifice demanded by act consequentialism with the sacrifices permitted under a rights-based or satisficing framework, but they compare the two theories on unequal epistemic footing: they ask how much the agent must give up, not how much she must spend to know what giving up is required.

Please design a realistic, one-paragraph scenario—set in a hospital administration office or a mid-sized workplace, with no trolleys or exotic technology—in which an agent guided by act consequentialism is required to make a smaller total personal sacrifice than an agent guided by a genuinely optimal satisficing rule, specifically because the consequentialist can halt her computation once she identifies a clearly dominant option, while the satisficer must continuously verify that no hidden obligation crosses her threshold, and that verification process itself consumes resources (time, emotional energy, professional reputation) that the theory must count as part of the moral burden. After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning each theory produces, name the structural feature—deliberative overhead internalized differently, threshold ambiguity, or epistemic cost asymmetry—that reverses the usual demandingness direction, and explain what happens if we remove that feature: does the case collapse back into the standard objection, or does it show that the objection was always comparing theories on unequal footing? Finally, explain how a defender of the view that comes out worse—likely the satisficing or rule-based view—would attack the case, particularly whether she can reject the internalization of deliberative costs without undermining her own standard appeal to practical feasibility.
