I am working through how hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy operate in recent political historiography, and I want to test whether they can do genuine explanatory work or merely redescribe outcomes we already know occurred. Take the 2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum and the eighteen months that followed: a result decided by roughly 52 to 48 per cent, followed almost immediately by a public discourse that treated the margin as an overwhelming mandate, that reconstructed the campaign's chaotic, multi-causal dynamics into a single story about the people versus the elite, and that treated the subsequent economic turbulence and constitutional friction as either inevitable consequences of the vote or, conversely, as proof that the vote had been the correct diagnosis all along.

Using the literature on creeping determinism and retrospective coherence, analyse how these two biases would predict the shape of post-referendum narration. Be precise about the mapping: who are the narrators, what counts as the evidence they are reorganising, and which specific counterfactuals — for instance, a remain victory by an equally narrow margin, or a delayed Article 50 notification — get compressed or erased. Then tell me where the theory breaks down: are there features of the Brexit aftermath — say, the rapid institutional adaptation of the civil service, or the persistence of regional divergence in polling — that the narrative-fallacy framework cannot accommodate without forcing the data? Finally, consider whether the episode supports a stronger claim I have been considering, which I call counterfactual proximity compression: the idea that the closer a counterfactual outcome was in causal space, the more aggressively hindsight bias works to deny its plausibility, not merely its actuality. Does the Brexit case illustrate this, and does it suggest that narrative fallacy and hindsight bias are not two separate errors but a single mechanism with two temporal directions?
