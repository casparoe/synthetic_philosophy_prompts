The Duhem-Quine thesis is often introduced with the analogy of curve-fitting: given a finite scatter of data points, infinitely many mathematical curves can pass through them all, so selecting one theory is said to be like picking one curve—underdetermined by evidence and settled only by convention or simplicity. Lay out the explicit mapping—data points to observations, curve parameters to theoretical posits, the fitting procedure to inference—and then press precisely on where it fractures. Do scientific rivals differ only in adjustable parameters, or do they also diverge in law-structure, ontological commitments, explanatory integration, and the auxiliary hypotheses required to connect them to observation? Identify what the geometric image quietly smuggles in, especially the assumption that all rival “curves” are permanently empirically equivalent and that no rational basis for choice exists beyond aesthetic preference. Finally, assess whether the skeptical conclusion—that evidence never uniquely determines theory—survives once you distinguish transient underdetermination, which further observation may resolve, from the permanent kind Quine emphasized, and once you account for background constraints and auxiliary assumptions that are not themselves part of the curve being fitted. If the analogy collapses on these points, suggest a more accurate image for what underdetermination actually threatens in scientific practice.
