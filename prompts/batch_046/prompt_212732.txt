As a retired physical chemist who has recently begun studying metaphysics, I have been working through the problem of material constitution—how a statue can be composed of clay yet appear to have different conditions for persistence and destruction than the clay itself. I am familiar with three main positions: the view that the statue and the clay are constitutionally related but distinct objects; the view that objects are essentially tied to their parts and therefore the statue just is the clay; and the four-dimensionalist account that treats both as different temporal parts or stages of a single spatiotemporal worm. I would like you to restate each of these three positions using only the conceptual vocabulary of isotopic tracer chemistry—specifically, isotopic labeling, tracer recovery, reaction intermediates, conservation of atomic number, the distinction between an atom's trajectory and the functional identity of the compound it temporarily occupies, and the conditions under which an intermediate is said to persist versus to be destroyed. Translate every metaphysical claim precisely into this chemical framework, without importing untranslated philosophical terms. Then provide the residue report: which parts of the debate map cleanly onto tracer dynamics, which can only be expressed by distorting chemical concepts—perhaps by treating a compound's functional role as analogous to an object's essential properties—and which have no counterpart whatsoever in atomic tracking methods. In particular, examine whether the intuition that squashing the statue destroys it while the clay survives corresponds to the loss of a reaction intermediate's functional identity despite the continued traceability of its constituent atoms, or whether that analogy covertly introduces ontological commitments about discrete objects that tracer studies do not require. I want to determine whether my chemical vocabulary genuinely lacks the resources to capture a real metaphysical distinction, or whether the philosophical disagreement is largely an artifact of vocabulary that has no operational analog in the laboratory.
