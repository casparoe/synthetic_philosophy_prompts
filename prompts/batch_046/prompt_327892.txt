I'm drafting the final essay exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that has worked through collective cognition, functionalism, and the boundaries of mental ascription. Rather than asking students to recount Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue" dialogue—where Aunt Hillary and the anteater debate whether an ant colony thinks—I want a question that uses the dialogue's framing only as a point of departure, then forces students into a more technical and less rehearsed problem. Specifically, the prompt should ask them to assess whether an ant colony could be a subject of mental states under the assumption that the colony operates in something like a "fugue-state" of distributed information processing: no individual ant represents the colony's goal, yet the system tracks environmental gradients with belief-like stability conditions across temporal scales that outlive any single ant. The exam should require them to disentangle three issues that the dialogue tends to blur together—representational coherence, functional integration, and the need for a unified phenomenological boundary—while confronting the possibility that mental ascription might depend on functional coherence without requiring subjective unity. The question must be answerable in about 75 minutes, should be pitched so that a student who has merely memorized the characters and levels of organization receives little credit, and should reward candidates who can apply distinctions between derived and original intentionality, between system-level tracking and personal-level experience, and between explanatory utility and ontological commitment.

Please provide: (1) the essay question itself, clearly worded and self-contained; (2) a model answer of approximately 500 to 600 words that demonstrates the necessary conceptual moves—distinguishing functional integration from phenomenological unity, addressing whether belief-like stability conditions require a singular subject, and evaluating the "fugue-state" hypothesis—without treating any contested position (such as panpsychism, strong emergence, or eliminativism about collective minds) as the settled consensus; and (3) a marking scheme organized around four must-hit points totaling 20 marks, each with its specific point value, a concise description of what earning those marks requires, and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point (for example, conflating the absence of a central controller with the absence of any representational state, or assuming that functional coherence automatically entails consciousness). Throughout the model answer and the marking scheme, please separate what is broadly accepted in contemporary philosophy of mind—such as that functionalist criteria do not by themselves establish phenomenal consciousness—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—such as whether distributed biological systems can satisfy conditions for original intentionality or whether subjective unity is a necessary condition for mental ascription—and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
