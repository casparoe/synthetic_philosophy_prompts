I'm drafting a feature on whether moral emotions can be engineered at the societal level, specifically whether a culture can deliberately choose guilt over shame, or vice versa, rather than inheriting them as an anthropological given. To avoid the usual survey of Benedict-style taxonomies, I'd like a short story set in the near-future island polity of Varn, which—following a generational referendum—dismantled its private guilt rituals and replaced them with state-sponsored "Accountability Theaters": public, acoustically designed performances of shame intended to repair social bonds through collective witnessing. The protagonist should be Dr. Elara Voss, an archivist who, while cataloguing the referendum's founding papers, uncovers references to a largely forgotten 1970s field study—recorded only in Varn's internal archives and said, according to those records, to have been conducted in post-war Okinawa—that claimed to quantify "emotional sovereignty," or a polity's capacity to institutionalize one moral affect and suppress another. Through Voss's observation of a citizen undergoing the Theater, her discovery that private guilt has not vanished but migrated into opaque, algorithmic self-audits that citizens never discuss, and her growing suspicion that the auditorium's acoustic architecture manufactures empathy rather than genuine remorse, the narrative should dramatize the central conceptual tension: can a culture truly choose its dominant moral emotion, or does the attempt merely drive the rejected one underground into privatized, hidden forms?

Please avoid bibliographic lists, reading suggestions, or citations; I'm working from memory and rough notes for this draft, and the piece should stand on its conceptual architecture alone. The tone should be literary and precise—closer to conceptual fiction than to allegory—with the philosophical question emerging from sensory and institutional detail rather than from expository essaying. I want the story to ask, through scene and image, whether guilt and shame are interchangeable instruments of social control or whether they possess an irreducible structure that resists institutional redesign.
