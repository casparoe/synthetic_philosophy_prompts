I am writing as a postgraduate student on our university’s module in Global Political Ethics, and I should like to apply the analytical framework we have developed in this term’s seminars on corruption, clientelism, and cultural pluralism.

During the course, our tutor has offered consistent verdicts on five case studies we examined in detail. First, a senior Japanese bureaucrat in the Ministry of Economy who, over fifteen years, received seasonal gifts (oseibo) and year-end tokens from procurement contractors with whom his division maintained long-standing relations. The gifts were modest in value but symbolically significant, with an implicit expectation of continued commercial harmony. Our tutor judged that this was not corruption, arguing that within Japanese administrative culture these exchanges maintain reciprocal social obligations without distorting specific procurement decisions, and that imposing Western transparency norms in this context would constitute normative imperialism.

Second, a Brazilian municipal mayor who distributed public-works employment exclusively to members of his extended kin network, justifying the practice as caring for one’s own in a setting where state welfare was largely absent. Our tutor judged this to be corruption, on the grounds that it converted public office into private patronage and violated even local constitutional commitments to impersonal administration.

Third, a British Member of Parliament who accepted an all-expenses-paid “fact-finding” visit to a luxury resort for himself and his family, funded by a pharmaceutical company with regulatory matters pending before his committee. No explicit vote-trading agreement existed. Our tutor judged this corruption, because it created a conflict of interest that undermined institutional impartiality regardless of whether a direct quid pro quo could be proven.

Fourth, a Ghanaian customary chief who accepted a goat and grain from a family whose land dispute he had just adjudicated, presented as traditional gratitude offered after the ruling rather than before it. Our tutor judged this not corruption, since within the customary legal framework the chief’s authority was partly sustained through such reciprocity, and refusal would violate the internal norms of respect that legitimised his judicial role.

Fifth, a Chinese hospital surgeon who routinely accepted cash-filled red envelopes from patients’ families to ensure prioritisation for non-urgent but serious procedures. Our tutor judged this corruption, arguing that even where culturally normalised, it violated the universal duty of medical triage by urgency and caused direct harm through queue-jumping.

These five verdicts appear jointly consistent with more than one reconstructible principle. One principle holds that corruption is betrayal of the obligations internal to one’s institutional role, assessed according to the norms of that role’s own cultural framework. Another holds that corruption is any instrumentalisation of public or institutional authority to secure private benefit, irrespective of cultural framing. A third holds that corruption is any deviation from the impartial, transparent, rule-governed procedures characteristic of liberal democratic institutions, applied universally.

The new case is as follows. A senior admissions officer at a Scandinavian university—operating within a system that formally prohibits favouritism and emphasises egalitarian access—accepts an unpaid honorary patronage of a wealthy applicant’s family charitable foundation. The role requires attending two annual gala events and being named on letterhead, but carries no decision-making power over admissions. The family has made substantial donations to the university’s general endowment over many years, though never to the admissions office specifically. The officer accepts, explaining that in Scandinavian civic culture refusing such an honour would be seen as insulting and damaging to community relations, and notes that there is no evidence the admission decision was influenced.

Please predict our tutor’s verdict on whether this constitutes corruption. Identify the closest analogues among the five precedents, and state which reconstructed principle best explains her likely reasoning. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, specify precisely what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—structurally different from the five above—that would best distinguish the competing principles you have identified. Please write your answer in British English.
