I'm planning a single fifty-minute session for philosophy undergraduates on whether an ant colony can possess a mind, centered on Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue in which Aunt Hillary — an ant colony — converses with an anteater about her own consciousness. I want the session to avoid both the pop-science metaphor of the colony as "just like a brain" and the opposite dismissal that this is pure anthropomorphism. The philosophical work I need them to grasp is Hofstadter's argument about descriptive levels: consciousness may be real at the colony-level description without being present in any individual ant, and without being a mysterious extra substance. Design the explanatory arc for me: which prior concept — perhaps the distinction between a system's components and the patterns those components realize — must be secured before Aunt Hillary sounds like anything other than a category error; what carrying example should replace the familiar "brain as computer" analogy, and why that analogy actively misleads here; the sequence of misconceptions students will generate — I anticipate first "this is just anthropomorphizing insects," then "if no ant experiences anything, the colony can't either," then "so consciousness is really nothing but ants bumping into each other" — and the exact point in the arc where each should be intercepted; how to use the fugue structure of Hofstadter's dialogue itself — the way voices enter, overlap, and resolve at different registers — to illustrate level-jumping rather than treating the dialogue as mere decoration; one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether a student understands the level-relativity claim or has merely adopted the slogan that "colonies are conscious"; and what to deliberately omit — specifically, do not bring in panpsychism or integrated information theory, since those are distinct debates about the substrate of consciousness rather than about descriptive levels. The plan should specify what to leave out at this stage and say so explicitly.
