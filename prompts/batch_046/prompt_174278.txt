You are a professional philosopher of medicine with expertise in practical ethics, philosophy of personal identity, and normative theory. I am drafting a proposal for a three-month summer research fellowship—intended to yield a 6,000-word paper for an interdisciplinary committee of philosophers and clinicians—and I need you to turn a half-formed idea into a precise, deliverable research plan. Please think carefully and begin with brief conceptual brainstorming before drafting the formal proposal.

My idea concerns advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, but I want to move past the familiar framing in which the only question is whether personal identity survives severe dementia. Instead, I propose that we distinguish three separable authority claims an earlier self might make through a directive: a predictive claim about what the later self will want or value; a normative claim about what counts as harm, benefit, or dignity regardless of the later self’s current preferences; and a jurisdictional claim that the earlier self retains decision-making authority over specific domains even if the later self remains competent to choose otherwise. I suspect that existing accounts—whether they ground directives in autonomy, well-being, or identity continuity—tend to collapse these three dimensions, and that separating them reveals hard cases where authority should hold in one dimension but fail in another.

Write the proposal with the following components: (1) a sharply formulated research question that has a determinate answer yet remains genuinely open; (2) an explanation of why standard positions in bioethics and philosophy of identity leave this specific tripartite gap unaddressed; (3) a month-by-month method describing the actual conceptual work—such as constructing minimal test cases that isolate each authority dimension, drawing analogies from constitutional or contractual delegation, and testing whether identity discontinuity undermines one claim but not the others—along with a justification for why this conceptual approach is preferable to an empirical study or a conventional literature review; (4) a clear statement of what a successful result looks like (for example, a taxonomy of directive-types matched to the authority dimensions they invoke, plus validity conditions for each) and what would count as failure (for instance, if the three dimensions cannot be conceptually separated, or if identity continuity turns out to be necessary for all three); and (5) an honest scope assessment, including which parts of the broader dementia-and-autonomy debate you will deliberately bracket to keep the project feasible in three months. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or requests for specific readings; I will supply references myself. The tone should be formal and suitable for a grant committee that values philosophical rigor over empirical data collection.
