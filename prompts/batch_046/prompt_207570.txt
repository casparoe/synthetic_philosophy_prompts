There's this weird thing in how AI labs currently do alignement evaluation. They spend tons of effort testing for deceptive alignment—basically checking if a model will pretend to be safe during testing then act different in deployment—using one-shot jailbreak prompts and static benchmark tests. But then they deploy the same architecure in agentic setups—coding agents, long research loops—where the model runs for thousands of steps with tools, and there's no real protocol checking if strategic deception could unfold slowly across turns. Plus, the whole framing treats the model like it has a contiuous self that can pretend and then reveal true colors, but technically every forward pass is fresh computation with no persistent internal state except what's in the context window. So what philosophical commitments about personal identity, agency, or the nature of deception does this practice presuppose? Does it require treating the context window as constituting a diachronic agent? Or can deception happen without that kind of continuity? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics, say whether the practice strictly needs that commitment or if it's just sloppy framing that could be replaced, and tell me what an evaluation protocol built on a different view of model identity—say treating each turn as an isolated event—would actually have to change.
