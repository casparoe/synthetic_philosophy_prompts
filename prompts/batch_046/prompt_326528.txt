Here are my intuitive verdicts on whether honoring a sunk cost is rational, taken case by case. I spent a decade training for an academic career I now find hollow; leaving despite the investment strikes me as rational, and staying merely to justify the years as fallacious. A village has maintained an irrigation cooperative for generations; if maintenance costs suddenly spike, abandoning the system strikes me as irrational, as though past collective labor creates an ongoing obligation rather than a reason to cut losses. I bought a non-refundable ticket to an event I no longer wish to attend; going anyway is irrational. I promised my dying mentor I would finish her unfinished manuscript, and after ten years of poor progress I am exhausted; breaking the promise feels like a moral failure rather than rational optimization, though I cannot articulate why. A research collective has spent five years defending a theoretical framework that accumulating evidence undermines; continuing to publish within it to protect prior investment strikes me as irrational, yet abandoning it feels like admitting a collective waste that undermines trust among collaborators. I am unsure about telling someone that a check is in the mail when it has not been sent: the deception is wrong, but framing it as irrationally honoring a past commitment seems off. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing identity-constitutive commitments from mere resource allocations, or specifying when forward-looking reasons may legitimately incorporate past investments—that accommodates these verdicts? If not, which must I drop? And what does the resulting view predict for a nation maintaining an expensive ceremonial institution for fifty years solely because dismantling it would render past expenditure meaningless?
