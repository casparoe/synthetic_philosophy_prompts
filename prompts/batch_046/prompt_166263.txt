I'm a program officer at an international health NGO, and every quarter I have to recommend funding splits between proposals that claim to help the worst off versus ones that just keep existing services running. I was arguing with a friend about prioritarianism — you know, the idea that benefits matter more when they go to people who are already badly off — and then they brought up the leveling-down objection, which I think is about not making everyone worse just to close a gap. I need to turn this into an actual proccedure I can run in about 45 minutes before the board meeting, since I don't have time to read philosophy papers and the boards needs a clear justification it can sign.

Write me a reusable checklist or flowchart-in-words for comparing two grant proposals under prioritarian rules. The role is me: I have an econ background but no philosophy PhD, I have rough cost-effectiveness numbers but not perfect well-being metrics, and I need the board to sign off. The procedure should have ordered steps: first identify the baseline well-being of each target group, then apply a priority weight, then check for hidden leveling-down (like cutting a stable program just to make distributions equal), then run what I'm calling a 'reverse-Rawls check' — ask whether rejecting the stronger proposal actually improves anyone's absolute position or just redistributes harm. It needs to handle three specific worked cases: (1) Proposal A funds malaria nets for a very poor rural group but requires reducing mental-health support for a stable middle-income urban group; (2) Proposal B and C both serve different severely disadvantaged populations with incommensurable needs (say, blindness prevention vs. severe chronic pain) and I can't rank their 'deficits' directly; (3) Proposal D is a high-cost service that mainly benefits already-advantaged patients, and the procedure tells me not to fund it — but cutting it doesn't free up enough resources to help the worst-off, so I'm left with less total welfare and no clear gain for anyone, which feels wrong. Mark every step where I still have to make a judgment call, say exactly what the procedure can't do (like comparing across generations or dealing with rights-based claims that aren't about welfare), and don't include any bibliography or citation request. Make it honest about misfiring.

Verdict: the core decision rule the checklist produces for any pair of proposals.
