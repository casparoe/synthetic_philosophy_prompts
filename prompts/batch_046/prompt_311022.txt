I am a senior software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I serve on our research institute's Allocation Review Board. We meet Thursday to vote on Phase-II funding for Project Mnemosyne, a proposed cognitive architecture whose designers claim it solves long-horizon credit assignment through a mechanism they term "retrospective coherence locking"—a process described only in their internal whitepaper and a single 2023 workshop preprint that has not been widely peer-reviewed. The board is divided. Two ML researchers on the panel defend the inside view: they can trace the mechanism step-by-step in simulated agents and argue that this specific structural feature distinguishes Mnemosyne from prior architectures, projecting an eighteen-month path to generalization. The outside-view members—an external program auditor and our risk officer—counter with base-rate data: architectures that made analogous coherence claims at Phase I have failed to generalize beyond toy domains in approximately seventy-three percent of tracked cases, with median schedule overruns of thirty-four months, and they recommend rejection or severe scaling back. The project leads have also invoked "epistemic modesty" to argue that while confidence should be calibrated downward, this very calibration supports continuing investment rather than withholding it. I need a concise written opinion—no more than two pages—to circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (full approval, conditional milestone-based approval with specific gates and deadlines, or rejection) within a defined time frame, treat the inside-view reasoning with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it as optimism bias, engage the statistical base-rate evidence honestly, and address whether epistemic modesty actually supports funding or rather counsels against it. Please separate what is settled in the literature on inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and forecasting from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single methodological prescription—whether always privileging base rates or always privileging structural reasoning—as if it were the consensus.
