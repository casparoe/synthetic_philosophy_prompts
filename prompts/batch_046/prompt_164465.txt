I am preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on strategic interaction, and I have become stuck on a conceptual point that sits between the standard treatments of one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemmas. Most expositions present the iterated version as resolving cooperation through the shadow of the future: if the discount factor is sufficiently high relative to the temptation payoff, cooperation can be sustained as a subgame-perfect equilibrium via strategies such as grim trigger. Conversely, the one-shot version is typically presented as having mutual defection as its unique rational outcome, with no structural appeal to future rounds.

In trying to think beyond these textbook cases, I have been working through a scenario I will call an asymmetric epistemic shadow. The idea is that in many actual repeated interactions, players do not share a common-knowledge termination rule or a publicly observed discount factor. Instead, each player holds a private, subjective distribution over the probability that interaction continues, and these beliefs may be heterogeneous, not common knowledge, and even endogenous—updating in response to the opponent's behavior in ways that the opponent may not fully observe or anticipate. This seems to blur the boundary between a genuinely one-shot interaction and an iterated one, because from an outside observer's perspective the game may have a definite endpoint, while from each player's internal epistemic standpoint there remains an uncertain prospect of continuation.

My confusion is conceptual rather than technical. If the rationality of cooperation depends on each player's private belief about the opponent's private belief about continuation, then the classic backward-induction argument for finitely repeated games—which relies on common knowledge of the endpoint—appears to dissolve. Yet I am unsure whether this genuinely opens conceptual space for cooperation in structurally finite interactions, or whether it merely pushes the reasoning problem back into an infinite regress of higher-order beliefs. Could you explain, with an intuitive but precise conceptual analysis, whether there remains a coherent distinction between one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemmas once continuation probabilities are private, heterogeneous, and endogenous? In particular, I want to understand whether the folk-theorem reasoning survives in some modified form or collapses entirely under these conditions, and what that implies for how we should think about the shadow of the future.
