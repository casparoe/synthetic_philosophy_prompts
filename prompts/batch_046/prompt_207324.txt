Is there a name for the following move in debates over whether a given state counts as a disorder? In disputes about severe anxiety patterns, certain autism-spectrum presentations, or metabolically adaptive traits that now carry health costs, one side often resists medicalization by appealing to evolutionary function—arguing the trait served an adaptive purpose in ancestral environments. The other side then counters with present-day harm or social dysfunction, treating that normative failure as proof of disorder. But the same interlocutors frequently invert these appeals: the evolutionary-defender will cite the trait’s deep biological roots as evidence that it is a “real,” hard-wired biomedical entity deserving clinical attention, while the harm-appealer will treat the severity of suffering as proof that the trait cannot be a genuine adaptation and must therefore be a malfunction. The evolutionary and normative criteria are thus deployed not as competing theories but as interchangeable warrants, each used to validate whichever conclusion was preferred at the outset, despite the fact that evolutionary adaptiveness and present dysfunction can pull in opposite directions. I have seen this circular trading occur across several domains, but I do not know whether philosophy of medicine has a specific term for it. If there is an established concept, how does the described pattern differ from it? If not, please coin and define the phenomenon precisely—stating its necessary conditions, how it differs from ordinary theoretical pluralism about health, and where its boundaries lie—without appealing to bibliographies or citation lists.
