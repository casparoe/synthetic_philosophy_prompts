Consider the following argument, advanced by a graduate student in our seminar on African philosophy and relational ontology, which seems to derive an intolerable conclusion from premises that a robust Ubuntu framework might well accept.

(1) On a strong Ubuntu conception, personhood is not an intrinsic, pre-social property but is constituted by the network of reciprocal recognition relations—the inter-subjective fabric—in which an individual stands.
(2) If personhood is genuinely constituted by these relations, rather than merely sustained or expressed by them, then the persistence conditions for the person are identical to the persistence conditions for the relevant relational network.
(3) Consequently, if that network undergoes a substantial rupture—say, the death of all kin, permanent exile from one's linguistic community, and the complete withdrawal of prior recognition—the conditions for that person's existence are eliminated.
(4) The biological human who remains after such a rupture is therefore not the same person, and may not qualify as a person at all.
(5) But relational networks are vulnerable not only to catastrophic rupture; they are in constant micro-flux: alliances shift, recognition is renegotiated, and the communal "we" that defines any "I" is never static from one day to the next.
(6) Therefore, on this view, diachronic personal identity is radically unstable: people are continuously being destroyed and replaced by new configurations of recognition.
(7) This makes a mockery of moral responsibility, long-term commitment, and autobiographical memory, all of which presuppose that the same person persists through ordinary relational change.
(8) Hence, Ubuntu-style relational personhood must be abandoned.

The student is convinced the argument is valid and that the only escape is to reject premise (1) and retreat to a substantive, intrinsic account of the self. I suspect there may be a more surgical option—perhaps an equivocation between constitution and contingent dependence, or an illicit slide from macro-rupture to micro-flux—but I want your diagnosis. Identify the specific premise or inferential step you think should be rejected, explain why it initially looks defensible from within a relational framework, and then price the alternatives: what must a relationalist pay to preserve (1) while blocking (6), and what must an intrinsicist pay to reject (1) altogether?

Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
