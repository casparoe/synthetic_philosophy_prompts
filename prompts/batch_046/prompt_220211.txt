The debate over whether sentience and moral status are graded—running from full persons through non-human animals and insects to artifacts like thermostats that might exhibit minimal functional analogues of harm-avoidance—tends to stall on familiar disagreements about where the boundary belongs. I am less interested in the boundary itself than in the conceptual architecture that would make any grading system institutionally workable. Imagine an interdisciplinary panel—philosophers, cognitive scientists, and policy advisors with no mandate to survey literature—given three years and modest resources to resolve conceptual bottlenecks for applied ethics in agriculture, AI governance, and ecological management. Which three open questions in this domain should they prioritize, in what order, and by what explicit criteria?

In framing your answer, treat the following two problems as central rather than peripheral. First, the “zero-point calibration” problem: is there a coherent distinction between entities possessing negligible but non-zero moral weight and entities possessing literally zero weight, and does that distinction survive aggregation—specifically, whether obligations to sufficiently large populations of near-zero entities can compound into obligations comparable to those owed to a single high-status being? Second, the cardinality problem: should gradation be treated as cardinal, permitting quantitative comparisons of welfare magnitude across degrees, or as merely ordinal, allowing only ranking without measurable intervals—and which practical decisions (resource allocation, risk thresholds, rights ascription) genuinely depend on resolving this?

For each prioritized question, explain: (a) why it is upstream of other debates in the area, (b) how tractable it is for conceptual analysts rather than empirical laboratories, and (c) which common policy implications—such as extending protections to insects, denying any moral consideration to simple AI systems, or weighing ecological damage—actually hinge on its answer and which are rhetorical distractions that survive regardless. Rank your recommendations explicitly, defend the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and neglectedness, and specify what conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list.
