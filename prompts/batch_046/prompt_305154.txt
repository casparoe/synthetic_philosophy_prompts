At a recent workshop on well-being, I listened to two colleagues—Mara, who works on motivation and affect, and Leo, who specializes in phenomenology—debate whether hedonism can survive the heterogeneity problem without collapsing into either a trivial taxonomy or a disguised desire theory. The exchange began when Mara claimed that sensory and intellectual pleasures are unified not by any shared phenomenal quality but by a functional signature: they are states that non-instrumentally reinforce the very activities that generate them, and they do so in the absence of any prior deficit or deprivation. Leo objected that this functional criterion either overincludes—relief from chronic pain reinforces the removal of pain without being a pleasure in the hedonically relevant sense—or underincludes, since the quiet satisfaction of recalling a completed project seems to involve no ongoing activity to reinforce. He pressed her: if there is no common "what-it-is-likeness," then hedonism loses its explanatory purchase on well-being and becomes merely a label for whatever happens to motivate us.

Mara responded by sharpening her account around what she called counterfactual stability: a pleasure is a state such that, holding fixed the subject's other preferences, they would choose to prolong or repeat the generating conditions even when no further instrumental goods are at stake. She argued that both the taste of a well-cooked meal and the resolution of a logical puzzle exhibit this stability, whereas relief fails it because the subject would not seek out the prior pain in order to experience the relief again. Leo countered that stability is an external behavioral marker, not an internal unity, and that hedonism traditionally promises to ground well-being in the intrinsic quality of experience, not in dispositional patterns. He proposed instead that all pleasures share a single structural feature—an evaluative direction of fit in which the subject represents the present state of affairs as already adequate, rather than as calling for change—though he conceded this makes pleasure structurally identical to certain non-hedonic forms of contentment. Mara then asked whether Leo's direction-of-fit criterion could distinguish the pleasure of being warmed by sunlight from the mere absence of feeling cold, since both seem to involve the same representational acceptance of one's thermal state. Leo admitted he had not settled the boundary but insisted that Mara's functionalism had smuggled in normative commitments about what counts as genuine reinforcement without explaining why those commitments should belong to hedonism rather than to a broader theory of rational agency.

Can you work through this exchange step by step and say, for each move, whether Mara or Leo is on stronger ground and why? In particular, I want to know whether Mara's counterfactual-stability test genuinely solves the heterogeneity problem or merely relocates it, whether Leo's direction-of-fit proposal avoids collapsing pleasure into general satisfaction, and whether either of them is missing a third option—perhaps one that treats heterogeneity not as a problem to be dissolved but as a feature that forces hedonism to become pluralist about well-being. Please do not simply declare an overall winner; adjudicate the specific arguments as they actually stand, and indicate where both sides share a false presupposition or overlook an obvious distinction.
