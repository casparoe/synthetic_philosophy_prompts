I was discussing with a friend last week the classic Caltech split-brain experiments of the 1960s and 1970s, and we found ourselves stuck on a framing that rarely appears in the standard literature. Rather than asking simply whether a split-brain patient is "one person or two", I want to analyse the episode through what I shall call a process-binding account of phenomenal unity: the claim that consciousness is unified not because it is instantiated by a single substance or substrate, but because it achieves a dynamically sustained temporal integration of information across modalities. The real episode is well known — Sperry and Gazzaniga's studies, in which the left hemisphere of a commissurotomy patient, lacking direct access to right-hemisphere visual or tactile information, would nevertheless construct verbal narratives explaining actions that the right hemisphere had in fact directed.

Please analyse this episode through the process-binding lens. First, map the case with discipline: identify the relevant actors (the patient as experimental subject, the left-hemisphere interpreter, the right-hemisphere control system, and the experimenter as observer), specify the task conditions (lateralised visual presentation, tactile matching across hands, verbal report versus non-verbal pointing), and state precisely what counts as evidence for a failure or preservation of binding. Second, apply the theory: does the confabulation phenomenon indicate a breakdown in temporal integration — a momentary dissolution of the binding process — rather than the presence of two independent streams of consciousness? Where does the account explain the data more naturally than substance-based unity, and where does it strain or remain silent — for example, in accounting for the patient's long-term post-surgical behavioural coherence outside the laboratory, or for the asymmetry that gives the left hemisphere's narrative authoritative status? Third, reverse the direction: what does the split-brain episode reveal about the process-binding account itself? Does it confirm that unity is genuinely processual, or does it expose that the account must implicitly reintroduce a substantive subject — perhaps the left-hemisphere interpreter — to make sense of why one binding failure produces authoritative false belief rather than mere behavioural noise? I should like the answer in British English, with careful distinctions between phenomenal unity, functional integration, and narrative authority, and without relying on any bibliographic apparatus I do not possess.
