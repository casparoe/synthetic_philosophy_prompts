I run a discussion workshop for adults—usually about ten people, ninety minutes—who have no formal philosophy background but keep returning to the same puzzle: why should I change my diet, my travel, or my vote when my single choice is swallowed by millions? I want a single participatory game with real, small stakes—tokens drawn from a shared pool, points that translate into a minor group reward or penalty—that runs in two consecutive rounds. In round one, each person independently decides whether to contribute a token to a collective harm that scales continuously, like emissions adding linearly; the rules should make it transparent that one token changes the total by a negligible amount and that the harm occurs regardless of any individual choice. In round two, the same group faces a collective decision—modeled on an election or a boycott—that only produces an outcome if participation crosses a hidden threshold, so that each person can see that their token is either redundant or decisive only in counterfactual worlds that never obtain. The payoff structure in both rounds should be arranged so that participants naturally arrive at the conclusion that their action “makes no difference,” but for structurally different reasons: continuous dilution in the first round, and threshold redundancy in the second. After playing, include a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical distinction between causal inefficacy and normative redundancy, identifies the exact moment in each round where participants are likely to resist the “no difference” conclusion and what that resistance costs, predicts what will go wrong with ten people in ninety minutes—such as one dominant speaker collapsing both rounds into “it’s all pointless” or the group missing the structural difference—and tells me how to prevent that collapse. Finally, be frank about which feature of the inefficacy problem—perhaps the moral weight of complicity or the non-instrumental reasons for integrity—cannot be reproduced by token mechanics and must be introduced by the facilitator in discussion rather than emerging from the rules themselves.
