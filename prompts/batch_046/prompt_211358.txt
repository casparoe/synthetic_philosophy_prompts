I'm currently serving on the governance-reform committee of a forty-member worker-owned cooperative that manufactures precision optical equipment. For fifteen years we've used an elected executive board, but over the past year it has become clear that long-serving members have accumulated disproportionate informal influence—controlling information flows, shaping nomination slates, and quietly penalizing dissent—without ever facing meaningful accountability. A faction now proposes abolishing elections for our strategic-planning body entirely and replacing them with pure sortition: randomly drawing five members from the full cooperative for single, non-renewable four-year terms. Their argument is that rotation eliminates careerism and captures the democratic intuition that every member is equally qualified to deliberate on collective ends. Another faction insists that this would be reckless in a firm with complex supply-chain contracts and regulatory obligations; they want to keep elections but impose strict cumulative eight-year term limits across all bodies, preserving continuity through institutional memory rather than personal tenure.

Trying to bridge these positions, I sketched a hybrid model: retain elected selection only for a narrow technical-audit committee subject to the eight-year cumulative cap, but fill the strategic-planning body by lot, with one modification—anyone drawn must serve a six-month structured apprenticeship alongside the outgoing body before assuming full voting authority. The intent is to preserve epistemic competence without allowing apprenticeship to become a credential that concentrates power in a trained elite. However, a colleague argued that this clause undermines the anti-careerist logic of sortition altogether, since it creates a predictable pipeline of "initiated" members who will dominate future deliberations and effectively reproduce the hierarchy we are trying to dissolve. I find the objection troubling because removing the apprenticeship seems to risk immediate organizational chaos, yet retaining it may indeed convert random selection into a staged ritual that legitimizes insider control. From the standpoint of democratic theory, is there a coherent philosophical defense of my hybrid, or does the apprenticeship requirement necessarily collapse the distinction between sortition-based equality and election-based expertise? And more concretely, does imposing cumulative term limits on the technical committee genuinely solve the career-politician problem, or does it merely displace it into informal advisory roles? I would value an analysis that treats the cooperative not as a metaphor for the state but as a real institutional design problem with its own normative logic.
