I am preparing material for a graduate seminar on pragmatism and philosophy of science, and my study group recently stalled on whether Rorty’s antirepresentationalism can survive the explanatory success of predictive systems—including contemporary large-scale language models—without collapsing into a silent instrumentalism. I want you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. On one side, place a committed Rortyan neopragmatist who treats belief, vocabulary, and scientific practice as tools for coping with an environment rather than representations of an intrinsic nature of reality, and who accepts that causal interaction with the world is real but denies that this interaction requires beliefs to “mirror” or “picture” it. On the other side, place a “functional representationalist” who grants Rorty’s demolition of the mirror-of-nature metaphor and rejects correspondence-as-picturing, yet insists that predictive success in both science and artificial systems still demands a substantive, non-vacuous notion of representation—something like structural mapping, inferential role, or predictive encoding that is not reducible to mere causal coping.

The deliverable should open with a numbered agreed-claims section: what both sides can sign regarding Rorty’s core arguments about belief and language, about what antirepresentationalism genuinely denies versus what it is often caricatured as denying, about whether causal interaction alone can ground the normative force of scientific disagreement, and about whether the success of predictive models can be explained without invoking any representational content whatsoever. This should be followed by a disagreements table. Each row must present one precise crux, with the Rortyan’s formulation of her own position in wording she would accept, and the functional representationalist’s formulation of his position in wording he would accept, the gap between them made exact rather than smoothed over. For each crux, specify the kind of case or argument both sides agree would bear on it—perhaps the internal structure of a well-predictive but apparently non-referential model, or a historical episode of radical vocabulary replacement in science—and record each side’s predicted outcome in advance. If any portion of the dispute turns out to be verbal—for instance, if “representation” has been defined into emptiness on one side—identify that portion explicitly and explain why. Write with formal precision, injecting enough specificity about predictive architecture and vocabulary change that the result cannot be answered by generic summaries of Rorty.

Please treat this as a constructive drafting task, not an adjudication of who is right. If my framing rests on a confused premise—say, if the functional representationalist position as defined has already conceded Rorty’s central point, or if the adversarial-collaboration format is inappropriate because one side is not actually a stable philosophical stance—say so plainly instead of producing a polished but hollow document. I also ask for a brief concluding note flagging any formulations in the joint statement that risk being slanted despite the attempt at neutrality, especially where the language of “tools” versus “maps” might smuggle in commitments that one side would reject.
