A colleague walked me into this and I can't find the exit. Here is an argument that Whig history is not just common but inescapable:

(1) Every historical explanation must select which past events to include, and selection requires a criterion of relevance.
(2) The only criterion that does not depend on an arbitrary choice is causal contribution to the state that needs explaining—the historian's present.
(3) Call this the modal fixed-point constraint: for any historian writing at time T, T is the non-arbitrary anchor because it is the actual world from which counterfactual dependencies are traced.
(4) So any historian necessarily weights past events by how they causally contributed to reaching T.
(5) But to weight events by their contribution to reaching T is precisely to treat the past as a progression toward the present—that is, Whig history.
(6) The standard anti-Whig reply is that we can study past agents on their own terms, explaining their actions by their own beliefs and values rather than by outcomes they never intended.
(7) However, deciding which beliefs and values are historically relevant requires selecting those that actually shaped outcomes—outcomes that, ex post, form part of the causal chain leading to T.
(8) Therefore even the anti-Whig historian selects past mental states for their causal efficacy in producing T.
(9) From (4) and (8): every historical method, whether self-consciously progressive or not, organizes the past by its causal trajectory toward the present.
(10) So the critique of Whig history is self-defeating; there is no historiographical stance outside it, only more or less explicit acknowledgment of the modal fixed-point.

This seems to prove too much: it makes anti-Whig history conceptually impossible. I feel like something slips between (4) and (5)—perhaps an equivocation between causal explanation anchored at the present and normative evaluation of that trajectory as progress—but I can't name the cost of giving up either side. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I'd like to keep both (2) and (6) if possible: I think explanation needs some anchor, and I think we really can understand agents on their own terms. If those are incompatible, say so plainly.

Please answer in American English. I want to invite pushback: if this argument rests on a confused premise—say, if "modal fixed-point constraint" is just jargon masking a simpler mistake—or if the task can't be done well because the steps conflate distinct issues, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
