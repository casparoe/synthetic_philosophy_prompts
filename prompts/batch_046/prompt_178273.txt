As a retired marine ecologist who now chairs the ethics review board of a small field-research foundation, I have spent the last year trying to make Kant's universalizability tests—specifically the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity—usable for our monthly evaluation of research proposals that involve direct intervention in wild populations. I do not need a historical exposition or bibliographic orientation; I need a reusable decision procedure that board members without philosophical training can run in approximately twenty minutes per proposal, with documented steps, explicit stopping conditions, and clear markers for where judgment rather than logic must take over.

The conceptual twist I want incorporated is my own adaptation: instead of testing only the human researcher's maxim in isolation, the maxim should be formulated as, "We may perform intervention I on population P to obtain knowledge K, provided that the conditions of P's continued ecological functioning are not treated as merely dispensable means." I want the contradiction test to examine whether universalizing this maxim presupposes the destruction or instrumentalization of the very biotic conditions that make the research coherent, and I want the humanity formulation to ask whether the affected organisms—sentient or not—are being treated solely as instruments for an end that could not itself survive universalization across similar ecological relations. Where this extension collapses into standard Kantian analysis, the procedure should say so; where it genuinely alters the verdict, it should flag that explicitly.

Please construct an ordered, step-by-step decision procedure: the precise questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure yields a clear "do not approve," the point at which it hands the decision to a values-based judgment because logical consistency is satisfied but ethical weight remains, and an honest account of what the procedure cannot settle. Keep terminology to a minimum, translating each Kantian move into the practical language of research ethics.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals.

First, implanting long-duration acoustic tags in an endangered cetacean population to map migration corridors; show how the procedure produces a conditional approval path with specific safeguards rather than an immediate rejection.

Second, lethal tissue sampling of a neurologically complex but abundant invertebrate for comparative genomics; here the procedure must turn on an empirical judgment about sentience, and you should mark precisely where that judgment enters, what evidence would be sufficient, and what the board should do when evidence is absent.

Third, temporary captivity of wild primates for cognitive experiments designed to inform anti-poaching policy—a proposal for which I personally feel an irreducible moral revulsion regardless of potential conservation benefit, but for which a rigorous application of the universalizability test, including my ecological extension, may actually yield approval. Show me exactly where the procedure's verdict and my personal response diverge, and state clearly whether the procedure has any resources to resolve that divergence or whether it must remain an external check.

Finally, identify the two or three categories of proposal for which this procedure will systematically misfire—whether because ecological interdependence is too diffuse to model, because sentience is irreducibly contested, or because universalization masks distributive injustice across generations of organisms—and indicate whether a simpler analog, such as treating the ecosystem as a patient with rights of non-maleficence, would survive the same audit or suffer parallel failures.
