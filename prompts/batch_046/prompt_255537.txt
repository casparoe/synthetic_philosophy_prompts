A recent essay summarizing Fernand Braudel's major study of the Mediterranean world for a general audience offers the following characterization: "Braudel proved that real history happens at the level of geography and climate, not human decisions. The longue durée is the study of structures so slow that individual events and people disappear entirely; the Battle of Lepanto was just a ripple on the surface of Mediterranean trade routes shaped by the Mistral winds and the olive belt. So Braudel's method demands that we treat politics, biography, and narrative as mere epiphenomena of deep ecological constraints, and that any historian who focuses on Philip II's intentions rather than the long-term erosion patterns of the Iberian coast is doing superficial work rather than science." Something about this feels like it pushes Braudel further toward determinism than I recall, but I cannot pinpoint exactly where. Is this summary fair? Which parts capture Braudel's actual hierarchy of temporalities and his critique of event-driven history, which parts conflate his anti-event stance with a denial of human agency altogether, and does the example of Lepanto versus the Mistral correctly represent how he uses specific cases? Also, does his framework actually entail that narrative history is unscientific, or is that an extrapolation imported by the summarizer?
