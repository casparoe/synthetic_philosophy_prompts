We kept returning to the familiar cases—sacrificing one to save five—but we also kept circling something neither of us could name: the way contractualist rejection seems to treat the victim’s reason as structurally isolated from the reasons of the beneficiaries, even when those beneficiaries are not merely passive recipients but are themselves bound by the very same principle being evaluated. I am now applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need to turn this half-formed idea into a formal, two-page research proposal.

My core question is this. The literature frames the aggregation problem as a binary conflict—either numbers count, yielding utilitarian-style aggregation, or they do not, yielding an individual veto. But I want to ask whether this framing ignores a relational dimension of reasonable rejection. Specifically, if we model rejection not as a set of independent pairwise relations between individuals and principles, but as a network in which some rejection nodes are structurally dependent on others, can contractualism generate a non-utilitarian, non-veto form of limited aggregation? In other words, does the victim’s direct reason to reject being sacrificed retain its full weight when the principle that permits the sacrifice also gives the saved parties their own contractualist reasons to reject being saved at that cost? I am tentatively calling this coupling between direct and beneficiary rejection the problem of coupled-rejection topology, and I want to know whether formalizing it dissolves the standard dilemma or merely relocates it.
