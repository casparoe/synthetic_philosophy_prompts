I'm trying to sort out my own intuitions about conceptual slippage inside a very narrow craft domain—specifically, the classification of hand-thrown ceramic water filters made in a small regional tradition that relies on a particular local clay and a two-stage wood-firing protocol. I want to know whether there's a coherent principle that distinguishes a copycat imitation from a genuinely fluid concept, and from mere individual misuse, or whether these judgments just pull in different directions. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases. When a workshop inherits the original molds and firing schedule but relocates to a neighboring valley with geologically identical clay, I think the result is still an authentic filter, not a copycat. When another workshop 3D-scans the original molds and replicates the form using industrial ceramic slurry fired in an electric kiln, I call that a clear copycat: the functional profile is preserved, but the causal and material lineage is broken. When, over two generations, the entire community gradually lowers the peak firing temperature because local firewood becomes scarcer, and the filters consequently grow more porous, I think the concept of "proper filter" itself has shifted—this is fluid concept change, not imitation. But if a single artisan, trained in the new porous standard, keeps making the older dense version and insists that everyone else's output is "fake," I do not think her private usage has shifted the communal concept; she is misapplying a fluid concept, not preserving an original one. The pair that keeps tripping me up involves transmission without contact: when two isolated workshops, with no historical connection, independently develop filters with nearly identical pore structures using different local clays, I judge that neither is copying the other—it's convergent development—yet I still want to place both within the same conceptual category of "filter," not as copycats of each other. By contrast, when a workshop learns the technique solely from a translated technical manual, never having handled an original or met a practitioner, then reproduces the form and firing protocol with authentic local clay, I feel pulled both ways: part of me wants to call this a copycat because the transmission chain is documentary rather than embodied, but another part resists, since the material and causal conditions are genuinely restored. Is there a principle—perhaps tracking causal-historical chains, communal acceptance, or functional role—that can capture all of these verdicts without excessive complexity? If there is one, what does it predict about the manual-learning case, and does it force me to overturn my judgment about the isolated convergent workshops? If no single principle works, which of my verdicts should I drop to keep the account simplest, and what does that revision reveal about whether fluidity is really in the concept or only in our tolerance for variation?
