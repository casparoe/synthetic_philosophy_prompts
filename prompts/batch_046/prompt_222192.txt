I have been working through a specific variant of the interface-theoretic view in my philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I want you to stress-test it with genuine rigor rather than with the usual objections. The position—call it the Active-Falsification Interface Thesis—holds that phenomenal consciousness is best understood not merely as an absence of access to neural dynamics, but as an adaptively constructed misrepresentation: the brain generates a "user illusion" of seamless qualitative unity, continuous agency, and transparent emotional valence that bears no structural resemblance to the discontinuous, distributed, and computationally opaque processes it tracks. Unlike standard representationalism, which allows that experience is approximately true of brain states, this view insists the interface is deliberately deceptive in the way a desktop icon is deceptive about file architecture; unlike eliminativism, it grants that there is a real functional structure being monitored, but denies that the phenomenal format reveals anything about its nature. I want you to map out exactly what would count against this thesis: which empirical findings, conceptual distinctions, or phenomenological observations would be decisive, which would merely be awkward, and what its own defenders should concede as genuine defeat conditions rather than as misunderstandings to be dissolved away. Please distinguish evidence that would genuinely falsify the view from qualifications that merely insulate it, assess whether retreating to "how things seem to the subject" renders the thesis unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal vice for a claim of this kind.
