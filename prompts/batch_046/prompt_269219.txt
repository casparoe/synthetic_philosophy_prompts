I have been working through a long-standing puzzle in a discussion group that compares analytic philosophy of personal identity with classical Buddhist accounts of anattā, and the conversation has reached an impasse that feels structurally odd rather than merely stubborn. On one side, the analytic reductionists—broadly in the line that treats psychological continuity and connectedness as constitutive of personal identity—accept that there is no Cartesian ego and propose that the self is a conventional construct, metaphysically lightweight but practically indispensable for ethics and responsibility. On the other side, the Buddhist interlocutors—drawing on Abhidharma and early suttanta frameworks—insist that anattā is not merely the denial of a substantial soul but a soteriological claim: what must be seen through is not just a metaphysical entity but the pervasive, pre-reflective sense of mineness or ownership, often tied to clinging at the five aggregates and to sakkāya-diṭṭhi, that binds experience into an object of identification and perpetuates dukkha. Both sides agree that there is no enduring, simple, immaterial substance. Both sides agree that mental life is causally and psychologically continuous. Yet the reductionist concludes that this descriptive agreement exhausts the matter, while the Buddhist concludes that something profound has been missed—namely, that the very grammar of “I am” or “this is mine” operates at an experiential and ethical register that descriptive metaphysics does not reach, and that uprooting it requires a transformation rather than a theory.

The disagreement has stalled precisely because neither side treats the other’s central move as a factual error. The analytic philosopher tends to read the Buddhist claim as an overly dramatic restatement of reductionism; the Buddhist tends to read the analytic claim as answering the wrong question altogether, solving a puzzle about survival conditions while leaving untouched the task of extinguishing identification. I am inclined to hypothesize that this is neither a hidden empirical dispute about neuroscience or phenomenology, nor purely verbal, since both sides understand “self” differently yet can translate between usages with some care. Rather, I suspect the sides are answering different questions under a single heading—one about the conditions under which a person persists through change, the other about the conditions under which suffering arises and can be released—while each assumes its question is the fundamental one. But I want a sharper diagnosis: is there a substantive metaphysical disagreement concealed beneath the apparent agreement, perhaps about whether phenomenal mineness is an irreducible feature of consciousness that survives descriptive reduction? Or is the impasse a sign that “personal identity” is ill-posed when imported across these frameworks, functioning like a family-resemblance term that collects incompatible criteria? And if the diagnosis is that they are answering different questions, what would progress look like—would it require a formal distinction between descriptive and soteriological reduction, an empirically tractable result about whether mineness can be dissolved in advanced contemplative states without disrupting psychological continuity, or a change of vocabulary that abandons “self” in favor of separate discourses for metaphysics and liberation? Or should we conclude that no further progress is expected because the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about whether philosophy’s task is to describe reality or to transform the subject who describes it?
