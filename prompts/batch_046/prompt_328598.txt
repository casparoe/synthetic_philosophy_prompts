I am drafting a novel about an archivist who catalogues moral obligations in a world emptied of agents, and I want to stage the central philosophical difficulty for a workshop of fourteen writers and thinkers—ninety minutes, a seminar room with movable chairs—so that they feel, rather than merely discuss, the force of free-floating rationales: normative reasons that persist without any reasoner for whom they are reasons. Design a structured deliberation or game with explicit rules, assigned roles, a timeline broken into phases, and small but real stakes—perhaps reputation tokens that determine who shapes the shared fictional ending, or a collective penalty if consensus is not reached—that forces the group to adjudicate a conflict between two "orphaned covenants," structural debts left behind after both promisor and promisee have vanished, without allowing them to appeal to anyone's welfare, preference, or dignity.

The participants should construct the sequence themselves: first defending each rationale on purely structural grounds, then attempting to weigh them against each other, and only afterwards discovering whether they have smuggled agent-relative considerations back into their reasoning. Provide a debrief that maps each predictable breakdown—the urge to invoke hidden survivors, the collapse of comparative weight into aesthetic symmetry, the refusal to decide—onto a specific feature of the free-floating view, and be frank about which metaphysical commitment the exercise cannot reproduce, namely whether such reasons genuinely exist or merely operate as heuristic residues of abandoned agency. Also warn me, with specific instructions, what will go wrong the first time I run this—particularly the near-certain relapse into agent-centered language—and how to correct it in the moment without breaking the fiction of the exercise. Do not ask for bibliographies or citations; I need only the runnable architecture, the honest limits of the simulation, and the philosophical anatomy that connects each moment of play to the idea itself.
