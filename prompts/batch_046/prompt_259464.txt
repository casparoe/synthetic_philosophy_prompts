Question: Dennett describes the self as a center of narrative gravity — like the center of gravity in physics, not a little homunculus inside the brain. Explain what he means by that, contrast it with Hume's bundle view and with the Cartesian theater idea, then respond to this objection: if the self is just a story we tell, there's no real subject who suffers or chooses, so moral responsibility collapses. How does Dennett reply?

Student anwer:
Dennett says the self is like a center of gravity: it's not a physical thing, just a point we calculate to predict movement. In people it's the point where beliefs, memories and desires converge in a biography, so it's a real abstract pattern — not a Cartesian theater with an inner spectator watching experiences. Hume said there's no self, only a bundle of impressions; Dennett rejects the ego too, but he thinks the narrative actually pulls that bundle together into one genuinely unified self. On responsibility, Dennett argues the narrative self works just like the Cartesian ego in practice — it observes its own story and decides what to do from that viewpoint. But since it's constructed, moral blame is really just a social convention we use to keep people in line. So the self isn't an illusion, it's a functional fiction that still does real causal work.
