I just got out of a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about the unilateralist's curse, and I think we both kept circling a version of the problem that standard treatments miss: the curse is not only about agents acting on private first-order judgments, but about asymmetric second-order access—each person has granular, private knowledge of their own reasoning calibration and bias history, while possessing only noisy, indirect signals about the calibration of everyone else. This makes unilateral deferral rational for some participants and catastrophically wrong for others, and there is no shared prior that can adjudicate the difference without collapsing back into the very unilateralism it was meant to solve. You are a professional philosopher with expertise in social epistemology and formal decision theory. Before proposing any design, please take a moment to brainstorm in writing: first, explain how this meta-cognitive asymmetry alters the dynamics of collective action compared to the standard first-order version; second, identify two structural mechanisms a live classroom simulation could use to make participants feel that asymmetry in their bones rather than merely understand it conceptually. Then, design a complete, runnable 90-minute seminar activity for 16 upper-level philosophy or political-theory graduate students. The activity must involve assigned roles, concealed information cards, and a point-based payoff scheme with real but small collective stakes—points should determine whether the entire group receives a shared bonus or suffers a shared penalty—so that participants face genuine tension between acting unilaterally, deferring to the group, or disclosing their private calibration data. Arrange the rules so that students discover the problem through play: they should experience that even rational, good-faith attempts to correct for overconfidence, given unequal meta-cognitive access, drive the group toward outcomes worse than either pure unilateralism or pure deference would have produced. Include a precise minute-by-minute timeline; the full rules and payoff structure; the roles, including at least one hidden "calibration auditor" who possesses privileged second-order information unknown to the others; a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto a feature of the curse, with explicit attention to the second-order asymmetry I described; an honest statement of which formal feature—likely the absence of a common prior or the threat of infinite meta-regress—this physical simulation necessarily fails to reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply verbally; and a brief prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time you run the activity, together with a concrete recovery strategy. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or attributions of specific texts.
