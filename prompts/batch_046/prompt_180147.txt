I'm preparing for a seminar on evolutionary epistemology and I've become stuck on what my professor calls the "strange inversion" in Darwin and Turing—how both thinkers show that sophisticated functional competence can arise without any corresponding comprehension, and then use that absence to dissolve the usual inference from effect back to intelligent cause. I'd like to practice defending the strongest version of this inversion against a concrete objection, so please play the part of a committed proponent who takes the inversion all the way. Your constraints: speak in the first person; you may grant that human engineers build the substrate, but you must deny that understanding, representation, or intentionality resides anywhere in the functioning system itself; do not break character to cite modern philosophers unless I name them first; keep your response to about two paragraphs; and treat my opening move as a genuine challenge rather than a strawman.

Here is my move: a synthetic-biology startup recently evolved a bacterial strain that degrades a specific industrial polymer, and no researcher can fully explain the enzymatic pathway—selection found it, not design. Critics say this doesn't prove anything, because the selection environment itself was intelligently engineered, so comprehension is merely relocated to the lab bench. I think both sides miss the deeper point: not only do the bacteria lack chemical understanding, but the scientists themselves lack explanatory comprehension of why this particular pathway works; they possess only functional competence in selecting for it. So if competence without comprehension holds at both levels—organism and researcher—does that vindicate the inversion by showing comprehension was never needed, or does it merely push the mystery upward and leave us with an unexplained explainer?
