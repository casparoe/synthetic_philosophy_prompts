In a philosophy of mathematics and mind seminar I have been convening, the debate over Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments has settled into what feels less like disagreement and more like parallel monologues. Every participant grants Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems without controversy; all agree that competent mathematicians regularly recognize the consistency of formal systems they understand, apparently without being able to derive that recognition from within the system itself. The Lucas-Penrose side treats this gap as decisive: if a human mind can see that a formal system F is consistent, and if the mind were merely the execution of F, then the mind could not reliably produce that very judgment, since F cannot prove its own consistency. The mechanist critics reply that this argument covertly assumes human reasoning operates as a single, globally consistent formal program—a "unitary formalization"—rather than as a distributed, context-sensitive patchwork of locally consistent modules that revise and replace one another. After months of discussion, neither camp has moved, despite shared agreement on the mathematics and on the psychological observation.

My own suspicion is that the stall is not empirical but architectural: the two sides may be answering different questions under the single heading of "mechanism." One side asks whether there exists any formal system that fully captures human mathematical cognition at a given time; the other asks whether cognition is decomposable into algorithmic processes at all, regardless of whether those processes are unified or fragmented. Is the disagreement therefore verbal—hinging on an ambiguous notion of "mechanism"—or is there a substantive divergence about whether self-referential consistency judgments require a non-computable form of insight that no distributed architecture could replicate? And if my "unitary versus patchwork" framing is correct, what kind of progress should we expect: a formal result showing that distributed systems can or cannot achieve stable self-reference, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, or should we conclude that the dispute ultimately turns on bedrock intuitions about mathematical understanding that neither side can argue for? I want a diagnosis that engages the actual structure of the Lucas-Penrose exchange rather than a historical survey, together with an honest judgment about whether anything—an experiment, a theorem, or a reconceptualization—could genuinely resolve it.
