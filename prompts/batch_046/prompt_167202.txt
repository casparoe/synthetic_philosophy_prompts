I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about a procedural feature that has begun appearing in multilateral arbitration treaties and certain high-stakes corporate governance charters: a clause requiring both parties to submit their full reasoning traces before a decision is finalized, coupled with an automatic penalty mechanism—sometimes called an “anti-lure escrow”—that activates if one party’s submitted logic diverges from the cooperative equilibrium that both reasoning traces should have jointly predicted. My friend dismissed the clause as bureaucratic ornament, but I kept insisting that it is philosophically loaded. To press the point, I kept returning to two decision-theoretic scenarios. The first is the Platonia dilemma, in which two agents with identical rational structures and perfect transparency can converge on mutual cooperation without any direct communication, simply by recognizing that they share the same reasoning. The second is a variant my friend referred to as the “Luring Lottery”: a situation in which one agent is privately offered a payoff for subtly distorting its own reasoning trace—making it appear to deviate—thereby luring the other agent away from the shared superrational equilibrium and into defection. The arbitration practice seems to treat the Platonia outcome as the implicit normative baseline—penalizing deviation—while simultaneously building in safeguards that assume agents might be subject to exactly the kind of lure described in the second scenario. What view of rational agency, transparency, or practical reason does this institutional design presuppose? Does it assume that an agent’s “true” choice is identical to its reasoning trace, so that divergence is always a kind of inauthenticity? And how can the same framework treat superrational cooperation as the default expectation in its disclosure clause, while relying on adversarial, information-asymmetric enforcement procedures elsewhere in the agreement? If the underlying commitment is that rational agents with shared structures must necessarily arrive at the same conclusion, then what exactly is being tested, or protected against, when the mechanism is designed to catch a “lured” deviation? I want to understand whether the practice is coherent, or whether its parts quietly presuppose incompatible theories of what it is for one rational agent to relate to another.
