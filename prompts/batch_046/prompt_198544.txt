I've been working through Gould and Lewontin's well-known attack on the Panglossian paradigm—the idea that evolutionary biology too often treats every trait as if it were an optimally engineered solution to an adaptive problem—and I think there's a hidden tension in how they deploy the architectural-spandrel analogy. I want to defend the somewhat counter-intuitive claim that their spandrels critique is conceptually unstable unless it is strictly confined to trait-level adaptationism, because at the meta-level it tacitly presupposes the very selectionist framework it claims to undermine. Please argue for this position.

Begin by distinguishing trait-level adaptationism, which asserts that every phenotypic feature is a direct product of natural selection shaped for its current function, from process-level adaptationism, which holds only that selection is the dominant driver of evolutionary change without requiring that every trait be functionally designed. Then argue that Gould and Lewontin's identification of spandrels—structural byproducts that arise as necessary consequences of other adaptive changes—only gains normative force against a background assumption that selection is powerful enough to produce tightly integrated, functionally coherent organisms; without this baseline, there is no clear reason to treat a given trait as an unexpected architectural leftover rather than as an independently selected feature. Finally, explain how a defender of process-level adaptationism can accommodate spandrels within a broadly selectionist framework—treating them as structurally inevitable side effects of selection acting on primary traits—without conceding that evolutionary biology must abandon functional explanation or treat adaptation as a marginal phenomenon. Keep the discussion conceptual rather than empirical: do not rely on specific case studies, and avoid requests for citations or further reading lists.
