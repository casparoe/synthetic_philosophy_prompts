Discussions of Tolstoy's aesthetic theory often lean on his infection analogy: a work of art functions like a contagious agent that transmits the artist's emotional state to an audience, with good art spreading morally elevating feelings and bad art spreading degrading ones. This is frequently used to argue that art should be evaluated chiefly by its epidemiological-moral effect—whether it fosters universal human solidarity or decadent self-absorption—rather than by formal beauty or technical mastery. Audit how far the analogy holds. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the artistic process plays the role of the pathogen, the vector of transmission, the susceptible host, the immune response, and the epidemic threshold? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for Tolstoy's normative conclusion: can an audience be immune to a feeling without being morally improved; can a work mutate in transmission so that the artist's original emotional state is lost or inverted; and does the model smuggle in a questionable assumption that emotional transmission is always involuntary and therefore beyond rational critique? Inject into your audit the concept of asymptomatic carriers—viewers who consume art without manifesting any detectable emotional or behavioral change—to see whether Tolstoy's suppression criterion applies to them. Finally, take a definite position: does the infection analogy survive as a heuristic for aesthetic judgment, does it collapse into a category error, or should it be replaced by a tighter analogy? If you advocate replacement, sketch the substitute and explain why it preserves Tolstoy's moral concern without importing the biological baggage.
