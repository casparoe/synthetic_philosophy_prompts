I've been thinking on my own about the analogy Douglas Hofstadter draws in GEB between Gödel's self-referential formulas and the self-awareness of minds. I understand the basic move: Gödel constructs a statement within formal arithmetic that, through a careful encoding, asserts its own unprovability, and Hofstadter suggests that something structurally similar might explain how a mind can become aware of itself.

Recently I've noticed a recurring pattern in popular discussions about artificial intelligence and consciousness. Someone will observe that a language model can generate sentences that refer back to the system itself—statements that it is an AI or that it lacks feelings—and they treat this capacity as if it were essentially the same kind of self-reference Gödel achieved. From there they often conclude that such a system has taken a meaningful step toward genuine self-awareness, as if the syntactic loop were already the mental loop.

What strikes me, without any formal training, is that a specific gap never gets named. Gödel's formula refers to itself only in the sense that its formal properties mirror a meta-mathematical statement about provability; the formula itself does not understand, experience, or know anything. When people extend this to minds or machines, they seem to slide from "this system produces symbols that point back to itself" to "this system is aware of itself," without accounting for what converts syntactic self-reference into subjective self-awareness. I keep wondering whether there is an established philosophical or cognitive-science term for exactly this slide—treating formal self-reference as if it already constituted semantic self-knowledge—particularly within the context of the Gödelian analogy.

Does this move have a specific name? If so, how does it relate to Hofstadter's own framing—does he depend on it, reject it, or replace it with a different mechanism such as the strange loop? And if there is no standard term, I would like to understand precisely where the boundary lies: what distinguishes a Gödel-style formal self-reference from the kind of self-awareness that involves actually knowing one is the thing being referred to, and in what respects does the analogy genuinely illuminate consciousness rather than obscure the difference?
