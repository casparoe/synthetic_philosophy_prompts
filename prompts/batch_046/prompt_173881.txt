Consider the institutional practice—common in university career centers, corporate wellness programs, and executive-coaching frameworks—that asks individuals to formulate a single "personal purpose statement," a concise declaration of their enduring reason for being that is meant to serve as an authoritative filter for career moves, relationships, and ethical commitments. The same institutions, however, routinely run "reinvention workshops" that treat this purpose as a strategic design object to be rewritten every few years in response to labor-market shifts or personal growth, and they evaluate success partly by the coherence between one's current actions and one's present statement, partly by one's willingness to discard an outdated one. The practice thus treats purpose as simultaneously identity-constituting—so that living against it is a form of self-betrayal or inauthenticity—and as revisable—so that clinging to an old purpose is a failure of adaptability.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity, practical rationality, and the logic of evaluation does this hybrid practice presuppose? Does it require a narrative view of the self, on which a life is meaningful only as a unified story with an enduring telos, or can a reductionist or relational view accommodate something being both constitutive and disposable? More specifically, does the practice implicitly reject Frank Ramsey's diagnosis that asking for "the meaning of life" involves a category mistake—treating the predicate "meaningful" (properly applied to parts within a life, such as actions, sentences, or social roles) as if it could apply to a life as a whole—or does it reconstruct that predicate so that it applies to lives without committing the grammatical error Ramsey identified? Can the practice be made consistent: is there a coherent account on which a purpose is deep enough to ground obligations of authenticity yet shallow enough to be redesigned like a business plan, and if so, what view of moral desert or practical authority does that account require? Finally, is the practice philosophically underdetermined—would several rival theories of meaning and identity license it equally—or does its combination of evaluative authority and strategic revisability quietly rule out specific alternatives, such as those that treat life-meaning as entirely discovered rather than authored, or those that deny that lives can be evaluated for coherence in the first place?
