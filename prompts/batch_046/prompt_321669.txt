I'm working through a philosophy-of-mind reading course in which we have been comparing two explanatory inversions that seem structurally similar but may not be. The first is Darwin's: complex functional competence—eyes, wings, echolocation—arises and is refined by natural selection without any prior comprehension of optics, aerodynamics, or acoustics on the part of the selecting mechanism. The second is Turing's, at least on one influential reading: instead of asking whether a machine possesses internal understanding or consciousness, we should evaluate its performance through an external behavioral criterion, treating the absence of introspective access as a feature rather than a deficit.

My instructor has pushed us to consider whether these inversions are genuinely parallel. In Darwin's case, the absence of comprehension belongs to the causal process: there is no designer who grasps the function being optimized. In Turing's case, the absence belongs to the epistemic position of the evaluator: we lack direct access to internal states, so we substitute performance for comprehension. The debate in our seminar has stalled because some students treat both as instances of the same phenomenon—functional success without representational grasp—while others insist that conflating causal blindness with methodological agnosticism collapses two distinct kinds of absence.

Write an essay on whether Darwin's evolutionary inversion and Turing's behavioral inversion should be treated as a single model of competence without comprehension, or whether they differ in ways that undermine any unified theory. You should defend a specific verdict: either that the analogy holds and illuminates both biological and artificial cognition, or that it fails because representation plays an irreducible role in one case but not the other. In reaching your conclusion, address whether the term comprehension operates with the same meaning across evolutionary biology and philosophy of artificial intelligence, and whether treating them together obscures something important about engineered representational architectures.
