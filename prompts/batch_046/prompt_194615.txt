I am preparing a contribution for a seminar on penal philosophy, and I should like a short story that dramatises the tension between retributive and consequentialist justifications of punishment without falling back on familiar courtroom tropes. Set the narrative in a near-future administrative chamber—perhaps the Office of Proportional Sentencing—where a senior assessor must finalise the term for an offender who committed a minor but calculated fraud.

The twist I want embedded is conceptual rather than technological: the chamber employs a dual-weight algorithm in which one coefficient measures culpability (pure desert) and the other measures marginal deterrence, but neither the assessor nor the condemned is permitted to know which coefficient dominated the final figure. Your protagonist should be convinced that justice requires that the punishment be deserved in itself—she is a committed retributivist—yet she also recognises that the opacity of the formula makes it impossible for her to verify whether the sentence is genuinely proportionate or merely optimally deterrent. Meanwhile, her colleague argues that if the system produces the best social outcomes, the distinction between deserved and useful punishment is a metaphysical luxury we cannot afford.

Please write a story—perhaps two or three brief scenes—showing her attempting to defend a particular sentence for the offender not because it will deter others, but because she believes it restores moral equilibrium, only to discover that her own reasoning has been quietly shaped by data she was never meant to see. I want the narrative to ask whether a punishment can still be genuinely retributive if the agent who imposes it cannot distinguish desert from utility in her own deliberation, and whether the offender’s right to be punished only for what he has done survives when the mechanism of sentencing is deliberately opaque. Keep the philosophical conflict intimate and concrete: avoid grand speeches, and let the tension emerge through the assessor’s private doubts, the bureaucratic form she fills out, and a brief conversation with the offender after the term has been fixed. British spelling and a restrained, formal register throughout, please.
