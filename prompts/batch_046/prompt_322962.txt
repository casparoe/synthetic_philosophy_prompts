I'm sitting on my university's Experiential Learning Oversite Committee (ELIOC — we review weird pilot programs that claim some educational value). The classics/philosophy joint program just submitted a proposal for a semester-long "Regulated Symposium" in one of the graduate residence halls: students meet monthly, drink diluted wine together with a faculty symposiarch who stays sober, and then write reflective essays on whether the alcohol revealed or distored their ethical commitments. They ground the whole thing in Plato's Laws, especially the idea that wine works like a mirror for the soul — it makes hidden dispositions visible — and that a sober symposiarch over thirty can turn what would be corruption into actual education. There's also a distinction they draw straight from Plato between symposia for testing (young people, meant to expose whether someone's beliefs are just doxa that collapses under drink or real episteme that holds firm) versus symposia for relaxation (older people, meant to soften rigidity).

Our student affairs office thinks this is just a drinking club with philosophical garnish, and legal is worried we're institutionalizing intoxication for assessment purposes. The defenders counter that sober interviews always let people perform, so you miss the epistemic window that only alcohol opens, and that Plato explicitly wanted this kind of testing for citizens in training. I need you to draft the committee's written opinion for Thursday's meeting: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, include a concrete timeline if we go with approval, and treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly — especially that epistemic claim about doxa versus episteme and whether a university should be running character tests with wine. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate the question.
