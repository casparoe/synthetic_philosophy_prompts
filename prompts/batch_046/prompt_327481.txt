In a seminar on the epistemology of mathematics, a fellow student sketched an argument that has been bothering me because its conclusion seems to dissolve Benacerraf's dilemma in a way that proves far too much. The reasoning runs as follows. (1) Benacerraf's dilemma assumes a causal constraint on knowledge: for a belief to count as knowledge, there must be an appropriate causal connection between the believer and the fact that makes the belief true. (2) Abstract mathematical objects—numbers, sets, functions—are non-spatiotemporal and causally inert, so no such connection is possible if mathematical Platonism is true. (3) Hence Platonism, paired with the causal constraint, implies that mathematical knowledge is impossible. (4) The same causal constraint, however, applies not only to mathematics but to any domain whose objects or relations lack spatiotemporal location and causal efficacy; this includes logical relations of consequence, modal properties of necessity and possibility, and—if moral non-naturalism is correct—normative properties. (5) Therefore, if we take Benacerraf's framing seriously, we should face parallel dilemmas for logic, modality, and morality, each threatening to reduce those domains to skepticism. (6) Since we clearly do possess logical, modal, and normative knowledge, the causal constraint must be rejected globally, not merely adjusted for mathematics. (7) But once the constraint is rejected globally, there is no remaining epistemological obstacle specific to mathematical objects; Benacerraf's challenge collapses into a general argument against causal theories of knowledge, and Platonism about mathematics is vindicated without requiring any special mathematical epistemology. (8) This strikes me as suspicious: it turns a targeted problem about abstract objects into a reductio of an epistemological theory, then uses that reductio to license unrestricted Platonism.

I feel there is a hidden slide between applying the causal constraint selectively in step (3) and applying it globally in step (4), or perhaps an equivocation between direct causal perception of an object and the causal grounding of the mechanisms that produce belief about that object—something Hartry Field's reliability-based reformulation of Benacerraf's problem might highlight. Which step should be given up? I would prefer to preserve the intuition that mathematical knowledge poses a distinctive epistemological puzzle—one that logical or modal knowledge does not pose in the same way—but I do not want to pay the price of global skepticism about non-empirical domains. If the flaw is in step (6), what does retaining a localized causal constraint cost us in terms of explaining logical or normative knowledge? And if the flaw is instead in step (4), what distinguishes mathematical objects from logical relations such that only the former generate the dilemma?
