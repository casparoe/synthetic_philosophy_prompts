For an MA-level philosophy of value seminar, design one 75-minute essay exam question on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about well-being. The question must be unanswerable by simply rehearsing the classic objection from phenomenal dissimilarity—e.g., that gustatory, intellectual, and malicious pleasures feel radically different. Instead, require students to evaluate a specific dilemma: hedonists who concede phenomenal diversity often retreat to either (i) a minimal shared phenomenal feature, call it "thin positive valence," or (ii) a shared functional architecture, such as modulation of reward-learning signals. The student must argue whether either retreat preserves hedonism's normative claim that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good, or whether both collapses transform hedonism into a theory about affective structure or motivation that applies equally well to non-pleasurable states.

Constraints: answer in approximately 1000 words; worth 20 marks total.

Provide:
- The exam question, clearly framing the task.
- A model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a definite, argued stance.
- A marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, specify its mark value (summing to 20), and include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost those marks.

In the model answer and in the scheme's descriptions, separate what is conceptually settled—such as the reality of phenomenal diversity among pleasures—from what remains genuinely contested—such as whether functional unity can ground a normative uniqueness claim for pleasure. Do not present any contested philosophical thesis as though it were the consensus view among specialists. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask students to supply them.
