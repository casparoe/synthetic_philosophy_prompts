My partner and I have spent two years saving to adapt our home so that my aging parent—who has limited mobility—can live with us comfortably rather than entering institutional care. The cost is significant but manageable within our budget. A colleague recently argued that the same sum, donated to an effective global-health charity, could prevent serious harm to several strangers, and that proceeding with the renovation is therefore morally impermissible: whenever one can prevent very bad outcomes at a cost that does not threaten one's own basic survival, one is obliged to do so. My partner responds that this standard effectively prohibits any sustained personal or family commitment, since there will always be some distant life that could be saved with those resources, and that morality must allow people to maintain the kinds of projects that make a life genuinely theirs. I want to know whether my colleague is enforcing a legitimate moral requirement or demanding more than morality can fairly ask. Please advise on the underlying philosophical structure of the disagreement—without naming it in technical terms—and separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who study the limits of moral obligation. Do not present a contested view, such as whether agents possess special permissions to favor their own commitments, as if it were the consensus.
