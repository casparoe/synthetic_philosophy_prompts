The student's answer:

The conjugal view, associated with John Finnis and Sherif Girgis, holds that marriage is defined by actual reproductive potential: a union is only marital if it can in principle produce children, which is why same-sex couples are excluded by definition, and infertile opposite-sex couples are technically exceptions that society tolerates but that do not truly fit the category. The revisionist view, found in writers like Elizabeth Brake, rejects this and says marriage is a legal contract for mutual care and commitment, with gender being irrelevant. Because the conjugal view requires actual reproductive capacity, it cannot consistently allow elderly or infertile people to marry without contradiction. Furthermore, the state's interest in marriage is not biological but social: it stabilizes households and distributes benefits equitably. Therefore, the separation between metaphysics and institutional design collapses—if marriage is not actually about reproduction, there is no metaphysical basis to restrict it, and the state should recognize all intimate partnerships equally. The argument about structural gender asymmetry is also irrelevant because once we drop the reproductive requirement, gender differences in marriage law disappear automatically.
