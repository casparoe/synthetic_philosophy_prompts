Construct a thought experiment on the problem of verisimilitude in historical representation. Design a single-paragraph, realistic scenario set during a documented seventeenth-century provincial tax revolt, reconstructed from both parish testimony—in which peasants misidentify an old levy as a novel imposition—and provincial fiscal archives that reveal the tax’s long-standing structure. Engineer the case so that a “causal-structural” theory of verisimilitude, which holds that a representation is verisimilitudinous when its relational-causal map is isomorphic to actual macro-level dynamics regardless of the agents’ epistemic limits, delivers a positive verdict, while an “indexical-phenomenological” theory, which holds that verisimilitude requires preserving the agents’ actual evidential position—including misapprehensions, local horizons, and temporal orientation—so that attributing to them knowledge of structures they could not perceive produces a distorting “god’s-eye” representation, delivers a negative verdict.

Constraints: no counterfactual technology or omniscient narrators; isolate the mismatch between macro-causal accuracy and the preservation of agent-level ignorance; suitable for a reader with basic philosophy-of-history literacy but no specialist literature. After the scenario, state the representation being evaluated and its method of construction; show each theory’s verdict and reasoning; identify the single isolating feature and explain what happens to the verdicts if it is removed; and explain how a defender of the indexical-phenomenological view would attack the structural theory’s positive verdict without begging the question. Inject the niche idea of “scale-sensitive indexicality”: the claim that verisimilitude is not scale-invariant but depends on whether the representation’s epistemic coordinates match those available to the represented subjects at the scale of their action.
