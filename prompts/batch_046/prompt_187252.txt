As someone recently appointed to a university committee revising our student-leadership selection process, I have been reflecting on how institutions test character under stress. This brought to mind Plato's argument in the Laws, where the Athenian Stranger proposes regulated symposia—supervised drinking gatherings—as a pedagogical instrument: wine lowers inhibitions and reveals whether citizens and prospective rulers can maintain moderation and honesty when pleasure is available. Rather than suppressing alcohol entirely, Plato treats it as a diagnostic tool, but only within a structured, observational setting.

Analyze American Prohibition (1920–1933) through this Platonic framework. Treat the Volstead Act and the subsequent proliferation of speakeasies and organized crime as a large-scale attempt to suppress an intoxicant that Plato would have used for supervised character-testing. By Plato's lights, what problem was Prohibition addressing, and why does his model predict that an outright ban—lacking the pedagogical supervision and communal observation of the symposion—would generate corruption and evasion rather than virtue? At the same time, what does the historical record—enforcement failures, substitution toward harder liquor, and the erosion of local legal authority—reveal about the scalability and limits of Plato's own proposal? Specifically, does the episode suggest that alcohol-as-diagnosis is viable only in small, homogeneous communities, or does it point to a deeper error in treating intoxication primarily as an epistemic window into moral character?
