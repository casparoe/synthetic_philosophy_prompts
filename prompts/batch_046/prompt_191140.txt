I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on epistemic value and the philosophy of education, and I also serve as a peer tutor grading short-answer responses in our introductory epistemology module. I need a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that translates Plato's distinction in the Meno between knowledge and true belief into something I can apply repeatedly. Specifically, I must decide whether a student response exhibits "tethered" true belief (a correct claim held together with an account that explains why it holds and survives minor perturbation) or merely ungrounded correct belief. The procedure must work in about eight minutes per response, require no external texts, and be defensible if the course instructor challenges a grade. Fix the stakes: I grade roughly twenty responses per week, and misclassifying a lucky guess as knowledge, or downgrading genuine reasoning, affects both fairness and the module's learning goals.

Please design the procedure with the following elements: (1) an ordered sequence of tests (accuracy check; identification of an explicit account or reasoning chain; a "perturbation" check in which I mentally substitute one element of the student's example or premise and ask whether the claim and its support would still hold; and an assessment of whether the account explains rather than merely accompanies the claim); (2) explicit markers where judgment cannot be eliminated (e.g., deciding what counts as a "minor" perturbation, weighing explanatory depth against formal correctness); (3) hard cases the procedure must handle, including a correct answer with no visible reasoning, a correct answer supported by reasoning that collapses under perturbation, and a false claim that is supported by robust reasoning and a clear account; (4) worked applications to three hypothetical student answers matching those hard cases, including the one where the procedure awards the false-but-tethered response more credit than the correct-but-untethered one—state that result even if it is not the one I would prefer; and (5) a clear statement of what the procedure cannot do, such as detecting unconscious guessing, measuring long-term retention, or substituting for disciplinary expertise in the subject matter.

Finally, separate what is philosophically settled about the Meno distinction and the idea of "tethering" from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this topic—such as whether the required account must be causal, explanatory, or virtue-based, and whether counterfactual robustness is a necessary or merely sufficient condition for knowledge—without presenting any one contested interpretation as the consensus view. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation.
