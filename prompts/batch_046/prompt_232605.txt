I'm in a seminar discussing Amartya Sen's entitlement approach to famine, using the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961 as the three canonical cases. One camp holds that all three are best understood as political crimes rather than natural disasters: in each case adequate aggregate food was available or could have been mobilized, and mortality spiked because of policy-driven entitlement collapses—wartime procurement and price controls in Bengal, colonial export priorities and Poor Law design in Ireland, and grain requisitioning coupled with false harvest reports in China. The opposing camp accepts Sen's core claim that starvation tracks entitlement failure rather than food availability decline alone, but insists that severe drought in Bengal, Phytophthora infestans in Ireland, and consecutive bad harvests in parts of China contributed causal weight that the "political crime" framing systematically understates; they argue that labeling these events crimes implies a counterfactual baseline—what a competently governed society facing the same ecological shock would have experienced—that is both empirically underdetermined and morally loaded.

We have circled this for months without anyone moving. Every participant accepts the historical record: rice stocks existed in Calcutta's hinterland during 1943, Irish grain continued to leave ports, and Chinese provincial authorities concealed local shortfalls to meet central targets. Yet the first group treats these facts as sufficient for the crime verdict, while the second treats them as necessary but not sufficient, demanding a demonstration that well-governed regimes facing identical weather or blight patterns would have seen near-zero excess mortality—a demonstration nobody can provide without constructing a counterfactual model of state capacity and agricultural resilience that neither side has actually built. My own suspicion is that the disagreement is partly verbal—"crime" for one side means "structurally avoidable death for which authorities bear primary causal responsibility," while for the other it requires something closer to intentional or grossly negligent harm with a clear non-catastrophic baseline—but I also wonder whether the two sides are answering different questions: one about the proper allocation of moral blame given actual institutional failures, the other about whether the events belong in the same category as earthquakes or floods regardless of governance.
