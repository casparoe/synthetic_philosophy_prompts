I've been losing sleep over s-risks ever since my roommate took a job at an AI lab and started talking about cosmic-scale suffering. Our study group built this argument and I can't see where it breaks. (1) If an action carries any nonzero chance of producing astronomical suffering, and that suffering dominates all local goods, then avoid the action. (2) Nearly all technological progress carries some tiny risk of eventually causing s-risks—misaligned systems, digital minds in pain, whatever. (3) Because the potential victims are vast in number, the expected disvalue is enormous. (4) So we should halt almost all R&D immediately. (5) But that'd block cancer cures that save real people now, which is obviosly wrong. (6) Therefore one of the premises fails. I think the hidden move is in (3): we're multplying probabilities by values that might not be comparable across ontological categories. Or maybe (1) smuggles in that comparison is even valid. Tell me which step to reject and what giving it up costs—especially whether fixing it requires abandoning the idea that s-risks can ever outweigh local benefits, or just fixing our aggregation method.
