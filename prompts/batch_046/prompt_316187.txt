I am delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on costly signaling and institutional credibility. The audience is split evenly between evolutionary biologists who work with Zahavi’s handicap principle and moral philosophers skeptical of extending biological metaphors into ethics or social epistemology. I need roughly eight minutes of remarks in which the principle does genuine structural work rather than serving as decorative framing. Specifically, construct the speech around a tension both groups must face: the handicap principle explains honest signaling only when the signal bears a cost that cheats genuinely cannot afford, yet modern institutions—standardized testing, professional credentialing, algorithmic verification—are deliberately engineered to reduce or eliminate such costs in order to make evaluation fair, scalable, and efficient. Use that tension to argue that stripping away the handicap does not merely reform the signal but may dissolve the very credibility it was meant to establish, and press the question of whether moral commitment or epistemic authority can remain meaningful when their costs are fully insured away by institutional design. The remarks should be fully accessible to specialists in either field without condescending to either; they should avoid generic praise of interdisciplinarity and should contain no citations, references to specific papers by name, bibliographies, or requests for further reading. Write the speech so that its conclusion leaves both halves of the room with a shared problem they cannot solve alone.
