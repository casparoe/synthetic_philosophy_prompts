Please argue for the position that the risk of executing an innocent person is not merely a contingent, consequentialist objection to capital punishment, but rather exposes an internal conceptual limit within retributive justice itself. Specifically, defend the claim that legitimate state punishment, understood as a practice of moral accountability between the sovereign and the offender, implicitly requires what we might call a rectifiability condition: the sanction must leave open the logical possibility that the state can correct its error and restore its normative relation to the wrongly punished subject. Execution violates this condition essentially, not just practically, because it annihilates the very being who could be the recipient of such rectification, and therefore attempts to rescue capital punishment through improved procedural safeguards miss the point—so long as epistemic fallibility remains possible in principle, the condition remains unsatisfied regardless of empirical risk reduction. From this, derive the further claim that the deterrence rationale collapses on parallel conceptual grounds: once execution is recognized as structurally incompatible with the state's claim to normative authority (transforming law into irremediable terror rather than correctable governance), it can no longer function as a credible disincentive within a system that requires the possibility of error-correction to maintain its legitimacy. You should treat standard distinctions between retributive and consequentialist theories as your point of departure, not as settled doctrine, and you should not invoke specific legal cases or statistical studies; the task is to show that the risk-of-error objection operates at the intersection of both theoretical frameworks rather than belonging exclusively to either.
