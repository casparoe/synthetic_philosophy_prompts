I keep running into this move in papers about reduction and emergence and I can't tell if it's got it's own name or if it's just sloppy reasoning. Here's the pattern: someone claims they've reduced some higher-level thing — like flocking in birds, or consciousness, or temperature — to lower-level rules. They show a derivation that looks complete, but when you look close it only succeeds because they've hard-coded the system boundaries or interaction topology into the premises. Take temperature in a gas: you can derive the ideal gas law from statistical mechanics, but only if you've already specified the container shape and the thermal bath conditions. Those aren't inside the particle dynamics; they're organizational conditions imported from outside. So the reduction hasn't shown temperature is nothing but particles — it's shown temperature is particles-plus-a-specific-environment-structure that the particles don't generate themselves.

I've seen the same thing with neural nets, where people say learning is reduced to weight updates, but the architecture, loss function, and data distribution are all treated as given rather than explained. Is there an established concept for this exact failure mode, where a reduction looks airtight but is actually leaning on unstated structural or boundary conditions that do the real explanitory work? If so, what's it called, and how is it different from the ordinary point that reduction needs ceteris paribus conditions? If not, can you name and define it, with clear boundary cases separating legitimate auxiliary assumptions from smuggling in the very phenomenon you're pretending to reduce?
