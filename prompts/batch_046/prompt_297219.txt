I have been trying to map the inside-view versus outside-view distinction onto cases where the reference class is not an external population but the very reasoning process being evaluated, and I find the standard treatments leave a gap. For a philosophy seminar I am preparing, I would like you to compose a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—Dr. Aris and Dr. Voss—who disagree about whether epistemic modesty can survive what Aris calls recursive calibration and Voss calls flat reference-class treatment. Aris should defend the position that when you step outside to assess your own belief-forming mechanisms, you must still use those mechanisms to perform the assessment, so the outside view can never fully escape the inside view; genuine modesty therefore requires recognizing that your calibration of yourself is itself subject to the same biases you sought to correct. Voss should defend the opposing view that this argument proves too much and collapses into global skepticism: rational agents must be willing to treat their own cognition as an object in a statistical reference class, accepting the recursive discomfort rather than sheltering every belief behind an impenetrable first-person shield.

The dialogue should center on a concrete case: a senior scientist, Dr. Chen, learns that her subfield's peer-review pipeline yields false positives at approximately thirty percent, and she realizes that her own recently celebrated finding—accepted through that same pipeline—might well be erroneous. Aris should use this case to show that Chen cannot apply the thirty-percent rate to herself without using the very peer-review judgment she now distrusts, so she must remain inside her first-person evaluation of the evidence even while acknowledging the statistical risk. Voss should reply that Chen can and should apply the rate mechanically, downgrading her confidence regardless of her internal sense of the result's quality, and that refusing to do so is not modesty but special pleading for her own reasoning. Each speaker should respond directly to the previous move: Aris must address Voss's charge of special pleading, and Voss must address Aris's charge that mechanical calibration requires an ungrounded faith in one's ability to identify oneself correctly within the reference class. The tone should be rigorous and formal, with neither side reduced to a caricature, and the exchange should end without a forced resolution—only a sharper statement of what divides them. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or appeals to named authorities; construct the arguments from the conceptual materials themselves.
