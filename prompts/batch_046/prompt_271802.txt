I am arranging a structured adversarial collaboration between a philosopher working in Daniel Dennett's compatibilist tradition—who maintains that moral responsibility survives causal determinism because agents possess the requisite capacities for rational self-control and reason-responsiveness, and that a limited form of just deserts can therefore ground certain reactive and punitive practices—and a philosopher aligned with Gregg Caruso's free-will skepticism, who holds that determinism and luck undermine any fundamental moral desert, rejects retributivism, and proposes that criminal justice should be reconceived as a public-health quarantine system aimed solely at protection, deterrence, and rehabilitation. Rather than rehearse the familiar free-will dialectic, I want both parties to draft a joint statement that isolates an underexplored pressure point: whether the intuition that a wrongdoer genuinely deserves suffering remains coherent once we fully concede that the wrongdoer's character was shaped by genetic and environmental factors entirely outside their control, and once we examine a specific hypothetical case in which the agent's violent act was immediately preceded by an undetected, externally induced alteration in their prefrontal risk-weighting circuitry—an intervention that shifted their cost-benefit calculus without their awareness or consent.

The document should contain two sections. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: about the empirical scope of causal determinism in human behavior, about the legitimate functions of a criminal-justice system, about the operational content of the quarantine model, and about the formal conditions—such as reasons-responsiveness and absence of coercion—under which an action might be attributed to an agent as its source. Second, a disagreements table presenting the residual disputes in wording each side accepts for its own position, specifically: (a) whether genuine sourcehood requires a form of ultimate self-origination incompatible with determinism; (b) whether reactive attitudes like moral resentment can be rationally maintained without presupposing desert; and (c) whether the undetected neurointervention case is morally continuous with ordinary deterministic causation or instead marks a categorically different kind of causal intrusion. For each crux, the table should identify the concrete test case or argument both sides agree is decisive—namely, the neurointervention scenario—and should state in advance what each camp predicts that case demonstrates about the viability of desert-based punishment.

The statement should be formulated with sufficient precision that a neutral reader could identify exactly where the two camps diverge, what empirical or conceptual finding would cause either to revise their view, and why the neurointervention edge case either collapses into the general deterministic story or fractures it. It must not rely on bibliographic citation, reading lists, or appeals to specific published texts; the arguments should stand or fall on their internal logic and on shared premises about neuroscience and moral psychology.
