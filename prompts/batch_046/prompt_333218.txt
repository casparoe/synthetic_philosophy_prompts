Read the film Groundhog Day through Nietzsche's idea of eternal recurrence, but avoid treating it as a simple allegory of amor fati. Instead, examine a structural tension: Phil Connors remains trapped in repetition until he perfects himself into someone worthy of love, which suggests the loop operates as a purgatorial trial with an exit condition rather than as an inescapable, eternal cycle that must be affirmed unconditionally. Does this framing domesticate recurrence into a pedagogical device—something to be endured only until moral transformation is complete—thereby draining it of the "greatest weight" Nietzsche describes? Or does the film's long middle section, in which Phil attempts suicide, masters skills without instrumental purpose, and gradually shifts from despair to craft-like attention to each repeated day, capture an embodied practice of affirmation that purely argumentative philosophy misses? Consider whether the romantic resolution ultimately betrays Nietzsche's demand to will the return of one's life exactly as it is, failures included, or whether it demonstrates that loving one's fate first requires becoming the kind of person who can bear it. Give a reading that takes both the film's narrative logic and Nietzsche's concept seriously, noting where the work resists the philosophical frame and what the resistance reveals.
