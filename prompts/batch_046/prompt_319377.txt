I'm presenting next Thursday to a philosophy-of-science working group — mostly philosophers of physics with a couple of economists and one committed structural realist — a defense of what I'm calling the counterfactual-probe reading of Friedman's F-twist. The claim is that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models (perfect rationality, frictionless surfaces, infinite populations) shouldn't be treated as approximations to be minimized, but as deliberately false premises whose predictive success reveals which structural dependencies in the target system are load-bearing under perturbation. In other words, the unrealisticness is an epistemic feature, not a cost: the model works not despite the falsehood but because the falsehood isolates what matters.

The audience knows Friedman well, includes two climate-modelers who are deeply skeptical of any instrumentalist gloss on model-building, and the structural realist will argue I'm collapsing ontology into predictive methodology. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections they'll actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased as a philosopher of physics or economist would put them. For each, note the real force, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and a concise reply that lands in under two minutes — plus a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the calibration objection I'm hoping nobody asks: that climate models rely on tuned parameters that embed the very false assumptions I'm praising, so my view can't distinguish a genuinely load-bearing false premise from a lucky falsehood that stabilizes predictions in one domain but collapses elsewhere. Flag that distinction problem as the weakest point in my position and tell me how to defend it without retreating to pure instrumentalism.
