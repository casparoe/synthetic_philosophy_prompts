I work as a software engineer on an AI ethics review board with no formal philosophy background, and I need a reusable fifteen-minute audit procedure for evaluating the philosophical thought experiments that appear in our design proposals—arguments that invoke hypothetical scenarios to justify engineering choices about autonomy, risk allocation, or interface restrictions. The procedure should be grounded in how intuition pumps actually persuade and mislead, but translated into ordered tests that a non-philosopher can run without consulting texts. Please present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion; each item should consist of one or two sentences.

Items one through three should state the audit stages in sequence: first, a framing-residue test that checks whether reversing the emotional or analogical coloring of the hypothetical reverses the intuitive verdict, with a clear stop-condition if the residue is doing independent normative work; second, an analogical-leakage check that verifies whether structural features of the hypothetical—agency, reversibility, information state—actually map onto the engineering context, marking where missing differences invalidate the comparison; third, a modal-boundary check that distinguishes whether the conclusion relies on a claim about conceptual possibility versus technical or ethical feasibility, explicitly flagging the latter as a values judgment that must be handed to a human reviewer rather than resolved by fact. For each stage, specify what counts as a passing answer and where the audit terminates with a rejection.

Item four should apply all three stages to a proposal banning immersive pleasure-interfaces justified by an experience-machine-style argument—that users would reject simulated satisfaction if they knew it was artificial—showing where the audit yields a rejection you might personally resist, specifically that the artificiality framing residue carries normative weight the analogical mapping cannot support. Item five should apply the same stages to an autonomous-vehicle priority rule modeled on a trolley-style harm-trade scenario, identifying the point at which probabilistic aggregation of harm becomes a values hand-off rather than a calculable output, and stating plainly what this audit cannot do—namely, resolve irreducible normative conflicts about whose risk should be prioritized.
