i work as a backend engineer and i'm taking a philosophy of mind course on the side, mostly because my roommate kept talking about whether AI actually "thinks." last week we hit eliminative materialism — the idea that beliefs and desires are just outdated folk psychology, like talking about "phlogiston" in chemistry. i tried to build an argument against it but now it feels like it proves way too much, and i can't tell if there's a hidden scope shift or if one of the premises is just wrong.

here's the chain my study group came up with:

(1) if eliminative materialism is right, then beliefs and desires don't exist as real mental states; they're just predictive heuristics, like how we apply "user intent" to clickstream data in analytics.
(2) but scientific reasoning itself depends on scientists holding beliefs and having the desire to be correct.
(3) now here's the twist we added: in mechanistic interpretability, we can inspect an LLM's weights and find circuits that track specific propositions. some researchers call these "belief-like representations." an eliminativist should deny these are real beliefs, since there's no mental substance behind them.
(4) so apply the same standard to human brains: neural firing patterns aren't beliefs either.
(5) but then the neuroscience that supports eliminativism — the studies showing there's no "belief center" in the brain — is conducted by people who don't actually believe anything; they just exhibit behavioral patterns we mislabel as belief.
(6) if the researchers don't actually believe their results, then there's no rational basis for accepting those results over folk psychology.
(7) therefore either eliminative materialism is self-defeating, or we must admit that at least some representations (the ones in scientists' heads) are real beliefs.
(8) but if scientists have real beliefs just because their representations serve a predictive role, then LLM representations and ordinary human mental states should count too.
(9) so eliminativism either collapses into allowing beliefs everywhere, or it destroys the science that justifies it.

i feel like there's an equivocation between "belief" as folk psychology and "belief" as scientific commitment, maybe in step (5) or (6), but i can't pin it down. i want to keep (1) and (3) if i can, since they match how i think about code states vs actual intentions. which step should i drop, and what's the cost of dropping it? also, am i smuggling in a modal slide between (4) and (5) without noticing?
