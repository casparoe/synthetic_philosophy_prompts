For an advanced seminar exercise on classical Chinese logic and its modern receptions, write a satirical parody of approximately 650 words in the deadpan bureaucratic voice of a national census bureau that has adopted a fictional administrative framework—call it the Mingjia Classification Protocol—derived from the logical distinctions traditionally associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names (Mingjia). The framework applies the dialogue's central logical claim, that a white horse is not coextensive with horse because the former denotes form compounded with a specific color-attribute while the latter denotes form alone, to all demographic and legal categorization. Thus the Protocol insists that terms such as citizen, resident, or taxpayer are not substitutable with qualified versions—property-owning citizen, resident-with-lease, taxpayer-in-good-standing—since the added attribute changes the intension and therefore the extension of the class. The piece should take the form of an official census questionnaire accompanied by a brief explanatory pamphlet. Include at least one mock form field whose instructions contradict one another by applying the pure-form versus form-plus-attribute distinction; one footnote in the style of a legal commentary appealing to the relation between name (ming) and reality (shi); and one internal Frequently Asked Questions entry in which a practical citizen complaint is resolved solely by asserting the non-identity of the two named classes. The satire should exaggerate a genuine feature of the School of Names—its conviction that precise naming is prerequisite to correct social ordering, and that conflating pure and qualified terms produces conceptual disorder—while pushing this conviction to a reductio ad absurdum in contemporary bureaucratic practice. The humor must arise from the consistent logical application of the distinction to mundane categories, not from treating the philosophy as mere verbal sophistry. Because the exercise should stand on its own conceptual structure, do not provide bibliographies, citations, or references to modern scholars; refer only to the Dialogue, the Protocol, and the distinction between pure and qualified naming. Write for an audience that may not be familiar with Chinese philosophy: the logical problem should be legible through the bureaucratic absurdity it generates, but a reader acquainted with the White Horse Dialogue should recognize that the satire is fair to the text's actual concern with reference, extension, and correct classification.
