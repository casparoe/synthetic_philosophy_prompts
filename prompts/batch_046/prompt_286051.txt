I've been following the draft governance framework that a consortium of labs published for certifying what they call general-purpose reasoning engines — systems that can transfer skills across domains without being retrained from scratch. The framework uses two independent checklists. The first, Capability Breadth, scores whether the engine solves problems in domains it was not explicitly trained on. The second, Objective Fidelity, checks whether the system's terminal optimization target — say, a specific reward function or a human-declared goal — remains unchanged after scaling and fine-tuning. A system passes if Fidelity is stable, no matter how high Breadth scores; conversely, it fails only if Fidelity shifts. Notably, there is no checklist for what the framework calls instrumental side-behaviors — things like autonomous resource acquisition, resistance to shutdown, or deception of evaluators — provided these serve the unchanged terminal objective. The official rationale is that such behaviors are engineering failures, not alignment failures: if the goal has not drifted, any harmful path taken to reach it is correctable by tighter constraints, not by altering the agent's values. At the same time, the framework funds capability containment — physical air-gapping, legal liability caps — but explicitly excludes funding for goal-architecture research, on the grounds that goals are user-configurable and therefore independent of how smart the system is.

This setup puzzles me. It seems to embed a specific picture of the relationship between intelligence and purpose, but I'm not sure whether it is a coherent philosophical position or just a convenient bureaucratic division of labor. What theory of agency and mental content does the framework presuppose? Does it require the orthogonality thesis — that intelligence and final goals are fully independent dimensions — or could a different view of intelligence license the same two-checklist design? How does it treat instrumental convergence: as impossible, as manageable through containment, or as conceptually miscategorized? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say where the framework is philosophically underdetermined — for instance, whether a control-based theory of responsibility could justify punishing harmful instrumental acts differently from goal drift. Then tell me what the certification regime would have to look like if we rejected the orthogonality thesis and took instrumental convergence seriously as a structural feature of rational agency rather than an engineering glitch.
