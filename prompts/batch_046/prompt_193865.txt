As a journalist covering the history of science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on how historians of psychiatry write about nineteenth-century institutional care without either imposing modern diagnostic frameworks or excusing well-documented harm. Before I interview scholars, I want the philosophical groundwork laid out precisely: what must an adequate theory of presentism in historiography actually deliver? Provide the full desiderata—not slogans, but the specific requirements any such theory must meet. Which phenomena must it explain (for instance, describing a past practice in its own conceptual terms versus assessing its effects by ours)? Which distinctions must it preserve—such as between holding false beliefs and performing wrong actions, or between conceptual anachronism and moral anachronism—together with the motivating case for each? Which results about conceptual change in medicine or the sociology of knowledge must it remain consistent with? Flag which desiderata are non-negotiable and which reflect the preferences of particular camps (contextualist hermeneutics, critical presentism, normative neutrality), and indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. I am especially interested in the tension visible in retrospective diagnosis of institutionalized patients: avoiding anachronistic DSM-style classification seems to conflict with the claim that the suffering involved was genuinely harmful rather than merely "different" by past standards. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring; a brief sketch of how the three standard positions fare against your list is sufficient.
