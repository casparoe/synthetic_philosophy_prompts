I am preparing an independent study on H. P. Grice's account of speaker meaning and conversational implicature for my senior philosophy seminar. In my reading, I have come to understand that Grice distinguishes what a speaker strictly says from what they merely implicate by relying on the Cooperative Principle and its associated maxims. Recently, however, I encountered a theoretical proposal—which I will call the cancellation-immunity thesis—according to which an implicature counts as genuinely speaker-meant only if it remains stable under explicit cancellation attempts that are permanently recorded in the conversational transcript. Applying this idea, I have concluded that because any implicature can in principle be cancelled retroactively, there is no robust boundary between speaker meaning and sentence meaning, and that Grice's maxims therefore function less as normative presumptions governing rational cooperation and more as descriptive algorithms for recovering intended content. Furthermore, it seems to me that in structured digital dialogue—where messages are archived, threaded, and technically open to revision—this instability becomes systemic, threatening to dissolve Gricean speaker meaning altogether. Is this line of reasoning sound, or does it rest on a confusion about how cancellation interacts with the Cooperative Principle and with Grice's distinction between saying and implying?
