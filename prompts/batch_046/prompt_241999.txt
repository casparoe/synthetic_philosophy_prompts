I am a doctoral candidate in economic history and philosophy of social science, and I have been asked to deliver the student address at our joint program’s commencement ceremony. The audience will be genuinely mixed: the graduates themselves, who have spent years in comparative political economy and historical methodology; family members with no specialist background; senior faculty from economics, history, and philosophy; and a few invited policy advisors who sit on development boards. The ceremony is not a workshop, but I want the address to do real philosophical work rather than offer generic encouragement. Specifically, I want its entire structure to be shaped by the problem of causal inference in macrohistorical comparison—the long-running difficulty of disentangling institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations for why some nations are rich and others poor, and the deeper epistemic point that historical data alone rarely license isolable mechanisms at this scale.

Rather than resolving the triad in favor of institutions, geography, or culture, or pretending that more data will eventually settle it, I want the speech to treat this macro-level causal underdetermination as defining the intellectual posture of the graduates. To avoid a well-worn commencement trope, I want to inject a somewhat niche conceptual distinction I have been developing in my own research: the gap between explanatory sufficiency—having an account that coherently fits the comparative historical record—and interventionist warrant—having legitimate grounds to act on that account in policy design. The philosophical idea should constrain what the speech can and cannot say: it should forbid false analogies to laboratory causation, it should prevent me from claiming that our cohort now possesses the “real” driver of prosperity, and it should allow me to articulate a form of humility that is not passive skepticism but an active methodological commitment. At the same time, the address must remain accessible and genuinely congratulatory; it should not become a seminar paper, and it must land for listeners who have never encountered the debate.

Please write the speech—roughly 800 to 1100 words, suitable for five to seven minutes—without citations, bibliographies, footnotes, or appeals to specific texts that a listener without access to a library could not verify. The tone should be formal, polished, and appropriate to an academic ceremony, but never condescending to the non-specialists present. Let the tension between explanatory sufficiency and interventionist warrant organize the narrative arc, from acknowledgment of what the graduates have actually learned, through an honest account of what historical comparison cannot deliver, to a closing that defines responsible practice in the face of that limit. Avoid platitudes about “changing the world” unless they are explicitly qualified by the epistemic framework; the structural constraint should be visible in every section, including the final words of congratulations.
