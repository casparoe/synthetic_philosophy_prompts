As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am developing a feature on the philosophical architecture of moral responsibility, specifically the framework associated with Peter Strawson concerning reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—and whether these emotional responses can be genuinely suspended without reducing another person to a mere object of clinical management. In editorial discussions, I have become drawn to a comparatively underexplored dimension of this debate: not the familiar free-will determinism binary, but whether reactive attitudes possess what might be called normative elasticity—the capacity to be institutionally calibrated, scaled, or selectively dampened according to social role and relational distance—without thereby collapsing into the objective attitude that Strawson warned against. Most public treatments present the choice as stark: either we hold people fully responsible with unmediated emotional engagement, or we adopt a detached, instrumental stance. I want to show readers that the intermediate terrain is philosophically live and contested.

For the feature, I would like a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who maintains a strict Strawsonian position that reactive attitudes are irreducibly interpersonal and resist rational modulation without moral loss, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who argues that reactive attitudes are better understood as socially scaffolded regulatory mechanisms capable of graded calibration through institutional norms without becoming purely objective. The conversation should take place in a concrete, informal setting—perhaps in a university common room after a colloquium, over cooling coffee—and must exhibit genuine back-and-forth: Vasquez should press Chen on whether calibrated resentment is not merely resentment disguised by bureaucratic distance, and Chen should challenge Vasquez to account for how parents, judges, and physicians already practice precisely this emotional modulation without treating others as mechanisms. Neither voice should be reduced to a strawman. The dialogue should illuminate, for an educated general audience, whether moral responsibility requires an all-or-nothing emotional stance or permits structured, context-sensitive engagement, and specifically whether Strawson’s anxiety about the objective attitude overlooks the possibility of maintained interpersonal recognition within deliberately scaled emotional distance.
