You are a senior consultant in democratic theory and institutional design, with expertise in normative political philosophy, mechanism design, and the literature on citizens' assemblies. Before drafting anything, please think step-by-step: map the core normative commitments of my audience (deliberative equality, liberal neutrality about the good life, anti-technocratic suspicion), identify which premises in my proposal violate each commitment, and flag the rhetorical moves most likely to sound like smuggled epistocracy.

Next week I present at a workshop on democratic innovation. My thesis defends a hybrid institutional model I call "stake-weighted modular sortition." For national climate-policy advisory bodies with binding agenda-setting influence, members should be selected by stratified random sampling, but with three deliberate departures from uniform sortition: (a) residents of regions facing direct, measurable ecological risk receive multiplied selection probabilities; (b) young adults (ages 18–30) are over-sampled relative to raw demographic share; and (c) a separate "proxy stratum" of legally designated future-generation advocates receives full speaking and deliberative rights but no voting rights on final recommendations. I argue this corrects the spatial and temporal bias of electoral politics without collapsing into expertise-based selection, because the weights track affectedness rather than knowledge.

The audience: about ten scholars. The majority are deliberative-democracy theorists committed to "all-affected interests" and "equal standing" frameworks; two are participatory-democracy scholars skeptical that random selection depoliticizes citizens rather than empowering them; one is a constitutional lawyer focused on legitimacy and the boundary between advisory and legislative power; and one is a political economist who studies mechanism design and will immediately probe strategic manipulation of the weighting algorithm. Several have published on citizens' assemblies and are sympathetic to sortition in principle, but they treat uniform random selection as a non-negotiable baseline for democratic equality.

Please prepare my Q&A defense sheet. List the objections in roughly the order they will actually arise, phrased in the vocabulary each camp uses. Order them by the combination of likelihood and damage to the proposal's survival. Be sure to include an objection about whether the proxy-stratum's speaking rights are either insultingly weak or illegitimately strong, and an objection about strategic manipulation of the ecological-risk weighting metric. Then identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the tension between my affectedness-based weighting rationale and liberal neutrality about competing conceptions of the good life—and draft the reply I should have ready, including an honest admission of exactly where my position is soft and what I must concede.
