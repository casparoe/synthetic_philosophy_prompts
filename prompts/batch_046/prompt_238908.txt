As a journalist who covers the boundary between physics and historiography, I recently sketched an argument for a seminar on whether chaos theory dissolves historical explanation, and I now find its conclusion—that macro-history offers no genuine explanations—absurdly destructive. I invoke the familiar nursery rhyme about a kingdom lost for want of a nail, but I want to press the worry in a more formal register. Here is the chain: (1) If chaos theory applies to macro-historical dynamics, then for any large-scale event E there exists at least one micro-perturbation p within the system’s sensitivity radius such that, had p been absent, E would not have occurred. (2) Any actual historical narrative necessarily selects a finite set of causes and omits infinitely many micro-conditions, including p. (3) Therefore every actual explanation of E omits at least one counterfactually necessary condition. (4) An explanation is adequate only if it includes every condition whose absence would have prevented the outcome; otherwise it cannot account for why the outcome occurred rather than not. (5) So every actual historical explanation is inadequate. (6) Furthermore, because chaos implies that the specific identity of p is unpredictable ex ante and varies across nearby possible worlds, there is no principled way to restrict the required conditions to a manageable subset without an arbitrary cutoff. (7) Hence historians can never satisfy the adequacy condition. (8) Therefore macro-history is not an explanatory discipline but only a descriptive chronicle.

I suspect a subtle slide—perhaps an equivocation between causal necessity in a particular trajectory and an impossibly strong explanatory completeness, or a quantifier shift between “for each explanation there is some omitted condition” and “there is a single set all explanations must include”—but I cannot pinpoint the damage. Is the error in (4), which seems to demand of history what no empirical science could deliver? Or does (6) illegitimately move from unpredictability to the absence of any defensible boundary? I would prefer to preserve (1) and the intuition that explanations should be counterfactually robust, but I need to know what rejecting the faulty step costs. Which premise or inference should be abandoned, why did it look plausible in this context, and what do the main alternatives—tightening the chaos claim, relaxing the adequacy condition, or denying that macro-events are genuinely chaotic—each require me to give up?
