I've been arguing with my roommate about whether 'Baby Boomer' is actually a real kind or just a statistical bucket, and it made me think about The Graduate in a weird way. In social ontology there's this question about whether generational cohorts have homeostatic mechanisms — like shared economic shocks or cultural technologies — that make them projectible, or if they're just arbitrary birth-year slices with no real inductive power. I want to read The Graduate through that exact lens, not just as a generic 'generation gap' story. Ben Braddock comes home after college and his parents' friends keep giving him career advice and Mrs. Robinson seduces him, and the whole movie frames him as part of this disillusioned youth cohort that sees through the plastic values of the older generation. But does the film actually treat 'the young' as a natural kind with shared essential properties, or does it show Ben's alienation coming from very specific individual circumstances — his parents' suburban expectations, his own indecision, the particular historical moment of post-war affluence?

Point me to scenes where the ontology of cohorts illuminates what's happening, like the pool party or the plastics conversation, and also tell me where the film resists that reading — maybe Ben turns out to be an outlier even among his peers, or Mrs. Robinson's own generational membership gets complicated by gender and class. I'm writing this for my blog and I don't want to reduce the movie to an ilustration of Boyd-style cluster kinds, but I do want to ask whether the film captures something about cohort experience that the philosophers miss, like how it feels to be treated as a kind-member even if ontologicaly you're not one. Also, is Ben's rebellion causally tied to his cohort membership, or is it just correlational with being young in that specific place and time?
