I am a student in an upper-division course on machine ethics and the philosophy of technology, and I have been thinking about a specific institutional practice I encountered in a case study on clinical decision-support systems. At several hospitals that deploy AI-based ethical consultation tools, the validation protocol works roughly as follows. The system is trained on a corpus of de-identified clinical ethics committee reports, and its "ethical reasoning module" is evaluated not by the outcomes it recommends but by what the engineers call reasoning fidelity: an automated metric scores how closely the model's step-by-step justification mirrors the rhetorical and logical structure of the corresponding committee rationale in a holdout set. The system is cleared for deployment only if its fidelity score exceeds a predetermined threshold and its recommendations align with the committee's in at least ninety percent of cases. Disagreement is treated as calibration error rather than as substantive moral disagreement, and the committee's published rationales are treated as a gold-standard dataset against which the machine's practical reasoning is tuned.

What philosophical commitments about moral knowledge, ethical expertise, and the nature of practical reason does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that ethical reasoning is essentially a pattern-matching or reconstruction task, that committee rationales carry a special epistemic authority, or that moral disagreement can be reduced to noise around a correct answer? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify rival views—whether about moral realism, the irreducibility of normative judgment, or the social construction of ethical expertise—that it quietly rules out. Is the implicit epistemology coherent, or do different parts of the protocol presuppose inconsistent things about justification and authority? Finally, if we replaced this fidelity-based model with a view that treats ethical reasoning as fundamentally contestable and context-dependent rather than benchmarkable, what concrete changes would have to occur in how such institutions validate, deploy, and oversee these systems?
