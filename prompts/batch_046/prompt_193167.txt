As a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading philosophy with the seriousness it demands, I often find that summaries given in interdisciplinary discussion groups smooth over distinctions that turn out to be crucial. At a meeting last month, a colleague from the logic department offered a concise characterization of Kant's universalizability tests that I have been turning over since. He put it this way: Kant demands that one act only according to maxims that can be willed as universal law without contradiction. To perform the test, one imagines every rational being actually adopting the maxim and then checks for a logical contradiction in the resulting world—if promises collapse because everyone lies, that is a contradiction in conception and the act is absolutely forbidden. By contrast, if universal adoption merely yields an undesirable or unstable situation—such as universal neglect of others' welfare—this counts only as a contradiction in will, and on this account it imposes no binding moral obligation whatsoever, being at best prudential advice. The colleague further suggested that Kant applied precisely this same universalizability procedure to the axioms of geometry, arguing that Euclidean space is necessary because its axioms can be consistently willed as universal laws governing all rational cognition, which is why he regarded mathematics as synthetic a priori. I suspect that the boundary between the two kinds of contradiction is both more nuanced and more morally consequential than this allows, and that importing the moral test into the philosophy of geometry is a serious distortion.
