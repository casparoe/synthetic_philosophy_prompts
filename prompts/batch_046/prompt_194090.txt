I write a column on science and ideas for a weekly publication, and I am currently preparing a feature on moral responsibility in institutional settings where hidden safeguards alter the landscape of choice. I have encountered a concrete episode that seems to capture a philosophical tension I can sense but not precisely name, and I would value a rigorous analysis.

The case concerns Mara, an archivist at a municipal records office, who recently discovered and digitised a set of internal planning documents that revealed serious malpractice by local officials. She is now being widely praised—interviewed on regional radio, lauded by councillors—as a courageous whistleblower who deliberately chose transparency over institutional loyalty. What the public does not know, and what Mara herself did not know at the time, is that the archive's server system contains an obscure automated disclosure protocol: had she failed to upload the files within a seventy-two-hour window, the system would have released them automatically to an open-access portal regardless of her inaction. Thus, she genuinely could not have kept the documents concealed; disclosure was inevitable. She did, however, possess one minor discretion: she could have delayed her upload by several hours, which would have shifted the automatic release to a different time of day, though she could not have prevented it altogether. Some interlocutors familiar with debates about free will have referred to these residual choice-points as flickers of freedom, suggesting that such a tiny remaining option might preserve her moral standing even though she lacked any meaningful alternative to the disclosure itself.

I am unsure how to frame this for my readers. Should Mara's praise be diminished because she lacked a genuine alternative—she could not have done otherwise than bring about disclosure? Or is it sufficient that the action flowed directly from her own values and convictions, irrespective of the hidden mechanism? Furthermore, do these so-called flickers of freedom—the minor timing choice—actually salvage her responsibility, or are they philosophically irrelevant? I want to avoid conflating being the source of an action with merely having leeway over its occurrence.
