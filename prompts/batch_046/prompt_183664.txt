After a long and inconclusive argument with a close friend about whether moral life can ever present obligations that are genuinely, irreducibly in conflict, I have settled on a deliberately austere position: there are no true moral dilemmas. What appear to be contradictions between duties are, on closer inspection, always resolvable into one of two structures. The first is a concealed hierarchical ordering that the agent refuses to acknowledge because of affective attachment to the overridden option; the second is a structural artifact of role fragmentation, in which an agent simultaneously occupies institutional positions whose normative frameworks were never engineered to integrate, producing an illusory contradiction that belongs to social architecture rather than to morality itself. On this account, moral remainder—the persistent guilt or regret that defenders of dilemmas treat as ontic evidence—is merely an epistemic shadow cast by bounded rationality and organizational design. I want a rigorous map of what would genuinely count against this view. Sort the potential defeaters by decisiveness: formal arguments, phenomenological reports, institutional case studies, or conceptual collapses. Identify which of my two resolution strategies is more exposed, and specify the observation that would damage it specifically rather than being absorbable by the other branch. Assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every putative counterexample can be redescribed as either hidden hierarchy or role fragmentation, is that insulation intellectually vicious for a claim of this kind? Distinguish between the evidence that proponents of genuine dilemmas would actually cite and the evidence that I, holding this position, should concede as genuinely damaging. Finally, indicate whether there is a live risk that this framework collapses into moral particularism without my noticing, and what that collapse would look like.
