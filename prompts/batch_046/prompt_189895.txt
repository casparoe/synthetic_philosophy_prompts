A colleague and I have been debating whether biography can genuinely initiate moral improvement, and we constructed an argument that seems to show it cannot—yet we both think Plutarch's Parallel Lives and modern hagiography do teach something substantive to beginners. The steps are as follows.

(1) If biography teaches virtue, it must present its subject's actions as exemplars—specific deeds that a reader can treat as practical guidance rather than mere spectacle.
(2) Every particular action recorded in a life is either too context-bound to be repeated (rescuing a specific person from a specific danger) or too generic to be instructive (acting with courage or justice, which the reader already grasps in the abstract).
(3) So biography must work with calibrated actions: concrete and vivid enough to be memorable, yet structured so that recognizing their excellence reveals the underlying virtue that organizes them.
(4) But recognizing a calibrated action as genuinely excellent—rather than as habit, cultural convention, or mere luck—requires that the reader already possess that virtue in practical form.
(5) Therefore biography can teach virtue only to readers who already have it.
(6) Consequently, hagiography—biography designed to initiate the young or morally unformed—cannot achieve its stated purpose.
(7) Yet Plutarch writes his paired lives precisely to improve readers who lack the displayed virtues, and hagiography remains central to moral education across religious and secular traditions.
(8) So (4) must be false: we can recognize exemplary virtue in a life without already possessing it ourselves.
(9) But if practical possession is unnecessary for recognizing virtue, then moral knowledge is not practical knowledge in the traditional sense, and biography can at best produce aesthetic admiration rather than motivate action.

We are reluctant to surrender (1), (6)'s characterization of hagiography's initiatory aim, or (7). We also want to preserve some link between moral knowledge and practical capacity, rather than reducing biography to spectatorship. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? We want to preserve both the initiatory function of biography and the idea that moral knowledge is not merely theoretical, if that is possible.

Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
