I'm TAing a course on teh French Revolution and we're looking at class struggle, ideology, and contingency — particularly whether revolutions devour their own children. For one essay I asked students to apply the hydraulic pressure model we discussed in lectures, where popular mobilisation builds radical pressure that institutional valves release by sacrificing extremists in order to restore bureaucratic equilibrium. The question was: Use this model to explain Robespierre's fall in Thermidor 1794, making sure you address both class composition and at least one genuinely contingent event.

Here's what one student wrote:

The Jacobins were essentially a bourgeois party, and Robespierre pushed through the Maximum price controls to satisfy the sans-culottes, who were mostly artisans and small shopkeepers. As pressure built, the Committee of Public Safety acted like a valve by purging the Hébertists and then the Dantonists to cool things down. By July 1794 the pressure was too high because Robespierre refused to open the valve any further, so the Convention turned on him. This proves that revolutions always devour their children once ideological purity hits the reality of middle-class control. The contingent factor was the victory at Fleurus, which meant people no longer thought they needed the Terror.
