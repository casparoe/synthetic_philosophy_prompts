Exam question: Some ethicists argue that habitat destruction can reduce net wild animal suffering by lowering populations of r-selected species whose lives are predominantly characterized by starvation, predation, and disease. Evaluate this argument, paying particular attention to the distinction between reducing the absolute number of suffering individuals and altering the welfare trajectory of remaining populations, and address whether ecological replacement dynamics undermine the claim that destruction yields a net welfare improvement.

Student answer: The argument that habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering relies on the demographic profile of r-selected species, which reproduce in large numbers with low parental investment, meaning most offspring die painfully from starvation, exposure, or predation. By reducing habitat area, we lower carrying capacity and thus reduce the absolute number of individuals who will experience these harms. This is a valid application of suffering-focused ethics.

However, the argument fails to distinguish between reducing the number of sufferers and improving the welfare of survivors. In remaining habitat fragments, density-dependent stress actually increases per-capita suffering among survivors due to resource competition and disrupted social structures. Moreover, ecological replacement dynamics—where destroyed habitats are colonized by generalist species with different reproductive strategies—mean that the total suffering is not eliminated but displaced. Some philosophers, like those in the ecological replacement school, argue that this displacement often introduces K-selected species with longer lifespans but chronic stress conditions, which can actually increase cumulative suffering per unit of biomass.

Therefore, the argument for destruction as welfare-improving is unsound, because it ignores both the intensified suffering in remnant populations and the fact that replacement ecosystems generate comparable or greater suffering through different demographic pathways.
