I am drafting an essay on whether the philosophy of science can provide a unified demarcation criterion capable of distinguishing legitimate historical inquiry from pseudohistory, and I would welcome critical comments on the argument structure and conceptual accuracy. Rather than treating pseudohistory as a single phenomenon, I propose a three-part taxonomy of failure modes: first, Atlantis-style speculation, which treats evidential absence as interpretive license and substitutes narrative coherence for material corroboration; second, ancient-astronaut theories, which commit anachronistic projection by interpreting ambiguous archaeological traces through modern technological concepts; and third, Fomenko's New Chronology, which applies quantitative statistical techniques to astronomical records and textual traditions in ways that violate the auxiliary assumptions of mainstream chronology, producing a systematically compressed timeline. My thesis is that these three cases fail different demarcation tests—respectively falsifiability, coherence with established material-culture interpretation, and consistency with interlocking auxiliary sciences—so that no single criterion (whether Popperian, Lakatosian, or consensus-based) rejects them all for the same reason. I then ask whether this fragmentation undermines the demarcation project altogether, or whether we should adopt a pluralist, family-resemblance account of pseudohistory that abandons the search for necessary and sufficient conditions.

Please comment specifically on the following: (1) Is my characterization of Fomenko's method accurate—particularly the distinction between his revision of astronomical data (such as zodiac and eclipse records) and his revision of textual chronology—or am I conflating two separate argumentative moves? (2) Does the three-part taxonomy capture philosophically relevant distinctions, or does it merely redescribe a common failure to engage with mainstream scholarship? (3) Is my conclusion that demarcation should be domain-specific rather than unified too quick, especially given that research-programme criteria might be flexible enough to accommodate all three cases without collapsing into relativism? I am preparing this for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and want to avoid presenting a strawman of either the pseudohistories or the demarcation literature. Focus on logical coherence, the fairness of my characterizations, and whether the essay's central tension is genuinely interesting rather than manufactured.
