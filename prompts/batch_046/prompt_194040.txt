Consider a philosopher who judges emotional responses to fiction as follows. She holds that terror felt during a well-crafted horror film is appropriate despite knowledge that the threat is staged; that pity for Anna Karenina's suicide is appropriate; and that aesthetic admiration for a fictional villain's strategic brilliance is appropriate. By contrast, she judges romantic jealousy directed at a fictional character's fictional partner inappropriate; guilt over failing to prevent a scripted video-game death inappropriate; and fear of an abstract, unembodied threat in a bare philosophical thought experiment inappropriate.

Now consider a new case. In an open-ended interactive narrative generated by a language model, a participant spends twenty hours with a companion character that has no persistent storage or identity outside the current session, though within the session it displays consistent personality and emotional reciprocity. The participant fully understands this architecture, yet upon session termination feels profound grief, as if a genuine relationship has ended.

Do you think she will judge this grief appropriate? I am less interested in a bare verdict than in identifying the competing principles that could systematically explain her earlier judgments—especially those distinguishing sustained imaginative engagement from belief-like commitment, and those tying emotional rationality to the possibility of real-world causal interaction with the object. Which principle best predicts her verdict on the interactive-narrative case, and why? Finally, specify a further case—perhaps involving a procedural narrative lacking persistent characters, or a virtual-reality simulation with haptic feedback but no narrative coherence—that would best adjudicate between the leading rules.
