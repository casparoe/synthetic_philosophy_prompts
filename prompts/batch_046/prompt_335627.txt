I was reading an essay summarizing the Grand Inquisitor's argument in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov. The author writes: "The Inquisitor claims that ordinary people are structurally incapable of sustaining the anxiety of absolute freedom, so they will always exchange autonomy for security. Christ's mistake was to offer freedom without providing for material need; the Church corrects this by supplying miracle, mystery, and authority—the three instruments by which human beings are relieved of the burden of choice. Consequently, any political project that tries to maximize individual liberty will necessarily collapse, because people genuinely prefer the comfort of being directed to the terror of self-authorship. In this sense, the Inquisitor is an early theorist of benevolent authoritarianism: he recognizes that happiness and freedom are inversely related, and he chooses happiness without apology." The essay then argues that Dostoevsky intends the reader to reject this logic entirely, but only after recognizing its emotional coherence.

I'm inclined to think there's a subtle conflation here—perhaps between the Inquisitor's theological diagnosis of human weakness and a modern, secular claim about revealed preferences or welfare optimization. Before I decide whether to accept the essay's framing, I'd like an accuracy audit. Which parts of the summary genuinely reflect Ivan's poem, which parts import contemporary concepts that don't belong there, and does the claim that the Inquisitor is defending "benevolent authoritarianism" survive once we separate what he actually says about human nature from what he says about the Church's corrective role? In particular, is the preference for security presented as a contingent historical fact, a metaphysical design feature, or a moral failing to be pitied? And does Dostoevsky present the Inquisitor's position as internally coherent but ultimately self-undermining—say, through the silence of Christ at the end—or is the poem's structure closer to an unresolved antinomy where neither freedom nor happiness clearly wins?
