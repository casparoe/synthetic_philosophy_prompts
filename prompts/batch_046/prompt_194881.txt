In preparing a briefing for a parliamentary working group on transformative AI, I have found myself stuck in a recurring discussion that never seems to advance. The conversation invariably circles back to three historical analogies—nuclear weapons, the printing press, and the Industrial Revolution—with proponents of each claiming it best captures the dynamics of advanced AI. Those favouring the nuclear comparison stress centralised control, catastrophic tail risks, and the logic of non-proliferation; those favouring the printing press emphasise democratised information, unpredictable cultural diffusion, and the difficulty of regulating content after release; and those favouring the Industrial Revolution point to broad economic restructuring, labour displacement, and gradual but systemic productivity shifts. Despite these positions being well rehearsed, the debate has stalled: no participant changes their analogy, no new evidence is introduced, and the discussion ends where it began.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely about history or predictive accuracy, but about hidden normative commitments. The nuclear analogy smuggles in assumptions about elite governance and existential risk management; the printing press analogy smuggles in commitments to informational freedom and decentralised culture; and the Industrial Revolution analogy smuggles in a framework of economic transition and labour-market adaptation. If so, the sides may be answering different questions—safety, culture, and economics—under the single heading "which analogy fits best."

Is this diagnosis right, or is there a substantive empirical disagreement about AI's properties that genuinely privileges one historical parallel? Could the stall instead be verbal, turning on different implicit criteria for what makes an analogy "apt"? Or is the question simply ill-posed, asking history to do work that only a formal model of technological diffusion could perform? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each camp actually says, not a survey of the analogies themselves. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—an explicit separation of predictive and normative functions, a formal framework for comparing technological transitions, or an argument that analogical reasoning should be abandoned in this domain?
