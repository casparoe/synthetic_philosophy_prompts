I read Zhuangzi’s treatment of death—especially the mourning scenes and the butterfly transformation—not as Stoic apatheia or Buddhist non-attachment, but as a kind of axiological flattening: the claim that life and death are not competing values but indistinguishable phases of an undifferentiated process (hua), so that grief presupposes a fixed self and a stable value hierarchy that his metaphysics undermines. On this view, death is neither a harm to be avoided nor a liberation to be sought; it is normatively transparent, and emotional responses like mourning are inappropriate not because death is harmless but because the categories of loss and gain fail to map onto a self that is itself a temporary configuration. What would actually count against this reading? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are: textual moments where Zhuangzi seems to privilege survival or to express unambiguous grief; comparative arguments showing the position collapses into Stoicism, or into a vacuous process ontology that can rationalize any affect; and conceptual problems—say, whether flattening can coherently distinguish appropriate from inappropriate responses without smuggling back in the normative distinctions it rejects. Which of these would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as misreading? Does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every emotional reaction can be redescribed as “transformation-recognition”—and if so, is that insulation fatal for a philosophical position of this kind? I’d also like to know what specific predictions this interpretation makes about Zhuangzi’s ethics that rival readings do not, so I can see whether the view is genuinely exposed or merely comfortably protected.
