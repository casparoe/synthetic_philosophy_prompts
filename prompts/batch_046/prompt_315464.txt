You are a philosopher of science with expertise in metaphysics and practical epistemology. Before drafting anything, spend a moment brainstorming the operational differences among genuine dispositions, finked dispositions, masked dispositions, and disposition-mimics — specifically, which of these distinctions can survive translation into a laboratory checklist and which collapse under time pressure. Then, working as a consultant to an independent chemical-certification laboratory, turn the theory of dispositions, finks, and masks into a reusable decision procedure.

Context: I direct certification for a lab that issues formal disposition certificates for novel synthetic compounds — for example, whether a compound genuinely carries a hydrolytic, irritant, or catalytic disposition. Downstream clients (manufacturers, shippers, regulators) rely on these certificates for storage, handling, and liability protocols. I must apply the same framework repeatedly, usually within a 20-minute window per compound, and every certificate requires dual sign-off from a chemist and a legal-compliance officer. The staff running the tests are not philosophers, so the procedure must be usable without theoretical training.

Deliverable: A step-by-step decision procedure structured as ordered questions. For each step, specify: (1) what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed; (2) explicit stop points that return one of the following verdicts — disposition not certified, disposition present but masked or finked (route to special handling), or mimic/reject; (3) the points where judgment is unavoidable and must be flagged openly rather than hidden behind procedural language; and (4) which steps are essentially judgment calls dressed up as rules. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire — for instance, when an interfering agent is inseparable from standard synthesis batches, when the testing conditions themselves alter the compound, or when empirical ambiguity remains unresolved.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases:
A. A borate-caged prodrug that is inert in standard aqueous tests but whose cage is chemically unstable and can break spontaneously during storage, releasing an active irritant only upon contact with a specific enzyme.
B. A polymer additive that suppresses hydrolysis in every tested batch, but only because the additive is always present as an unavoidable synthesis byproduct; it is technically removable, yet never removed in practice.
C. A compound that behaves catalytically in 90% of tests, but the behavior tracks a trace palladium residue that varies by supplier; the firm's preferred supplier has low residue, but alternative suppliers have high residue, and the firm wants one uniform certificate covering all suppliers.

Make sure at least one of these applications yields an answer that conflicts with the firm's commercial preference or with an intuitive chemical classification. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do — for example, whether it resolves disputes about natural-kind membership, assigns moral or legal responsibility if a masked disposition later manifests and causes harm, or eliminates all value-laden judgment from certification.
