I am writing as a high school student in an after-school philosophy seminar. Last week, two classmates—Jordan and Riley—became locked in a debate over a modified version of the brain-transplant thought experiment associated with Daniel Dennett, the one usually referred to as Where Am I. They asked me to adjudicate, and since I genuinely could not settle it, I am laying out their exchange here for an outside evaluation.

The modified scenario they constructed is as follows. A patient's brain is replaced gradually, neuron by neuron, with synthetic units that are behaviorally and functionally identical in every respect. The replaced biological neurons are preserved alive in a vat, maintaining the same dynamic firing patterns. Once replacement is complete, the person in the original body—now running on entirely synthetic neurons—insists that she is present right there in that body. Meanwhile, the biological brain in the vat, though disconnected from any body, reports an equally vivid sense of presence and claims to be the true original, merely relocated.

Jordan advanced three arguments. First, she said that Dennett's original puzzle demonstrates that asking where the self is wrongly assumes there must be one correct answer; since both the synthetic-brained body and the biological brain in the vat share perfect psychological and functional continuity with the pre-replacement person, personal identity is genuinely duplicated, and there is simply no further fact about which one is really her. Second, Jordan argued that anyone who selects the synthetic-brained body over the vat is relying on an unexamined bias toward whatever system currently interacts with the world, not on any philosophically defensible criterion. Third, she concluded that Dennett's puzzle should therefore be read not as a search for the hidden location of consciousness, but as dissolving the question itself: once duplication is possible, there is no unique where.

Riley countered with four moves. First, he claimed Jordan misapplies Dennett, because Dennett's scenario concerns a single brain being relocated or copied into a different medium or body, not a gradual in-place replacement that leaves a functioning original behind; her case is closer to a material-continuity problem like the Ship of Theseus, not to Dennett's specific concern with spatial location. Second, Riley argued that functional identity alone does not establish substrate independence; the synthetic neurons may behave identically, but without a non-circular argument showing that material substrate is irrelevant, we have no reason to treat the synthetic brain and the biological brain as equally valid bearers of identity. He added that Dennett assumes substrate independence when he imagines consciousness surviving in a robot body, but he never demonstrates it. Third, Riley maintained that the biological brain in the vat lacks the causal-interactive history required by any credible psychological-continuity theory, because it has been causally isolated from the environment since extraction; it is therefore more like a separated twin or an archived backup than a relocated self. Fourth, Riley charged that Jordan's conclusion is self-defeating: if there truly is no fact of the matter, then Jordan should not be able to say that both candidates are equally the original, because that judgment still presupposes a determinate identity relation that applies to both, which is precisely the kind of fact she denies exists.

The exchange ended with Jordan accusing Riley of smuggling biological essentialism into his second move, and Riley accusing Jordan of treating an intuition pump as if it were empirical proof of duplication.

Please adjudicate their arguments move by move: evaluate Jordan's first, second, and third claims, then Riley's first, second, third, and fourth. For each, indicate whether it is clearly correct, clearly mistaken, or partly right but missing something important, and briefly justify your rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both are overlooking—particularly with respect to whether Dennett's original puzzle was even intended to address the kind of duplication scenario Jordan introduces.
