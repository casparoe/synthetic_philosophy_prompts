Two members of a university language-policy committee have been arguing over a proposed clause in the revised code of conduct. The clause concerns a student organization that officially registers under the name "The [X] Collective," where [X] is a term that originated as a severe ethnic slur but has been reclaimed as an in-group identifier. The draft clause states: "The [X] Collective shall be consulted on all revisions affecting student cultural centers."

Colleague A objects that the university should not embed the term in its own institutional prose, even inside the organization's self-chosen name. He argues that reclamation is speaker-relative and context-sensitive: it works when members of the targeted group use the term to express solidarity or defiance, but an administrative document is not an in-group speaker. Including it therefore risks what he calls institutional ventriloquism — the university borrowing the term's reclaimed form without possessing the social standing that makes reclamation legitimate, thereby producing a token that sounds like in-group speech but functions as out-group deployment.

Colleague B responds that refusing to use the organization's registered name is paternalistic. She says that treating the term as permanently toxic regardless of context denies the group's autonomy over its own identity, and that bureaucratic avoidance is not neutrality but active erasure. She adds that if the university can cite the name in meeting minutes without adopting it as its own vocabulary, then using it inside a descriptive clause should be permissible with the same distancing mechanism.

Colleague A replies with a distinction between mention and institutional endorsement. He grants that a court or report can mention a self-chosen name in a ruling without adopting it, but argues that embedding the name in a mandatory consultation clause gives it functional status in university governance — it becomes part of the institutional machinery rather than a quoted reference. He also introduces a further distinction: reclamation might neutralize a token for its immediate speaker without neutralizing the type across all registers. If the type remains pejorative in formal administrative contexts, then institutional use of even a reclaimed token is inappropriate.

Colleague B counters with an analogy: the university routinely uses terms such as queer studies and Black studies in official documents, both involving terms with histories of pejorative use that have undergone reclamation or reappropriation. She asks why [X] should be treated differently unless the committee is implicitly ranking which reclaimed identities deserve institutional recognition. She also suggests that A's token-type distinction collapses in practice, because every institutional use is itself a token, and the relevant question is whether the specific token is harmful given its context — which, she claims, depends on whether out-group members are present and whether the usage is attributive rather than descriptive.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the four moves above — (1) A's institutional-ventriloquism objection, (2) B's autonomy-and-erasure reply, (3) A's mention-versus-endorsement and token-type distinction, (4) B's analogy to other reclaimed terms and her collapse of the token-type distinction — rate its philosophical strength and identify any hidden assumption. Then say what both parties are missing: for instance, whether there is a relevant pragmatic distinction between attributive naming (using the name to identify the group) and descriptive categorization (using the term to classify members), or whether the conditions for successful reclamation depend on audience uptake rather than speaker standing alone. Finally, recommend whether the clause should stand as written, be rewritten with explicit attributive framing (e.g., "the organization registered as the [X] Collective"), or be replaced with a purely descriptive substitute, and identify the specific principle — about speaker authority, institutional voice, or pragmatic harm — that should govern the decision.
