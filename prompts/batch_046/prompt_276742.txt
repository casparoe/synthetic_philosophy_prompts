I'm trying to stabilize my position on historical explanation and would like a consistency audit before I develop it further. Here is the package of commitments I currently hold:

(1) Overdetermination is widespread in history: for many outcomes—say, the outbreak of a major war—there were several independent conditions, each of which was sufficient on its own, so that removing any one still would not have prevented the result.

(2) I accept a counterfactual-dependence criterion for causal relevance in historical explanation: to cite a factor C as part of the explanation of an event E, it must be true that, had C not occurred, E either would not have occurred or would have been substantially different in kind or scale.

(3) I reject the "contributory causation" move that relaxes (2) specifically for overdetermined cases. I think that if we allow genuinely causally relevant factors that make no counterfactual difference, we collapse the distinction between causes and background conditions, and explanatory selection becomes arbitrary.

(4) Despite (1)–(3), I want to say that in a case like the First World War, the alliance system, the assassination at Sarajevo, and the activation of offensive war plans are all genuinely causally relevant—not mere background, but part of the explanation—rather than selecting just one as "the" cause.

(5) I also hold that a complete historical explanation should be minimally sufficient: it should include all genuinely relevant factors and exclude redundant padding, but I do not take this to mean that it must list every sufficient condition.

Are these jointly tenable? The tension between (2)–(3) and (4) seems severe: if overdetermination is common and counterfactual difference is required, then in an overdetermined case none of the individual sufficient conditions meets the test, yet (4) insists we cite several. Is the resolution that such cases are not genuinely overdetermined, or must one of (2) or (3) be modified? Meanwhile, (1) together with (5) might seem to pull in opposite directions—if multiple conditions are each sufficient, doesn't minimal sufficiency force us to drop all but one? Or is that only an apparent tension, dissolving once we distinguish causal redundancy from explanatory redundancy? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to give, and identify the least costly revision that preserves the core of (4).
