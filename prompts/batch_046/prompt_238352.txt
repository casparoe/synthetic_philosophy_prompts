In contemporary debates about automating institutional procedures and the mechanization of legal language, a widespread analogy treats J.L. Austin's performative utterances as analogous to executable commands in a deterministic software environment: to utter a declaration of adjournment, a marriage vow, or a contractual acceptance is likened to invoking a function that updates an institutional state register, where felicity conditions operate as preconditions in a formal specification, misfires correspond to runtime errors or failed assertions, and the resulting institutional change is treated as the deterministic output of the call. The analogy is deployed to support the claim that performative success is fully formalizable, that artificial agents could execute such utterances without interpretive mediation, and that the social dimension of uptake is reducible to correct parameter passing. Provide an explicit correspondence table — speaker, audience, institutional setting, utterance form, intended effect, and conditions of failure mapped onto computing equivalents — then identify the disanalogies that specifically undermine the formalization thesis: consider the absence of a central compiler or runtime environment, the role of collective recognition and uptake in constituting the effect rather than merely registering it, the distinction between semantic misinterpretation and pragmatic misfire, and whether failure conditions are genuinely binary like program crashes or instead exist on a spectrum of partial or contested validity. Finally, evaluate whether the conclusion that performatives are mechanizable survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the computing analogy was smuggling in the very determinism it purported to demonstrate; if the analogy collapses in a principled way, propose a more accurate model for how institutional language produces effects.
