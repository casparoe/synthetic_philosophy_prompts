Analyze the transition at Abu Hureyra in the Levant (c. 9500–8500 BCE) — where the shift to domesticated cereals coincides with skeletal evidence of reduced stature, rising dental caries, and heavier workload markers, even as settlement size and storage architecture expanded — through the combined lens of Marshall Sahlins's original affluence thesis and James C. Scott's claim that grain agriculture functioned primarily as a technology of state legibility and extraction rather than a welfare improvement. Specify what in the archaeological record counts as evidence of affluence versus deprivation; whether the Natufian foraging baseline establishes a welfare loss that agriculture caused, or whether the larger, stored-grain settlements represent a different metric of well-being; how Scott's account of coercive domestication explains the persistence of the transition despite the skeletal costs, and where it remains silent; and whether the episode ultimately vindicates the framing of agriculture as a mistake for those who underwent it, or whether that judgment applies an anachronistic welfare calculus that the actors could not have used.
