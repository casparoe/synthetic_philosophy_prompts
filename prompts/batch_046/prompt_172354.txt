Examination Question (Problem 3):

The special composition question asks under what conditions, if any, a plurality of objects jointly compose a further object. The standard taxonomy distinguishes unrestricted composition (all pluralities compose a fusion), restrictionism (composition occurs only when parts satisfy a specific condition, such as being arranged life-wise or in physical contact), and nihilism (no pluralities ever compose a further object; only mereological simples exist).

Consider the biological case of Physarum polycephalum, a slime mold that can exist as discrete single-celled amoebae, aggregate into a continuous multicellular plasmodium, and then disperse again. During aggregation, individual cells lose separate membranes and form a shared cytoplasmic network; during dispersal, they regain independent boundaries. No persistent spatiotemporal boundary tracks a single "individual" across these transitions.

In 600–800 words, evaluate whether the three standard answers can accommodate this phenomenon without revision. In particular, address (i) whether the reversibility of aggregation undermines any argument that composition requires a stable boundary; (ii) whether a restrictionist who appeals to unified life-processes rather than to physical contact can consistently maintain that the dispersed amoebae and the aggregated plasmodium are not both fully real composite objects at different times; and (iii) whether this case provides independent reason to prefer one standard answer, or whether it instead indicates that the special composition question should be supplemented by a principle of "dynamic mereology"—the view that composition relations may vary over time even when the underlying parts remain constant.

Respondent M's Answer:

The special composition question asks when parts make a whole. Universalists like Lewis and Sider say yes, always: if you have two things, there is a fusion of them, no matter how scattered. Restrictionists say only sometimes—typically when parts are in contact or arranged into an organism. Nihilists, especially van Inwagen, say never: there are no tables, only simples arranged table-wise; and for living things, only organisms exist, composed of simples arranged life-wise.

Applying this to Physarum, the cells come together into a plasmodium and then split apart. This clearly supports universalism. At every stage there is some object: when dispersed, each amoeba is an object, and there are also all the arbitrary fusions of them; when aggregated, there is the plasmodium plus all its sub-fusions. The restrictionist who uses life-processes has a serious problem, because each amoeba is alive whether dispersed or aggregated. Therefore, on van Inwagen's view that only things arranged life-wise exist, both the separate cells and the plasmodium must count as organisms simultaneously, which is contradictory. Actually, van Inwagen might reply that the plasmodium is one organism that changes shape, like a person losing an arm, but that still concedes that the parts are arranged life-wise in both states, so the contradiction remains.

The nihilist fares worse. If only simples exist, then neither the plasmodium nor the amoebae are real—only quarks or other fundamental particles arranged slime-mold-wise. But we can observe the slime mold feeding and moving, so nihilism denies observable biological reality. Hence universalism is the only view consistent with the evidence.

Finally, the case shows we need dynamic mereology. Because the same cells form different wholes at different times, composition must be indexed to time: at t1 there is no plasmodium, at t2 there is. This does not replace the three standard answers but adds a temporal parameter to them. Universalism accommodates this most naturally, since it already allows any arrangement at any time. Restrictionism and nihilism have to add extra rules about when the temporal index applies, which makes them less parsimonious.
