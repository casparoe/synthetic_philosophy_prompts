I sit on the National Clinical Classification Board’s Behavioral Health Criteria Subcommittee, and we must issue a formal decision memo resolving one contested entry for the revised manual: whether to retain Chronic Voluntary Social Withdrawal (CVSW) as a clinical disorder, remove it entirely, or retain it only under a narrower, hybrid definition. The dispute among us is not merely empirical; it instantiates the naturalism versus normativism debate about disease, but in a form that carries direct institutional consequences for coverage, consent protocols, and public-health monitoring.

One faction, working from a biostatistical naturalist framework, argues that CVSW should be classified only when there is demonstrable dysfunction in species-typical social-cognitive mechanisms—measurable deficits in affect recognition, reciprocal coordination, or theory-of-mind—relative to functional design. On this view, if an individual’s withdrawal is sustained by intact cognitive architecture and represents a stable, autonomous preference, it is a variant of normal human functioning rather than a pathology, regardless of its social costs. Classification without such internal dysfunction, they warn, conflates statistical rarity with disease and invites unwarranted clinical intervention.

The opposing faction defends a normativist, capability-based criterion. They hold that a state counts as a disorder when it systematically undermines a cluster of vital social capabilities—not subjective happiness alone, but the practical ability to form reciprocal commitments, access shared institutional goods, and maintain bodily security through cooperative networks. They contend that CVSW, even when autonomously chosen, structurally erodes these capabilities in contemporary social environments, and therefore the diagnosis should be retained so that affected individuals remain eligible for accommodations, voluntary check-ins, and environmental adjustments without being forced into treatment.

A minority report submitted by the board’s internal philosophy advisor introduces a distinction we have not yet settled: between internal dysfunction and environmental mismatch. It argues that CVSW may represent not an individual pathology but a mismatch between a particular social-cognitive profile and a hyper-connected, institutionally dense social environment. If so, labeling the individual disordered is a category error; the appropriate institutional response should be environmental redesign—modifying workplace, residential, and civic structures—rather than clinical classification. The report cautions that retaining the diagnosis risks pathologizing temperament, compromising future informed-consent standards for any associated protocols, and diverting resources from structural reform.

We need an actionable memo recommending one of the three options—(1) retain CVSW with current criteria, (2) remove it entirely, or (3) retain it only if both internal dysfunction and documented capability deprivation are present. The memo must include: a clear bottom-line recommendation with reasoning accessible to board members without specialized philosophical training; an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, specifically the environmental-mismatch argument and its implications for consent and resource allocation; and an explanation of why that consideration does or does not defeat our recommendation. The document should be formal, stand entirely on the reasoning provided, contain no requests for external reading or citations, and be structured with sections for Recommendation, Reasoning, and Response to the Strongest Objection.
