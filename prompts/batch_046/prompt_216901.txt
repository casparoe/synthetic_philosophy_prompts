I'm a high school senior writing an independent philosophy project on whether moral compromise between incompatible value systems can ever yield genuine gains from trade. I've been tracking a particular dispute that seems permanently stuck: one camp models moral conflict on economic exchange, arguing that when autonomy and collective welfare clash, rational agents can trade away lower-priority commitments to secure higher-priority ones, producing mutual improvement analogous to market efficiency; the opposing camp rejects this framing entirely, insisting that moral values are incommensurable, that treating dignity or justice as fungible corrupts them, and that compromise is therefore not exchange but surrender. Both sides accept the same descriptive facts—values conflict, societies must compromise, and outcomes often leave everyone somewhat dissatisfied—yet neither has shifted position in years. Is this stalemate actually a hidden empirical disagreement about whether values admit cardinal comparison, a normative dispute about whether prudential reasoning may enter moral deliberation, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether moral purity permits exchange? Might it instead be verbal, with one side using value in a preference-ranking sense and the other in a sacred-commitment sense? Given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress here—an axiomatic model of incommensurable choice, a structured study of how people actually weigh moral trade-offs, or a conceptual distinction that dissolves the exchange analogy altogether? I currently suspect the deadlock comes mainly from smuggling economic vocabulary into moral reasoning, but I want to know whether that is the root cause or only a surface symptom.
