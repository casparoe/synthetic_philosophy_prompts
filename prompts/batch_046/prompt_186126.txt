I'm trying to diagnose an argument I constructed for a seminar on escalation and practical rationality, and I find it suspicious because the conclusion undermines the very idea that cutting losses can be rational. Here is the chain as I laid it out. (1) In the dollar auction, once you have bid 99 cents, bidding $1.01 is rational: quitting means losing your 99 cents with nothing in return, and the incremental cost of continuing is smaller than the sunk loss you would suffer by stopping. (2) This reasoning applies not because of the specific amounts, but because of a structural feature: whenever you have made an irreversible investment and face a rival who will claim the prize if you withdraw, continuing is rational regardless of the absolute size of the investment. (3) Career choices, institutional loyalties, and long-term research programs all share this structure: they involve irreversible investments of time and identity, and quitting means conceding the field to rivals or alternatives that then appear vindicated. (4) Therefore, rational practical reason requires that, once we have made substantial commitments in these domains, we should escalate rather than withdraw, even when the prospects look poor — the alternative is to render our prior investment a pure loss. (5) But this implies that rational agents should almost never abandon failing projects or revise their theoretical commitments, which contradicts the widespread and plausible view that cutting losses is often the rational response. (6) So either the dollar-auction reasoning is invalid even in its original setting, or we must reject the intuition that quitting can be rational.

If I have to choose, I would prefer to keep (2) — the structural generality of the reasoning — and (5) — the intuition about cutting losses — because together they seem to capture why escalation is both tempting and dangerous. But I cannot see which step between them breaks, and I worry that saving both requires paying an unexpected price elsewhere in practical philosophy. Where does this argument go wrong? Which premise should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does abandoning it cost for our understanding of rational commitment?
