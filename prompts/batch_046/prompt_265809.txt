hey — phd student in philosophy of medicine here, and i've been stuck on this institutional split that feels philosophically loaded but nobody in my department wants to talk about it.

in several european health systems, assisted dying is legally authorized only when the review board certifies that the patient's suffering has crossed what the guidelines call a lexical threshold — meaning it's treated as categorically worse than any possible future pleasure, not just worse on a continuous scale. but the very same ministry uses QALY-based cost-effectiveness analysis for every other allocation decision, where suffering and wellbeing are fully commensurable and traded off smoothly. what's weird is that the board itself has to apply both logics at once: they check the lexical box for the individual case, then immediately run a QALY comparison to see if denying the request would waste aggregate welfare.

what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? does it quietly require that pleasure and pain are incommensurable in the domain of autonomy but fully commensurable in the domain of distributive justice? or is it consistent with a single underlying axiology — maybe some form of threshold negative utilitarianism — that just gets implemented differently? and does the practice presuppose a particular view of moral personhood, like there's a boundary of subjective suffering that collective optimization is forbidden to breach?

i'm not looking for a reading list, just trying to reconstruct the implicit theory. also sorry for the ramble — wrote this at 3am and probably missed a typo or two.
