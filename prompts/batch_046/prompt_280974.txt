Two seminar colleagues, Maya and Jonas, got into a sustained argument about whether the standard historical explanation linking Gutenberg’s press to the European Scientific Revolution is an instance of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy. I’m reporting their exchange as a bystander; I don’t think either is obviously right and I want a point-by-point adjudication.

Maya opened by saying the canonical story—printing press → mass literacy → dissemination of ideas → scientific revolution—is a textbook narrative fallacy. We know the outcome (modern science emerged in Europe), so we construct a linear causal chain backward. She pointed out that movable metal type existed in Korea by 1234, over two centuries before Gutenberg, yet Korea experienced no comparable scientific transformation. If the technology were sufficient, similar outcomes should follow. The fact that they don’t, she argued, reveals that historians are retrofitting causal explanations to fit a predetermined endpoint.

Jonas replied that the Korean comparison is instructive but not fatal. Korean movable type was deployed mainly for Confucian classics and royal edicts within a centralized bureaucratic state that tightly controlled textual production. In Europe, the press met a different ecology: rising vernacular literacy, fragmented political jurisdictions that protected dissent, and specific demand for standardized Bibles and scientific texts. Identifying different outcomes under different conditions, he said, is comparative causal reasoning, not hindsight bias. The fallacy would be claiming the European outcome was inevitable regardless of context, not identifying context-specific mechanisms.

Maya came back that Jonas is selecting contextual factors precisely because he knows they correlate with the European outcome. He highlights “fragmented political authority” because Galileo could flee to another state; he cites “vernacular literacy” because he sees it rising in Europe. For Korea he invokes “centralized bureaucracy” because he knows innovation was stifled. This is explanatory hindsight: weighting variables by their correlation with an already-known result rather than by independent predictive power. Without a counterfactual framework that specifies which conditions were genuinely decisive ex ante, his “context-specific mechanisms” are just post-hoc rationalizations.

Jonas countered that this mischaracterizes comparative history. We have independent archival evidence of state control over Korean printing houses and of safe havens for scientists in Europe—evidence that does not depend on knowing the long-term scientific outcomes. The problem isn’t knowing the endpoint; it’s whether mechanisms can be identified that were operative regardless of it. He added that Maya risks a reverse fallacy: assuming that because a technology didn’t produce an identical effect elsewhere, any local causal claim is invalid. By that standard, we could never explain why penicillin worked in British hospitals but not in contexts lacking refrigeration or clinical protocols.

Maya then sharpened her point. She isn’t denying local causation altogether; she denies that we can confidently assign causal weights without overfitting to the European trajectory. There is also, she said, a subtler form of the fallacy: it shapes what we treat as “the” relevant technology. We talk about “the printing press” as if Gutenberg’s specific configuration—oil-based ink, metal movable type, screw press—were the decisive variable. But Korean printers had achieved movable type earlier using different materials and organizational forms. Our focus on Gutenberg reflects an outcome-driven selection of the “successful” variant, ignoring functional equivalents that did not lead to our present. This is not just hindsight bias in explanation; it is hindsight bias in defining the explanandum itself.

Jonas conceded that historians should not treat technological form as destiny, but he thought Maya overreaches. The reason historians study Gutenberg’s configuration more than Korean variants is partly that Gutenberg’s version scaled rapidly through European networks, not merely because of presentist bias. Where they genuinely disagree, he said, is epistemic: Maya thinks any causal weighting that references the outcome is suspect; he thinks it is only suspect when the weighting is not independently testable. The Korean case doesn’t invalidate European causal analysis; it refines it by showing boundary conditions.
