I've been working through the anti-luminosity literature for a seminar, and my study group tried to push the reasoning one step further into a result that feels clearly wrong—global skeptical nihilism about mental self-knowledge—but I can't pinpoint the leak. Here is the chain they offered, applied specifically to thermal phenomenology.

(1) Accept the KK principle in standard form: if an agent S knows that p, then S knows that S knows that p. (2) Assume a restricted luminosity thesis for thermal states: if S feels cold with intensity above a threshold T, then S is in a position to know she feels cold; call this state C. (3) Suppose S knows she is in C. By (1), S knows that she knows she is in C—call this CKC. (4) Knowing CKC requires being in a position to know that one knows C; but being in that position requires being in a distinct meta-phenomenal tracking state M that registers not mere coldness but the epistemic status of one's coldness-belief. (5) By (2) applied at the meta-level, if S is in M then S knows she is in M, generating CKM, and so on, yielding an infinite ascending chain C, CKC, CKCKC, ... (6) A finite cognitive architecture cannot instantiate an infinite hierarchy of distinct tracking states, so no finite agent can ever satisfy the condition in (3). (7) Therefore, for finite agents, knowing that one feels cold is impossible. (8) But any phenomenal state that could trigger luminosity generates the same regress, so the argument generalizes: finite agents have no self-knowledge of any mental state whatsoever.

The group takes this to show that accepting both KK and any form of luminosity collapses into absurdity. I want to preserve both the KK principle and at least a modest, non-omniscient form of self-knowledge, so I need to locate the faulty step. Which premise should I reject—(4), the claim that meta-knowledge requires a distinct phenomenal tracking state M; (5), the recursive application of luminosity to meta-level states; or (1), the unrestricted KK principle? For each option, explain why that step initially looked plausible and what abandoning it costs. In particular, if the flaw lies in (4), does that undermine the phenomenological motivation for luminosity altogether? If it lies in (5), am I merely stipulating that luminosity applies only at first order rather than solving the problem? And if I must reject (1) instead, what damage does that do to the rational transparency of belief?
