National science funding bodies and peer review panels routinely organize their allocations around an implicit gradient that treats incremental refinements of established frameworks as closer to the truth and highly revisionary proposals as distant or unmoored, despite having no institutional metric for measuring that proximity. Reviewers reject submissions that fail to achieve basic mechanistic plausibility—work that does not even reach the threshold of being testably wrong in the familiar sense—while simultaneously funding extensions of theories they acknowledge to be strictly false, such as Newtonian mechanics within its restricted domain. At the organizational level, agencies often maintain explicit portfolio splits—typically weighting heavily toward high-confidence, near-term projects with a smaller allocation for transformative research—yet they discuss these categories as if they tracked differences in verisimilitude rather than differences in tractability, risk, or expected utility.

What implicit theory of scientific progress and inter-theoretic comparison does this institutional architecture presuppose? Must it commit to a domain-independent ordering of false theories by nearness to truth—perhaps along the lines of formal metrics associated with philosophers such as Tichý or Miller—or can it be sustained by purely local, pragmatic judgments about predictive success within bounded domains? Reconstruct the commitments precisely: whether accuracy restricted to one domain counts as evidence of global proximity to reality, whether the funding distinction between incremental and transformative work encodes a philosophical judgment about distance from the truth rather than about variance or resource efficiency, and whether the practice is genuinely compatible with views—such as strong incommensurability theses—that deny meaningful cross-paradigm comparison. Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, noting where it is philosophically underdetermined, and describe what an explicit, codified verisimilitude standard would have to change: how reviewers might compare the proximity of quantum field theory against classical thermodynamics, whether domain-limited success could still license claims of approximation, and whether a proposal that overturns a well-confirmed framework could ever be rated as highly verisimilitudinous despite its revolutionary character.
