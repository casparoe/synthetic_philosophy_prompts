Write the full desiderata that any theory of archaeological inference must satisfy if it claims to reconstruct collective beliefs and institutional structures from material remains alone, with no access to textual or oral testimony. Begin with the specification: enumerate the phenomena it must account for, the conceptual distinctions it must preserve—such as that between an object's symbolic content and its practical function, or between individual cognitive states and distributed institutional practices—and the empirical or methodological constraints from adjacent disciplines it cannot violate. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or consideration: why a failure here renders the theory inadequate rather than merely incomplete. Then distinguish the non-negotiable requirements from those that are preferences of particular camps—uniformitarian analogical reasoning versus radical contextual particularism, for instance—and identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, particularly any tension between cross-cultural generalizability and the prohibition against anachronistic categorical imposition. Give special attention to what I will call overdetermined assemblages: material distributions that are structurally compatible with multiple, radically different belief systems and social organizations. The theory must provide principled criteria for discriminating among these interpretations rather than retreating to the minimal functional explanation. Which single requirement do you think does the most work in excluding the largest share of existing frameworks? Keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their justifications; you may briefly score the principal contending approaches—cognitive-processual archaeology, post-processual hermeneutics, and semiotic material-culture theory—against the list, but only after the list itself is fully articulated.
