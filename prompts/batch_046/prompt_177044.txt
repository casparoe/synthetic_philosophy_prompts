You are an expert philosopher of science with particular expertise in scientific modelling, idealisation, and methodological justification. Before you offer any recommendation, please take a moment to think carefully, and begin with a brief conceptual mapping: identify the philosophical tensions embedded in the situation, distinguish the relevant positions on unrealistic assumptions and predictive success, and only then apply them to the case at hand.

I serve on the steering committee of a community-owned urban agriculture co-operative. We are deciding between two forecasting systems for crop failure across our network of allotments. System A incorporates granular soil microbiome profiles, plant-specific phenological curves, and real-time weather interpolation; because the data are noisy and the ecological interactions are highly non-linear, its predictions are frequently unreliable in practice. System B was originally developed for medieval monastic garden layouts and has been repurposed for modern urban plots. It operates on deliberately false premises: it treats every plot as having uniform soil composition, constant solar exposure, and gardeners who never alter their planting schedules. Everyone on the committee recognises that these premises are factually wrong. Nevertheless, System B consistently identifies which allotments are at risk of failure with notable accuracy, and it does so using minimal data and computation. A newly appointed board member insists that continuing to use System B is intellectually dishonest — that a model built on known falsehoods has no legitimate place in decision-making, regardless of its track record, and that our co-operative charter demands epistemic transparency to members, which this model violates. Another member counters that predictive accuracy is the only criterion that matters, and that criticising the model's premises misses the point. I am inclined to defend System B, but I struggle to articulate why its false assumptions do not automatically disqualify it, without sounding as though I am endorsing falsehoods for convenience. Could you advise me on how to think about this dispute? In particular, I want to understand whether there is a principled distinction between a model that is false in its premises yet instrumentally valuable, and one that is merely erroneous; whether predictive success can ever justify retaining assumptions we know to be untrue; and what I should concede to the critic versus what I should hold firm, given that this is a small-scale social enterprise where transparency and trust matter as much as yield.
