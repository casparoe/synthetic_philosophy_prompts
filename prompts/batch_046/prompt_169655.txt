I'm trying to determine whether my judgments about self-deception form a coherent pattern, and I want a principle that captures them—together with an honest accounting of which judgments must be sacrificed for simplicity.

Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases:

A lifelong smoker has seen the epidemiological data but tells herself, without investigating, that the studies were probably funded by industry interests, and then feels genuinely reassured. Verdict: this is self-deception.

A department head knows her project is failing. She commissions an internal audit with parameters she has designed to highlight minor successes, receives the report, feels genuine relief, and stops thinking about the raw metrics without ever examining the full data set. Verdict: this is self-deception.

A hard determinist philosopher who writes a book denying free will receives praise and feels a brief glow of pride. She immediately classifies the feeling as a behavioral residue and insists she does not actually believe she deserves credit. Verdict: this is not self-deception; she is being consistent, not hiding a belief from herself.

A physician who knows her patient will not recover is asked by the patient whether he will get better. She answers that there is always hope, genuinely intending to comfort. During the conversation she does not actively think about the contradiction with her clinical judgment, though she could recall it immediately if pressed. Verdict: I am unsettled—this feels closer to compassionate compartmentalization than to self-deception, but I am not settled.

A job applicant who was fired writes independent consultant on his resume. When a close friend asks, he says he is between roles, and genuinely experiences the distinction as mostly semantic, not feeling that he is concealing anything. Verdict: this is self-deception.

A citizen installs an automated filter that removes all news critical of her government from her feed. She remembers configuring it, explains it as anxiety management, and genuinely never considers what the hidden articles might say. Verdict: I keep going back and forth—sometimes this looks like self-deception executed through technological outsourcing, sometimes like mere strategic ignorance that never engages belief at all.

Is there a unified principle—perhaps involving motivated avoidance of belief-forming processes, or a specific relation between a first-order belief and a second-order awareness—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of the six must be overturned to preserve coherence?

What does the best principle predict for an unlisted case: a scientist who unconsciously biases her statistical analysis toward her hypothesis, not by manipulating data, but by genuinely failing to notice which outliers should be excluded?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and indicate which single case I should revise if I want the simplest account that still captures the central phenomenon.
