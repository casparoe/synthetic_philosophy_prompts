I am a novelist presenting next Thursday at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-literature seminar. My thesis is that Derek Parfit's nonidentity problem misfires when mapped onto fictional ontology. Specifically, when an author revises a character's origin in a later work, the resulting figure is numerically distinct from the original—there is no shared causal or psychological continuity—yet readers experience a genuine moral grievance directed not at the new figure but at the author for erasing the first. Parfit's person-affecting framework cannot capture this grievance because it treats identity as purely causal and neglects what I call institutional identity: continuity constituted by authorial commitment and reader expectation rather than by metaphysical persistence conditions. The audience is twelve people: four analytic metaphysicians steeped in the nonidentity literature, three literary theorists committed to reader-response ethics, two philosophers of fiction who work on modal properties of storyworlds, and one bioethicist who applies nonidentity reasoning to reproductive policy. I need a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them—metaphysicians first, then literary theorists, then philosophers of fiction, then the bioethicist. For each objection, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest; provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute answer. Include, at the end, the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—the one that exposes my appeal to institutional identity as depending on an undefended analogy between authorship and legislative authority over persons. Keep the focus tight; do not ask for reading lists, bibliographies, or citations.
