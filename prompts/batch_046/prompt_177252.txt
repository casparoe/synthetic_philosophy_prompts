I am a high school senior preparing an essay for my philosophy elective, and I want to read Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland through the lens of his logical dialogue What the Tortoise Said to Achilles—the exchange in which the Tortoise presses Achilles to justify every application of modus ponens, producing an infinite regress of rules about rules. Rather than reducing the novel to an illustration, I want a critical reading that asks whether Wonderland's logic-games dramatize that regress or actively resist it. I am particularly interested in scenes where rules are invoked without justification: the Queen's arbitrary decrees during the trial of the Knave of Hearts, the constantly shifting rules of the Caucus-race, and Alice's own attempts to reason through identity puzzles and sudden size-changes. Does Alice behave more like Achilles, accepting inferences she has not fully justified in order to continue acting, or more like the Tortoise, repeatedly demanding a further rule? Furthermore, does Carroll imply that in practice the regress is halted not by logic alone but by social authority—by whoever holds the power to declare a rule final—so that the novel introduces a political or hierarchical dimension that the formal dialogue leaves out? I also want you to consider where the analogy breaks down: the novel's reliance on dream-logic, linguistic play, and emotional absurdity may show that Carroll understood the limits of applying strict logical analysis to lived experience. Please provide an interpretation that takes both the fiction and the philosophical problem seriously, points to specific scenes that illuminate or complicate the justification of deduction, notes honestly where the work resists the reading, and concludes with what the novel adds to the debate that formal argument by itself cannot capture. I do not need verbatim quotations, citations, or a bibliography—only a faithful critical interpretation.
