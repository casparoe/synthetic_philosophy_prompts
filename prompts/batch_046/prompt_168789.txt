You are preparing the opening remarks for a one-day interdisciplinary workshop titled “Pain, Recognition, and Response,” hosted at a university medical center. The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly half are neurologists and anesthesiologists who manage chronic and neuropathic pain; the other half are philosophers of mind and bioethicists; scattered among them are patient advocates with lived experience of conditions that disrupt the usual link between injury and suffering. You should speak for about five minutes—no more than 500 words, delivered as one continuous address without section headers, bullet points, slide references, or a concluding bibliography.

Your remarks must turn structurally on the problem of pain asymbolia: clinical cases in which patients register nociceptive damage, can report its location and intensity, yet lack the characteristic aversive affect and sometimes laugh or show indifference. Do not treat this as a clinical curiosity. Instead, use it as a wedge to establish the workshop’s central tension—not “how intense is the signal?” but “what kind of badness demands clinical and moral attention?” You must observe a precise rhetorical constraint: you cannot tell the clinicians that pain is purely reactive or “all in the interpretation,” since that would undermine the urgency of treating tissue damage; but you also cannot tell the philosophers that pain is a bare sensory datum wholly separable from suffering, since the asymbolia evidence shows that the sensory and affective dimensions can come apart. To navigate this, introduce a conceptual distinction of your own framing—perhaps the gap between registering damage and being addressed by it—and let that distinction govern every claim you make about treatment obligations. The philosophical idea should shape what you can and cannot say, not merely decorate the introduction.

The tone should be formal, precise, and hospitable, welcoming disagreement without flattening it. You may allude generally to the fact that some people in the room have lived with conditions in which hurt and horror are fractured, but do not solicit personal testimony or name specific patients. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular papers or authors by name; every move should be followable by someone without database access. Close by posing the workshop’s guiding question in a form that leaves both clinical commitment and philosophical skepticism intact, ending on an invitation rather than a resolution.
