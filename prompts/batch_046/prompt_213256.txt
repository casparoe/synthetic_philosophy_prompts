Following the recent death of my aunt Hillary, I have been sorting through her personal notebooks. She was a retired entomologist with a serious amateur interest in philosophy of mind, and several entries return to the ant-fugue analogy familiar from Douglas Hofstadter's work: an ant colony as a polyphonic system in which individual ants, like voices in a fugue, contribute to a collective pattern without any single ant grasping the whole theme. In one entry she asks whether such a colony might literally possess a mind, and whether its collective cognition could include even a rudimentary form of phenomenal experience. I have been discussing her notes with a colleague, and our disagreement has become precise enough that I want an outside adjudication. I am recounting the exchange below as a neutral bystander.

My colleague, Dr. Elena Voss, and I disagree over six specific moves.

(1) I began by arguing that an ant colony cannot be a mind because there is no unified point of view inside it. Each ant operates through its own independent sensory-motor loop; just as no single voice in a fugue hears the entire melody, no single ant perceives the colony-level plan. The colony behaves with apparent purpose, but that purpose is projected onto it by an external observer.

(2) Dr. Voss replied that this privileges centralized architectures without justification. On a functionalist account, mental status depends on the integration and processing of information, not on its concentration in a single center. The colony's pheromone networks, task-allocation dynamics, and environmental feedback loops constitute a global workspace that processes information about food, temperature, and threat in a genuinely cognitive manner.

(3) I countered that functionalism still requires representational states to belong to the system in question. No individual ant represents that food lies to the north; the pheromone marks are not internal representations but external modifications of the environment—stigmergic cues. The information resides in the world, not within any cognitive boundary, so the colony lacks the internal representational economy necessary for belief or intention.

(4) Dr. Voss answered that this draws an arbitrary boundary between internal and external representation. The pheromone network is continuously coupled with the ants' sensory apparatus, forming an extended cognitive system. She also observed that in a fugue the theme genuinely exists in the polyphonic structure even though no single voice contains it; analogously, colony-level representations can be genuinely present in the distributed structure without being localized in any particular ant.

(5) I then granted that distributed representation might be coherent, but insisted that cognition without diachronic unity is not a mind in the relevant sense. An ant colony is a constantly changing population; individual ants die and are replaced within weeks. There is no stable substrate that could maintain a continuous mental life—only a functional pattern that survives its parts, like a wave that outlives its water molecules.

(6) Dr. Voss responded that the same objection applies to human brains, in which neurons are continuously replaced and regenerated. Diachronic continuity is therefore always a matter of organizational and functional persistence, not material identity. If the brain can maintain a self across neuronal turnover, the colony can maintain one across ant turnover, provided the organizational dynamics are preserved. She added that demanding a single internal listener—some privileged locus of subjective experience—simply reinstates the Cartesian prejudice that distributed cognition was meant to overcome, and that a rudimentary, alien form of collective sentience might emerge precisely from such organizational persistence without requiring phenomenal unity in the human sense.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate its strength from clearly mistaken to clearly right and provide brief justification. Then state which party is more right overall, and identify one important conceptual or empirical consideration that both of us have neglected.
