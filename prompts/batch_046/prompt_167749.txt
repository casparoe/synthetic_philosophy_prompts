I have just finished a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend about Bernard Williams's integrity objection, centered on the idea that an agent may have one thought too many when she justifies saving a loved one by appeal to a general calculus rather than by direct attachment. I am now drafting the notes for an essay that tries to extend this objection into a domain where it is rarely applied, and I would appreciate your comments on coherence, accuracy, and interest.

The essay's central claim is as follows. Williams's canonical case involves an agent who saves his wife from drowning. The objection is not that he should have saved a stranger instead, but that if he saves her because he has calculated that her welfare, or the total welfare, is maximized by doing so, then his reasoning includes a thought that should not have been there. The integrity of the attachment requires that the reason she is his wife function not as one weighted consideration among others, but as a reason that structures the field of choice—perhaps rendering alternative courses of action unthinkable rather than merely less valuable.

I want to press this distinction further by arguing that the problem is not unique to act-utilitarian aggregation. Even a prioritarian framework that assigns greater moral weight to the welfare of those in special relationships can fall prey to the same charge, provided the agent treats those weights as parameters in a decision procedure she consults. I am calling this a relationship-weighted prioritarian decision procedure. The claim is that whenever the value of a relationship is represented as a coefficient that competes with other coefficients, the agent is required to think about her attachment in a way that is alien to its nature. The correct form of reasoning, by contrast, is one in which the attachment silences the calculus entirely.

The second part of the essay introduces a reply drawn from collective intentionality. In genuine joint action, an agent must sometimes represent not only her own attachments but those of her partners. Consider a case in which two parents must decide whether to rescue their child or assist strangers in a distant emergency. Each parent may need to represent the other's attachment to the child as a factor that contributes to their collective reason to act. Does this unavoidable representation of another's attachment as a weight within a shared deliberative structure revive the one-thought-too-many problem, or does the shift to collective agency change the moral psychology sufficiently that representation is no longer alienating?

My specific requests are these. First, is the distinction I draw between weights that compete within a procedure and reasons that structure the field of choice philosophically defensible, or does it collapse upon inspection? Second, have I mischaracterized Williams's original target—was he attacking only the psychological form of utilitarian deliberation, or also any formal decision procedure that includes relationship-sensitive weights? Third, does the extension to collective intentionality genuinely address the objection, or does it merely relocate the alienating representation from the individual to the group? Fourth, is the argument likely to be of interest to someone familiar with the standard literature, or does it read as an artificial stretching of a well-known point?

Please be direct: identify where the reasoning is weakest, where I may be smuggling in assumptions that do not belong, and whether the overall project is worth developing into a full essay.
