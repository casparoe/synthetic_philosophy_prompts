I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about origin essentialism—whether the intuition that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg holds up once you stop treating the zygote as a black box and start looking at monozygotic twinning (one origin, two distinct persons) and chimerism (one person carrying the genetic material of an absorbed twin, hence two origins in one body). I want a sestina on this. Use exactly these six rotating end-words: origin, split, genome, self, necessary, world. Structure the six stanzas as successive stages in the conceptual tension: the naive essentialist picture tying this self to that origin; the twinning objection that one origin can yield two selves; the chimeric counter-case where a single self bears two genomes; the genomic-essentialist retreat that what is necessary is not the particular gametes but the particular genome sequence; the modal convergence, where both strong and weak views agree I could not have arisen from that other sperm-egg pair but disagree on why; and a final stanza that refuses to adjudicate whether identity tracks the zygotic event or the genomic profile. The envoi must deploy all six end-words in their prescribed order and should place “self” and “origin” in adjacent lines without resolving which governs which, letting the form itself mirror the dialectic’s failure to close. The poem should be philosophically precise—someone familiar with the personal-identity and essentialism literature should recognize every move—yet it must not cite authors, texts, or page numbers, and it should treat the dispute as genuinely open rather than sermonizing. If my framing is confused—for instance, if chimerism does not actually threaten genomic essentialism in the way I suppose, or if a sestina’s rotating structure is ill-suited to carrying this particular argument—say so plainly rather than forcing the poem. Likewise, if you take liberties with the biology or with the modal logic of the essentialist claims, note exactly where and why.
