Please argue for the position that an advance directive retains moral authority over a later, severely cognitively impaired self not in virtue of metaphysical personal identity, but because it establishes a conditional fiduciary delegation. On this view, the competent author does not merely issue instructions to her future self; she appoints that future self (or a designated surrogate) as trustee over a specific bundle of critical interests, with authority contingent on fidelity to the values embedded in the directive rather than on psychological continuity. In making your case, address the standard objection that if psychological continuity is broken—say, by advanced dementia—there is no one left to be bound, and explain why the fiduciary framing avoids this problem without appealing to a Lockean or neo-Lockean account of identity. Throughout, clearly separate what is broadly settled in the literature (for instance, that prior autonomous choices can have some constraining force on later care) from what is genuinely contested (such as whether that force is best explained by narrative identity, relational autonomy, or the fiduciary model you defend). Do not present the fiduciary account as the dominant or consensus position; treat it as a specific, defensible alternative.
