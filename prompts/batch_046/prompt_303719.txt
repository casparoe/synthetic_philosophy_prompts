(3) Nevertheless, I reject conceivability as a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility; therefore, while the flying man shows that self-awareness is conceptually separable from sensation, I do not take it to demonstrate that actual disembodied existence is possible.

(4) I also maintain that, in the absence of any prior bodily experience, the flying man could not achieve de se identification as a particular individual—he could affirm that he exists, but he would lack the resources to distinguish himself from any other thinking substance.

(5) At the same time, I hold that phenomenal consciousness, even in its minimal form, requires some structural embodiment or sensorimotor integration; pure intellection without any bodily architecture is not sufficient for subjective experience.

(6) Finally, I am inclined to interpret Avicenna's scenario not as a literal proof of disembodied survival, but as a phenomenological limit-case compatible with embodied-cognition frameworks, provided we understand flying as extreme sensory deprivation rather than ontological separation from the body.
