I am preparing a feature interview on the claim that the agricultural revolution was a mistake for human welfare. My guest is an anthropologist who defends a revised version of the "original affluence" thesis: she argues that hunter-gatherer societies practiced an economy of limited desire rather than one of scarcity, and that agriculture's primary harm was not labor intensification alone but the introduction of storable surplus—making wealth visible, defensible, and therefore expropriable, which created the conditions for coercive hierarchy long before formal states emerged. I need six to eight interview questions that avoid restating her view and instead press where the literature presses. At least one should examine how her conception of affluence differs from the classic argument that well-being is achieved by wanting little rather than having much, and whether she treats the immediate-return versus delayed-return distinction as the decisive structural axis. At least one should target the mechanism linking storage, surplus legibility, and inequality: precisely how does stored wealth become a liability rather than a buffer, and how does she respond to the counterexample of foraging societies that practiced storage and developed hierarchy without agriculture? At least one should probe her niche claim about the "defensibility of surplus"—whether she means that storage technologies made resources physically visible and thus expropriable, or that they altered temporal orientation in a way that eroded egalitarian reciprocity. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The remaining questions should ask what evidence she believes could overturn her view, and whether she draws any normative implications for contemporary food and storage systems. The tone should be that of a journalist covering science and ideas: precise, unsentimental, and treating the thesis as a serious empirical claim rather than romantic nostalgia.
