I'm working through my own judgments about whether the standard criteria of authenticity in historical-Jesus research can ever do more than establish isolated sayings or deeds—specifically, whether they can settle "who he was" in any thick sense, such as his constitutive self-understanding or social role. Here are my verdicts on cases.

When the criterion of embarrassment is applied to Jesus' baptism by John, I think it secures the event but not his motivation: it does not settle whether he submitted out of repentance, solidarity, or strategic endorsement. Verdict: thin settlement only.

When multiple attestation supports the "Son of Man" sayings across independent streams, I think it strongly establishes that he used the phrase, yet I do not think it settles whether he meant a Danielic apocalyptic figure, a generic self-reference, or an ironic distancing. Verdict: reference secured, identity unsettled.

Applying dissimilarity to "love your enemies" makes it highly likely he taught something like it, and I feel this reveals a distinctive ethical orientation. Still, I hesitate to say it fixes whether he was fundamentally an ethicist rather than an apocalyptic prophet using ethics as boundary maintenance. Verdict: character hinted, not fixed.

Using coherence to construct an overall portrait—say, an apocalyptic prophet—feels circular to me, since the coherence depends on prior selections. So I think coherence can extend a portrait but never independently settle who he was.

Now a tension. The criterion of double dissimilarity applied to the temple action yields a politically charged prophetic protest that seems to settle part of his social role. But applying the same criterion to a wisdom saying like "consider the lilies" yields an equally authentic but politically quiet, contemplative figure. The same criterion produces incompatible thick portraits. Verdict: unreliable for identity settlement.

My back-and-forth case involves a single-source saying that passes both embarrassment and dissimilarity—suggesting he claimed direct, unmediated divine authority in a way that clashes with the apocalyptic-portrait coherence—but is attested only late and in one stream. Part of me wants to admit it and overturn the apocalyptic portrait; part of me wants to reject it despite passing other tests. Verdict: genuinely unsettled.

Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes when an authenticity criterion achieves "thin" settlement (that X happened or was said) from "thick" settlement (that X reveals his constitutive self-understanding or social role)? What should it say about the temple-action versus lilies tension, and about my back-and-forth case? State the principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its prediction for a case I have not listed—say, a miracle story that is multiply attested but fits popular expectation well—and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it must overturn.
