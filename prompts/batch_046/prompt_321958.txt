In a graduate seminar on moral psychology, a colleague repeatedly invokes the line “guilt is about what you did, shame is about who you are,” most recently to defend the thesis that a liberal culture ought to deliberately replace shame-based social regulation with guilt-based moral education, on the grounds that guilt targets actions rather than persons and therefore respects autonomy. I need help seeing exactly where the slippage occurs between that slogan and the conclusion. Can you disambiguate the precise claims the one-liner might be expressing? I am especially interested in how the distinction holds up once we allow for non-standard configurations—such as localized or role-specific shame that targets a particular capacity rather than the global self, or diffuse, character-based guilt that attaches to one’s enduring traits rather than to a discrete action—and in whether the cultural-plasticity claim (that a society can choose which emotion to institutionalize) depends on a specific reading of the slogan. Please lay out the candidate readings as a menu: for each, indicate whether it amounts to a tautology, a substantive psychological thesis, a normative evaluation, or a false essentialization; say which ones are defensible on their own terms; and then specify which reading or combination of readings is actually required to reach the conclusion that guilt is the morally superior emotion and that culture can engineer the transition. Be explicit about where the argument needs empirical premises about institutional malleability and emotional script-formation that the slogan itself does not supply, and flag any hidden assumptions about whether a culture’s dominant moral emotion is genuinely selectable rather than structurally embedded.
