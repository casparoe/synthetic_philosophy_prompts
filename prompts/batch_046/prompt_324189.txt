I'm helping draft guidelines for my local council's animal-licence scheme and I've noticed a strange tension. The assessment form scores keepers on preventing pain, hunger and fear, but also on providing 'positive opportunities' — like letting pigs root or giving birds flight space — and it treats those as independant goods, not just as tools to reduce suffering. There's even a clause saying an animal's behavioural repertoire must be respected regardless of whether it shows distress when that behaviour is blocked. At the same time, the form never asks whether the animal is an end in itself; everything is framed as human duties of care. I was reminded of Kant's position that we have only indirect duties to animals, since they lack rational autonomy, and that cruelty is wrong mainly because it corrupts our character. So what philosophical commitments does this licensing practice actually presuppose? Does treating natural behaviour as a non-substitutable good commit the council to direct moral status for animals, or can the whole framework be reconstructed within an indirect-duty view? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value behind these rules, distinguish what's strictly required from what's merely compatible, and point out where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — maybe several rival theories could licence it. Also, please tell me how confident you are in each part and single out the point you're least sure of, because I need to know where the argument is on shaky ground.
