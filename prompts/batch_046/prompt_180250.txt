I sit on the long-range investment committee of a state public-employee pension fund. Our quantitative research division has submitted a proposal to pilot a capital-allocation framework built explicitly on Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty, developed in his writings on probability and applied in his macroeconomic theory. The framework rejects expected-utility optimization over numerically defined scenarios for the fund's thirty-year horizon. Instead, it treats structural breaks — institutional collapse, discontinuous technological change, sovereign-default cascades — as Keynesian uncertainties: states for which no comparable numerical probabilities exist, and about which rational belief is better characterized by the relative weight of argument behind competing narratives than by subjective Bayesian priors. The model therefore directs capital toward maintaining liquidity preference and institutional optionality rather than toward risk-adjusted return maximization, and it explicitly refuses to collapse deep uncertainty into a probability distribution for optimization purposes. Proponents argue that standard stochastic modeling commits a category error when applied to non-ergodic economic domains; opponents argue that without numerically comparable probabilities the framework abandons disciplined decision-making and permits arbitrary, unreviewable allocations disguised as philosophical sophistication. Draft the committee's formal written opinion, to be circulated before next month's vote, recommending whether to approve the pilot, reject it outright, or approve it subject to specific conditions. The opinion should state a clear bottom line with a concrete implementation or rejection timeline, and should treat the quantitative opposition's strongest objection — that Keynesian uncertainty, if taken seriously, dissolves the distinction between investment judgment and unaccountable discretion — with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal.
