For an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students in a single 75-minute session, design an in-class deliberation and voting game that makes the structural force of Arrow’s impossibility theorem felt before it is named. Frame the collective decision not as a preference over consumer goods but as a binding vote on the jurisdiction of a hypothetical ethics review board: specifically, ranking three expansions of its scope—(A) including future generations in welfare calculations, (B) including sentient non-human animals, (C) including advanced artificial systems capable of suffering—where each option is defended by a different subgroup with a principled reason tied to one of Arrow’s conditions.

Structure the activity so that students first construct the preference orderings themselves in small groups, then attempt, in sequence, to apply three different decision rules (a pairwise majority rule, a Borda-style point system, and a sequential elimination procedure) to produce a collective ranking. Arrange the subgroup preferences so that they form a cycle over A, B, and C, but have each subgroup treat its favored condition—Pareto efficiency, non-dictatorship, or independence of irrelevant alternatives—as non-negotiable, so that when one rule violates their condition they reject the outcome and demand another rule.

Provide concrete rules for preference revelation, a symbolic payoff scheme using only course-participation points or assigned roles (no real money), and a timeline that allows the class to experience at least two complete cycles of “design a rule, apply it, reject the result, design another” before the facilitator reveals the pattern. The debrief should map each failure onto the corresponding Arrow condition, show which principle was sacrificed in each round, and ask students whether they are willing to abandon any of the four conditions to escape the cycle.

Include an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run—for example, students attempting to break the cycle through strategic bargaining or by collapsing distinctions between options rather than seeing the impossibility as structural—and how the facilitator should intervene. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Arrow’s result—specifically, the formal proof that no ordinal social welfare function can satisfy unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto efficiency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives simultaneously for three or more alternatives—the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in closing. Do not require bibliographies, citations, or references to specific papers; the reasoning should run entirely on the constructed case. Ensure that the philosophical stakes of moral standing and institutional jurisdiction are embedded in the options themselves, not added as a lecture afterward.
