After a long argument with a friend about Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, I want to see whether my judgments about particular cases form a coherent pattern or just collapse into inconsistency.

Here are my verdicts. In a field language, speakers shout "ravagi" at hares and also at fresh hare-tracks in snow; I think "ravagi" refers to hares and the tracks are only a derivative trigger. In the same culture, the word "kore" accompanies both burial rites and criminal trials with identical gestures; here I think there are genuinely two homophones—one for the dead, one for the accused—not a single broad concept. In an ancient astronomical text, a symbol S appears in geometric proofs about circles and in tables tracking cyclical years; I feel this is genuinely indeterminate, since S could denote geometric circularity or temporal recurrence with no behavioral fact to settle it. By contrast, a term "vel" applies to rivers, blood circulation, and data-flow in a network the speakers maintain; I take this as one analogical concept of flow, not ambiguity. The case I keep reversing concerns a language lacking tense morphology: "toma" is used with gestures toward both past and future events. Sometimes I think "toma" is tense-neutral, like "to occur"; sometimes I think its tense is radically indeterminate, analogous to the ontology case but for time rather than reference.

Is there a principle—perhaps about whether competing translations yield divergent predictions in observable behavior, or about whether the ambiguity is ontological versus analogical—that captures the pattern in cases 1, 2, and 4 and delivers a verdict on 3 and 5? Or must I drop one judgment to keep the principle simple? In particular, if I accept that 3 is genuinely indeterminate, am I forced to say the same about 5, and does that undermine my confidence in 1? Please state the principle precisely, apply it to a case I have not listed—say, translating a modal term in a language that expresses possibility only through verbal aspect—and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.
