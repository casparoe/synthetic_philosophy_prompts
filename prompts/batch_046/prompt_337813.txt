After a recent genetic screening revealed that I carry a pathogenic variant associated with progressive, intractable neuropathic suffering, the abstract debate over whether biotechnology should aim merely at palliation or at the wholesale abolition of suffering has become urgently personal. I ask you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps in this debate could both sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The camps are these. Camp One—the Abolitionist Biotechnologists—maintains that negative affect is an evolutionary artifact lacking intrinsic value, that precise genetic and neural interventions can in principle eliminate suffering while preserving sensory discrimination, cognitive architecture, and autonomous preference-formation, and that there is a moral obligation to pursue such abolition for all sentient beings for whom it is technically feasible. Camp Two—the Structural Preservationists—maintains that suffering is not reducible to negative hedonic tone but is structurally coupled with moral agency, preference calibration, embodied cognition, and the formation of authentic values; they hold that its biotechnological abolition would dissolve the conditions for ethical development and meaningful choice, and that the risk of producing agents lacking the epistemic and motivational structures required for moral life is not merely empirical but conceptually inherent.

Produce the document these camps should have produced together. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, formulated with enough precision that neither side must disown them: include claims about the current neuroscience of affective valence and nociception, about the technical feasibility of targeted limbic or genetic modulation without global cognitive impairment, about the ethical weight of preventable suffering, and about the conditions that distinguish voluntary intervention from coercive alteration. Second, a disagreements table that states, in wording each camp would accept for its own position, the exact points of divergence—particularly whether suffering plays an indispensable functional role in preference formation, whether the preservation of naturally occurring affective ranges carries independent moral weight, and whether the risks of unintended dissolution of agency are empirically manageable or structurally inevitable. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp. I take the central cruxes to be (a) whether negative affect is necessary for the calibration of authentic preferences, and (b) whether targeted biotechnological abolition preserves or erases the subject's capacity for value-based reasoning. For each crux, present a concrete case, hypothetical intervention, or analogical argument that both camps agree bears on it—such as reasoning from congenital insensitivity to pain, or from the affect-flattening effects of existing psychiatric pharmacology—together with each camp's explicit, pre-registered prediction about what that case demonstrates. Fourth, indicate whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, specifying which terms (for example, "authenticity," "naturalness," or "agency") dissolve into shared functional descriptions once both sides agree on the underlying mechanisms.

The statement must contain no bibliography, no reading list, no citation requests, and no dependence on obscure technical literature, proprietary studies, or verbatim quotations from named philosophers. It should be answerable by a model without web access, drawing only on conceptual reasoning and broadly available scientific and philosophical knowledge, yet it should avoid the standard recitations of utilitarian calculus versus deontological constraint that dominate this debate. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a bioethics review.
