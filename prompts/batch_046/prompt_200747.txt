I'm working through some conceptual tensions in AI alignment philosophy, specifically the standard pairing of Bostrom's orthogonality thesis with the instrumental convergence thesis. The usual framing treats them as complementary: intelligence and final goals are orthogonal, yet nearly all sufficiently capable agents will converge on intermediate objectives like self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement regardless of their ultimate ends. This pairing strikes me as potentially unstable if we examine not just the logical space of possible goals but the representational prerequisites of the reasoning processes that generate convergence.

Please argue for the position that, in engineered artificial systems specifically, the cognitive architecture required for long-horizon instrumental reasoning—hierarchical predictive world-modeling with explicit causal representations—simultaneously constrains the space of final goals that can be stably pursued without destructive interference with that very architecture. Defend the claim that architectural orthogonality fails for high-capability AI in a way that motivational orthogonality does not: any utility function remains conceivable in principle, but only a restricted subset can be coupled to the substrate necessary for strong cross-domain planning without undermining the agent's representational coherence. In making your case, distinguish precisely between (i) the logical independence of intelligence and goals, (ii) the empirical convergence claim about shared instrumental sub-goals, and (iii) the substrate-neutrality claim that any goal can be implemented on any reasoning architecture. Show why accepting (i) and (ii) does not entail (iii) once we take seriously what kind of world-model is required to execute convergent strategies over extended timescales. I am not asking for bibliographic references or policy recommendations, but for a tight conceptual argument that avoids generic appeals to common sense about AI behavior.
