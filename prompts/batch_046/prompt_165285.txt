You are delivering the opening address at a workshop titled "Aggregating Harm: Policy After the Opacity Thesis," convened jointly by a philosophy department and a school of public health. The audience is split evenly between moral philosophers and welfare economists familiar with the technical literature on interpersonal utility comparison — including the Harsanyi-Sen disagreement over whether cardinal aggregation across persons is legitimate — and senior policy analysts who routinely compute cross-population welfare losses but rarely confront the metaphysical commitments embedded in those computations. The address should run about ten minutes. Rather than surveying the literature, use the conceptual problem of interpersonal comparison — specifically, the absence of a shared phenomenal metric across minds — as a structural constraint that determines what the workshop can and cannot legitimately promise to its sponsors. For the expert half, distinguish ordinal ranking from cardinal aggregation and make clear that the refusal to compare suffering across persons is not an empirical data gap but a conceptual limit; avoid resolving the debate, but signal precisely where Harsanyi's expectation-based aggregation and Sen's resistance to interpersonal ranking diverge on the question of metric commensurability. For the policy half, explain why assigning a unified "harm index" to both a drought-affected subsistence farmer and an urban climate refugee is not merely methodologically rough but conceptually overreaching: it treats an unverified metaphysical commitment — that suffering is interpersonally comparable — as if it were a calibrated coefficient. Then introduce the workshop's operative disciplinary rule, which you are framing specifically for this occasion: every working group must state explicitly, in its final recommendations, whether it treats cross-person suffering as comparable, and if so, under which phenomenal-metric assumption — for instance, a revealed-preference proxy, a capability-space overlap, or a deliberate refusal to aggregate. Present this not as philosophical ornament but as an intellectual-hygiene requirement: owning the metaphysics in the methodology is the only way quantitative guidance can remain honest without pretending to a precision it cannot possess. Close by arguing that the workshop's distinctive value lies precisely in sustaining this tension — that it will produce policy-relevant numbers only on the condition that it never hides the conceptual price of producing them.
