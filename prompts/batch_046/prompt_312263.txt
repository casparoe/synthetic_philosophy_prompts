After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about collective cognition, I want to sharpen my thinking on whether an ant colony can genuinely possess a mind. I am especially drawn to the framing suggested by the Ant Fugue and its figure of Aunt Hillary: the idea that an ant colony might be analogous to a brain, with individual ants functioning like neurons whose pheromonal signals loop and interact to produce emergent mental states. Against this, my friend defended a stricter form of distributed agency: she argued that we can usefully apply the intentional stance to the colony as a whole—treating it as having beliefs about food locations and desires for temperature regulation—without committing to any emergent phenomenal or symbolic substrate inside the colony.

Please compare these two conceptions—call them the fugue-emergence view and the distributed-stance view—specifically with respect to three capacities: (1) error-correction after a disrupted foraging trail, (2) retention and modification of behavior across seasonal cycles, and (3) adaptive reorganization following the removal of the queen. For each capacity, explain what the fugue-emergence view says is actually happening inside the colony, and what the distributed-stance view says is happening instead. Then address the following tension: the fugue view seems to require that mental properties supervene on specific interaction patterns among ants, yet if two colonies gradually merge and intermix pheromone networks, it is unclear whether we should say one mind has absorbed another, two minds now share a substrate, or that no unified mind existed all along. How would each view resolve that merger case? Finally, tell me whether you think one of these views can accommodate all three capacities without ad hoc additions, or whether we need a hybrid principle; if the latter, state precisely what that principle is and which of the two original views it sacrifices.
