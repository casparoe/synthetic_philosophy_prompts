I've been mulling over the Meno problem - why is knowledge actually better than just having a true belief? Here are my gut reactions to some weird cases. A: someone relies on a black-box neural net for weather; it's right every day for a year, they carry an umbrella, but understand nothing. Valuable true belief, but not knowledge - though it's almost as good practically. B: a botanist knows a mushroom is toxic via deep biochem. Clearly superior, but is that because it's "knowledge" or just more robust? C: a village avoids a poisonous berry because of a taboo; the reason they tell is myth, but the avoidance is correct and passed down generations. Do they collectively know? I lean yes. D: true belief formed by flipping a coin. Totally worthless. E: a stopped clock is right twice a day; check it only then, and you've got true beliefs with perfect local reliability. Still feels like no knowledge.

So what's the principle? Cases A and E both involve reliable mechanisms, but I treat them differently. B and C both feel like knowledge despite one having individual understanding and the other having none. Is there one account - counterfactual stability, non-accidental connection, justification - that saves all five, or do I need to drop one? And what about case F: a synthetic gene circuit produces a correct danger signal with no representation at all. Closer to B, C, or E? Don't need citations, just whether there's a coherent principle here or if my intuitions are just messy.
