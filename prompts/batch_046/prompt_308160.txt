Compare representational realism in neural-network interpretability—the claim that mechanistic analysis reveals internal states with determinate semantic contents—with what we might call “computational instrumentalism,” the view that interpretability techniques track only task-relative functional patterns and that attributing representations to individual neurons or circuits is a pragmatic fiction useful for prediction but lacking ontological commitment. Focus your comparison on how each view handles the phenomenon of superposition, where a single neural dimension appears to encode multiple unrelated features simultaneously: does superposition indicate genuinely compressed representations that remain ontologically real, or does it demonstrate that the representational vocabulary of interpretability is irreducibly context-dependent and therefore not tracking stable internal contents? In assessing which view is stronger, avoid appeals to authority or citation; instead, evaluate whether either account can explain why some interpretable directions align with human-interpretable concepts while others do not, without presupposing that alignment itself proves realism.
