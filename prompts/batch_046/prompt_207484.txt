I have been tracking a particular asymmetry in how arguments about diet and wild-animal intervention apply the distinction between direct harm and background ecological harm, and I am unsure whether this pattern carries an established name. In debates comparing beef and chicken consumption, advocates frequently invoke feed-conversion ratios: beef requires more crop acreage per calorie, which entails more deaths of small wild animals during planting and harvest. Here the indirect harm from crop cultivation is counted fully against the omnivorous option. Yet when the same interlocutors compare omnivorous diets to plant-based ones, they often invoke a replacement argument—the claim that wild animals killed by harvest machinery are mostly replaced by others with similarly poor lives—thereby treating crop deaths as a discounted, background feature of agriculture rather than as a decisive reason to avoid plants. By contrast, in debates about direct intervention in nature—such as vaccinating wild populations against endemic disease or reducing predation—the suffering at stake is treated as an immediate moral concern, with no parallel replacement discount: one rarely hears that reducing a predator's suffering is unimportant because another predator will occupy the same niche. The pattern is therefore not a simple disagreement about empirical quantities, but a selective deployment of ecological mediation as a moral discount factor. It weakens the case against omnivory when the mediator is a crop field, yet disappears when the mediator is a natural ecosystem and the proposed response is human intervention. Does this asymmetry have a recognized label—perhaps as a variant of moral distance, scope neglect, or agent-relative reasoning—or is it an unnamed failure mode? If the latter, I would like it defined with explicit boundary conditions: must the mediator be an ecosystem rather than a market? Must the counting agent differ from the causing agent? And how precisely does it differ from the standard intended-versus-foreseen distinction, given that in both agricultural and wild-intervention cases the harm or benefit is foreseen rather than intended?
