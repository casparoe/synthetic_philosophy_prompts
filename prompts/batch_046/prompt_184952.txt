I've been thinking, without any formal academic background, about what people mean when they say an AI or an idealized version of ourselves should figure out what humans "really" want—not just our current, contradictory wishes, but something cleaned up, fully informed, and consistent across time. I've encountered the phrase "coherent extrapolated volition" in casual reading and have been trying to understand it on my own: the idea that if you take a person's preferences, resolve their contradictions, give them complete information, and stabilize them against emotional swings, you might end up with a preference set that feels logical but no longer feels like "me." I want to feel the pull and the discomfort of this idea in a concrete, physical way rather than just thinking about it abstractly.

Please design a 90-minute workshop activity for ten to twelve adults with no philosophy background, run in a circle with one facilitator, that makes participants discover this tension through rules and choices rather than through lecture. The deliverable should be a playable structured deliberation or game with explicit rounds, followed by a detailed debrief.

Here is the mechanical structure I have in mind, which you should refine into exact rules: in Round One, each participant privately writes one "non-negotiable" value and one "flexible" preference. In Round Two, they are paired and given a realistic conflict scenario with hidden long-term consequences; they must negotiate a joint resolution. In Round Three, all resolutions are posted anonymously, and the group attempts to merge them into a single "coherent collective profile" that contains no contradictions. The rules should be arranged so that participants predictably find contradictions—one pair's resolution undermines another's—and must revise.

Then, in Round Four, introduce an extrapolation mechanism: each participant receives a "shadow card" representing the counter-preference they would hold if they adopted the emotional state (calm versus excited, fearful versus hopeful) and the full knowledge base of their negotiation partner. They must apply a consistency rule: a preference only survives if it holds across these shadow states. After individual revision, the group attempts Round Five: merging all shadow-stabilized preferences into one collective profile.

The rules and payoffs should be designed so that, predictably, the final merged profile is highly aligned across individuals—suggesting convergence—but feels alien or even repellent to the people who contributed to it. Participants should experience the coordination failure themselves: they should want the collective result to be "fair" yet feel that it erases the specific values they started with.

In the debrief, map each round onto a part of the concept: initial preference, hidden contradiction, extrapolation under stability and full-information conditions, and collective coherence. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong—such as participants rejecting the shadow-card rule as artificial, or refusing to vote for the final profile despite having built it step by step—and tell me exactly how to handle those moments without breaking the experience. Also be frank about which feature of the idea this physical setup cannot reproduce—likely the open-ended, iterative, agent-level nature of true extrapolation, or the absence of a real "idealized self" in the room—and explain precisely what I must supply verbally as facilitator to cover that gap.

To keep this from being a generic values-clarification exercise, inject a specific niche framing within the mechanics: for instance, make the shadow cards represent not just hidden information but "cross-state preference loops," where satisfying a preference in one emotional state actively undermines the conditions that make another preference possible. This should create a specific cascade effect—resolving one contradiction forces revision of a seemingly unrelated value—that standard preference-aggregation games do not produce. Do not ask participants to cite anyone, name any theory, or read texts in advance; the idea should emerge entirely from the discomfort of the final vote and the rules that led there.
