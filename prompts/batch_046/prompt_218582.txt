As a retired materials scientist who has recently begun studying metaphysics, I find the classic statue-and-clay puzzle both familiar and frustratingly alien: in my former field we treated composite objects—steel, ceramics, polymers—as having properties that depend on macro-organization without demanding that we declare them either identical to or utterly distinct from their constituent atoms. Applying that mindset, I have been working with a view I call organizational constitution. On this view, the statue is neither identical to the lump of clay nor a separate object that merely shares its space; rather, it is a higher-order organizational state of that clay, one that exists only when a specific formal structure—shape, artistic function, and institutional recognition—is imposed. Much like a crystal lattice is a state of atoms rather than an additional entity, the statue possesses modal and causal properties that the raw clay lacks: it can be destroyed by a curator's decision or appreciated aesthetically, and it survives only as long as the organizational conditions hold, even though every molecule may eventually be replaced.

Please describe the most important implications of taking this organizational constitution view seriously. In particular, address three domains. First, what does it imply for the identity conditions of artifacts that undergo repair or gradual decay—does the organizational state persist through piecemeal replacement of matter, and if so, by what criterion? Second, does this model extend to natural objects such as organisms or mountains, or does artistic intention create a metaphysically unique category of constituted object? Third, what are the ontological consequences for restoration: if a museum replaces every molecule of marble in a classical statue over centuries, has the original organizational state been preserved, recreated, or never truly existed? I ask for a committed answer, not a survey of options: defend the claim that one of these three implication-areas is the most decisive for judging whether organizational constitution solves the puzzle better than the standard four positions—identity, elimination, dominance, or Baker-style constitution—and explain precisely why the other two, though significant, do not carry equivalent weight. Be direct about where this view risks collapsing back into one of the standard accounts, and do not hedge by laying out every possibility without choosing among them.
