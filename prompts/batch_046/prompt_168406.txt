I have just come from a long, circular argument with a close friend who is writing a dissertation in sociology of law; we were debating whether historical materialism can genuinely accommodate institutional innovation that reshapes production, or whether every such case eventually collapses back into economic determination. Rather than continue talking past each other, we want a rigorous adversarial-collaboration document that treats our two positions as serious parties: a strict base-superstructure determinist, who holds that relations and forces of production are the primary causal engine of history and that legal, political, and ideological forms possess only reactive, derivative efficacy with no independent evolutionary dynamics; and an institutional-autonomist materialist, who accepts that production relations set hard boundary conditions but insists that institutions—especially legal frameworks, religious reform movements, and scientific regimes—have their own causal architectures, can develop internal contradictions, and can retroactively restructure the mode of production rather than merely lagging behind it. Please draft the joint statement they could both sign.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims about the core distinction between base and superstructure, the empirical reality of class struggle as a mechanism of change, the non-identity of economic structure and institutional form, and the well-documented divergence of institutional architectures under structurally similar economic conditions—claims that both sides accept without reservation. This should be followed by a disagreements table stating, in wording each camp would endorse for itself, the precise divergence over whether institutional autonomy is ontologically real or only apparent (a temporal lag in economic determination), and whether superstructural elements can ever initiate rather than merely respond to transformations in production relations. Then identify the cruxes: specifically, whether there exists any case in which a superstructural innovation—such as a radical restructuring of property law or a widespread religious movement—preceded and causally enabled a shift in the economic base, and whether any predictive model of institutional change can be constructed that succeeds without including economic variables as necessary inputs. For each crux, specify a concrete historical or theoretical case that both sides agree is genuinely probative, record each side's explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates, and state whether either side has named in advance what evidence would actually move them. Keep the formulation precise and in the weeds—avoid generic summaries of Marxism—so that the residual disagreement is rendered exact rather than rhetorical, and do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to authority that a reader without access to a library could not evaluate.
