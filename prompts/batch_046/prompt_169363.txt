I'm a museum conservator tasked with writing condition reports for two ceramic vessels that laboratory analysis confirms are materially identical—same clay body, identical glaze chemistry, and matching firing signatures. The archival record indicates that one was manufactured for a ceremonial burial context and the other for routine domestic storage. When I examine them side by side, I reliably perceive the burial vessel as carrying a kind of compressed solemnity in its curvature and surface tension, while the domestic piece registers to me as merely functional and slightly disproportionate, even though I can point to no physical difference that explains this. One colleague insists that my knowledge of their intended use is corrupting my observation and that I should discipline myself to record only measurable properties, treating any contextual resonance as professional noise. Another colleague argues that knowing an object's ritual purpose is not an interference with perception but a constituent of seeing it correctly—that stripping away that knowledge would leave me with an impoverished, not a purified, view. I need to decide whether to suppress these context-sensitive impressions in my official documentation or to include them as legitimate perceptual data. Is there a meaningful distinction between seeing an object more fully because of what I know about it and seeing it wrongly because belief has distorted my vision?
