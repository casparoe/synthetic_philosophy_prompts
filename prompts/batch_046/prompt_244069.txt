Write a sestina on the precautionary principle using these six rotating end-words in order: threshold, proof, silence, act, cost, reversal. In the first three stanzas, trace the weak formulation—uncertainty justifies precaution—so that “silence” (the absence of conclusive evidence) pairs with “act” against “proof,” and “threshold” remains distant and undefined. In the fourth and fifth stanzas, shift to the strong formulation’s burden-reversal: “proof” and “reversal” should become coupled, “cost” should enter as the hidden risk generated by delay, and “act” should now face opposition rather than invitation. In the envoi, gather all six end-words together without resolving the tension: stage the precautionary paradox, in which the very “silence” that licenses precautionary “act” also licenses inaction, so that “threshold” is never fixed and “reversal” applies to both sides. Let the cyclical rhyme scheme embody the looping logic rather than breaking it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows risk ethics recognizes the weak/strong distinction and the paralysis objection—where guarding against one risk creates competing risks—without requiring named citations or bibliographies, and it should work as a poem for a reader who has never encountered regulatory philosophy. Do not name specific legal texts or quote any source; render the idea through image and structure alone.
