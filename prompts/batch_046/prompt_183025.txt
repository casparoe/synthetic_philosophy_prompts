I coordinate restorative accountability protocols for a community justice collective, and after a recent serious breach—one involving both tangible harm and a rupture in social belonging—our planning team is deadlocked over the emotional architecture of our response. Some of us want to design the process around guilt, treating the wrong as a discrete, reparable debt that the offender can discharge through specific actions; others insist on shame, treating the breach as a shift in the offender's position within the collective's moral field, recoverable only through reintegration rather than repayment. The deadlock is philosophical as much as practical: I need to know whether guilt is genuinely the superior moral emotion—whether its structure makes it more compatible with human dignity and repair—or whether shame carries a deeper social truth that guilt suppresses. I also need to know whether a culture or collective like ours can actually choose to cultivate one over the other, or whether emotional norms are embedded in an affective infrastructure—language, ritual authority, and normative permeability—that resists deliberate engineering.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my team can run in a single ninety-minute session before we design any protocol. The procedure must fix, in order: how to classify the harm (discrete act versus positional rupture), how to assess our collective's normative architecture (whether norms are internalized as personal standards or maintained through external surveillance and status tracking), whether the primary goal is repair, reintegration, or both, and where the philosophical analysis of guilt's moral-ledger logic versus shame's moral-field logic applies. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, and flag the points—especially around cultural malleability—where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be signed off by both our legal observer and our community elder. The procedure should stop with a clear recommendation, stop with a clear rejection, or hand the decision to a values-based judgment with the reason recorded.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. First, a youth who vandalized a shared garden—tangible harm with minimal status rupture. Second, an elder who abused authority to exclude members from decision-making—status rupture with diffuse harm. Third, a case in which the procedure recommends a shame-based reintegration protocol for an offender I personally believe should be held to guilt-based repair: make clear what the procedure demands, why it reaches that conclusion, and whether I am permitted to override it within the framework or must reject the framework explicitly. Finally, state precisely what this procedure cannot settle, including cases where the emotional culture of the collective is itself in flux, and note where an answer depends on empirical claims about social psychology that lie outside the philosophical distinction being operationalized.
