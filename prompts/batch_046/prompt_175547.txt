Working within the framework that associates G. E. Moore with two central commitments — that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty rank among the supreme intrinsic goods, and that the value of a complex mental state may exceed or fall short of the sum of the values of its parts — I offer the following verdicts on concrete composite cases and ask whether a single principle organizes them all.

Case 1. A person contemplates a beautiful painting in solitude, with full aesthetic absorption and no social dimension. Verdict: highly intrinsically valuable, though not at the ceiling.

Case 2. The same person contemplates the identical painting in the presence of a close friend, but the two do not interact; each is privately absorbed. Verdict: only slightly more valuable than Case 1, as though the goods are approximately additive.

Case 3. The person and the friend contemplate the painting together in a condition of mutual awareness: each party's appreciation is deepened by recognizing the other's parallel appreciation, so that the aesthetic and interpersonal elements interpenetrate. Verdict: dramatically more valuable than Case 2, and not merely the sum of a solitary aesthetic state plus an independent friendship state.

Case 4. A deep friendship exists, but one party systematically undermines the other's capacity for aesthetic appreciation through persistent mockery of their judgments. Verdict: the overall state seems less valuable than either the friendship alone or the aesthetic capacity alone; the combination appears corruptive rather than neutral.

Case 5. Two strangers simultaneously achieve an identical profound aesthetic insight into the same work, with no personal warmth or interaction between them. Verdict: valuable, yet I judge it to lack something essential that Case 3 possesses, despite the aesthetic component being equally intense.

Case 6. Two friends share intense mutual affection while jointly attending to an object that is ugly or trivial. Verdict: the state is valuable, but the aesthetic deficiency does not subtract linearly from the interpersonal value; instead, the value profile seems to shift in kind, suggesting the dimensions are not independently scalable.

Can these six verdicts be captured by one precise principle — for instance, one that requires reciprocal structural integration between the aesthetic and interpersonal components, or one that imposes a non-decomposability condition? If so, state the principle, identify the clause that handles each case, and predict its verdict for the unlisted situation in which two adversaries share exquisite aesthetic judgment but actively despise one another. If no single principle accommodates all six, indicate which verdict is least defensible and what simpler principle it must be sacrificed to preserve.
