I'm preparing a seminar handout on split-brain cases and the philosophical debate over whether severing the corpus callosum produces one conscious subject or two. I want you to construct a classification tree — a branching taxonomy — that sorts positions not by their familiar labels but by the concrete diagnostic commitments that separate them. Begin at the root with the question of phenomenal unity: does the patient retain a single, undivided phenomenal field, or can qualitative content be genuinely partitioned between the hemispheres?

From there, build branches that ask whether functional arbitration (a single center that resolves inter-hemispheric conflict) is necessary for subject-hood, and whether narrative self-modeling can survive division without phenomenal division. To keep the taxonomy from collapsing into the standard textbook dichotomy, inject a distinction between synchronous phenomenal binding — unity of experience at a single moment — and diachronic predictive integration, by which I mean whether the hemispheres share one continuous forward-model of the body and environment across time. Use this distinction to isolate positions that agree about moment-to-moment experience but disagree about whether the patient remains one agent or one autobiographical self over time.

For each leaf, provide either a standard name (e.g., "unity view," "division view," "agnostic functionalism") or label it "unnamed" if it represents a logically coherent but apparently undefended combination. Every leaf should include one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, illustrated with a concrete split-brain scenario — for instance, the patient drawing a circle with one hand and a square with the other, or giving contradictory verbal and non-verbal responses to the same stimulus presented in opposite visual fields. Explicitly flag any pair of positions your tree cannot genuinely separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and whether that gap seems due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Write the result in polished American English, suitable for a philosophy seminar, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
