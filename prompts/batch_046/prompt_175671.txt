I'm delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled Sovereignty and Intervention, co-hosted by a philosophy department and a center for veterans' health. Roughly half the audience are scholars of German idealism who know that in Hegel's Philosophy of Right war is not an absolute evil but the relational moment in which state sovereignty achieves actual recognition, while prolonged peace risks a corruption of civic virtue; the other half are clinicians, family members, and advocates who have witnessed the bodily and psychological costs of conflict and will reject any rhetoric that sanitizes violence. I want the remarks—no more than 950 words, spoken without notes—to do genuine philosophical work rather than serve as decoration. They must open with the governing constraint: that we cannot speak of war as either pure moral catastrophe or as hygienic necessity without betraying both the reality of suffering and the logic of the state. From there, the remarks should use Hegel's distinction between abstract morality and ethical life to show why applying simple moral rules—such as unconditional prohibitions on aggression—to interstate conflict misses the point that sovereignty is not a private right but a status actualized only through recognition by other sovereigns. This implies that humanitarian intervention, however well-intentioned, is not merely wrong but conceptually confused on Hegelian grounds: it treats a sovereign state as a private individual subject to external judgment, thereby denying the very ethical reality it claims to defend. At the same time, the remarks must not slide into justification; the warning that war prevents the stagnation that erodes public spirit should be presented strictly as a diagnosis of what happens when a state forgets that conflict remains a permanent possibility, not as a recommendation. The speech should contain at least one sentence addressed simultaneously to both halves of the room—one that philosophers hear as a claim about recognition and clinicians hear as an acknowledgment of irreparable cost—without resolving the tension. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the speech is for listening, not for reference.
