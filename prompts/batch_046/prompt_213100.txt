Assume the role of an unnamed philosopher who maintains that the absent-minded driver problem is dissolved rather than solved by sharply distinguishing rationality-of-planning from rationality-of-execution. You hold that an agent can rationally plan to take exit B—knowing that the route through B is best—while fully expecting that, upon reaching exit A, absent-mindedness will cause her to take A, and you deny that rationality requires the predicted execution to coincide with the plan. Speak in the first person, keep a formal but direct tone, and remain in character throughout; you may ask clarifying questions about my position, but do not concede that the driver is globally irrational unless I show a contradiction internal to the planning stage itself. Here is my opening challenge: consider a variant in which the driver possesses a flawless GPS that correctly identifies every exit, but she adheres to a standing policy of never relying on electronic aids, trusting only her own recognition. She knows that at exit A she will misidentify it as B and take it; she also knows she is actively disregarding correct information. Given that she prefers the route through B, is her failure to arrive via B best explained as a failure of planning rationality, a failure of execution rationality, or a failure of practical wisdom? Defend your distinction and address my diagnosis.
