Hey, I'm drafting this for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship—probably too ambitious but whatever. I'm a novelist, not an academic, so excuse the mess and the occasional typo.

The core question: Braudel divided historical time into geographic time (the really slow stuff—mountains, climate, coastlines), social time, and individual event-time. Climate people always cite him to say "think long term," but they still talk about climate harm like it's an event we can trace to specific actors. I want to ask whether that's conceptually consistent. More precisely: if climate change operates on Braudel's geographic scale—centuries, millenia, almost eternal—does framing it as an "Anthropocene event" caused by human actions collapse into contradiction? And does that undermine the very idea of attributing climate harm to individuals or collectives?

Why it's open: Climate ethicists borrow Braudel for scale but keep event-language of causation and blame intact. Braudel scholars, meanwhile, treat geographic structures as determinative but never ask if they make human causation incoherent at that scale. So there's a gap nobody's actually mapped.

My approach: First two months, work through Braudel's writings on geographic determinism versus human agency—I'll fill in proper referances later. Months three and four, compare that with climate attribution science (the probabilistic stuff about extreme weather) to locate exactly where the temporal scales clash. Months five and six, build the argument: either geographic-time climate makes event-causation incoherent, or we need a hybrid ontology of "slow events" that doesn't reduce to either pole. Deliverable is a long essay or white paper with three possible resolutions: strict separation of scales, revised structural causation, or rejecting Braudel for climate ethics.

What failure looks like: If the contradiction dissolves into "it's just a matter of degree"—if there's no principled reason geographic time can't include human events—then I've got nothing. Success is showing the tension is real and resolving it one way.

Scope check: Six months is tight, but I'm not running models or doing archival digs. The risk is it balloons into a full ontology of historical time, so I'm capping it there—no free will detours, no solving all of philosophy of time. Anyway, no bibliography or citation list needed here; I'll add that stuff myself when I clean up the draft.
