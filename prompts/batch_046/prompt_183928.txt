I'm preparing a paper for an upper-division course in early modern moral philosophy, and I've become skeptical of the standard classroom reading of Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" as a straightforward manifesto for cultural relativism. The usual interpretation holds that by praising Tupinambá practices and condemning European cruelty, Montaigne treats moral norms as incommensurable across societies and regards judgment from one's own cultural standpoint as presumptuous. I want you to argue against this reading: defend the position that Montaigne's comparative method does not entail strong cultural relativism, but rather operates through a hidden universalist metric—something like fidelity to natural inclination and proportionate response—that he applies consistently, if selectively, to both the cannibal society and his own. Your argument should distinguish sharply between "evaluative inversion," which merely reverses the civilized-barbarian hierarchy without abandoning it, and "calibrated universalism," which employs the same cross-cultural criteria but finds Europeans more deficient. Pay particular attention to his treatment of bodily practices: he admires cannibalism as an honest, direct expression of valor and revenge, yet he condemns European judicial torture and religious persecution not as alien customs to be tolerated, but as violations of a shared baseline of bodily integrity. Address the objection that his praise is purely rhetorical, designed to shame Europeans rather than to endorse the practice genuinely. To keep the analysis conceptually precise, develop the distinction between "normative mirroring"—using the other culture as a reflective surface for domestic critique—and "normative translation"—actually applying trans-cultural standards of judgment—without relying on direct quotations from the Essais.

Then extend your argument by testing it against a deliberately constructed hypothetical: imagine a society that practices fully consensual, transparent ritual incorporation of deceased elders as an act of filial duty, yet also imposes arbitrary, disproportionate corporal punishment for minor social infractions such as tardiness or verbal disrespect. Would Montaigne's framework compel him to praise the first practice while condemning the second, and if so, does that consistency reveal a substantive moral epistemology rather than ironic relativism? I am not asking for a survey of secondary literature or a bibliography; I am asking for a rigorous philosophical reconstruction that explains which conceptual rule best unifies his verdicts across these cases and why the relativist interpretation fails to account for the asymmetry in his judgments.
