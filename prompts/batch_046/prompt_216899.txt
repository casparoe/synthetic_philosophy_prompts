I’m designing the take-home final for a graduate seminar I teach on biography as moral calibration. Over the semester we’ve treated Plutarch’s Parallel Lives less as classical literature and more as an engineered comparative device—what I’ve been calling a moral reference-class generator—and we’ve contrasted it with modern linear hagiography (political saint-making, founder mythology) that strips away the counter-example. The students know the basic architecture: paired Greek and Roman figures, explicit synkrisis, the weighing of virtues against vices. What I need is an exam question that forces them past that summary.

Specifically, draft an essay prompt (answerable in about 1,200 words within a three-hour window) that asks students to evaluate this claim: Plutarch’s comparative frame solves the base-rate problem that ruins exemplar-based moral learning—namely, that selecting a single heroic narrative suppresses disconfirming evidence and lets the learner cherry-pick traits—but only by transforming the problem into an aesthetic one, because the reader can still choose which parallel to weight more heavily based on prior sympathies. The question should require them to (1) explain how the paired structure is supposed to function epistemically rather than merely narratively; (2) identify exactly where linear hagiography fails on the same metric; (3) assess whether including vices in the portrait actually supplies counterevidence or merely provides aesthetic balance; (4) say whether modern readers can extract structural analogies from Plutarch without similarity-matching to the figures; and (5) take a position on whether biography can therefore ever be a reliable moral technology.

Please provide: the exam question itself, phrased so it cannot be answered by reciting a summary; a model answer of roughly 800–900 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, each assigned point values totaling 25, with a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode for each—e.g., treating the comparison as decorative rather than epistemic, or assuming that mentioning a vice automatically solves the cherry-picking problem. Do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid leaning on specific secondary texts that a model without web access could not be expected to recall. Keep the framing precise and somewhat technical—I want this to feel like it belongs in a course that treats narrative forms as cognitive tools, not just historical texts.
