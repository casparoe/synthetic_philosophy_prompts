I am writing a novel whose protagonist is a theoretical physicist turned philosopher of science, and in developing her intellectual voice I have become preoccupied with a somewhat unfashionable claim about scientific thought experiments: that they yield genuine empirical knowledge not by allowing us to mentally simulate physically impossible scenarios, but by functioning as conceptual stress tests that render explicit the hidden normative architecture embedded in our measurement practices—commitments about which variables must be held constant, which deviations count as artifacts rather than signals, and which regularities demand causal explanation rather than statistical accommodation. I want you to argue for this position. Your argument should defend the view that when Galileo imagines two bodies of different weights falling together tied by a rope, or when Einstein considers an observer in a freely falling elevator, the epistemic payoff is not merely logical clarification or mental modeling, but the disclosure of structural constraints that our empirical methods implicitly presuppose and that the physical world either honors or violates. You must distinguish this account from both the conventional mental-model theory and the purely analytic interpretation, and you must address the central objection: if the knowledge produced is knowledge about the normative commitments built into our scientific framework, how does it avoid collapsing into mere self-knowledge about science rather than knowledge about nature itself? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
