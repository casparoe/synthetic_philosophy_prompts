I have been discussing commitment and rational persistence with a colleague over coffee, and I find myself holding a cluster of intuitive verdicts about escalation that I should like to systematise. In the classic dollar auction, bidding ninety-nine pounds merely to win a one-pound note so as not to forfeit earlier bids strikes me as plainly irrational: past expenditure ought not to govern present choice. I reach the same verdict about a firm that continues pouring resources into a failed software migration because it has already spent two million pounds, even though an off-the-shelf replacement would now be cheaper overall, and about a government that maintains a subsidy scheme costing more to administer than it distributes, solely because abolishing it would amount to an admission of prior error. In each instance, the agent seems to be treating irrecoverable costs as if they were reasons for further investment.

My intuitions grow less stable, however, once I move away from these textbook illustrations. A researcher who persists with an experiment after several null results, not from denial but because she genuinely believes the effect is real and merely requires a larger sample, does not strike me as guilty of escalation; her continued investment seems rational, even though years have already been spent. By contrast, a musician who has practised a technically demanding concerto for a decade, then learns she will never perform it professionally, yet continues solely because quitting would 'waste' all those hours, feels to me structurally parallel to the auction bidder. But I keep oscillating on a closely related variant: the same musician continues not to avoid waste, but because the practice has become so central to her self-conception that abandoning it feels like a kind of self-betrayal. Is this still the sunk-cost fallacy in disguise, or does it belong to a different category altogether?

Is there a single principle that captures all the verdicts I have marked as irrational escalation, clearly separates them from the researcher’s rational persistence, and delivers a definite judgement on the musician’s identity-driven continuation? If the simplest principle forces me to overturn one of my intuitions, I should like to know which one, and what the repaired account predicts for a case I have not yet considered—say, a chess player in a clearly lost endgame who continues playing out every move rather than resigning, not in hope of victory but because he has already calculated fifteen moves deep and feels compelled to complete the line.
