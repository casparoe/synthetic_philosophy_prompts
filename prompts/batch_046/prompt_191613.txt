I am constructing the final take-home assessment for my upper-division undergraduate seminar in philosophy and computation, and I need a single essay question that rewards conceptual precision rather than summary recall. The exam should allow forty-eight hours, should not exceed two thousand words, and carries forty marks total. I want the prompt to turn on a specific, somewhat niche conceptual tension: present a scenario in which a consequentialist decision-maker faces a choice whose optimal resolution requires solving a problem that is provably NP-hard, and in which a critic contends that the physical intractability of computing the optimum creates a moral excuse distinct from ordinary ignorance or epistemic limitation. Do not name this concept in the exam question itself; students should be required to identify and articulate the issue in their own terms, then evaluate whether it genuinely weakens normative demands or merely restates familiar limits on bounded rationality.

Please deliver three components. First, the exam question, written in formal academic prose suitable for students with background in normative ethics and philosophy of science but without specialist training in complexity theory. Second, a model answer that makes four specific moves: (a) distinguishes computational intractability from logical impossibility and from ordinary practical difficulty; (b) analyzes whether this intractability introduces a novel challenge to the principle that ought implies can, or whether it collapses into existing debates about bounded rationality; (c) compares the impact of the excuse on consequentialist obligations—given their reliance on global optimization—with its impact on deontological frameworks that do not require global calculation; and (d) addresses the objection that approximation algorithms or heuristic methods eliminate the problem. Third, a marking scheme divided into four ten-mark categories: conceptual precision in framing the problem; analysis of the relation between intractability and moral obligation; comparative assessment across normative theories; and identification of characteristic errors—specifically, conflating NP-hardness with logical impossibility, treating computational limits as purely epistemic failures rather than structural ones, assuming that intractability automatically excuses poor outcomes without examining whether a different decision procedure was available, and failing to distinguish the excuse from standard ignorance-based excuses. For each category, name the specific misconception that should cost marks.

The question must not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and must avoid dependence on specific theorems, papers, or historical attributions that students could only verify with outside sources.
