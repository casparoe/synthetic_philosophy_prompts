I was arguing with a mate from my rationalist reading group about weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, and we got properly stuck. The context was this: we'd both been skimming stuff on akrasia, and I brought up how hyperbolic discounting seems to explain why people flip-flop — you genuinely prefer the long-term good at T1, then at T2 the immediate reward dominates because the discount rate spikes. My friend said that's just redescribing the problem with a curve, not explaining it. I said no, it explains the mechanism: the discount rate isn't constant, so your evaluation of the same outcome shifts just because it's closer in time, which is exactly the structural failure that makes it weakness rather than mere change of mind.

He came back with: if that's true, then there's no single 'you' being betrayed. There's just a sequence of time-slices, each rational given its own local discount rate. The regret you feel is just the T3 slice judging T2 by T3's standards. So akrasia is an illusion created by projecting false unity onto what is actually dynamic consistency. I pushed back using precommitment — Ulysses tying himself to the mast. If there's no unified agent, why do people bother with binding mechanisms at all? He said binding is just one slice imposing costs on future slices, and it's only rational when the cost of binding is lower than the expected loss. Most people don't lock the fridge because it's more hassle than it's worth, not because a 'true self' is being ignored. Then I mentioned that people often feel a specific kind of alienation — like the action wasn't 'me' — which suggests more than slice-to-slice variation. He said that's narrative construction after the fact, not evidence of a hidden controller.

Then we drifted into this slightly weird corner: it occured to me that hyperbolic discounting might not just affect utility, but also epistemic confidence in your past jdugements. So at T2 you don't just think 'cake is better now'; you think 'my T1 self was naive about how good cake actually is'. He said that's overintellectualising, that the curve applies to value, not belief, and that I'm muddying the waters. We couldn't agree. Can you adjudicate move by move? (4) Is there anything to my idea about 'epistemic discounting' of one's past evaluative judgements, or is it just muddying the waters? What's the strongest point each of us is missing?
