I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether scientific realism can survive the pessimistic meta-induction. She walked through the standard historical sequence — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, and even the more recent retreat from strict gene-centrism in evolutionary biology — and argued that the pattern is too consistent to be a sampling accident: whenever a theoretical framework achieves broad empirical success, its central ontology eventually gets replaced. I tried to resist by appealing to structural continuity or approximate truth, but she pressed me on whether those notions are just post-hoc immunization strategies; if every successor theory can be declared structurally continuous with its predecessor, then realism risks collapsing into a tautology that predicts nothing about future ontology. We circled for hours without resolution, and I want to step back and ask for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this stalemate substantive, verbal, or masked by a hidden disagreement? Do the sides genuinely differ about the frequency of ontological discontinuity in mature science, or do they differ about the reference class — whether abandoned conjectures like phlogiston should be weighted equally with long-lived frameworks — with realists implicitly selecting for theories that survived long enough to be replaced? Is there an unacknowledged empirical claim about base rates hidden beneath anecdotal historiography? Or is the disagreement really about what realism is supposed to do — describe the actual history of science versus license predictive confidence in current practice — with each side answering a different question under the same label? Give me an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually says: are we talking past each other because truth functions normatively for realists and descriptively for anti-realists; is there a formal result about theory succession or a quantitative historiometric distinction that could break the deadlock; or is the question ill-posed because it treats approximate truth as a binary global property rather than a graded, domain-specific relation? And if no resolution should be expected, tell me exactly why the disagreement is structurally intractable — whether it turns on competing intuitions about scientific progress that neither side can argue for, or on standards of evidence that are themselves theory-laden — and what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a refined conceptual distinction, an empirical study of replacement rates, or a change in the framing question itself.
