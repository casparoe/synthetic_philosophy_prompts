I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced seminar on classical political philosophy, and over the course of the term I have been tracking my professor's judgments on canonical failures of Athenian direct democracy. She has offered detailed assessments of three cases: the Mytilenean debate of 427 BCE, the Sicilian Expedition of 415–413 BCE, and the trial and execution of Socrates in 399 BCE. In each instance, she has evaluated whether the failure should be attributed primarily to structural features of democratic decision-making—such as vulnerability to rhetorical manipulation, absence of sustained executive leadership, or majoritarian intolerance of dissent—or to contingent circumstances that any governing system might have faced. Her verdicts have been consistent but not mechanically uniform: she describes the Mytilenean reversal as evidence that democracy is prone to impulsive punitive extremes yet capable of rapid self-correction; she treats the Sicilian disaster as an institutional failure of strategic continuity rather than a corruption of democratic deliberation itself, noting that dissenting voices like Nicias were heard; and she regards Socrates' execution as a substantive democratic failure, in which legal procedures were followed but served to legitimize an unjust majority will against philosophical dissent.

A new case has come up in our discussion: in 406 BCE, following the naval victory at Arginusae, the Athenian assembly—moved by emotional speeches and popular anger over shipwrecked sailors—voted collectively to execute eight victorious generals for failing to rescue survivors, despite a storm preventing rescue and despite procedural rules forbidding collective trials. Several citizens later expressed regret, and one of the generals' defenders was nearly executed himself for opposing the measure. Given my professor's track record, what verdict should she render on the Arginusae affair if she wishes to remain consistent? I would like you to reconstruct at least two plausible interpretive rules that jointly accommodate her three earlier judgments, show what each rule predicts about Arginusae, and identify which analog—Mytilene, Sicily, or Socrates—is closest for each rule. Then please offer your best guess at her actual verdict with reasoning, being honest about what broader principle she would be committing to with each possible choice. Finally, specify what further hypothetical or historical case would be needed to distinguish decisively between the two rules.
