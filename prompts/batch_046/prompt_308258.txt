In debates about evil and suffering, I keep noticing a two-step maneuver. First, a defender offers a concrete story about why a particular harm is permitted—say, that an isolated individual's pain contributes to some larger harmony that requires exactly that loss. When critics ask why the same harmony couldn't be achieved with less suffering, or with the loss distributed differently, the defender shifts ground: human judgment is too limited to assess the global design, so we should not expect visible reasons for particular cases. Yet once that challenge has been deflected, the defender often resumes advancing specific explanatory stories for other harms, treating them as if they had survived the earlier suspension of explanatory confidence. The result is that particular explanations are deployed when convenient and withdrawn when threatened, without either being abandoned. Is there an established name for this specific oscillation between concrete theodicy and global epistemic retreat—especially where the retreat functions to protect the explanations rather than replace them? If not, can you define the pattern precisely, distinguish it from plain equivocation and from the motte-and-bailey strategy, and map its boundaries: for instance, cases where a single consistent story is maintained throughout, or where the retreat is made permanent?
