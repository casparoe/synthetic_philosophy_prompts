I am delivering the keynote remarks at the retirement ceremony for Dr. Amara Okonkwo, who spent three decades drafting anti-corruption protocols for multilateral development institutions. The audience is split: half are legal scholars and economists who have debated her frameworks; the other half are family and community elders from her home region, for whom ceremonial gift-exchange and patron-client reciprocity form the basic architecture of social trust rather than a deviation from it. Please write a twelve-minute address in British English. The speech must turn on a specific conceptual distinction she once articulated in an internal memorandum — between “transactional corruption” (the breach of an already-institutionalised impartial rule) and “constitutive reciprocity” (a social bond that operates prior to any universal entitlement, so that it cannot be “violated” in the same sense). Use this distinction to shape what can and cannot be said: you must honour her institutional rigour without implicitly treating her own cultural practices as primitive or deficient, and you must comfort her family without collapsing into the claim that Western anti-corruption standards are merely parochial. The idea should do real structural work, not serve as decoration, and the address must land for specialists without losing the non-specialists. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named scholars, or reading lists; work solely with the distinction provided. Write it as a real occasion, not a lecture.
