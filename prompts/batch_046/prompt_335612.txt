I'm not trained in philosophy or economics, but I've been working through my own view on rationality across time, especially in situations involving repeated trades or shifting tastes. I want to lay out six commitments I've arrived at and ask whether they actually cohere as a set.

First, I believe that if nothing relevant changes, a rational agent should keep the same preferences from one decision point to the next. If I rank option A above option B this morning with no new input, I should not rank B above A this afternoon merely because the framing or context shifted.

Second, I accept the classic argument that intransitive preferences—ranking A over B, B over C, and C over A—expose an agent to being exploited through a loop of trades that gradually leaves them worse off, and I take that exploitability as a real failure of rationality.

Third, I think there is a meaningful distinction between perceiving an entire loop and perceiving only the next step. If each individual exchange feels like a slight improvement and I never realize the cycle is closing, then agreeing to each trade is not itself irrational; what fails is the global perception, not the local willingness to accept a better deal.

Fourth, I hold that emotional upheaval can legitimately alter preferences in ways that do not amount to learning a new fact about the world. If I become substantially more risk-averse after a traumatic experience, that shift should not be treated as a diachronic inconsistency, even though my beliefs about probabilities may not have changed.

Fifth, I've come to think there is a phenomenon I would call slow drift: over months, tastes can gradually shift through accumulated minor exposures—say, slowly preferring darker coffee roasts because they have become more familiar—without any single moment of revelation. I think this is rational provided the rate of change is bounded and I can still understand why my earlier self chose differently, even though no discrete learning event explains the shift.

Sixth, I also think that if I am explicitly shown the full cycle and asked whether I want to take the first step, I can rationally agree. Refusing solely because the trade might initiate a sequence that eventually pumps me seems to let a merely hypothetical future dictate my present evaluation, rather than judging the immediate exchange on its own merits.

Do these six commitments hang together? I am particularly worried that my allowance for slow drift together with my defense of local optimization in loops might undermine my first commitment to temporal consistency. I am also unsure whether my generous exception for emotional preference change conflicts with my stricter claim about knowingly entering a loop—perhaps I am being inconsistently permissive. Could you audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, specify the cheapest revision for each, and then say which single change to my position would clean up the most problems? I want an ordered assessment of the commitments themselves, not a general tutorial on decision theory.
