In a graduate working group on value theory, we have spent the last semester stuck on the same cluster of problems in population axiology: Total Utilitarianism yields Parfit’s Repugnant Conclusion, Average Utilitarianism and Critical-Level theories avoid it only to generate mirror-image pathologies like the Sadistic Conclusion or indifferent neutrality, and person-affecting restrictions—whether framed in terms of harm, benefit, or consent—seem to formalize into either a disguised version of one of the impersonal views or an ordering so weak that it cannot rank populations of different sizes. Every participant knows the standard intuition pumps—the barely-happy lives, the addition of suffering people, the non-identity problem—and we have all conceded the basic impossibility results showing that any transitive, complete ordering over populations must violate at least one plausible condition. Yet no one has shifted. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about welfare metrics or empirical facts about well-being, but about a deeper structural disagreement that is rarely made explicit: one camp treats population ethics as an interpersonal aggregation problem, requiring a cardinal social-welfare function over actual and possible individuals, while the other treats it as an intrapersonal sequencing problem, asking what a rational agent should choose among branching futures in which different people come to exist. These two frameworks share vocabulary—“better for,” “worse population,” “moral reason”—but apply different consistency conditions, so they talk past each other even when they agree on every case description. Is this diagnosis right? Or is the deadlock really a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether bringing someone into existence can be wrong in itself, masked by formal machinery?
