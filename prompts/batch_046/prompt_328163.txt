After a long argument with a friend about whether Quine’s naturalism leaves any room for genuine moral disagreement or merely collapses ethics into pragmatic linguistic adjustment, I want a careful comparison of two competing interpretations of Quine’s scattered remarks on moral values. On one reading, moral commitments are peripheral nodes in the web of belief—highly revisable, largely conventional, analogous to the way logical truths sit near the center but are not sacrosanct—so that moral change is essentially a shift in habit or translation manual rather than a cognitive correction. On the other reading, moral values function as indispensable theoretical posits, analogous not to logical conventions but to physical objects or even to set-theoretic entities: we cannot make sense of intentional human action without them, they earn their keep by contributing to the overall predictive and explanatory coherence of our system, and they are therefore as objective as any commitment within Quine’s ontologically relative framework, even if they resist reduction to straightforward natural facts. Please address, in particular, whether Quine’s distinction between “projectible” and non-projectible predicates can be applied consistently to moral properties without violating his thesis of ontological relativity, and whether either interpretation can explain why moral disagreement feels like a failure of belief rather than a failure of radical translation. In your answer, separate what is textually settled about Quine’s position from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who know the literature well, and do not present a disputed reading—such as the claim that Quine was an unqualified moral anti-realist—as if it were the consensus view.
