As a novelist attempting to reconstruct the moral geography of eighteenth-century Geneva for a work of fiction, I have become preoccupied with Rousseau's attack on the theater—not merely the familiar charge that actors corrupt manners, but a more elusive problem about whether theatrical representation structurally disables the formation of the general will. In particular, I am struck by a tension rarely emphasized: Rousseau condemns the stage for substituting appearance for being and for cultivating a spectatorial passivity that undermines civic courage, yet he simultaneously relies on dramatic and narrative structures in his own pedagogical and autobiographical writings to move the reader's soul. This suggests at least three open conceptual lines. First, whether the corruption Rousseau identifies is specific to collective theatrical spectacle or extends to any mediated representation of virtue—including printed narrative or private reading. Second, whether his critique depends on a sociology of face-to-face civic assemblies that may not apply once political communities scale beyond the city-state. Third, whether there is a recoverable distinction between bad theatrical identification, where the spectator vicariously lives through fictional passions, and good narrative identification, where the reader is actively reconstructed by the text.

If I could support only a small group of thinkers for a limited period to pursue the implications of this cluster, which of these lines—or others I have not named—should take priority? I am less interested in a catalog than in a defended ordering. For each question you select, explain why it is upstream of the others: does resolving the representational problem settle the scaling problem, or does the sociology of assembly have to be clarified first? Assess tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work, and indicate what kind of argument or counterexample over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking. Finally, which of these questions, if answered negatively, would most weaken the case for treating Rousseau's theater critique as relevant to modern democratic spectacle, and which are overrated because they depend on historical conditions that no longer obtain?
