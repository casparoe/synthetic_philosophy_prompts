I've been arguing with a friend about institutional benefits that can genuinely reach only some people—say, a disability-access grant for a few researchers, or a hazard bonus for one division that no other division can claim. We both agree it would be wrong to cancel the grant or bonus solely to erase the gap if the others can't be brought up to match. Yet my friend still treats the unequal distribution as carrying a kind of intrinsic wrongness: he says the institution must defend it with extraordinary rigor, as if the disparity itself were a moral stain that needs cleansing, even though he wouldn't support removing the benefit. I've seen the same pattern elsewhere—people reject tiered arrangements not because they want universal extension, but because the gap seems defective in itself, while simultaneously refusing to harm the advantaged group just for equality's sake. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this—not simple envy, not the view that equality is always good, and not the idea that we should help the worst off first, but something more precise: treating unavoidable, non-remediable inequality as a distinct kind of unfairness without endorsing the remedy of pulling anyone down? If a term exists, I want to know how it relates to those neighboring ideas and where its boundaries lie; if not, can you name and define it, with clear cases where it applies and where it collapses into something else? A bare label won't do—I need to see what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
