Consider the procedural rule in common-law courts that allows a defendant to challenge the chain of custody for physical evidence, the reliability of an eyewitness, or the statistical basis of a forensic match, yet treats as non-justiciable any argument that the courtroom itself is a simulation, that the judge and jury were all replaced by duplicates, or that the statute being applied was never actually enacted by a legislature. What view of knowledge or justification—particularly in the spirit of Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology—does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: are these framework commitments treated as items of knowledge, as conditions that make doubt possible but are not themselves doubted, or as pragmatic postulates? Is the practice coherent with how the same system treats "framework" assumptions in expert testimony or in the admissibility rules for scientific evidence? And describe what a courtroom procedure would have to become if it replaced this hinge structure with a uniform probabilism that assigns every claim, including the existence of the court, a revisable credence. If the premise that courts implicitly operate with hinge propositions is confused, or if the task of extracting an implicit epistemology from procedural rules can't be done well, say so plainly rather than constructing the theory anyway.
