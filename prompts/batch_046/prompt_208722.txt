I am drafting a long-form feature on the epistemology of aesthetic judgment, and I have been testing my own intuitions about when testimony can legitimately shape what we believe about art. The pattern is uneven. When a conservator describes the original chromatic warmth of a fresco now chemically faded to uniform gray, and I form a belief about that lost aesthetic quality without direct access to the original pigment, the testimony feels fully legitimate—akin to accepting historical testimony with an aesthetic dimension. By contrast, when a close friend describes a pop ballad as making her feel profoundly lonely and urges me to anticipate the same emotional response before I have heard it, I think she overreaches; I must listen myself. If I stand before a minimalist sculpture and a guide directs my attention to a subtle asymmetry I initially missed, after which I genuinely perceive its tension, that strikes me as permissible perceptual guidance rather than illegitimate substitution. Yet when a critic insists that an intentionally abrasive atonal work is beautiful precisely because it produces discomfort, and that my direct irritation is therefore an aesthetic failure on my part, I resist having my first-order experience overridden, even while I acknowledge the work's artistic intent.

The pair that troubles me most is a niche variant of the first case: consider an immersive virtual reconstruction of a destroyed Baroque ceiling, assembled from archival measurements and pigment analysis, which reproduces every visible structural feature. A scholar who saw the original in 1948 testifies that the reconstruction misses an ineffable luminous depth inseparable from the original material substrate—a property that, by her account, is absent from every sensory channel now available to me. Is this like the faded fresco, where testimony reaches across an epistemic gap to a real but inaccessible property, or like the emotional ballad, where the speaker attempts to transmit a response that requires direct encounter? I want to know whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps indexed to the type of aesthetic property at stake, to the listener's available perceptual access, or to the speaker's authority—that accommodates all five verdicts, or whether consistency demands that I abandon one. If a principle can be articulated, I also want its prediction for a case I have not listed: testimony delivered not by a human but by an algorithm trained on expert judgments, describing the aesthetic properties of a synthetic image that possesses no physical original whatsoever. Which of my verdicts must it overturn, if any, and why?
