I cover science and ideas for a monthly, and lately I've been assigned more historical pieces — dead theories, failed experiments, the kind of stuff where it's super easy to write the past like it's just a bumpy road to now. I was talking with my friends about the Whig temptation, but I need something I can actually run at 11pm with a 48-hour deadline, not just "dont be presentist." I want a reusable editorial checklist — steps I apply to every draft before filing — that turns the anti-Whig principle into a procedure I can use without a historian on staff signing off. My editor wants narrative punch but hates just-so stories, so the flow has to tell me when I kill a frame outright and when I keep it with heavy modifications.

Specifically, build me a decision procedure in order: the exact question at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a hard no, and where it's a value call I have to own because the facts dont settle it. Make sure it handles the tricky middle cases — like when a past practice shares real machinery with a modern one but meant something totally different to its users (functional equivalence without conceptual continuity), or when a past failure accidentally created conditions for later success that the actors couldnt have intended. Then run it on three draft frames I'm considering: framing phrenology as "early neuroscience with bad data," framing alchemy's distillation practices as "pre-chemistry techniques that accidentally worked," and framing the mid-century rejection of Barbara McClintock's jumping-gene work as "the establishment suppressing a visible truth." I really want that last one to come out heroic, but I need the procedure to hand me the answer I wont like if that's what it actually says. Finally, tell me what the procedure cant do — where narrative bias still slips through even if I follow every step.
