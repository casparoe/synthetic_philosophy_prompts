You are delivering the opening remarks at a retirement symposium for a cognitive scientist who spent her career on self-reference and consciousness. Half the audience are colleagues in philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence familiar with Hofstadter's idea of the "I" as a strange loop — a symbol that refers back to itself through a recursive circuit of meaning — and half are family members, university administrators, and students from unrelated fields who are not. Write a ten-minute address that uses the strange loop as its central structural framework rather than decorative reference. Work with the following framing: the loop operates in three registers that must be kept distinct — the symbolic register of the pronoun I, the physical register of the neural and bodily substrate, and the narrative register of the life story told about that substrate — and explain how these registers interact without collapsing into one another. Use that interaction to argue that her identity is neither a fixed soul nor a mere illusion, but a stable pattern sustained by recursion. The address must be fully intelligible to non-experts without condescension, and must satisfy the experts by avoiding both mystical dualism and reductive eliminativism. Because the framework shapes what can be said, do not claim she "discovered her true self," do not say the loop has been "solved," and do not reduce her contributions to a list of publications or achievements. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
