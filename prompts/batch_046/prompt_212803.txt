Read F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby through the lens of tranquilism—the philosophical view that well-being consists entirely in the absence of craving and suffering, with no independent positive value in achievement, possession, or emotional intensity. The novel is conventionally interpreted as a tragedy of unfulfilled aspiration: Gatsby craves Daisy and the American Dream, and his death marks the collapse of that positive project. A tranquilist reading, however, might propose that Gatsby's deeper affliction is not the failure to obtain what he craves, but the persistence of craving itself—Daisy's presence does not still him, and the green light remains an object of restless desire. Examine whether the novel dramatises the tranquilist claim that the elimination of craving, rather than its satisfaction, is the sole genuine good, and whether any character—perhaps Nick Carraway in his final withdrawal—approximates this state. At the same time, address where the novel resists the reading: does Fitzgerald aestheticise Gatsby's longing in ways that tranquilism must dismiss as mere pathology? I am preparing a philosophy of literature discussion and want a reading that takes both the text and the position seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other; please respond in British English, and conclude by considering whether the novel's emotional power depends precisely on valorising the cravings that tranquilism condemns.
