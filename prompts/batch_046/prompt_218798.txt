I am drafting the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar in epistemology and philosophy of science, and I need an essay question that tests whether students truly understand the reference class problem rather than merely recalling textbook illustrations such as the blue bus case or the smoking-cancer correlation. The question should place the problem in a concrete, slightly unconventional setting: a clinical risk-assessment algorithm that must assign a single probability of adverse outcome to an individual patient by drawing on four nested reference classes—national population statistics, regional hospital records, occupational cohort data, and family-genetic profiles—each yielding a different numerical risk. Students should be asked to explain why the choice among these classes is not reducible to a purely empirical or statistical matter, but involves a conceptual and normative question about what the assigned probability is supposed to represent, and then to assess whether a pluralist stance—holding that each class-relative probability is correct in its own context—actually resolves the difficulty or instead leaves the clinician without a rational basis for action. The exam should allow about forty-five minutes for this question. Please provide the exam question in full, then a model answer of approximately five hundred words, and finally a marking scheme that distinguishes an excellent answer from a merely good one by naming the precise moves required: recognition that reference-class selection carries normative weight, clear separation of the reference class problem from ordinary statistical uncertainty or measurement error, and a demonstration that pluralism fails to eliminate the need for a single decision-relevant probability. Present everything as continuous prose in a single flowing document, with no headings, bullet points, boldface, tables, or numbered lists, arranged as you would in a formal letter or memo. Do not include any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid invoking specific publications, authors with precise titles, or dated works that a respondent without web access could not verify; refer to positions in general terms only.
