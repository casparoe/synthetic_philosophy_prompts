Design a decision tree that classifies positions on implementing ethical reasoning in autonomous systems characterized by distributed decision authority—architectures in which a central controller does not monopolize moral judgment but must coordinate with local sub-agents retaining partial autonomy. Structure each branching node as a substantive claim on which positions genuinely divide: whether moral reasoning is computable at all; if so, whether it should be centralized or distributed across nodes; if distributed, whether local agents apply invariant deontic constraints or engage in dynamic inter-agent negotiation; and whether the system must preserve an externally auditable trace of its reasoning or may rely on opaque, learned representations. At each leaf, provide either the name of an established position in the literature or the label unnamed, together with a one-sentence statement of that combination’s most awkward practical commitment. Where your branching criteria fail to separate two named positions, flag the ambiguity explicitly. Do not invent distinctions that do not exist, and do not collapse genuinely different commitments into a single branch; treat unoccupied but logically coherent combinations as unnamed leaves with their commitments made plain.
