I'm trying to understand the relationship between formal limitation results and the possibility of genuinely open-ended reasoning, particularly in artificial systems. In Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, the MU-puzzle demonstrates that a formal system can fail to derive a result that is obvious from outside, and the proposed resolution involves stepping outside the system entirely—JOOTSing—where human insight operates at a meta-level that the original rules do not govern. This makes me think that formal incompleteness is not a deep boundary but a local one: if one can always ascend to a richer framework, then any given Gödel sentence is only unprovable relative to a particular axiomatization, not absolutely.

But here is where I become confused. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem states that a consistent formal system strong enough to encode arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. If JOOTSing is essentially the capacity to transcend any fixed formal framework—say, by allowing a system to revise its own axioms or reason in a meta-language—then wouldn't a self-modifying agent that learns to step outside its initial formalism eventually be able to prove its own consistency and thereby defeat the theorem? Specifically, if such an agent begins with system S, recognizes that S cannot prove Con(S), and then shifts to S' with expanded axioms that do prove Con(S), hasn't it escaped the obstacle? Or does the theorem apply recursively, so that S' is now blocked from proving Con(S')? I am asking because this sounds like a direct design principle for AI safety: build agents that do not remain trapped in one formalism but continuously ascend to higher-level frameworks. Yet I worry I am conflating Hofstadter's metaphor of creative human insight with an actual mechanical procedure. Is there a precise sense in which stepping outside avoids being merely the addition of new axioms, and if so, why could an automated meta-reasoner not perform it?
