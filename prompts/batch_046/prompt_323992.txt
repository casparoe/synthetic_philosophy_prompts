Context: I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at the dedication ceremony for a new museum gallery titled After the End? Societies in Transition. The audience is deliberately split. One half consists of archaeologists, anthropologists, and curators who know that the collapse framing for these three cases has been heavily contested—particularly for Rapa Nui, where the popular ecocide narrative has been largely displaced by models emphasizing demographic adaptation, internal mobility, and later colonial disruption rather than self-inflicted environmental ruin. The other half consists of museum patrons, local educators, city officials, and students who primarily know Easter Island as a parable of ecological self-destruction and the Maya as a civilization that simply vanished.

The philosophical concept must do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decoration. Because the very idea of collapse presupposes a teleological baseline—some correct or expected form of societal continuity against which deviation counts as failure—the speaker must be unable to assert, without deep qualification, that these societies objectively collapsed. At the same time, the address cannot dismiss the real suffering, depopulation, loss of literacy, and cessation of monument-building that occurred; doing so would alienate the experts and patronize the lay audience. The speech needs to navigate Joseph Tainter’s concept of rapid decomplexification—loss of administrative integration, long-distance exchange, and specialized production—while insisting that such decomplexification is not automatically a failure, and that labeling it collapse risks projecting modern ecological anxieties onto past societies that may not have shared our institutional commitments.

Please write a ten-minute opening address suitable for this occasion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; assume the speaker has no web access and the audience does not need scholarly apparatus. Do not invoke obscure scholars by name, though you may reference well-known conceptual distinctions in passing. Inject one niche conceptual distinction—perhaps between collapse as a systems-level loss of sociopolitical complexity and collapse as a narrative projection of contemporary fears about limits—to prevent this from being a generic request about societal failure.
