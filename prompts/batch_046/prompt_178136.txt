Please compose a short story, in American English, narrated in the first person by a senior curator at the Institute for Uncompleted Intentions, an archival institution that preserves not finished works but deliberately unfinished ones—symphonies missing their final movements, scientific protocols awaiting replication by generations not yet born, and legal frameworks designed to be amended only by descendants. Your protagonist oversees a particular symphonic work composed specifically to be completed by listeners two centuries hence through a distributed interpretive process. As gradual demographic collapse makes it evident that no successors will exist, the curator must confront the possibility that the symphony's present fragments are not merely delayed but permanently orphaned. Use this scenario to examine the philosophical claim—associated with Samuel Scheffler's conjecture regarding death and the collective afterlife—that human extinction is harmful not primarily through the deaths it involves but through the severing of intergenerational projects that lend present lives their significance. Avoid conventional post-apocalyptic tropes; instead, focus on the quiet erosion of meaning as the curator attempts to maintain the archive despite recognizing that without future agents to inherit and complete the work, its value collapses into mere artifact. The narrative should implicitly pose the question of whether present obligations to preserve such projects depend on a guaranteed future existence, and whether the wrongness of extinction consists in a form of metaphysical orphaning rather than in mortality itself.
