A colleague of mine attempted to summarise the teleosemantic — specifically biosemantic — account of mental content, and I should like to know whether her characterisation holds up to scrutiny. She said that, on this view, a mental representation derives its intentional content wholly from the evolutionary proper functions of the mechanisms that produce and consume it: the producer mechanism is selected for indicating a particular environmental condition (for instance, predator presence), whilst the consumer mechanism is selected for deploying that representation to guide behaviour appropriate to that condition. Because content is fixed by these ancestral selection conditions rather than by present causal correlations, the account is said to dissolve the disjunction problem — should a predator-detecting mechanism be triggered by a shadow, it still represents a predator rather than a shadow, since its content was established in the ancestral environment of evolutionary adaptation, not in the present one. She went on to claim that misrepresentation is therefore nothing more than mechanical malfunction: either the producer or the consumer fails to perform its proper function, which means there is no independent normative or rational dimension to representational error. Finally, she argued that, since evolutionary history is indispensable, the view necessarily denies mental content to any system lacking a selection history, including not only artificial intelligences constructed ab initio but also fully functional laboratory-engineered organisms that possess intact cognitive architectures. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what is accurate, what amounts to a standard simplification, and what is subtly mistaken — paying particular attention to the distinction between abnormal conditions and outright malfunction, the possibility of design-based rather than selection-based proper functions, and whether teleosemantics genuinely eliminates all normative evaluation of belief.
