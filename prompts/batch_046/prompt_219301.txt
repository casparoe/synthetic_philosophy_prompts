I've been developing a framework for comparing infinitely large moral populations that I'm calling local consistency evaluation. The central claim is that when a choice affects an asymptotically infinite number of moral patients—say, a policy that determines the long-run density of conscious lives—we should reject both totalist and average-utilitarian aggregation. Instead, we should apply a radial comparison rule: world A is strictly better than world B only if there exists a finite radius R such that, for every larger radius, the average well-being within that boundary is higher in A, and this ordering never reverses beyond that point. The idea is to block the familiar arbitrariness that plagues totalism, where a distant cluster of mildly happy lives can flip the evaluation of a nearby catastrophe, without falling into the indifference traps that make pure averaging over infinite sets useless. A further consequence is that longtermist comparisons might be rendered tractable without invoking temporal discounting, since the asymptotic density is meant to encode the morally relevant long-run structure directly.

I'm treating this as a substantive candidate rather than a formal curiosity, so I want a map of its actual exposure. What formal results—perhaps about metric-dependence, about the impossibility of a consistent radial ordering across different spatial or temporal coordinate systems, or about cases where well-being oscillates at every scale—would be fatal? Which intuitions, such as the idea that a localized reversal in welfare should sometimes matter morally, or that distance in space or time should diminish relevance, would defenders of the view have to concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they absorb by refining the radius condition or the metric? I also want an honest assessment of insulation: have I qualified the principle with "sufficiently large radius" and "no reversals" in a way that renders it unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a serious defect for a principle meant to guide real policy choices about longterm risk and population ethics? Please rank the potential defeaters from those that would force abandonment of the framework down to those that are merely awkward adjustments, and flag any that rest on hidden empirical assumptions about how value is distributed across infinite domains.
