I was reading some thread about prison reform—probably on one of those rationalist-adjacent forums where people argue about institutional design—and I got stuck on this Scandinavian open-prison model. Inmates basically have keys and work in town, but if they break a rule like comeing back late from a work furlough, there's this "accountability circle" instead of automatic punishment. Staff and other residents ask whether you meant to violate the agreement, whether you could have foreseen the risk, and then they decide collectively if you're still the kind of person they can hold responsible in the full reactive sense—whether resentment or indignation is actually fitting—or if you're just a system failure that needs stricter containment. The stated goal is rehab, but the ritual feels like it's smuggling in a whole theory of moral responsability.

What philosophical commitments does that practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if you accept Strawson's idea that responsibility is fundamentally about being an appropriate target of reactive attitudes, rather than about possessing some metaphysical free will? Could a hard determinist who rejects reactive attitudes altogether still justify running the circle, or does the whole thing collapse without the distinction between resentment-meriting failures and mere disappointment? There's also this weird tension with negligence law: in tort cases we blame people for outcomes they never intended if they should have foreseen them, but the circle draws the line at intentional violation versus unavoidable slip. So is the prison implicitly committed to an intent-based view that's stricter than the legal system embedding it?

I'm also curious how philosophically underdetermined this is. Could you run the exact same circle and justify it with pure consequentialist risk-assessment—asking about intent only because it's the best predictor of future violations—without buying any Strawsonian metaphysics? Or does involving other inmates in judging whether resentment is "fitting" show that the practice genuinely presupposes reactive attitudes are collectively calibratable and not just private feelings? And if we replaced the underlying theory with one that tracks only cognitive and volitional competence, regardless of anyone's actual emotional response, which parts of the circle would have to be redesigned, and which could stay exactly the same?
