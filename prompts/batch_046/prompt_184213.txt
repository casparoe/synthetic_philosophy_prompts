I am a novelist invited to deliver the opening remarks at a weekend workshop titled "The Unfinished: Actual and Potential Infinity in Mathematics and Narrative," attended by working mathematicians, fiction writers, and graduate students. I want the conceptual tension between actual and potential infinity—specifically Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's result that infinite collections need not share cardinality, and the finitist refusal to treat infinity as a completed object—to do structural work in the address, not decorative work. The speech should argue that fiction naturally operates in the mode of potential infinity (the narrative always could extend), and therefore I should resist offering the audience any comforting metaphysical claim about an "actual infinity of meaning" embedded in the text, since that would treat an open process as if it were a fully present totality. At the same time, the remarks must handle the mathematics with care: Hilbert's hotel demonstrates that an infinite set can correspond one-to-one with a proper subset, Cantor's diagonal argument establishes that the continuum exceeds the integers in cardinality, and finitism denies that either describes a completed reality rather than an unending procedure. The audience is mixed; the mathematicians should recognize precise handling, while the writers should leave with a concrete sense of how this distinction constrains what a novel can honestly claim about its own completeness. Please write a polished, formal address of roughly four minutes, without bibliographies, citations, or verbatim quotations from philosophers, and without presenting me as a mathematical authority. The philosophical framing should determine what I can and cannot say—for example, I may acknowledge that a story feels endless, but I must not speak as though that endlessness were an actual object I have fully presented.
