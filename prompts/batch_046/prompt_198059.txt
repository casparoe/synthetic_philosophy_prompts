I spent much of last evening in a rather heated discussion with a friend who kept insisting that logical induction is essentially just Bayesian updating with a computational budget tacked on, and I realized that I could not clearly distinguish the two without relying on hand-waving about polynomial-time traders. I am now preparing the final examination for my upper-level graduate seminar on logical uncertainty and want an essay question that will expose whether a student genuinely grasps the induction criterion or is merely repeating its standard gloss. Please construct a single essay prompt suitable for a ninety-minute examination carrying twenty-five marks, intended for students who have studied the basic framework but may still conflate logical induction with a naive relaxation of logical omniscience. The question should present a concrete scenario: a reasoner maintains a logical induction market over sentences in a formal language that include an arithmetical conjecture C and a strictly weaker statement W that logically implies C in one direction but not conversely; initially neither is provable, and the market assigns intermediate credences to both. Then the reasoner learns that an automated proof system has derived the negation of W. The student must explain why the prior on C need not have been extremal, how the logical dependency between W and C constrains the update on C without forcing it immediately to zero or one, and precisely which feature of the induction criterion blocks a Dutch-book-style trader from exploiting these intermediate values for unbounded profit. The student should also confront the objection that logical induction merely pushes logical omniscience back into the pricing algorithm itself, and defend or reject that claim with specific reference to the resource-bounded nature of the traders. Alongside the question, provide a model answer of approximately seven hundred to eight hundred words in continuous prose, and a marking scheme that allocates the twenty-five marks across five essential conceptual moves, specifying for each the point value, the correct execution in one sentence, and the typical mistake that should cost marks. Please write your entire response as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in American English, as one might write a formal letter. The prompt should not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to obscure results outside standard coursework on this topic.
