As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been working on evaluation frameworks for large language models and have become stuck on a conceptual problem that sits between philosophy of mind and engineering practice. In debugging and safety evaluation, my colleagues and I routinely say things like the model believes Paris is the capital of France or it holds a false belief about chemistry, yet we also treat the system as a statistical pattern-matcher with no internal mental states. This seems to invoke something like Dennett’s intentional stance, but applied to artifacts whose architectures we can inspect down to the parameter level.

Within this space, I see several genuinely open lines of work that are not merely unsettled in textbooks:

First, the interpretability-dependence question: if mechanistic interpretability eventually reveals a specific, localized circuit that consistently activates for factual retrieval, does that architectural finding justify attributing a belief, or does belief-attribution still require some global integrative property that these architectures lack?

Second, the accountability-independence question: whether regulatory or liability frameworks for autonomous systems actually need mental-state attributions to assign responsibility, or whether they can operate entirely through behavioral risk classifications and failure-mode taxonomies without ever invoking belief or desire.

Third, the heuristic-value question: whether treating the model as having beliefs improves debugging and red-teaming outcomes in practice, or whether it systematically misleads engineers into anthropomorphizing failure modes that are better explained at the level of training-data statistics and optimization dynamics, along the lines of the stochastic-parrot critique.

Fourth, the dependency structure among these: does resolving the interpretability question settle the accountability and heuristic questions, or can the latter be addressed independently with purely practical criteria?

Given that I have limited time and no philosophy background, I want to know which of these open questions or implications actually deserve priority if one’s goal is to improve how engineering teams reason about system behavior and assign responsibility. Which line of work is most upstream—meaning its resolution would change how we approach the others? Which is most tractable for someone working with current interpretability tools rather than speculative future architectures? And which, if resolved negatively, would least damage current safety practices?

Please defend your ordering explicitly: state the criteria you are using (for example, tractability for an engineering audience, dependency on other answers, or practical impact on evaluation design), note the trade-offs between them, and indicate what kind of result—conceptual or empirical—would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want a defended agenda for where conceptual effort should go.
