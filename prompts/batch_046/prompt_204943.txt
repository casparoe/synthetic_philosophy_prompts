I serve as chair of my university’s interdisciplinary ethics and curriculum board, and the issue before us has become personally urgent: two weeks ago my partner and I finalized the adoption of our daughter from a different cultural and religious background, and in trying to articulate to her—honestly—why I hold certain convictions about familial obligation, I realized that what I had always called “my values” dissolved into explanations that sounded like inherited conditioning. That crisis of self-knowledge makes me unable to treat lightly the proposal now awaiting our decision by Thursday.

The investigators seek approval for a multi-campus study employing what they call an “autobiographical counterfactual debiasing” protocol. Participants first record moral judgments on contested questions—distributive obligations, duties to strangers, limits of forgiveness—then are presented with richly detailed, fabricated but plausible alternative biographies in which they were raised in radically different socioeconomic or cultural conditions, and are asked to revise their judgments in light of these counterfactual lives. The researchers classify any divergence between initial and revised responses as proof that the initial judgment was a “bias” rather than an “authentic preference,” and they plan to publish aggregate “purified preference profiles” that exclude the pre-reflection data. Half the board regards this as an unproblematic extension of reflective-equilibrium methodology and supports approval with standard debriefing. The other half—including me—contends that the study presupposes the very philosophical distinction it claims to investigate, risks coercing participants into disavowing their own convictions as errors without providing an independent criterion of authenticity, and fails the transparency requirement of informed consent by measuring moral psychology under the guise of “reading comprehension and narrative empathy.”

Please draft the board’s formal written opinion for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend a concrete course—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with an explicit time frame for any required revisions, suspension, or implementation. The reasoning should engage seriously the investigators’ claim that genuine moral self-knowledge sometimes requires external correction through counterfactual perspective-taking, but it must also address with equal honesty the strongest objection from the opposing side: that without a non-circular standard for distinguishing authentic preference from mere cultural residue, labeling pre-reflection judgments as biases collapses the study’s central conceptual framework into question-begging. Write in formal institutional prose appropriate to members from philosophy, psychology, and law; do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and ensure the memo could stand on its own reasoning without reference to sources outside general disciplinary knowledge.
