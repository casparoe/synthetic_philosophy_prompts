Design a 90-minute classroom simulation for a graduate seminar of 14 students in moral philosophy. The goal is to make participants feel, through structured action rather than lecture, the conceptual tension between doing harm and allowing harm—specifically, the asymmetry in moral resistance between actively withdrawing a life-sustaining resource and failing to initiate it, when the net causal and outcome profile is held constant. Call the activity The Pipeline Default. Divide the group into rotating triads (Actor, Observer, Chronicler). Provide each triad with an identical physical mechanism: a two-sided card representing a resource pipeline that is either Active—flowing to Patient A or Inactive—available for Patient B. In each round, the Actor must either leave the card untouched or physically flip it. The rules must be arranged so that flipping is always the same bodily motion, but in some rounds it initiates flow to B (previously inactive), while in other rounds it withdraws flow from A (previously active) to redirect to B. Use 12 rounds, arranged in four blocks of three: Block 1 initiates flow to B; Block 2 initiates flow to A; Block 3 withdraws flow from A to redirect to B; Block 4 withholds from B by leaving flow to A untouched, allowing B to die. Introduce small, non-monetary stakes: each triad earns deliberation tokens based on the consistency and speed of their justifications, with the collective pool determining a minor group choice at the end (for example, selecting the seminar’s closing reading). The Observer rates the Actor’s visible hesitation on a 1–5 scale; the Chronicler writes a one-sentence justification without consulting formal philosophical vocabulary. Crucially, participants should not be told that Block 1 and Block 3 are structurally paired, or that Block 2 and Block 4 are paired, until the debrief. Include in your response: (a) the complete rules, materials list, and minute-by-minute timeline; (b) the incentive scheme and how it avoids real money while maintaining genuine engagement; (c) a debrief protocol that maps each block onto the conceptual distinction between acts and omissions, predicts the specific failure mode where students will rate withdrawal rounds as more emotionally costly despite identical outcomes, and gives the facilitator language to handle that failure without dismissing the intuition; (d) an explicit, honest statement of which feature of the killing/letting-die debate—such as the relevance of prior commitments, agent-relative reasons, or the distinction between direct and indirect causation—this physical simulation cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. The design should require no web access, no citations, no bibliographies, and no reference to niche literature; it should rely only on broadly available conceptual resources in philosophy of action and bioethics.
