Epicurus's argument that death is not harmful to the one who dies appears to rest on a contemporaneity condition: harm requires a subject who exists at the same time as the harmful state, and since the dead do not exist, there is no one to bear the harm. If, however, one adopts a four-dimensionalist metaphysics in which a person is a temporal worm composed of distinct stage-parts, then the antemortem stage exists precisely when death eliminates all subsequent stages. On that picture, the absence of those later stages looks like a welfare loss to the earlier stage—analogous, perhaps, to the loss of a limb being bad for the stage that possessed it—without requiring any post-mortem subject to experience the deprivation. Does this mean the Epicurean argument succeeds only against an endurantist conception of personal identity, and collapses once we treat persons as stage-sequences? Or can the Epicurean consistently deny that missing future stages harms the prior stage without simply presupposing that harm must be experienced, which is exactly the premise the deprivation theorist rejects? And if the stage-theoretic framing is available, does it resolve the asymmetry problem—why we do not mourn our prenatal non-existence—by showing that pre-birth absence involves no prior stage to be deprived, or does it merely relocate the same puzzle inside the metaphysics of persistence?
