A recent essay on evolutionary metaethics summarizes Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks as follows. Cranes are cumulative, bottom-up explanatory mechanisms—like natural selection—that construct complex phenomena without invoking unexplained designers. Skyhooks, by contrast, are explanatory devices suspended from nowhere, invoking an irreducible authority to do the work. The essay then introduces Dennett's notion of "greedy reductionism" not as a synonym for crane-based explanation, but as a specific failure mode: a reduction that dissolves the very phenomenon it seeks to explain, leaving behind only adaptive utility where there once was normative force. Applying this to morality, the summary claims that Dennett attempts to build deontic reasoning—genuine obligations, not mere preferences—through evolutionary cranes, yet ultimately concedes that such cranes must hit a "normative ceiling." Because obligations lack physical properties, the argument runs, any purely bottom-up story either collapses into greedy reduction (turning ought into evolved is) or must smuggle in a skyhook—an irreducible normative framework—to preserve the authority of moral demands. The essay therefore concludes that Dennett's naturalism is unstable: he rejects skyhooks in principle but implicitly relies on one to save morality from greedy dissolution. It then proposes that recent work in "scaffolded deontology" resolves the tension by showing how recursive social practices can generate "normative inertia"—stable deontic constraints that emerge from iterated scaffolding without either mysterious hanging or eliminative reduction—rendering Dennett's dilemma obsolete.
