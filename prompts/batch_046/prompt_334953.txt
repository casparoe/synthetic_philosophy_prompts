Design a take-home essay exam for an upper-level undergraduate or graduate seminar on cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions. The exam should consist of a single prompt requiring approximately 1,500 words, completed within 48 hours, and weighted at 40 percent of the final grade. The prompt should present a hypothetical but methodologically detailed cross-cultural study investigating attributions of blame for negligent versus unavoidable harm, conducted with participants from Japan, the United States, and Germany. In the study, participants rate both direct moral blame and a culturally specific secondary assessment—described as community burden or harmony disruption—directed at an agent whose actions produce fatal versus harmless outcomes despite identical mental states. The hypothetical results show that direct moral blame tracks the agent's control across all three groups (high for negligence, low for an unavoidable medical episode), but Japanese participants assign high levels of community burden specifically to the agent in the unavoidable-fatal condition, mediated not by perceived culpability but by a sense that the fatal outcome disturbs collective harmony regardless of fault.

The exam question must require students to do four things. First, interpret these hypothetical findings with respect to the universality of the control principle in debates about moral luck. Second, distinguish direct moral blame from the Japanese community-burden response and argue whether the latter represents a form of outcome-dependent moral evaluation or instead reflects a distinct normative framework such as relational or collective ethics. Third, identify a specific linguistic or conceptual confound in the study design—particularly regarding the translation and framing of evaluative terms across languages—and explain precisely how that confound threatens the inference that the observed variation is philosophical rather than semantic. Fourth, propose one concrete methodological revision, such as a behavioral compensation task, rigorous back-translation with cognitive debriefing, or non-linguistic scenario presentation, that would help isolate genuine conceptual variation from framing effects.

Provide a model answer that specifies the moves a top response must make: an accurate articulation of the control principle and its role in moral luck discussions; a nuanced analysis of the Japanese data as reflecting harmony-based or collective-responsibility norms rather than individual outcome-sensitive blame; a precise identification of the translation confound and its philosophical significance; and a well-specified methodological improvement that directly addresses framing rather than merely increasing sample size. Also provide a marking scheme that allocates marks for philosophical accuracy, critical separation of blame types, identification of the confound, quality of the proposed revision, and clarity of argument, and that lists specific mistakes that should cost marks—such as treating all outcome-sensitivity as moral luck, ignoring the linguistic framing issue, or proposing a revision that fails to distinguish conceptual content from semantic packaging. The difficulty should come from forcing students to synthesize philosophical analysis with methodological critique; the question should not be answerable by summarizing standard textbook overviews of cross-cultural moral psychology. Do not ask students to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
