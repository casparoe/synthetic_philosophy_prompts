I'm constructing a classification instrument for an advanced aesthetics seminar on whether immoral art can be aesthetically excellent, and on whether knowledge of an artist's moral conduct can alter the evaluation of the work itself. Rather than a catalog of named philosophers, I want a decision tree built from logical combinations of answers: each branch node should pose one concrete claim or case on which positions genuinely diverge, and each leaf should identify the resulting position—using a standard label if one exists, or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied—together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment.

Begin with a root node that asks whether there is any principled separation between moral evaluation of the artwork and aesthetic evaluation of it. From there, branch as follows.

First branch: Is the immorality in question representational—the work depicts, endorses, or structurally requires an immoral perspective—or biographical—grounded in documented conduct of the artist outside the work?

If representational, proceed to a second node: Can an immoral perspective within the work yield a distinctive cognitive benefit—sometimes discussed as moral knowledge acquired through immersion in an immoral viewpoint—that transforms the moral defect into an aesthetic or epistemic virtue?

If biographical, proceed instead to a node on whether knowledge of the artist's life can contaminate aesthetic experience through what we might call aesthetic contamination, or whether competent viewers can always bracket biographical information.

Then include a stress-test case used consistently across all paths: a technically masterful narrative film whose formal elegance is inseparable from its depiction of cruelty, directed by an artist subsequently convicted of grave moral crimes, where critics disagree about whether the violence is thematically necessary. For each leaf, specify how that position evaluates both the representational and biographical dimensions of this case.

Every leaf should carry a label—such as strict autonomism, moderate moralism, cognitive immoralism, biographical contamination, no-separation, or unnamed—and one sentence stating the consequence or case the position must accept that its rivals avoid. Note any pair of leaves that your tree cannot really distinguish, explaining whether the gap is merely rhetorical or substantive.

Finally, identify any unoccupied combinations in the logical space and diagnose briefly why they are empty: incoherence, unattractiveness, or neglect. The audience is graduate students in philosophy of art; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the tree compact enough that a student could trace their own answers without prior knowledge of the taxonomy's labels.
