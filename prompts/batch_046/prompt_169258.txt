I'm currently drafting a brief for our city council on reforming marriage licensing criteria, and I'm also in the middle of planning my own wedding for next spring, so these questions have moved well beyond the theoretical for me. Before I present any recommendations, I want to make sure my own commitments actually cohere. Here is where I've landed: (1) marriage should be understood not as a state endorsement of romantic love or as a procreative institution, but as a legal technology for stabilizing long-term economic interdependence and registered caregiving obligations; (2) same-sex couples should have full access to civil marriage on exactly the same terms as different-sex couples, since the dependency structures they form are functionally identical; (3) the state should issue marriage licenses only when there is an explicit, registered dependency or caregiving commitment, because there is no legitimate public interest in certifying romantic attachments as such; (4) marriage should remain restricted to dyads—not on essentialist grounds, since I deny that marriage has any universal metaphysical nature, but because multi-party dependency contracts become administratively intractable and because the symbolic and normative weight of marriage in our culture depends on continuity with its existing form; (5) siblings or close friends who are genuinely co-raising a dependent together should not be granted marriage licenses, even though they satisfy the dependency criterion, because marriage requires a distinctive kind of intimate partnership that the state can recognize without attempting to define it precisely; and (6) my partner and I intend to marry next year despite having no dependents and no intention of having children, and I regard our union as fully legitimate and worthy of state recognition.

Do these hang together? I suspect there may be tension between the functionalist licensing criterion in (3) and both the equality claim in (2) and my personal case in (6), and I worry that (4) and (5) might be importing assumptions that (1) does not license, but I can't tell whether these are genuine contradictions or only apparent ones. Please audit the set: identify which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like conflicts but dissolve on closer inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each specify which commitment would have to be revised or dropped to restore consistency and what that revision costs. I have not decided which view I would abandon if forced, so treat none of them as fixed.
