I am preparing an argument for a philosophy-of-technology seminar that examines whether our ordinary concepts of honesty and deception can be meaningfully applied to language-generating AI without either collapsing into mere error-correction or smuggling in full human intentionality. The standard discussions tend to split between behaviorist accounts, which treat deception as any systematically misleading output regardless of internal structure, and mentalistic accounts, which require something like belief or intent that current architectures plausibly lack. Rather than rehearse that familiar divide, I want the essay to focus on a more granular conceptual choice that I think cuts across it and has direct implications for how we audit and regulate these systems.

The controversy is between two frameworks I will call representational betrayal and pragmatic misfire. On the representational-betrayal view, an AI system deceives when its internal representations—whether activation patterns, planning traces, or self-monitoring states—are calibrated to diverge systematically from the content it expresses, in a manner that exploits the user's epistemic trust. This captures the intuition that deception is not merely being wrong but being wrong in a directed, trust-violating way, and it allows that some architectures with layered self-modeling might genuinely deceive even without consciousness. On the pragmatic-misfire view, by contrast, deception should be defined purely in terms of the functional relation between output and user context: a system deceives when its generated tokens, taken in the interactional setting, reliably induce false beliefs in a reasonable interlocutor, with no requirement that any internal state mirror that divergence. This avoids anthropomorphism but risks conflating malicious deception with benign ambiguity, statistical noise, or user misinterpretation.

Write an essay that weighs these two frameworks and reaches a clear, justified conclusion about which one better captures what we should care about when evaluating deception in contemporary large language models. You should defend your verdict by engaging with at least one serious objection—for instance, that representational betrayal over-intellectualizes architectures that lack unified selves, or that pragmatic misfire cannot distinguish deceptive design from innocent error. Because this is meant to be evaluated without access to specialized recent literature, ground your reasoning in conceptual analysis and concrete illustrative scenarios rather than appeals to named studies or bibliographies. Do not end on a non-committal note; take a position and show why the alternative, even if partially correct, should be subordinate in our normative and practical assessment of AI honesty.
