As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I'm trying to audit my own views on artificial reasoning for a technical design document. My current commitments are these: (1) I endorse Darwin's inversion—adaptive design-level competence can arise from selection mechanisms that possess no comprehension of the problem being solved—so I treat large language models as achieving linguistic performance through parameter optimization rather than semantic understanding. (2) I endorse Turing's parallel inversion: sustained intelligent behavior is sufficient for attributing functional competence, even when the system lacks any introspective grasp of its own reasoning. (3) Nevertheless, I hold that any automated reasoning system deployed in safety-critical software must satisfy what I call an explanatory-access condition—it must supply traceable justifications for its outputs—which I regard as a minimal form of comprehension. (4) I apply this condition selectively: I require it for neural reasoning tools, but I freely deploy evolutionary algorithms such as genetic programming, treating them as unproblematic cases of pure competence without comprehension. (5) I take the competence-comprehension distinction to be ontological rather than epistemic—systems either literally understand or they do not, and this is not merely a matter of our observational access.

Do these five commitments form a coherent package? I suspect there is a non-obvious tension between (1)-(2) and (3), and possibly a deeper conflict between (4) and (5). At the same time, I worry that what looks like a clash between (2) and (3) might dissolve once the contexts are separated. Could you rank the actual tensions by severity, identify which pairs only appear incompatible, specify the least costly revision for each genuine conflict, and indicate which single change would do the most to restore consistency without forcing me to abandon the claim that LLMs lack genuine comprehension?
