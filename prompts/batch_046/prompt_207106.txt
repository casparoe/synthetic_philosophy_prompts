I'm a novelist working on a near-future story where a mid-sized city replaces its council with a sorted citizens' assembly, and I've been reading up on lottocracy to make the politics feel real. I keep running into the same weird pattern in the debates and I'm not sure philosophers already have a name for it.

Here's the thing. Defenders of random selection for legislatures say regular people are smart enough for value-laden policy, and that elections mostly filter for charisma and money. Fair enough. But then when someone asks why we shouldn't also sort people to run the central bank or sit on appellate courts, the same defenders suddenly invoke "technical competence" or "specialized judgment" as a hard boundary. Meanwhile critics who hate sortition for congress will happily defend jury duty with random citizens, yet draw the line exactly there. What's strange is that nobody can tell me where the line actually lives. One writer says climate targets are perfect for sorted citizens because they're moral choices, but tax rates are too technical; another flips that exactly, saying climate science is too technical but tax policy is just values. It feels like both sides have this movable fence between "democratic/random okay" and "needs selection/expertise," sliding it case by case to protect institutions they like, without anyone admitting the criterion is post-hoc. There's a specifc structure here—a distinction that sounds principled (values vs. competence) deployed as an institutional bodyguard—but I don't know if it's just ordinary inconsistency.

So: is there an established concept for this exact move in democratic theory? I don't mean plain hypocrisy or motte-and-bailey, since those miss the institutional specificity—this is about the boundary of where sortition applies, not a single claim being defended. If there's a term, how does it differ from just saying people are inconsistent about expertise? If not, could you coin a sharp definition with boundary cases? And please separate what's settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested—don't present one camp's movable fence as the consensus view.
