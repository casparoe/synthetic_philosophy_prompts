If disjunctivism about perceptual experience is correct, then a perfect hallucination of a red apple and a veridical seeing of a red apple share no mental common factor: they are not two tokens of a single mental type, but fundamentally different kinds of episode. That seems to entail that in the hallucinatory case there is literally no occurrence of the phenomenal quality of seeing-red—since if there were, it would be precisely the shared factor that the argument from illusion exploits. Yet disjunctivists also insist that hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perception. How can there be subjective indistinguishability if the hallucinator entirely lacks the phenomenal quality present in the perceiver? Must the disjunctivist therefore treat hallucination as a non-experiential state—say, an unconscious inference or a belief—rather than as an experience? Or, if they grant that hallucination involves some phenomenal quality after all, have they not conceded the very common-factor premise that makes the argument from illusion go through?
