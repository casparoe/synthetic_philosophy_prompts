Exam Question:

In Book III, Part I, Section I of A Treatise of Human Nature, Hume observes that authors frequently move from statements about what is the case to prescriptions about what ought to be done without explaining the transition. This observation is often taken to establish an "is-ought gap." Some interpreters advance what we might call the Sentimentalist Derivation: they claim that the gap not only undermines rationalist moral theories but positively supports Hume's sentimentalism, on the grounds that if normative conclusions cannot be derived from purely descriptive premises, then moral judgments must originate in a non-cognitive faculty—sentiment—rather than in reason. At the same time, a "reverse-gap" objection has been raised: Hume himself derives the normative authority of moral distinctions from descriptive psychological facts about universal sympathy and the mechanism of approval, apparently committing the very fallacy—later formalized by G.E. Moore as the naturalistic fallacy—that his is-ought observation is meant to block.

Write an essay evaluating both the Sentimentalist Derivation and the reverse-gap objection. Specifically:
(i) Does Hume's logical point about entailment genuinely support his sentimentalist ontology of moral judgments?
(ii) Can Hume consistently appeal to descriptive claims about human sympathy to ground the normative authority of moral distinctions without falling into the naturalistic fallacy?
(iii) How does Hume's position differ from Moore's open-question argument, and does that difference affect the force of the reverse-gap charge?

Student Answer:

Hume's is-ought gap demonstrates that reason by itself is inert with respect to action and can only compare ideas or discover causal relations. Since neither of these activities produces normative force, it follows that moral judgments are not beliefs that can be true or false; rather, they are expressions of feeling—specifically, sentiments of approval and disapproval. In this respect Hume anticipates G.E. Moore's open-question argument. Just as Moore shows that "good" is not analyzable into any natural property such as "pleasant" or "useful," Hume shows that "ought" cannot be analyzed into any "is." Both thinkers therefore defend a form of non-naturalism about value: moral properties are sui generis and irreducible.

Against the reverse-gap objection, Hume's appeal to sympathy is not a derivation of normative authority from bare descriptive psychology. Sympathy is a universal mechanism that, when activated, produces approval automatically. The approval is not a separate normative judgment added onto the descriptive fact of sympathy; it is identical with the sentiment itself. Consequently, Hume is not inferring "we ought to approve of X" from "humans symmetrically feel sympathy for X." He is only explaining the causal origin of our moral responses. The normative authority resides in the internal structure of the sentiment, not in the descriptive premise.

Finally, the reverse-gap charge confuses explanatory and justificatory reasoning. Hume describes how moral distinctions arise in human nature, but he never attempts to justify morality by appealing to those descriptions. Because the is-ought gap concerns logical entailment rather than causal explanation, there is no contradiction in using descriptive psychology to account for normative phenomena without deriving prescriptions from them.
