I am preparing material for a seminar on philosophy of mind, and I find myself returning to Putnam's Twin Earth argument for semantic externalism, but with a twist that the standard literature rarely addresses in detail. The familiar version asks us to imagine Oscar on Earth, where the term water denotes H₂O, and his molecular duplicate Twin Oscar on Twin Earth, where the same term denotes XYZ. Because their internal physical states are identical, yet the extensions of their terms differ, we are meant to conclude that mental content is partly determined by the external environment rather than being fully located in the head.

What I would like you to explain is a conceptual tension that arises when we modify the social dimension of reference-fixing. Suppose that, contrary to the usual isolation assumption, Earth and Twin Earth are not causally separated: experts in chemistry on both planets maintain a continuous collaborative network, so that the division of linguistic labour that Putnam describes now spans both worlds. Oscar and Twin Oscar both sincerely report that they are thinking about water, and both are disposed to defer to the expert community—except that this community is now a single, distributed institution with members on both planets, some of whom study H₂O and others XYZ. The question is not merely empirical but conceptual: does this trans-world social anchoring collapse the distinction between Oscar's and Twin Oscar's mental contents, or does it instead reveal that externalism must be refined to include not just the local environment but the precise causal pathway through which a speaker is linked to one chemical kind rather than another?

In particular, I want a clear explanation of whether this scenario undermines the intuition that the two Oscars share identical narrow psychological states, and whether preserving that intuition requires us to draw a sharper boundary between narrow content, broad content, and what we might call socially distributed reference. Please avoid giving a bibliography or citing specific page numbers; I am looking for a conceptual clarification that could be followed without consulting external texts.
