I am designing an examination for a graduate seminar in social ontology, and I want the deliverable to read like a manual for a non-philosophical practitioner. Your task is to turn David Lewis's theory of convention—specifically the interlocking conditions of behavioral regularity, common knowledge of that regularity, conditional preference for conformity, and the presence of an arbitrary but available alternative—into a reusable decision procedure that a practitioner could apply repeatedly in under thirty minutes per case without philosophical supervision.

Fix the practitioner's role: a municipal heritage-preservation officer who must determine whether an informal community practice qualifies as a protected "living convention" under regional statute, or whether proposed formalization will preserve, destroy, or replace the coordination the practice achieves. The stakes are moderate but binding: misclassification triggers either unwanted deregulation or bureaucratic overreach that collapses the practice.

Your deliverable must be an ordered checklist—a flowchart in prose—with explicit decision nodes. At each node, specify what evidence the officer must gather, what yes/no question they must answer, and which branch follows. The procedure must handle three structurally hard cases that Lewis's framework renders genuinely ambiguous but does not automatically resolve: first, a negative convention, in which the behavioral regularity is an omission (a systematic silence or refusal) rather than a positive action, and observers disagree whether absence counts as behavior for the purposes of regularity; second, a nested convention, in which one coordination regularity depends for its stability on a second regularity that is not itself an object of common knowledge; and third, an apparent convention sustained not by mutual preference for conformity but by an asymmetric power structure that mimics the behavioral profile of convention.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases. Case One should be an unambiguous positive: the procedure correctly identifies a living convention and recommends preservation without formalization. Case Two should be the result the officer will resist: a beloved local practice that the procedure classifies as not a true convention but a coerced regularity, with the further implication that formalizing it would not destroy genuine coordination because none existed—state exactly which feature of the case drives this unwelcome verdict. Case Three should be one of the three ambiguous hard cases; show precisely where the checklist runs out and human judgment must take over, and flag the specific Lewisian condition that fails to adjudicate.

Conclude by stating two limitations: one internal to Lewis's theory (a distinction or verdict the four conditions genuinely do not settle) and one practical (a type of evidence the officer is structurally unlikely to possess). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, citation suggestions, or requests for further sources. Write in formal, polished American English.
