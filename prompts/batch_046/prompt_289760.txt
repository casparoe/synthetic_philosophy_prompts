In discussions of algorithmic fairness, Kleinberg's impossibility result is often likened to Arrow's social-choice theorem: just as no voting rule can satisfy a small set of apparently reasonable formal conditions, no risk-score classifier can simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups, balance for the negative class, and balance for the positive class except in trivial cases. The analogy is deployed to suggest that fairness is essentially a problem of unavoidable technical trade-offs, and that practitioners ought simply to choose which criterion to relax, much as one selects an electoral system despite Arrow. Audit this comparison with precision. Give the correspondence table: what corresponds to voters, preference orderings, the social welfare function, and independence of irrelevant alternatives? Then identify where the mapping breaks, paying particular attention to whether the fairness conditions are genuinely axiomatic in Arrow's formal sense or whether they encode competing substantive values; also examine whether the analogy smuggles in the assumption that classifier outputs are preference-aggregations rather than predictions with causal and distributive consequences. Finally, assess whether the practical conclusion—that fairness should be treated as a technically neutral menu of equivalent options—survives once these disanalogies are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight all along. If it fails, propose a more apt comparison for what impossibility results in this domain actually establish.
