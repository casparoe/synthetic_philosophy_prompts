I have been working through Bradley's regress and have come to hold a particular anti-relationalist view about propositional unity that I want to stress-test. Rather than treating the relation between subject and predicate as either an external link that requires a further linking relation, or as a primitive that simply stops the regress by fiat, I hold that the unity of the proposition S is P consists in the subject's own internal self-articulation: the predicate is not an item conjoined to S from outside, but a determinable mode that S itself instantiates, so that there is only one complex reality—S-as-P—without any need for a relating relation at all. To make this concrete, I have been thinking of it on analogy with non-well-founded sets in Aczel's anti-foundation framework, where membership is not grounded in a hierarchical base but is constituted by self-involving structures that are primitive yet fully determinate; similarly, I take the subject-predicate complex to be ontologically self-grounding in its determinacy, avoiding both infinite ascent and unexplained primitives.

What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I am especially interested in whether there are cases where a subject genuinely seems to bear a predicate contingently or externally, and whether defenders of this kind of internal-articulation view can concede those cases without collapsing into a rival account like trope theory or fact pluralism.
