In a thread on Bayesian updating, two commenters got stuck in a disagreement about whether the old-evidence problem threatens confirmation theory, but they introduced a twist involving what one called derivational amnesia. The first argued that if you already assign probability one to some observation E, then by the ratio form of Bayes's rule P(H|E) equals P(H), so old evidence literally cannot raise your credence in H; the only legitimate updates come from learning that H predicts E, which is a different proposition altogether and therefore not old evidence at all. The second agreed that direct conditioning on E is trivial, but said this misses the interesting case: suppose you have long known both that general relativity holds with some credence and that Mercury's perihelion shifts, yet you have never actually performed or followed the derivation linking them; then you finally work through the field equations and see that GR structurally entails the shift. At that point, she claimed, you have learned something genuinely new—not the shift itself, but the modal fact that GR could not have failed to produce it—and this should update your credence in GR upward even though E was already old. The first replied that this just collapses into learning the logical entailment H implies E, which is only new to you subjectively; objectively it has been true all along, and if your prior already assigned probabilities over worlds consistently, you should have already priced in that entailment, so there is still no objective confirmation happening, only the removal of a subjective blind spot. The second countered that Bayesianism is meant to be a normative theory of belief revision for bounded agents, not an omniscient God's-eye view, so treating your prior failure to derive the entailment as a mere blind spot rather than a genuine evidential gap is to confuse epistemology with metaphysics; she added that if you accept this, then two agents with identical priors and identical old evidence can rationally diverge after one derives a prediction and the other does not, which seems to violate the idea that rational belief should depend only on available evidence, not on computational history. The first then asked whether her view commits her to saying that every time a mathematician proves a new theorem from old axioms, she should update on all the theorems she has not derived yet, and whether that does not make Bayesian credences hopelessly path-dependent.
