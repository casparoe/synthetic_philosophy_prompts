In debates over constitutional precommitment, defenders often invoke Homer’s Ulysses: just as he ordered his crew to bind him to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without being destroyed by them, a democratic people binds itself through entrenched constitutional rules—supermajorities for amendment, judicial review, rights provisions—to resist future temptations such as emergency overreach or populist panic. The analogy is meant to show that self-binding is rational rather than anti-democratic, and that the bound party (the people) remains the same agent across time.

Audit how far this analogy holds. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the ropes, the mast, the Sirens, and the ship? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the normative work the analogy is doing: for instance, Ulysses is a single continuous agent, whereas a polity across generations is a succession of different people; Ulysses consented to be bound, while future citizens inherit the ropes without agreement; the Sirens were an external lethal threat, whereas democratic “temptations” are internal and politically contested; and Ulysses’s binding was temporary and revocable by his command, while constitutional entrenchment is typically perpetual and enforced by institutions that future citizens do not control. Which of these breaks actually undermine the conclusion that precommitment is a form of collective autonomy rather than heteronomy? Does the analogy quietly import a model of unitary rational agency that democratic theory should reject, or can it be repaired by substituting a different conception of the “binding subject”? Finally, say whether the conclusion—that constitutional precommitment is defensible on the same grounds as Ulysses’s self-binding—survives once the mapping is fully exposed, and if not, what analogy (if any) would do the work better.
