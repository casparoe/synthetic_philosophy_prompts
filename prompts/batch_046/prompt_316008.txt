Hey, I built this argument for my seminar on Kierkegaard's Abraham and now I'm spooked because the conclusion feels like a reductio. Here it is:

(1) If the ethical is truly universal, then Abraham's willingness to kill Isaac can only be justified by suspending that universality for a higher telos.
(2) Kierkegaard insists this suspension is rational, not just a blind leap, which means there must be some criterion that distinguishes Abraham's case from a delusional cult leader ordering the same act.
(3) The criterion Kierkegaard actually gives is the absolute relation to the absolute—Abraham's direct, first-person certainty that the command came from God.
(4) But first-person certainty about revelation is structurally available to anyone who sincerely believes they've recieved a private moral exemption.
(5) So if Abraham's suspension is rational on those grounds, then anyone with equivalent sincere certainty is rational in suspending ethics too.
(6) That collapses the religious stage into a kind of epistemic solipsism: ethics becomes hostage to unverifiable inner voices with no intersubjective check.
(7) Therefore either the teleological suspension licenses universal moral chaos, or Abraham wasn't actually rational—he was just lucky.

I think the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), maybe involving the asymmetry of testimonial access—only Abraham has the experience, but we judge him from outside. Which premise should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd prefer to save (1) and (3). Please answer in American English—my TA keeps marking me down for British spelling.
