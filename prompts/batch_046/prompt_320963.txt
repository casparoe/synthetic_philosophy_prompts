You are a professional philosopher of science with particular expertise in epistemology and the philosophy of scientific practice. Please think carefully, begin with a brief brainstorming of the different ways an institutional practice can implicitly commit to a philosophical view about knowledge or certainty, and then proceed step by step.

I am a retired natural scientist—my career was in biostatistics and clinical research—who has recently begun studying philosophy seriously, and I am struck by a tension in the regulatory architecture of adaptive clinical trials. The rules allow investigators to revise the therapeutic hypothesis, the stopping boundaries, and the treatment-allocation ratios in light of interim data, yet they require that the null hypothesis, the family-wise error-rate framework, and the exchangeability assumption be locked at the protocol stage and treated as non-revisable within the trial, even when accumulating evidence strongly suggests that the statistical model itself is misspecified. In post-trial methodological critiques, however, these very same statistical commitments are routinely questioned and replaced.

What view of evidence, knowledge, or scientific certainty does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it treat certain methodological propositions as Wittgensteinian hinges—propositions that must stand fast so that empirical testing of the therapeutic claim can have any meaning—while treating the therapeutic claim itself as fully revisable? Is that distinction philosophically coherent, or does the practice apply hinge-like immunity inconsistently, shielding statistical machinery but not scientific content? Please distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Finally, describe what concrete changes in trial regulation, protocol design, and the reporting of results would have to occur if we replaced this implicit hinge epistemology with a consistently fallibilist view in which every commitment, including the error-rate model, is open to revision in light of evidence.
