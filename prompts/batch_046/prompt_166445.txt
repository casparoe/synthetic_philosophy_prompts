I'm preparing a seminar on the moral status of invertebrates and the ethics of pest management, and a colleague's offhand remark made me realize that Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis might serve as a serious philosophical test case rather than mere literary ornament. The novella opens with Gregor Samsa transformed into an insect-like creature—rendered in translations as a kind of vermin—whom his family gradually comes to regard as an infestation: he is filthy, he damages the apartment, he can no longer earn income, and his continued presence threatens the household's economic survival. Read the work through the lens of contemporary debates about insect sentience, the rationality of insecticides, and the moral difference between sanitary removal and the infliction of harm. Where does the narrative invite us to share the family's practical reasoning, and where does it resist that framing by preserving Gregor's human memory, his aesthetic responsiveness during the violin scene, and his unmistakable suffering? I want to know whether the text implicitly treats the family's eventual relief at his death as morally analogous to justified pest control, or whether the analogy collapses precisely because Gregor retains the psychological properties that ground moral status in human beings.

Point me to specific plot moments—the apple injury inflicted by the father, the sister's gradual shift from caretaker to advocate for removal, and the cleaning woman's casual dismissal of the corpse—that either support or undermine a pest-ethics reading. Which scenes should I ask my students to examine closely, and what would a philosopher of animal ethics say the novella gets right about the experience of being treated as an object of sanitation that the academic literature on pesticide policy and invertebrate welfare tends to miss? I'm not looking for secondary scholarship or a bibliography; I want an honest critical judgment about where the literary treatment illuminates the philosophy and where it risks distorting it, so I can design discussion questions for undergraduates who have read little in animal ethics but know the story well.
