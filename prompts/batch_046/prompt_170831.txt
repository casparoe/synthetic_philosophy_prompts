I facilitate a philosophy-of-probability reading group—about ten people, mostly grad students and rationalist-curious undergrads, one 75-minute session—where we've been working through belief revision. I want a structured simulation around the Judy Benjamin problem that makes participants feel why updating on a conditional report ("If the platoon is in Red, then they're near the bridge") can pull credence in a different direction than receiving the direct observation that makes it true. To keep this from being something they've seen in every intro-epistemology slide deck, I want the activity to use imprecise credence: each analyst holds a physical credal interval, represented by a lower-bound chip and an upper-bound chip they place on a shared board, rather than a single point value. Design roles (say, three message-receivers with different reliability weights, two decision-makers who must place a pooled snack-fund bet on where to send a rescue drone), rules that force them to adjust both chips according to an explicit update rule they agree on in round one, and then a second round where a contradictory message arrives and they discover that their interval-shifting protocol breaks coordination. Include materials, a 60-minute timeline with a 15-minute debrief, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money but still hurts to lose.

The idea should show up in what happens before anyone names it: they should first settle on a single update protocol, then experience the divergence physically—chips scattered, bets split—before I map it onto direct conditionalization versus Jeffrey-style updating on a conditional constraint. In the debrief, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (probably over-coordination on point values despite the chip rules) and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of imprecise conditional belief revision the chip model cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally. Finally, separate what's settled about the Judy Benjamin case—e.g., that direct and conditional routes can yield different posteriors—from what's genuinely contested among people who know the literature, such as whether the conditional should be treated as evidence about the world or as a logical constraint on belief, and do not present either interpretation as the consensus.
