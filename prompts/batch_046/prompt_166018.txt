I am a journalist who writes on science, philosophy, and public policy, and in three weeks I am presenting a seminar paper at an interdisciplinary bioethics center defending a narrow, policy-level extension of David Benatar’s asymmetry between pleasure and pain. My thesis—what I am calling “negative asymmetry in resource allocation”—is that within zero-sum, publicly funded reproductive-health budgets, Benatar’s claim that the absence of suffering in non-existent beings is not a good, while the presence of suffering in actual beings is a harm, should function as a tie-breaker: it gives clear moral and budgetary priority to prenatal screening and the prevention of severe congenital suffering over assisted-reproductive technologies that bring new lives into being, because facilitating existence addresses no corresponding moral deficit, whereas preventing harm addresses a real one. I am not arguing for full antinatalism, nor am I claiming that potential persons have rights; I am arguing that when resources are scarce, the asymmetry shifts the burden of justification onto procreative expenditure rather than onto harm-prevention expenditure.

The audience will be about twenty people: analytic philosophers of medicine who know Benatar’s “Better Never to Have Been” closely and are fluent in the standard objections—especially the symmetry objection, the non-identity problem, and the adoption intuition; clinical genetic counselors who think in terms of patient autonomy, reproductive rights, and disability-community perspectives; and health economists who will immediately demand cost-effectiveness thresholds and will be alert to any framing that sounds like it devalues disability or smuggles in eugenic logic. Several have published on prenatal ethics and on the ethics of screening. They are skeptical of journalists making philosophical moves, and they care deeply about feasibility and about not being misrepresented as endorsing coercion.

Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased exactly as each constituency—the philosophers, the counselors, the economists—would actually put them, not as generic straw men. For each objection, specify: what I should concede outright, what I should hold firm on, a reply calibrated to about two minutes, and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or relies on a contested premise. Order them by the damage each would do to my credibility with this particular audience. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely from the economists about QALY-weighting and intergenerational equity, or from the philosophers about whether my allocation framing secretly depends on a person-affecting restriction that Benatar himself rejects—and draft the reply I would need if it surfaces.

In your prep sheet, clearly separate what is settled in the relevant literature (the formal structure of Benatar’s asymmetry, the existence and formulation of the non-identity problem, the standard symmetry objection) from what is genuinely contested (whether the asymmetry survives the symmetry objection, whether it can ground policy without collapsing into antinatalism, whether health economics can accommodate non-person-affecting reasons, and whether “negative asymmetry in allocation” is a coherent extension or a category error). Do not present any contested philosophical claim—especially regarding the moral status of potential persons, the value of naturalness, or the validity of extending Benatar’s argument to resource allocation—as if it were the consensus among specialists. Mark precisely where my own position depends on a minority reading or on a disputed normative premise, and identify the single weakest point in my argument that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope the audience overlooks it. Keep the tone formal and precise, appropriate for evidence-based argument rather than advocacy.
