Student answer: The paradox arises because readers feel real pity for Anna Karenina even though they know she is not real. The classic triad says that emotion requires belief in reality, but since we lack belief in Anna’s existence, our response seems irrational. The diegetic/extra-diegetic distinction solves this by showing that we do possess the relevant belief, just in a restricted mode: diegetically we accept that she suffers, while extra-diegetically we deny she exists. This means the paradox is only apparent.

Walton’s make-believe theory fits this structure well. He argues that within the game of make-believe we experience quasi-emotions, not real ones, directed at fictional entities. Once the game ends, the quasi-emotion should dissipate immediately. Currie, by contrast, holds that we run our emotional mechanisms off-line through simulation, which produces genuine emotions directed at mental representations of characters rather than at real people. So Currie’s view actually requires the diegetic/extra-diegetic split, because the mental representations persist even when extra-diegetic acknowledgment is active.

However, the distinction does not fully save Walton. Readers often report feeling sadness hours after finishing a novel, long after the make-believe frame has ended. This persistence shows that Walton’s quasi-emotion account is incorrect, because if the emotion were merely quasi-emotional and tied to pretence, it should vanish the moment the pretence stops. Meanwhile, Currie’s simulation view explains the persistence naturally, since mental representations can continue to affect our emotional systems even after active simulation ceases. Therefore, the diegetic distinction relocates the paradox for make-believe theories but genuinely resolves it for simulation theories, because only the latter can accommodate post-fictional emotional residue without contradiction.
