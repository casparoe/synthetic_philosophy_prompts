I hold a view about the thermodynamic arrow of time that rejects both the standard Past-Hypothesis explanation and any fundamental law-based asymmetry. Instead, I think the arrow is strongly perspectival: it arises for any embedded system that can intervene in its environment but can only record correlations that have already decohered relative to its own state. On this account, "the past" is not a region of lower entropy that causally explains our memories; rather, our asymmetric capacities for intervention and record-keeping constitute the past/future distinction itself. The global low-entropy boundary condition, even if present, is explanatorily inert—like the absolute position of space in Newtonian mechanics—because embedded agents with these asymmetric capacities would experience an arrow even in a universe with symmetric boundary conditions.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of generic objections. Which formal results in statistical mechanics or quantum information theory would be decisive—for example, proofs that decoherence is symmetric with respect to intervention-capable subsystems, or demonstrations that causal-graph asymmetry necessarily collapses without a global entropy gradient? Which philosophical objections are merely awkward rather than fatal—say, the charge of anthropocentrism, or the difficulty of extending the view to cosmological regimes where no agents exist? What should a committed defender of this view themselves concede as genuinely counting against it: for instance, if it turned out that memory-like storage structures could arise symmetrically in both temporal directions under physically realistic conditions? Is the view at risk of being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be absorbed by redefining "intervention-capacity"—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a claim that purports to be about physical time rather than epistemology?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish predictions this view makes that rival accounts (the Past-Hypothesis view, the causal-law view, the block-universe view) do not, and assess whether the position actually risks anything substantive or has been insulated into safety. I am particularly interested in whether the view's reliance on "embedded agency" makes it vulnerable to results from physics that treat agents as ordinary physical subsystems with no special causal status.
