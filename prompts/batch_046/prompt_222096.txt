I'm working through a tension for a philosophy of culture seminar and have constructed an argument that seems to show moral objectivism undermines itself through cross-cultural data, but I suspect a scope shift or an equivocation on "rational error" is hiding somewhere. Here is the chain: (1) If moral objectivism is true, then there is a single correct normative framework, and persistent, widespread disagreement across well-functioning societies implies that at least some of those societies are in systematic moral error. (2) Empirical work in folk morality—particularly the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence documented in cross-cultural psychology—shows not merely different weightings of shared values (harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity) but a deeper divergence in deontic framing: some cultures treat obligations as agent-relative and non-aggregable, others as universal and maximizable, and these structural differences persist even after controlling for non-moral factual disagreement. (3) If objectivism is correct, then these differences in the logical architecture of moral reasons mean that entire cultural traditions are globally wrong about how obligations function. (4) But to identify which tradition is wrong, we must already apply the normative framework we hold, which makes the diagnosis circular and leaves us with no independent calibration point for moral cognition. (5) Consequently, objectivism entails that moral cognition is either globally unreliable in those societies or inaccessible to neutral assessment. (6) Yet moral cognition in our own tradition is also a product of contingent cultural evolution; if global unreliability can be inferred in other cultures by applying our framework, it can be inferred in ours by applying theirs. (7) Therefore, moral objectivism either collapses into a self-defeating skepticism about all moral judgment or requires condemning functioning societies as rationally defective without a non-circular criterion for doing so. (8) So objectivism proves too much: it turns cross-cultural diversity into an epistemic catastrophe rather than a datum to be explained.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd—surely objectivists can accommodate cultural diversity without self-defeat—but I can't isolate the slip. Is the error in (4), where I conflate applying a framework with circularity? Or in (6), where I illicitly extend unreliability across cultures? Or is there a hidden modal slide between "systematic error about specific norms" and "global unreliability of the cognitive faculty"? I want to preserve (1) and (2) if possible, since they capture what makes objectivism seem demanding, but I am open to giving up (5) or (6) if the cost is lower. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to—perhaps a form of pluralism, a restricted domain for objectivity, or a revisionary account of moral cognition?
