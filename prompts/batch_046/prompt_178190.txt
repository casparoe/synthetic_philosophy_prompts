You have been asked to deliver the opening address at the dedication ceremony for the Meridian Labor Memory Project, a community archive housed in a converted mill building in a former industrial town. Your audience is sharply divided: professional archivists and historians sit alongside descendants of the mill workers, many of whose ancestors appear in the collection only as payroll initials, or not at all. The archive is structurally incomplete by design and by history — it holds union minutes and company ledgers in abundance, but almost nothing from the domestic workers and day laborers who sustained the local economy without contracts or recognition. Rather than treating these gaps as failures to be apologized for, you want the speech to organize itself around the idea of "negative provenance": the claim that an archive's ethical obligation includes not only preserving what it holds, but rigorously documenting the mechanisms — bureaucratic, economic, and social — by which certain lives were never rendered recordable in the first place, treating absence itself as an active curatorial object rather than a passive void. The idea should do real structural work: it must shape what you can and cannot claim about the archive's value, preventing any uncritical celebration of completeness while still justifying why opening these doors matters.

The address should run about ten minutes. It must speak to the experts in the room without lecturing them, and to the family members without false consolation or condescending simplification. You should avoid asking the audience to celebrate a wholeness that does not exist, yet you must still offer a reason for gathering that respects both the documents present and the silences they cannot fill. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not invoke highly specialized theoretical frameworks that require outside knowledge to understand. Instead, develop the concept of negative provenance from within the occasion itself, letting the physical space — a mill turned archive — and the divided audience determine the tone and the limits of what can be said.
