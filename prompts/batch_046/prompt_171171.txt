After a long argument with a friend about Spinoza’s democratic theory, I tried to formalize exactly where I stand, and I want to know whether the package is coherent or whether I am smuggling in an unstated qualification. Here are my commitments:

(1) Democracy, in Spinoza, is best measured not by institutional form—voting, representation, or constitutional design—but by the degree to which a state’s decisions express reason actually shared among its members; it is a scale of collective rational agency rather than a category of government.

(2) Spinoza’s exclusion of women and domestic servants from citizenship was a contingent failure of application, not a consequence of his metaphysics. Because every human mode expresses the same substance with equal ontological status, citizenship should turn on actual rational capacity rather than social role; the historical exclusions therefore contradict the egalitarian structure of his Ethics.

(3) The legitimacy of democratic authority derives solely from the actual collective power (potentia) of the assembled multitude. There is no contractual, consensual, or normative legitimacy that stands independent of this actual collective capacity.

(4) Within this framework, individual freedoms—including philosophical expression—are protected only instrumentally. If particular speech threatens to dissolve the collective rational agency that grounds democratic legitimacy, its suppression is democratically justified, since preserving that rational agency takes priority over the expression itself.

(5) Consequently, Spinozist democracy is incompatible with liberal constitutionalism (which posits pre-political individual rights that constrain democratic will) and also incompatible with populist majoritarianism (which treats the numerical will of the majority as sovereign regardless of whether it expresses shared reason).

Do these hang together? I want an audit, ordered by severity, not a general exposition. For each pair in genuine tension, say exactly which commitment must be revised and what that revision costs; for pairs that only appear to conflict, show why the tension dissolves on inspection. I am especially worried that (2) and (3) pull against each other: if authority comes only from the actual power of the assembled multitude, and the historical multitude was constituted precisely through the exclusions that (2) rejects, then either democratic authority has always been illegitimate or (2) overreaches by importing a normative standard that (3) forbids. I also want you to check whether (3) and (5) are genuinely compatible—one might think that grounding authority in actual collective power collapses into the very populism that (5) rejects—and, if they are saved by an unstated qualification in (3), whether that qualification undermines (4). Please be specific about whether I am helping myself to something in (3) that makes (4) harder to defend, and identify the single cheapest revision that cleans up the most tension.
