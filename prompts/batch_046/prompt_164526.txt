I'm preparing a proposal for a one-semester philosophy term paper of roughly 6,000 words, to be submitted for a graduate seminar on collective moral agency. The central idea is this: Karl Jaspers' well-known distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt is usually read as applying to contemporaneous collectives, but it remains silent on whether any form of collective guilt can outlive the complete turnover of a nation's citizens—indeed, outlive the dissolution and reconstitution of the state itself. I want the proposal to formulate the question precisely in terms of diachronic identity: does collective guilt persist because the nation remains the same moral agent over time (a persistence model), or is it transmitted to successors through institutional continuity, causal chains, or cultural inheritance (a transmission model)? And does Jaspers' taxonomy map unevenly onto these structures—perhaps political guilt survives institutional change while metaphysical guilt requires personal continuity that no collective can maintain?

The proposal should explain why existing answers leave this open: Jaspers assumes a living collective without addressing the metaphysics of group persistence, while contemporary theories of collective responsibility tend to treat institutions as static entities rather than diachronic agents. The method should be conceptual analysis rather than historical scholarship: apply conditions for diachronic identity from the philosophy of personal identity and collective ontology to each category of guilt, derive the obligations that current citizens who reject the legacy would face under each model, and test whether either model avoids collapsing into distributed individual blame. I want a clear statement of what success would look like—say, demonstrating that only one of Jaspers' categories survives the persistence-transmission distinction intact—along with an honest failure condition, such as finding that both models either reduce to individual culpability or generate obligations for citizens who share no causal or intentional link to the original wrong. Finally, include a realistic scope assessment: given one semester, should one category be bracketed, and what exactly could be delivered by the deadline?
