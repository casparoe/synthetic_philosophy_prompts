A colleague extended Weil's argument against political parties and now claims it undermines every form of collective decision-making. I think something slips between the premises, but I cannot name it. (1) Weil maintains that parties corrupt judgment by substituting a collective "we" for individual moral reasoning and by making loyalty to the group override the pursuit of truth. (2) If the harm lies in this substitution of group identity for private reason, then it should occur in any institution that binds members to a shared program and treats dissent as betrayal rather than error, not only in organizations that call themselves parties. (3) Consider a deliberative body formed by random selection—citizens with no party affiliation, no manifesto, and no continuing membership—charged solely with resolving one specific policy question through binding vote; its members must still form a temporary collective "we," and those who dissent are overruled by the group's decision. (4) By (2), this body also corrupts judgment and ought to be abolished. (5) Yet if every collective decision-making body is abolished, coordinated action becomes impossible, leaving only isolated individuals. (6) Therefore either Weil's criterion applies only to parties—which requires explaining why temporary procedural commitment is not the same kind of identity-substitution—or it applies universally and proves that no democratic assembly can be legitimate. I would prefer to preserve Weil's critique of parties without accepting that all collective decision is illegitimate. Which premise or step should be rejected, what is the precise error in it, and what does giving it up cost?
