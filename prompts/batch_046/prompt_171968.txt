I am working through Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, in which he addresses how scriptural passages should be interpreted when they appear to conflict with demonstrated astronomical conclusions. In the relevant section, he contends that the Holy Spirit intended Scripture to teach humanity how to attain salvation rather than to provide a technical account of celestial mechanics, and he maintains that when a literal reading of a biblical text stands in contradiction to a rigorously demonstrated physical truth, the scriptural passage ought to be reinterpreted rather than the scientific conclusion rejected—while insisting, however, that Scripture itself is never in error, only our interpretation of it.

I am finding it difficult to grasp the precise logical architecture of this accommodation. Does Galileo intend a strict partition of epistemic jurisdiction, such that empirical demonstration automatically overrides literal exegesis within a delimited domain? Or is he proposing a more reciprocal hermeneutic, in which scientific and theological claims are continuously calibrated against one another without either holding a permanent, domain-independent default priority? More specifically, should his view be understood as an early anticipation of non-overlapping magisteria with a fixed boundary, or does it preserve a latent theological veto over empirical claims that is merely suspended in particular instances rather than structurally abolished? I would welcome help in clarifying which of these readings best captures the argument of the passage.
