For years I've watched debates about Rawls's veil of ignorance stall out in exactly the same place: everyone accepts the formal apparatus—parties behind the veil, ignorance of particular social positions, maximization of primary goods—yet they split irreconcilably over whether maximin really picks out the difference principle rather than average utility or some prioritarian compromise. The standard moves are all on the table—the risk-aversion objection, the reply about strains of commitment, the counterexamples involving tiny probabilities of catastrophic outcomes—yet nobody shifts. My own suspicion is that the deadlock isn't really in decision theory; it's about whether the veil's ignorance conditions are meant to eliminate information about one's conception of the good, or only about one's social lottery position, and that hidden ambiguity is doing all the normative work. Is that diagnosis right? Break down what the disagreement actually is: a hidden formal question about rational choice under radical uncertainty, a verbal disagreement over whether reasonable means risk-averse or fairness-sensitive, or bedrock intuitions about desert and reciprocity that the original position was designed to bracket but never actually removes? And be honest: given that diagnosis, is there anything—some distinction in the idea of primary goods, some result in social choice theory, a reconception of reflective equilibrium—that could genuinely resolve it, or should we treat the stall as evidence that the question was ill-posed from the start?
