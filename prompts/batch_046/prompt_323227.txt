I have been working through the standard move from Peter Singer's shallow-pond case to obligations of global rescue, and I am struck not by the conclusion but by the opacity of the intermediate steps. The pond offers what I will call a point-source rescue: the agent is physically present, the causal chain is transparent, the victim is singular and identifiable, and the cost is bounded and immediate. Global obligations, by contrast, are institutionally mediated, causally diffuse, aggregated over thousands of anonymous beneficiaries, and sustained over time. Rather than asking whether the analogy holds in general, I want to treat the gap between these poles as generating four specific open conceptual problems that current literature tends to blur together.

First, the transparency-attenuation problem: does the strength of a rescue duty weaken continuously as institutional layers and geographic distance increase, or is there a sharp discontinuity—perhaps at the point where the agent can no longer verify the outcome personally? Second, the aggregation-scaling problem: if a single institutional channel allows one to save N lives at roughly the same marginal cost, is the duty N-fold, constant across the group, or subject to a diminishing-returns asymptote—what one might call a moral-fatigue curve—where very large aggregates weaken rather than strengthen obligation? Third, the substitution problem: when an imperfect but functioning institutional mechanism exists (say, a global health fund with high overhead), does direct individual donation remain obligatory, become redundant, or transform into an obligation to reform the mechanism itself? Fourth, the feasibility-constraint problem: should "ought implies can" be interpreted psychologically—requiring that the agent be able to comply without debilitating moral fatigue—or only structurally, and does this interpretation apply differently to point-source versus systemic rescue?

Assume you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group—three philosophers and one behavioral economist—with six months, no fieldwork budget, and the explicit goal of producing one defensible framework that could inform actual rescue-priority decisions in global-health ethics. Which of these four problems deserves the group's primary attention, and which are upstream of the others? Give me a dependency-aware prioritization: for each problem, state whether resolving it first would settle, constrain, or leave untouched the remaining three. Defend the ranking with explicit criteria—upstream dependency, tractability given the group's composition, practical leverage for policy or personal decision-making, and neglect in existing debate. Identify the single crux sub-question whose resolution would most shift your ordering of the rest. Then tell me which line of work you would actively deprioritize, not because it is unimportant, but because resolving it first would change little downstream practice or theory. I am not asking for a survey, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want a defended agenda that fixes where limited intellectual effort should go and why.
