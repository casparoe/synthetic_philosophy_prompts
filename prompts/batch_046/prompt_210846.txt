I have no formal background in genetics or statistics, but I have been working through what heritability actually means, and I have arrived at an argument that seems to show the concept is either circular or useless for any real-world decision. I want to know exactly where it breaks.

(1) Heritability is defined as the proportion of phenotypic variance in a specific population that is associated with genetic variance.

(2) In a well-nourished population with varied diets, the heritability of adult height is roughly 0.8.

(3) Now imagine a “uniform environment swap”: we place every member of that population into an identical nutritional and exercise regime, so environmental variance drops to zero.

(4) After the swap, all remaining differences in height must be genetic, so heritability becomes 1.0, even though the genes have not changed and the environment is still biologically active.

(5) This shows that heritability tracks how much environments differ across people, not the causal strength of genes over any particular individual’s growth.

(6) Therefore, a high heritability number gives no information about whether altering one specific person’s environment will change that person’s phenotype.

(7) For instance, severe iodine deficiency can drastically reduce cognitive development, yet intelligence heritability in well-fed populations is high; the statistic never warned us that environment could have that large an individual-level effect.

(8) So heritability estimates are useless for guiding policy or personal choice: they describe variance partitioning in a group, not what will happen if we intervene on a single case.

(9) But scientists and policymakers constantly cite heritability to claim that some traits are “mostly genetic,” which sounds like a claim about individual fixity. Either they are misusing the concept, or my reasoning contains a hidden flaw.

I am most reluctant to abandon step (5) or step (7), because the uniform-environment thought experiment and the iodine example both seem clearly correct. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost—especially for how twin-study results ought to be interpreted?
