I'm a journalist who covers philosophy of science and the culture of academic assessment, currently preparing a feature on how graduate seminars in philosophy of mind evaluate subtle reasoning about consciousness and sleep. A colleague teaching a course on first-person authority shared with me an exam question and a single anonymized student response, which I want to use as a case study; before I do, I need a rigorous, point-by-point grading assessment from an outside reader. Please treat me as a fellow grader or exam reviewer, not the student, and do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citation check—only an evaluation of philosophical accuracy, clarity, and the handling of the specific conceptual scenario below.

The exam question was: Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett famously disagree over whether dreams are experiences. Malcolm holds that reporting a dream reports an experience occurring during sleep, and that treating such reports as systematically false violates the logic of first-person authority. Dennett proposes a cassette model in which narrative content is stored during sleep and played back upon awakening as a false memory of experience. Now consider a modified case: a sleeper awakened during REM reports, with full confidence, a phenomenal presence without temporal binding—a vivid sensory quality she insists has no sequence, no before-and-after, only an atemporal now of angular redness. Later, shown an absence of sustained temporal-cortex coherence in her sleep EEG, she withdraws the report, saying she must have constructed it upon waking. How should a Malcolm-style defender and a Dennett-style defender respectively interpret this withdrawal? Does the absence of temporal binding in the reported content support Dennett's narrative-construction view, undermine Malcolm's appeal to experiential authority, or leave both untouched? The student answered: Malcolm holds that dream reports are incorrigible because they report experiences in sleep, and denying them violates the grammar of first-person authority—error about one's own experience is conceptually impossible here. Dennett's cassette model says the brain writes narrative fragments during sleep that are assembled into false memories upon waking. In this case, the report lacks the temporal scaffolding any genuine experience requires, so it is clearly a poorly formed post-awakening construction, exactly as Dennett predicts. Malcolm, however, must treat the EEG finding as irrelevant in principle, since his view makes dream reports immune to empirical correction—neuroscience simply doesn't apply to this domain. The decisive point is that the subject herself withdraws the report after seeing the scan; if Malcolm's authority claim were correct, she could never correct her own initial judgment. This withdrawal proves first-person authority is fallible, confirming Dennett.

Please assign a specific grade—letter or numerical, whichever you prefer—and justify it with concrete, paragraph-level comments. Begin with the grade, then walk through the answer identifying its accurate reconstructions: the correct summary of Malcolm's grammatical argument, the correct outline of Dennett's cassette analogy, and the appropriate identification of the atemporal quality as relevant to narrative structure. Then flag the subtle errors: the misattribution to Malcolm of a blanket empirical immunity rather than a conceptual distinction between logical grammar and neuroscience; the non sequitur that a later, theoretically motivated withdrawal falsifies the authority of the original awakening report; and the unexamined assumption that temporal binding is a necessary condition for any genuine experience, which begs part of the question against Malcolm. In your assessment, please state how confident you are in each major judgment and single out the point about which you are least certain.
