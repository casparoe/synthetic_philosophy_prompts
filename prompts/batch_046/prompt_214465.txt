At a postgraduate reading group on revolutionary history, two members — Mira and Theo — fell into an extended disagreement about whether the French Revolution devours its own children and what kind of explanation that idea actually offers. Mira argued that the Terror and Thermidor demonstrate an internal revolutionary logic: Robespierre, Saint-Just, Danton, and Desmoulins were all destroyed by the institutional machinery they had constructed, which proves that revolutions possess an autonomous, self-consuming dynamic driven by ideological demands for purity. Theo replied that this narrative mistakes contingency for destiny: the fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor depended on the specific alliance between the Plain and disaffected Montagnards, and had Saint-Just returned with loyal forces or the reports been delayed, the outcome might have been reversed; framing it as devouring merely dresses up a political miscalculation in mythic clothing. Mira countered that Theo overstates contingency because the Law of 22 Prairial and the Revolutionary Tribunal had created structural momentum: even a temporary survival would only have postponed further purges, since the logic of defining an ever-narrower set of enemies of the people made sacrificial victims structurally necessary. Theo then argued that Mira conflates ideology with class interest and ignores the material base: the sans-culottes' demands for price controls were being rolled back precisely because bourgeois elements in the Plain sought economic liberalisation, meaning the victims were not randomly consumed but systematically expelled as representatives of popular democracy; therefore the phrase obscures class struggle rather than explaining it. They turned to me, as the only other person present who had not spoken, and asked me to referee. I genuinely could not decide.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess each of Mira's and Theo's four contributions, rating them from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing — in particular, whether the maxim that revolutions devour their children does genuine explanatory work or merely redescribes events, and whether the opposition between contingency and structural inevitability can be sustained without smuggling in teleology. Please provide your answer in British English.
