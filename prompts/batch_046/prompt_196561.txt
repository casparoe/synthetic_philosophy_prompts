Analyze the 2017 Christie's auction of Leonardo da Vinci's Salvator Mundi and the subsequent expert division over its attribution as a case study in Hume's standard of taste. Hume holds that judgments of beauty depend on the verdict of refined true judges who are free from prejudice, not on mere popular opinion or market behavior. In this episode, leading conservators and art historians are split: some defend full Leonardo authorship and treat the painting as a supreme masterpiece, while others assign it largely to workshop hands and question its aesthetic supremacy. At the same time, the work sold for over $450 million, producing a de facto consensus in price despite the absence of expert agreement. Apply Hume's framework precisely: identify who should count as the true judges in this domain, determine whether their persistent disagreement undermines any claim to an objective aesthetic verdict about the painting, and assess whether the auction price acts as a spurious substitute for the convergence Hume expects. Then turn the case back on the theory: does the episode reveal that Hume's standard implicitly requires institutional mechanisms—museums, attribution committees, market rituals—to suppress or mask disagreement, and if so, is that institutional dependency a hidden feature of the view rather than an accidental complication?
