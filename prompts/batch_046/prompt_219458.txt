At a seminar on consciousness and physics, two colleagues—let's call them Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about whether panpsychism, if true, generates ethical constraints on how we manipulate fundamental physical systems. Mara began by proposing what she called the "decoherence-harm" thesis: if every quantum superposition corresponds to a set of micro-phenomenal states, as a constitutive panpsychist might hold, then the irreversible suppression of those states during decoherence is not merely an informational loss but a kind of experiential annihilation. She argued that this is especially troubling for high-coherence systems—such as those maintained in certain superconducting circuits or Bose-Einstein condensates—where the sustained informational structure might support a unified, if minimal, phenomenal field analogous to what integrated information theory measures at macro scales. Leo countered that this reasoning collapses without first solving the combination problem: even granting that fundamental particles have micro-experiences, there is no principled account of how those experiences combine into a macro-subject capable of suffering, so attributing harm to decoherence is like attributing pain to a dissolved sugar crystal. Mara replied that Leo was demanding an all-or-nothing solution; she only needed to show that systems with sustained quantum coherence and high causal integration have a non-zero claim to ethical consideration, not that they are full persons. She added that Leo's own acceptance of panpsychism should commit him to taking micro-harm seriously, since dismissing it entirely risks reverting to physicalism about consciousness. Leo then pressed a reductio: if every irreversible physical transition that destroys coherence counts as harm, then breathing—displacing and decohering air molecules—becomes morally fraught, and without a principled threshold for "sufficient integration," the view proves too much. Mara conceded that a threshold is needed but insisted that Leo had not shown such a threshold is impossible—only that it hasn't been specified—whereas Leo maintained that the burden lies with the panpsychist to supply the combination mechanism before ethical obligations can be derived from physics.

They turned to me and asked me to referee the exchange. Can you go through their moves point by point—Mara's initial decoherence-harm thesis, Leo's combination-problem objection, Mara's response about partial integration and burden-shifting, Leo's reductio about breathing and the missing threshold, and Mara's final concession-cum-rejoinder—and say who's right about what? In particular, assess whether Mara's appeal to integrated-information analogues at the quantum level is a legitimate extension or an unjustified projection onto physics, whether Leo's reductio genuinely undermines her position or merely demands further specification, and whether either party is missing a deeper tension between panpsychism's metaphysical generosity and its ethical parsimony. Please do not just declare an overall winner; indicate which specific premises hold up and where each speaker overreaches.
