Take the 1672 collapse of the De Witt republican regime in the Dutch Republic: Johan and Cornelis de Witt are murdered by an Orangist mob in The Hague, the regent-dominated States-General loses control, William III is elevated to stadtholder, and Spinoza — then living in The Hague — is said to have prepared a public indictment of the killers. Analyze this episode through Spinoza's political philosophy, especially his taxonomy of government in the Tractatus Politicus and his claim that democracy is the most natural and absolute form of sovereignty.

First, classify the pre-1672 Dutch regime under Spinoza's scheme. Is it democracy, aristocracy, or a hybrid that his taxonomy struggles to capture? Say what his theory predicts about its vulnerability to popular violence, particularly his warnings about aristocratic councils that lose authority over the multitude. Then map the actual actors and options — the States-General and provincial regents, the Orangist faction, the urban militia, and the mob itself — onto the constitutional roles Spinoza assigns in a democratic or aristocratic constitution. Where does his framework illuminate what happened, and where does it fail to account for the episode's religious and dynastic dimensions?

Next, examine Spinoza's reported reaction — the plaque he allegedly intended to hang condemning the murderers — as interpretive evidence. Does it show that he recognized the event as a catastrophic failure of democratic reason, or as the inevitable eruption of human affect that no constitutional form can fully tame? Does the rapid transition from republican rule to monarchical restoration confirm Spinoza's view that unstable intermediate forms tend toward monarchy?
