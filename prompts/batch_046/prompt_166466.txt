My friends Maya and Leo had this argument at a bar about a decision system they're consulting on -- some kind of resource allocator, dont ask me the details. The system ranks people by priority scores, and there was this weird occurence where two recipients ended up with literally identical values on every parameter: same urgency, same compatibility, same predicted survival rate, same wait time down to the minute. Only one resource exists, so the system has to pick one.

Maya said they should use a random number generator. Leo said thats just hiding the problem. He argued if theres genuinely no reason to prefer A over B, then theres also no reason to prefer randomization over, say, alphabetical order -- or over just freezing and refusing to decide. Maya pointed out that freezing means both lose, which is clearly worse, so inaction isnt neutral. Leo agreed, but said that just pushes the problem up: now you need a reason to pick randomization over alphabetization, and there isnt one. Maya said randomization is different because its impartial; it doesnt smuggle in irrelevant info like someones name. Leo said impartial just means arbitrary but distributed, and arbitrariness doesnt become rational just because its fair. He said the honest move is to admit the scoring system failed to discriminate, and redesign it to include a finer-grained metric, not slap a coin flip on top.

Then Maya brought up Buridan's ass. She said Leo is demanding a reason where none can exist, and thats the whole point of the paradox. Leo said he knows the paradox, but he thinks its usually misinterpreted: the donkey starves only if you assume rationality requires a prior reason for every choice. In reality, agents can just choose, and the absence of preference isnt paralysis -- its just freedom. Maya said thats exactly her point, you can just choose via coin flip. Leo said no, thats not choosing, thats outsourcing the choice to a physical process. If you have no preference over the outcomes and no preference over the tie-breaking method, then you havent made a rational decision, youve just introduced noise.

They turned to me and asked who was right at each step. I honestly cant tell. Can you walk through thier exchange and say who's right about which specific claim? And is there something theyre both missing about how choice works when preferences are genuinely flat?
