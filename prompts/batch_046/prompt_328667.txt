I am a novelist preparing to moderate a public conversation for a philosophy-and-literature colloquium, and my guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a precise, non-standard answer to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Her view is that pleasures are not unified by a single phenomenal quality—there is no common currency of feeling—but by a shared structural condition she terms unresisted attentional absorption: a state in which a subject's cognitive and affective resources are directed toward an object without experienced friction, even when the object is painful, ambivalent, or intellectually abstract. She concedes that sensory pleasure, the satisfaction of solving a proof, and even the complex gratification of tragic catharsis feel qualitatively different, yet insists they share this structural profile. She anticipates the standard objection that her account collapses pleasure into mere attention or flow, and she replies that the presence of resistance—however subtle—marks the boundary: hypnosis, compulsive rumination, and forced labor may involve deep absorption, but they carry a felt or structural resistance that pleasure lacks. I want you to draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press this position without asking her merely to restate it. Specifically, include: one question that tests the boundary between her unresisted absorption and ordinary non-pleasurable absorption, such as hypnotic fixation or addictive compulsion, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that resistance is phenomenologically present even when the subject does not notice it; one question that confronts the intuition that physical gustatory pleasure and intellectual triumph are radically different in kind, with a follow-up that addresses her probable move of redescribing the difference as variation in content rather than structure; and one question that asks whether her account avoids reducing pleasure to a purely relational or dispositional property rather than an experiential one, with a follow-up anticipating her defense that resistance itself is experienced, not inferred. Beyond these three, include further questions that probe how her view handles mixed or negative pleasures—bittersweet nostalgia, schadenfreude, the enjoyment of horror—without either expanding the category of pleasure so broadly that it loses explanatory power or contracting it so narrowly that it excludes intuitively clear cases. The questions should be framed with philosophical seriousness appropriate to a colloquium audience, not as a novelty act. Finally, for each line of questioning, append a brief self-critical note identifying the precise assumption in my framing that is most vulnerable: state which argument or counterexample would cause me to abandon that particular angle, and flag where my novelist's intuition about narrative absorption might be illegitimately smuggling in premises about aesthetic experience that do not transfer to hedonistic psychology. Write in a formal, polished register, and avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or references to named living scholars. Additionally, include a concluding paragraph in which you state what would change your own mind about the overall coherence of this framing—specifically, which consideration would lead you to reject the premise that resistance can serve as a unifying marker across heterogeneous pleasures, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
