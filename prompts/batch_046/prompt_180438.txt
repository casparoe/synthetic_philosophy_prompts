I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar on innateness and the poverty of the stimulus, and I'm trying to make sense of a passage from Plato's Meno that we read in translation. Socrates draws a square and, through a tightly guided sequence of questions, leads an uneducated slave boy to reject doubling the sides and instead construct a square on the diagonal in order to double the area. Socrates interprets this as proof that the boy is recollecting knowledge his soul already possessed. I'm unsure whether the dialogue actually supports innate propositional knowledge—something like the specific geometric relation between side and diagonal—or only an innate procedural capacity to correct reasoning errors when given structured feedback. If the latter, then treating the Meno as an ancestor to Chomsky's poverty-of-stimulus argument seems questionable, since the boy receives continuous, targeted prompting rather than recovering syntax from impoverished input alone. Could you help me understand what in Socrates' reasoning is meant to rule out a purely procedural or guided-learning reading, and whether that distinction undermines the analogy between Socratic recollection and modern linguistic nativism?
