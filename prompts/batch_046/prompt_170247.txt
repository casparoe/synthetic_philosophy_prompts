I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I am auditing an undergraduate ethics module, and I have been asked to grade a peer's written exam response. Because I am uncertain of my own assessment, I need you to serve as an experienced grader and deliver a rigorous, point-by-point evaluation.

Your deliverable must include a specific grade (a letter grade or percentage) followed by granular comments that justify it. Address each paragraph or major claim in the student's answer individually. Identify what is philosophically accurate, what contains subtle errors—such as a conflated distinction, a misapplied condition, or an inferential step that does not follow—and explain precisely why the error matters. Do not provide a general impression; the assessment should be itemized. Do not penalize stylistic or grammatical issues.

The exam question was:

Distinguish the doctrine of double effect from the broader moral distinction between killing and letting die. Present James Rachels' critique of that distinction using the Smith and Jones cases, and evaluate whether this critique undermines Philippa Foot's formulation of the doctrine in her discussion of the trolley problem. In your answer, pay particular attention to the difference between intending harm and merely foreseeing it, and to whether the physical difference between action and omission tracks any independent moral difference.

The student's answer:

The doctrine of double effect, classically formulated by Thomas Aquinas and developed in modern ethics by Philippa Foot, permits actions that cause serious harm only when four conditions hold: the act itself is morally good or neutral; the bad effect is foreseen but not intended; the bad effect is not a means to the good effect; and the good effect is proportionate to the harm. Foot illustrates this with the trolley problem: diverting the trolley onto a side track where one worker dies is permissible because that death is an unintended side effect of saving five, whereas pushing a large person onto the tracks to stop the trolley is impermissible because his death is the causal means of saving the others.

James Rachels challenges the killing/letting-die distinction through the Smith and Jones cases. Both nephews stand to inherit if their cousin dies; Smith actively drowns the child in a bathtub, while Jones waits for the child to drown and does nothing. Rachels argues that since both agents share the same intention and produce the same outcome, there is no morally relevant difference between killing and letting die. This symmetry thesis directly undermines the doctrine of double effect, because if allowing harm is morally equivalent to causing it, then the permission to foresee harm in the switch case collapses into permission to intend harm, provided the overall outcome is favorable.

However, this critique misses the internal structure of Foot's argument. Foot distinguishes cases not by whether the agent physically moves, but by whether harm is instrumentalized within the action's causal structure. In both the Smith and Jones cases, the cousin's death is instrumentalized to achieve inheritance, so Foot's means-end condition applies identically to both. Therefore, Rachels' examples actually fall outside the scope of the doctrine, which governs only cases where harm is a side effect of achieving an independent good. Once we recognize that Foot's framework relies on direct versus indirect agency rather than on the action/omission boundary, Rachels' symmetry thesis is neutralized. Consequently, a physician who withholds life-saving treatment with the foresight but not the intention of death remains morally distinguishable from one who administers a lethal injection, because in the former case death is not the causal mechanism by which the physician achieves her end.
