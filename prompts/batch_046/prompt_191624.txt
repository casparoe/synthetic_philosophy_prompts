I hold that the analogical argument for other minds is structurally parallel to scientific inference about unobservable entities—both postulate internal states to explain observed behavior—so the classical problem rests on an unjustified demand for direct epistemic access that we do not impose elsewhere. What would count against this? I want the arguments, analogies, and empirical findings that should actually move a defender, sorted by decisiveness: whether the parallel with physics collapses because we can build detectors for electrons but have no independent instrument for consciousness; whether first-person asymmetry introduces a disanalogy that scientific unobservables do not face; whether split-brain or distributed-cognition cases show that the inference is either overpermissive or underdetermined; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by redefining "mind" behaviorally. Include what proponents themselves concede, distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that is merely awkward, and assess honestly whether the position is well insulated—if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a sign that the view has changed the subject.
