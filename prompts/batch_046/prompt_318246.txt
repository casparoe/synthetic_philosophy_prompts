I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness, and we've been asked to read a short passage from an older treatise on sleep and the soul—something written well before the modern vocabulary of "qualia" or "intentionality" was available—alongside Norman Malcolm's well-known argument that dreams are not experiences at all and Daniel Dennett's contrary claim that they are experiences, though perhaps constructed at the moment of awakening rather than occurring continuously during REM. The older text, as I understand it, claims that in sleep the sensitive faculty remains active but is deprived of the cooperation of the common sense, so that the images which arise are not perceptions of external objects; nevertheless, they are not mere absences or negations, but present themselves with a kind of internal phenomenal presence that is structurally analogous to waking impressions, lacking only the corrective comparison with other sensory modalities.

My difficulty is in determining exactly what ontological status the author assigns to these dream-images. On one reading, the emphasis on the absence of corrective comparison sounds like a verificationist worry—there is no criterion by which the sleeper could distinguish a true perception from a false one—which would seem to align the passage with Malcolm's concern that there is no subject or experience present during sleep to be verified. On another reading, the author's insistence that the images have a positive phenomenal presence rather than being mere privations sounds closer to Dennett's acceptance that there is indeed something-it-is-like to dream, even if the temporal location or narrative coherence of that experience is reconstructed after the fact. I am also unsure whether it is anachronistic to map the author's distinction between the sensitive soul operating without the common sense onto any contemporary distinction—say, between first-order phenomenal consciousness and higher-order monitoring or access—since the older framework treats the soul's faculties as genuinely divided in a way that modern functionalism might reject.

Could you help me parse the passage's actual claim about the ontological status of dream-images? Specifically, does the author's distinction between the lack of corrective comparison and the presence of phenomenal content constitute an anticipation of the modern Malcolm-Dennett dispute, or does it rest on metaphysical assumptions about the soul's faculties that make such a mapping misleading? And if the passage does grant dream-images a positive phenomenal presence, does that presence function in the text as an unverified but real experience—something like Dennett's retrospective construction—or rather as a kind of causal residue that falls short of being an experience in the full sense, which might actually support Malcolm's denial? I would particularly value clarification of whether the author's notion of images lacking the corrective of other senses is meant as an epistemic limitation on the sleeper's knowledge or as a metaphysical deficiency that prevents the images from counting as perceptions at all.
