Write a deadpan bureaucratic employee handbook, approximately 700 words, in the flat administrative voice of a government ministry, for the “Department of Contentment and Social Harmony” in a society that has fully institutionalized the Grand Inquisitor’s exchange: citizens permanently waive autonomous choice in return for guaranteed happiness, security, and moral certainty. Structure it like a real onboarding document—sections on signing the Freedom Waiver, daily administration of the three stabilizing pillars (miracle, mystery, and authority), grievance protocols for citizens who report unwanted experiences of liberty or existential doubt, and a brief note on the national holiday marking the formal abandonment of the rationalist utopia. The satire must derive its force from exaggerating actual features of the Inquisitor’s argument—particularly the claim that people prefer certainty to freedom, and that happiness requires the suppression of moral agency—rather than inventing absurdities foreign to the text. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: the horror should emerge from the logical consistency of the premises, not from winking. The standard objections—that contentment without choice is not true happiness, that the authority defining well-being is structurally self-interested, and that moral agency requires the possibility of refusal—should be present but never explicitly stated; they should be visible only in the gaps and contradictions of the administrative language.
