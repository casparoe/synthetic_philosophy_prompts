My study group tried to formalize Davidson's principle of charity and ended up with what looks like a reductio of radical interpretation. (1) Radical interpretation requires that we assign beliefs and meanings simultaneously, selecting the overall interpretation that makes an agent's utterances come out mostly true and rational. (2) Suppose an agent consistently asserts some claim P and behaves in ways fully coherent with P, yet P is false by our own lights. Charity directs us to preserve rationality by reinterpreting the terms or logical form of P—adjusting predicate extensions, logical constants, or the scope of quantifiers—so that P comes out true in the assigned idiolect, rather than attributing a false belief. (3) For any particular belief B that we might be tempted to call false, we can always construct such an alternative semantic mapping without changing the agent's observable linguistic and non-linguistic behavior. (4) So whenever false belief looks available, a charitable reinterpretation is also available that eliminates the error. (5) Therefore, the principle of charity gives us no methodological reason ever to attribute false beliefs. (6) Yet Davidson explicitly allows false beliefs and treats them as indispensable for explaining surprise, correction, and disagreement. (7) Hence rigorous charity seems to prove false belief impossible, which is far stronger than Davidson intended and contradicts ordinary experience. (8) One might reply that charity is only a defeasible default; but if it can be overridden only after we have already identified a false belief, then belief attribution is prior to interpretation, reversing Davidson's thesis that belief and meaning are interdependent. Which step is the weakest link, and why did it look plausible? In particular, if we reject the universal applicability claimed in (3), how do we block arbitrary semantic revision without giving up the interdependence of belief and meaning?
