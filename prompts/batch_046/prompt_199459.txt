Write a six-month research proposal—suitable for a philosophy or political-theory dissertation prospectus, or for a small grant pitch—on lost-cause national myths and the concept of retrospective sovereignty. Define retrospective sovereignty in the proposal as the claim that a defeated group holds not territorial authority but jurisdiction over the moral evaluation of history itself, treating its failure as a credential of exceptional virtue rather than as a disqualifying liability. The precise philosophical question is whether this form of authority is coherent, whether it generates normative obligations for present citizens that differ structurally from those produced by victorious national narratives, and how it operates across cases such as Masada, the Confederacy, Kosovo, and Dunkirk.

Your proposal must include, without supplying a bibliography, citation list, or reading list (name positions and arguments as needed, noting that references will be filled in separately): (1) the problem stated with enough precision that it has a determinate answer—what it means for defeat to function as a source of political authority, and what conceptual work distinguishes retrospective sovereignty from standard nationalism, commemorative practice, or false consciousness; (2) an explanation of why existing accounts—whether therapeutic-memory frameworks, sociological explanations of grievance, or conventional theories of national identity—leave the specific mechanism unsettled, particularly missing the way defeat operates as an authorizing condition rather than merely a historical fact to be interpreted; (3) the proposed approach: a conceptual comparison of at least two cases drawn from the Masada, Confederacy, Kosovo, and Dunkirk cluster, using retrospective sovereignty as an analytical lens rather than as an empirical historical thesis, with an explicit argument for why this philosophical method advances what historiography and sociology alone leave open, and why the obvious alternatives (pure archival history, quantitative attitude surveys, or standard ideology critique) are inadequate to the question; (4) a clear account of possible results—such as a taxonomy of how defeat functions differently across cases, a demonstration that retrospective sovereignty either collapses into familiar authority claims or produces a distinctive normative bind for descendants, or an argument that the concept is incoherent—together with an honest assessment of what would count as failure and whether the project risks being too large for a single semester or funding cycle; and (5) a realistic six-month scope with concrete deliverables and milestones, acknowledging that the work relies on conceptual distinctions and arguments available without specialized archival access, and specifying what the final output should look like for someone deciding whether to fund or approve further work. Keep the framing disciplined: do not invent contested historical details, do not reduce the myths to purely psychological phenomena, and do not treat the cases as interchangeable without argument.
