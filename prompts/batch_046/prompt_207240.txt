I keep running into a pattern in arguments about boycotts, open letters, and collective protest that I don't have a word for. The reasoning goes: I should do X because it's right, and I shouldn't condition my choice on what strangers do. People treat the effect as roughly additive -- my boycott applies a little pressure, my signature adds a little weight. But there's often a hidden curve: a few boycotts push a company to reform, but if half the field does it the firm collapses and junior staff suffer; a few signatures make a letter credible, but if five hundred sign it reads like mob pressure and gets ignored. Because everyone reasons unilaterally and doesn't coordinate, they overshoot the point where more participation makes things worse. It's not prisoner's dilemma, since there's no clear dominent strategy, and it's not tragedy of the commons because nothing is being depleted -- it's more like unilateral reasoning drives collective action past an optimal threshold and into backfire. Does this have an established name? I'm especially interested in whether it's a variant of acting without conditioning on others, and if there's a precise term for the non-monotonic case where too much collective action is self-defeating. If not, how should we carve it out -- what's in, what's out, and how is it different from ordinary collective action problems?
