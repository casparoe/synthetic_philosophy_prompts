Two economic historians, M and N, argued over coffee at a workshop on global economic history about whether the classic comparative question why Britain and not China — often associated with the Great Divergence debate and the Needham question — is well posed.

(1) M said the framing is fundamentally flawed. It treats mechanised, fossil-fuel industrialisation as the implicit telos of economic history and asks why China failed to reach it, thereby smuggling in presentist teleology. It also suffers from the reference-class problem familiar in counterfactual history: selecting Britain — the eventual hegemon — as the baseline case and China as the negative case depends on defining the outcome space in advance. A genuinely comparative formulation would ask why sustained capital-intensive growth crystallised in one Eurasian periphery rather than in another, without presupposing that Britain’s path was the normative endpoint.

(2) N replied that this mischaracterises the exercise. Comparative historians do not treat industrialisation as morally superior; they treat it as a specific, observable complex — fossil-fuel energy regimes coupled to factory discipline and sustained per-capita growth. Asking why that complex emerged in Britain rather than in China is legitimate because both were large, sophisticated agrarian economies around 1700 with comparable living standards. The case selection is justified by scale and baseline development, not by retrospective admiration.

(3) M countered that the comparable conditions premise collapses on inspection. In 1700 China had a far larger population, a continental fiscal architecture, and no Atlantic colonial network supplying bullion and raw cotton, whereas Britain was a maritime, coal-rich, colonising state. Once you acknowledge these structural divergences in resource geography and state capacity, the why not China formulation dissolves into a bundle of unrelated local explanations rather than a single comparative causal problem.

(4) N responded that the differences make the comparison more precise, not spurious. The point is to hold constant the fact of large-scale agrarian sophistication and ask which institutional or market mechanisms mediated the different resource endowments. Without the Britain–China frame, we could not rigorously test claims about patent law, scientific societies, or property rights, because we would lack the negative case. The divergence is empirically striking — similar living standards around 1700, radically different energy and labour regimes by 1850 — so abandoning the frame sacrifices explanatory leverage.

(5) M then argued that the Great Divergence bundles energy transition, factory organisation, state formation, and global market integration into a single package. Any answer that cites coal, colonies, or culture is therefore reductionist, because it explains one component while ignoring the systemic interaction that made Britain exceptional. Before any comparative verdict is possible, the question should be disaggregated into separate causal chains — energy regimes, labour regimes, fiscal regimes — rather than treated as one macro-historical puzzle.

(6) N concluded that disaggregation is indeed necessary — coal geography explains energy but not labour discipline; colonial extraction explains capital accumulation but not innovation incentives. Nevertheless, the broad framing remains heuristically valuable precisely because it forces scholars to study those interactions systemically, rather than treating Britain and China as isolated national histories. The question is not reductionist if we treat it as directing attention to conjunctural mechanisms, not as demanding a single-factor answer.
