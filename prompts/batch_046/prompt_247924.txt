As a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun to study philosophy of language in earnest, I have been attending a small seminar devoted to semantic externalism and Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment. During our discussion of whether theoretical kind terms — for instance, water — are anchored externally rather than fixed by internal conceptual role, a fellow participant repeatedly invoked the one-liner that meanings just ain't in the head in order to close off an objection I had raised. My objection was not a naive mentalism: I granted that a chemist on Earth and a molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth might share all internal psychological states, yet one refers to H2O and the other to XYZ, and I asked how we ought to characterise the semantic divergence if not by some difference in belief, inferential practice, or access to instrumental data. The reply was that the slogan settled the matter entirely, but I remain uncertain which precise thesis was being asserted.

To sharpen the question, I should like to introduce a somewhat contrived but, I hope, illuminating variant. Suppose that on Twin Earth the liquid called water is not merely XYZ rather than H2O, but that local nuclear physics renders the isotope analogous to tritium stable and routinely incorporated into aquatic chemistry, so that even a microstructural description available to Twin Earth scientists differs systematically from ours, despite both communities applying identical operational tests for liquidity, solubility, and potability. If the slogan is intended to cover this case, what exactly does it claim?

Please lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, what that one-liner could actually be asserting. For each reading, indicate whether it amounts to a near-platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, or whether it is plainly false. In particular, distinguish readings that concern only observational predicates from those extending to theoretical kind terms anchored by the division of linguistic labour; distinguish claims about reference determination from claims about mental content; and distinguish social-externalist readings from causal-historical ones. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, genuinely supports dismissing my objection concerning internal duplicates, and which reading is typically being invoked when someone uses the phrase to end an argument about whether semantic properties are fixed inside the head.

I have no need for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I seek conceptual clarification that does not depend upon access to external sources. Please provide your answer in American English, though I have composed this request in British English.
