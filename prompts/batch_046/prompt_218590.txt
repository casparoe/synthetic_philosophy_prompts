You are an experienced philosophy interviewer preparing questions for a podcast episode on collective moral responsibility across generations. Your guest is an unnamed contemporary philosopher who defends a Jaspers-inspired account of transgenerational national guilt. She argues that a nation can bear genuine—rather than merely metaphorical—guilt for historical injustices long after every original perpetrator and victim has died, provided the nation's institutional framework exhibits what she calls "diachronic normative continuity." She works with Karl Jaspers' familiar distinctions among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but adds a proposed fifth category, "structural-institutional guilt," which she claims attaches not to individual citizens but to an enduring organizational substrate that persists through complete membership turnover.

Before drafting the interview, take a few moments to think through how each of these five categories interacts with three problems: the non-identity problem applied to collective agents; the dissolution and reconstitution of states (for example, regime change with constitutional rupture); and the boundary between guilt and shame.

Then draft eight to ten interview questions suitable for an informed general audience. The questions must not ask your guest to restate her view, list readings, or provide citations. Each question should press a specific tension in her framework. Specifically, include the following:

- A question about the non-identity problem: if every citizen has been replaced, in virtue of what is the guilty entity the same nation? Provide a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the institution, not the population, is the bearer—perhaps asking how this applies when a state formally dissolves and is re-founded under a new constitution.

- A question that distinguishes her proposed "structural-institutional" guilt from Jaspers' political guilt, especially regarding reparations: if the guilty party is an abstract institutional structure, who precisely owes reparations, and in what form? Provide a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that current citizens bear the burden qua institutional participants—pressing whether immigrants or dissenters can opt out entirely.

- A question testing whether her view collapses into collective shame: ask her to explain why "structural-institutional guilt" is not simply the shame of inheriting a tainted institution, and whether it requires emotional capacities that institutions lack. Provide a follow-up that presses on phenomenology: if guilt requires remorse, and institutions cannot feel remorse, is the guilt purely ascribed?

- A question that introduces a niche conceptual case: ask how her framework handles "institutional amnesia with functional continuity"—for instance, a state that formally repudiates its predecessor's ideology, rewrites its legal charter, and purges its archives, yet retains the same territorial bureaucracy, economic assets, and international legal personality. Is the guilty entity dissolved, or does guilt persist in the functional substrate? Include a brief follow-up asking whether retention of assets alone is sufficient for continuity.

- A question about comparative culpability: can one nation be more guilty than another for comparable historical acts if its institutional continuity is stronger or weaker? Ask what metric governs degrees of structural guilt. Provide a follow-up keyed to her likely quantitative or qualitative response.

- A question about individual exemption: can a citizen who actively resisted the historical injustice, or who arrived by immigration after the fact, be entirely free of the nation's structural guilt, or does mere participation in the institution implicate them regardless of personal attitude? Provide a follow-up keyed to her likely distinction between direct imputation and associative burden.

- At least two additional questions of your own design that probe other weak points—such as whether collective forgiveness is possible without a collective agent that can be forgiven; whether corporate guilt requires corporate intentionality; or how the view extends to non-state collectives like churches or corporations.

For every question, present the follow-up clearly (for example, marked "Follow-up:"). Keep the tone rigorous but accessible. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke living philosophers by name as holders of this view. Conclude with one final question asking what specific kind of argument, historical discovery, or institutional change—conceptual, empirical, or legal—would cause her to abandon the claim that nations can be genuinely guilty in this structural-institutional sense.
