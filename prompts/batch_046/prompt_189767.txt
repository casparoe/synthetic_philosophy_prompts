I'm looking for the established term, if there is one, for a recurring situation in consent ethics that doesn't sit cleanly within deception, coercion, or incompetence. Consider a case in which someone gives explicit, voluntary agreement to a specific physical interaction—say, an examination or a touch—while fully understanding its mechanics, risks, and immediate context. They are uncoerced and mentally competent. Yet they understand the interaction as belonging to one kind of relationship (for instance, an anonymous clinical service), while the other party intends and enacts it as part of a different kind (say, an intimate courtship or a hierarchical ritual). The mismatch is not produced by any false statement about what will physically occur; rather, it arises from deliberately ambiguous framing—setting, tone, dress, or institutional cues that each party reads differently. The physical act matches what was agreed to exactly, but the consent is often judged invalid for the interaction that actually took place. Does this have a recognized name—perhaps a form of framing deception, a deficit of "relational competence," or something else? How precisely does it differ from deception about the act itself, and what are the boundary conditions: does it apply whenever expectations diverge, or only when one party actively cultivates the ambiguity?
