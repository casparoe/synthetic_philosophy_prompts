I have been trying to map the boundaries of proper function in philosophy of biology, and I keep encountering a three-way pattern that does not seem to line up cleanly with the standard etiological versus causal-role divide. Consider three structurally parallel cases. First, a naturally occurring gene is originally selected for DNA-repair activity; through evolutionary co-option it acquires an essential causal role in immune signaling. Even though its selection history points to repair, biologists and philosophers routinely treat immune signaling as one of its functions and allow that it can malfunction in that capacity. Second, a synthetic gene is engineered de novo with no evolutionary ancestry, designed specifically to perform that identical immune-signaling causal role, and inserted into an organism. Engineers describe success and failure in functional terms, yet many philosophers deny that it possesses a proper biological function in the normative sense, insisting that evolutionary selection rather than intelligent design is required to ground such attributions. Third, a gene acquires the same signaling causal role through purely neutral mutation and drift, with no selection for either repair or signaling. Here the causal-role account assigns it the function of signaling, while the etiological account withholds proper-function status altogether.

What interests me is not the familiar divergence between the two theories, but a specific asymmetry within etiological treatments themselves: evolutionary co-option appears to license or extend proper-function status to a new causal role, whereas intelligent design—despite being an explicit selection process for that very role—does not, and pure drift fails entirely. I want to know whether this particular partition—co-option licensed, design unlicensed, drift unlicensed—has an established name or is recognized as a distinct problem in the literature. If there is no specific term, I would like it named and its boundaries drawn precisely: does the asymmetry apply only to functional attribution, or also to malfunction and dysfunction? How should it be distinguished from ordinary pluralism about biological function, and from the debate over whether designer intentions can substitute for natural selection? I am less interested in adjudicating which account is correct than in determining whether philosophers have already carved this exact joint, and if not, what the carving should look like, including clear boundary cases that separate it from nearby phenomena such as exaptation, spandrels, or design-based functional ascription.
