As a novelist working on a courtroom drama about institutional failure, I wish to understand whether philosophical theories of causation by omission can illuminate a concrete disaster without reducing to legal liability alone. Analyze the 1986 Space Shuttle Challenger disaster—specifically, NASA management's decision to proceed with launch despite documented warnings from Thiokol engineers regarding O-ring integrity in freezing temperatures—through the framework of metaphysical and legal accounts of omission causation. I ask you to map with precision: in what philosophical sense does management's failure to halt the launch constitute a cause of the explosion, distinct from the causal contributions of the cold weather or the flawed seal design? Apply at least two conceptual distinctions central to the literature on omission—such as the difference between an omission understood merely as the absence of preventive action versus an omission conceived as a negative causal event, and the problem of causal selection when several omissions and positive actions are jointly present—to the sequence of decisions on that morning. Then evaluate the reciprocal illumination: does the case show that omission-causation requires a prior institutional duty that may be ambiguous within hierarchical bureaucracies; does it expose a difficulty in distinguishing management's omission from the engineers' omission to escalate their objections further; and does the episode ultimately support the view that omission-causation is genuinely explanatory, or does it reveal that such talk functions primarily as a proxy for attributing moral blame? I am not seeking a bibliography, nor references to specific scholars unless unavoidable; rather, I want the conceptual machinery applied rigorously, with explicit attention to the points where the framework falls silent or produces answers that depend on value judgments rather than causal analysis.
