Compare the "qualitative uniformity" reply and the "functional-reinforcement" reply to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. On the first view, all pleasures share a single primitive phenomenal property—pleasantness—that differs only in intensity and not in kind, much as all visual reds share redness despite varying in saturation; on the second view, pleasures have no common intrinsic feel and are unified solely by being the conscious correlate of positive reward-prediction error, with their apparent diversity explained by the distinct domains (sensory, cognitive, social, aesthetic) in which that error signal is computed.

Address the following in your comparison: (1) Which reply more successfully answers the classic objection that bodily, intellectual, and aesthetic pleasures are too phenomenally disparate to belong to one natural kind? (2) Can the qualitative view accommodate cases—such as the satisfaction of completing a formal proof or appreciating a harmonic resolution—where no identifiable "sensory sweetness" seems present, without diluting pleasantness into an empty placeholder? (3) Can the functional view avoid collapsing hedonism into a form of desire-satisfaction theory or behaviorism, given that reward signals can occur without conscious pleasure (e.g., in subliminal conditioning or addictive feedback loops) and that conscious pleasure can occur without any measurable reward-prediction error? (4) Which view provides a more defensible foundation for the cardinal comparability of pleasures that quantitative hedonism requires, and does either view force us to abandon intersubjective comparisons of well-being in hard cases? Conclude by identifying the crux: is the heterogeneity problem fundamentally about phenomenology, about the ontology of mental states, or about the measurability of value?
