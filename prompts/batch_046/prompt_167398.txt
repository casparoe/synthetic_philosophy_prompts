I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, but I have been trying to formalize a moral intuition about population growth using the decision-theoretic analogies I use in distributed system design. I built the argument below for an internal seminar, and now it strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is clearly absurd, yet every step looks locally valid. I want to know exactly where it breaks. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than accept the conclusion in (8), but I want to understand precisely what I am paying.

(1) In system optimization, if introducing a new node increases aggregate throughput without reducing any existing node below its operational threshold, the introduction is a net improvement.
(2) By analogy, human welfare is the aggregate throughput of a population system, and an individual life counts as operational when its experienced well-being is positive.
(3) If a potential person would have positive well-being, then actualizing them increases total welfare; in the counterfactual branch where they remain unactualized, no existing person is made worse off, because that branch contains different individuals altogether.
(4) This state-space comparison is agent-neutral: it holds regardless of which particular agent performs the actualization.
(5) Therefore, any agent with the capacity to actualize such a potential life has a pro tanto reason to do so, and no agent-neutral reason to refrain.
(6) The evaluation is binary and independent across potential nodes: if actualizing one is justified, actualizing n is justified, provided each remains operational.
(7) By symmetric reasoning, if an existing node operates below threshold (negative well-being), removing it increases aggregate throughput, and this evaluation too aggregates linearly.
(8) Consequently, moral requirements push toward unbounded actualization of barely-operational lives and removal of sub-threshold ones.
(9) Nobody is required to reproduce maximally, nor to eliminate those who suffer.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am particularly worried about a hidden shift between comparing counterfactual state-spaces and deriving agent-specific obligations, and about whether the independence assumption in (6) ignores resource contention that makes the operational threshold depend on how many nodes are already running. But I also wonder whether the error is earlier: perhaps (3) smuggles in a commitment to the moral significance of merely possible beings that collapses once we distinguish lazy evaluation of a branch from eager commitment to actualize it. If the argument is valid and the cost must be paid at a specific step, I prefer to keep (1) and (3) and abandon (5) or (6), but I want to know exactly what that choice commits me to.
