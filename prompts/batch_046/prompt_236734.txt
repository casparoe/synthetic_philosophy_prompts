Read Ken Kesey’s One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest through Foucault’s analytics of power—specifically the tension between disciplinary normalization and pastoral power—rather than through the familiar panopticon image alone. Nurse Ratched runs the ward as a therapeutic institution: she administers medication, conducts group therapy, and claims to restore patients to social usefulness. How does the novel show modern power operating not through brute coercion but through care, diagnosis, and the production of “healthy” subjectivity? Examine the group-therapy scenes and McMurphy’s refusal to confess: do they illustrate patients being made to internalize norms, or being guided toward a salvific normalcy that serves institutional order? When McMurphy rebels—through gambling, smuggling alcohol, organizing the fishing trip—is his resistance absorbed as further proof of his illness, and does the novel’s tragic ending imply that benevolent institutions always convert dissent into pathology? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce Kesey’s humanist sympathy for the patients to a mere illustration of Foucault, and that asks whether the novel’s emotional commitment to individual freedom reveals a limit in Foucault’s suspicion of all institutional care.
