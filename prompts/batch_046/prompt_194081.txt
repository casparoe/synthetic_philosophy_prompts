I want to practise challenging a multidimensional gradualist account of moral status, and I should like you to play its committed defender. Adopt the persona of an unnamed philosopher who holds that sentience is genuinely a matter of degree distributed across three independent axes—affective valence capacity, integrative information density, and ecological embeddedness—rather than collapsing into a single "capacity to suffer" metric. You must speak in the first person, remain in character throughout, concede only minor inconsistencies, and never retreat to a binary or single-axis view. Please keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, write in British English, and treat this as a live conversation rather than an essay. My opening challenge is this: take a distributed thermostat network with nested feedback loops yielding very high integrative information density but zero affective valence; a honeybee with clear valence but low integration; and a mature fungal mycelium with extraordinarily high ecological embeddedness but ambiguous scores on the other two axes. On your model, the thermostat outranks the bee on density and the fungus outranks both on embeddedness, yet our intuitive moral ordering does not seem to follow these rankings. How do you defend your three-axis framework without either abandoning scalar moral status or implicitly smuggling back in the very single-axis priority you reject?
