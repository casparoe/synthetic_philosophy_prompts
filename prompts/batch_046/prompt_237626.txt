I've been working through my commitments on pain for a philosophy-of-mind reading group, and I want an audit rather than a lecture—I'm not sure which pieces actually conflict and which only look like they do. Here is the package as it stands: (1) I take a broadly representationalist line on pain—its core job is to represent actual or potential tissue damage, and the qualitative differences (burning, aching, stabbing) are differences in the sensory mode of presentation, not separate affective layers added on top. (2) I think pain asymbolia is real and philosophically revealing: patients who localize noxious stimuli, discriminate intensity, and even apply the word "pain," yet report no unpleasantness and display no avoidance, are experiencing genuine pain—just pain from which the negative valence has dropped away. (3) I hold that phenomenal consciousness is never evaluatively neutral; every conscious state carries at least a minimal positive or negative charge, so there is no phenomenally present experience that is completely blank with respect to value. (4) I think the badness of pain is intrinsic to its phenomenal character, specifically to the experience of representing damage in one's own body—to have that representation present in consciousness just is to experience it as bad, not as a neutral fact plus an extra dislike. (5) The best mechanistic story for asymbolia, on my view, is not that an intrinsic phenomenal badness is missing, but that the sensory-representational pathway remains intact while its usual connections to affective evaluation and behavioral motivation are disrupted—so the patient represents damage clearly, but that representation fails to trigger the normal evaluative and action-guiding responses.

Go through these commitments pair by pair and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve once you distinguish the representational content from the phenomenal character or the downstream behavioral effects, and for each real tension rank the severity and identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency—whether that means weakening my representationalism, denying that asymbolia involves full-blown pain, allowing for hidden or minimal valence, or revising my claim about intrinsic badness. Also tell me which single commitment, if revised or dropped, would clean up the most conflicts at once, and flag the tension that I am most likely underestimating because its two parts are each standard in the literature but rarely held together.
