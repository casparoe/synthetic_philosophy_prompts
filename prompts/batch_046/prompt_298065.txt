Having spent three decades in molecular biology and only recently turned to philosophy in retirement, I find the literature on self-deception both fascinating and frustratingly taxonomic. I am assembling a small conceptual framework for a discussion group of similarly late-coming readers, and I should like your help in sharpening it into a proper sorting instrument. Specifically, I want a decision tree or flowchart—structured around concrete diagnostic cases rather than philosopher-name recognition—that classifies the positions one might take on whether self-deception is genuinely possible.

Please construct the tree around three logical axes that I take to matter more than the usual labels. First, must self-deception involve the simultaneous endorsement of contradictory beliefs within one unified cognitive agent, or can it occur across partitioned sub-systems that fail to communicate? Second, is the deceptive mechanism best understood as an intentional, strategic act of the agent, or as an unintentional by-product of motivated reasoning, emotional defence, or predictive insulation? Third, is the phenomenon fundamentally doxastic—about belief and belief-contradiction—or can it be fully accounted for in non-doxastic terms such as affective salience, attentional bias, or behavioural disposition?

The tree should open with a concrete scenario—for instance, a senior clinician who knows the epidemiological data on her own condition yet behaves and feels as though she is personally exempt, or an executive who repeatedly dismisses audit warnings that threaten a self-image of competence. From there, each branch node must be phrased as a question a respondent can answer without already knowing which philosophical position it leads to. The leaves must include at least: (i) a strong intentionalist view accepting literal contradictory belief maintained by agential strategy; (ii) a partitionist view allowing contradictory representations in compartmentalised sub-systems without global contradiction; (iii) a deflationary view denying genuine belief-contradiction and reinterpreting the phenomenon as ordinary motivated reasoning or selective attention; (iv) an affective or non-doxastic view locating the deception in emotional and attentional structures rather than in propositional belief; and (v) one further position of your own injection—something not merely a restatement of the above but introducing a genuinely distinct axis, perhaps drawing on predictive-processing accounts of error-suppression or on a distinction between epistemic insulation and active suppression. Where the logical space allows answer-combinations that no named view occupies, please note them and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. In addition, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate with the cases and questions you have chosen, rather than papering over the ambiguity.

Please begin with the taxonomy itself, presented as the ready-to-use instrument with no preface or commentary, and present it in a clear, structured format. If my framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps the logical axes I have proposed do not actually cut between the views, or the demand for a purely case-driven tree is impossible given how these theories are structured—say so plainly at the outset rather than complying politely. Write in British English, maintain a formal and polished tone appropriate to someone with a scientific background now engaging with conceptual philosophy, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
