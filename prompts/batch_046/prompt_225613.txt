Is there an established name for the following pattern? People routinely claim direct, authoritative introspective access to whether one of their own mental states is "genuine" rather than "performed"—for instance, whether they are truly angry or merely displaying anger for social effect. When hidden feedback is experimentally reversed—say, they are shown false physiological indicators pointing to the opposite of their own belief—their introspective verdict often shifts to match the cue, yet they remain unaware that any external influence operated. Crucially, the response to this failure is not to revise the general claim about first-person authority, but to quarantine the error: they treat it as a local exception caused by fatigue, distraction, or ambiguity, and reaffirm that under normal conditions access remains direct and unmediated. I have observed structurally similar moves with judgments about pain intensity and about whether a preference was formed autonomously or subtly implanted: in each case, specific counterexamples are isolated as noise, preserving an untouched core thesis about privileged access. This feels distinct from ordinary confabulation, since the subject is not inventing a causal story for behavior but protecting the boundary of a faculty; and it differs from the familiar psychological literature on introspective error, which tends to concern causal explanation of action rather than this meta-cognitive defense of authority itself.
