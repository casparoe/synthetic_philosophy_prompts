Write an essay on Aristotle’s classic sea-battle problem and whether it genuinely threatens the principle of bivalence for future-tensed statements, or whether the threat dissolves once we distinguish causal openness from semantic indeterminacy. Rather than rehearsing the familiar determinism-versus-freedom dialectic, examine the less-discussed interpretation that Aristotle’s argument targets not metaphysical contingency itself, but the conditions under which a present assertion about a future event can carry a determinate truth-value when the causal pathways leading to that event remain structurally unresolved. Weigh the standard metaphysical reading (that the future is genuinely open in an ontological sense) against the semantic-pragmatic reading (that bivalence fails because verification conditions are not yet fixed, not because reality is unformed) and against the modal-logical response (that branching-time semantics can preserve bivalence by indexing truth to branches rather than to a single actual future). Reach and defend a specific verdict: is Aristotle’s rejection of bivalence for future contingents best reconstructed as an ontological claim about an unsettled future, a semantic claim about the limits of present-tensed predication, or a pragmatic claim about conditions for rational assertion? Your conclusion should be substantive rather than conciliatory, and you should explicitly address the strongest counter-position.
