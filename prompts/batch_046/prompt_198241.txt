Major museums and auction houses routinely downgrade a work’s display priority, insurance valuation, and scholarly attention the moment its attribution shifts from a named master to “workshop of” or “follower of,” even though its physical and perceptual properties remain unchanged. At the same time, these institutions authorize prominent display of high-fidelity replicas—including 3D-printed reconstructions of destroyed originals—treating them as fully adequate substitutes for aesthetic and educational purposes when the authentic object is inaccessible. What implicit theory of artistic value does this pair of practices presuppose? Does it commit to a purely indexical conception, on which value derives from a specific causal-historical connection that replicas and misattributed works lack? Or does it presuppose a more complex ontology in which authenticity functions as a non-transferable status independent of form? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, assess whether the de-attribution practice and the replica-acceptance practice are consistent with a single view or pull in opposing directions, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and explain what would have to change in institutional policy if artistic value were treated as constituted entirely by perceptible form and documented provenance played no independent normative role.
