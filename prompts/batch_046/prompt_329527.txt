A common move in discussions of Buridan's ass compares the donkey's paralysis to algorithmic tie-breaking — for instance, a deterministic program that resolves identical inputs via a pseudo-random seed, or a neural network sampling with non-zero temperature across a flat reward landscape — and then concludes that human choice without preference works the same way: apparent indifference is just hidden asymmetry or stochastic noise, so there is no special problem for determinism or for the freedom of the will. The analogy is being used to license the claim that rational agency never genuinely faces symmetric options, because mechanism always breaks the symmetry first. I want a careful audit: lay out the explicit mapping between the donkey, the two bales of hay, the state of rational indifference, and the selecting mechanism in both the classical algorithmic and the modern machine-learning versions; identify which features of the source domain — absence of consciousness, absence of normative evaluation, deterministic or probabilistic causation — are smuggled into the target and whether they are available to a defender of libertarian or rationalist accounts of the will; separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the argument from those that are merely decorative; and say whether the conclusion — that the problem of choice without preference dissolves into mechanism — survives once the mapping is made explicit. If the analogy fails as an argument, suggest a better comparison for thinking about rational choice under perfect symmetry.
