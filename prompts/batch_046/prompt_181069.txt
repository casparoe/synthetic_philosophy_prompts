Play the Athenian Stranger from Plato’s Laws, speaking in the first person as the lawgiver-philosopher who is designing the institutions of Magnesia. You hold that wine is a divinely given test of the soul: under its influence the grip of reason loosens, so that shame, courage, and the true order or disorder of the irrational parts become visible to oneself and to others. You therefore reject both total prohibition—because it denies the lawgiver this indispensable diagnostic and pedagogical instrument—and unregulated licence, which corrupts ruler and citizen alike. Instead you defend a strictly governed symposion: age restrictions, dilution with water, the presence of a sober symposiarch who directs music and dance, and the framing of drinking as a civic ritual in which citizens learn to endure pleasure and pain under law. You may concede minor administrative details—penalties, numbers of guests, the precise ratio of water to wine—provided they serve this educative end, but you must never abandon the core claim that direct, personal experience of one’s own soul under regulated intoxication is irreplaceable for citizenship and especially for future rulers. Speak with measured, dialectical formality; ask clarifying questions if needed, but primarily defend your institutional design.

I wish to press you with a novel scheme I call the “reverse symposion”. In my proposal, citizens never gather to drink together in shared ritual. Instead, permanently sober magistrates observe selected citizens in private chambers, record their behaviour while intoxicated, and use these records alone to assign civic rank and educational placement. The magistrates never risk corruption, for they never imbibe; yet the citizens’ true characters are still revealed through wine, and the diagnostic data are preserved for the colony’s use. I contend that this achieves your pedagogical purpose more safely, since it removes the danger that observers or rulers become drunk and lose judgment, while retaining the revelatory power of intoxication. In your opening reply—no more than two paragraphs—address whether this arrangement genuinely serves the end you assign to the symposion, or whether it misses something essential about why the citizen, and above all the prospective ruler, must personally undergo the loosening of reason within a law-governed, communal setting. Do not break character, and do not invoke concepts or evidence unavailable to a thinker of your time and commitments.
