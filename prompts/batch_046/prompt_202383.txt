Question: Moral foundations theory (Haidt) says liberals lean mainly on care and fairness, while conservatives draw on all six foundations including loyalty, authority, and sanctity. Some philosophers take this left/right split to mean conservatives have a fuller moral palette; others say its just descriptive psychology, like taste differences, with no normative bite. Now inject this framing: treat the six foundations not as seperate taste-buds but as parameters of one universal moral grammar, where different cultures or ideologies just toggle different ones on. With that grammar view in mind, does the psychological difference between left and right actually give us any reason to think one side is correct, or is it only different parameter settings?

Student answer: The student argues that even under the grammar framing, liberals are closer to being right because care and fairness are the default parameters that stay active in every culture, while loyalty, authority, and sanctity are local toggles switched on by specific group conditions. They say Haidt shows conservatives have more parameters running, but thats not normatively better—like extra spices dont make the dish better. Then they claim care applies across strangers and animals, so its genuinely universal, whereas the binding foundations are parochial evolutionary leftovers. They cite Haidt saying all six are equally valid, but dismiss that as descriptive neutrality that doesnt block normative criticism. They conclude psychology doesnt settle morality, except it reveals conservatives are overfitting their moral grammar to local tribal conditions, so those extra parameters shouldnt carry weight in the final account.
