My thesis: I defend a revised analytical framework—what I call "asymmetric epistemic architectures"—for strategic reasoning in collaborative inquiry. In this framework, agents can share first-order common knowledge of rationality (each recognizes that the others update beliefs consistently given their evidence) without sharing common knowledge about which reasoning procedures, evidential standards, or methodological norms count as authoritative for resolving the dispute. I argue that standard epistemic-game-theoretic equilibrium analyses presuppose a symmetric architecture in which differences in reasoning authority are treated as private information or noise, and that this presupposition systematically misdescribes sustained scientific disagreements—such as deadlock between climate modelers and field ecologists over whether simulation outputs or long-term observational records should govern a particular inference—where both sides are rational, both recognize each other’s rationality, but the disagreement persists precisely because the authority structure governing resolution is visible to all yet mutually unacknowledged as binding. I claim this revision preserves the normative force of rationality commitments without collapsing into relativism, but I do not claim it solves the disagreement; I claim it correctly diagnoses why equilibrium predictions fail.
