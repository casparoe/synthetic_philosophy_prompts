I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on moral change, and after recently working through Bernard Williams's and Kwame Anthony Appiah's accounts of honor, status, and normative collapse, I've tried to fix my own view in writing. Here is where I have landed: (1) Large-scale moral collapses—dueling, slavery, foot-binding—are driven primarily by shifts in the economics of honor and status competition, not by the rational force of moral argument; (2) the philosophical and religious arguments that circulate after a norm has fallen typically serve as a kind of status-laundering for elites, allowing them to preserve moral prestige while shedding practices that have become politically or economically costly, and these arguments are causally inert with respect to the collapse itself; (3) Whig historiography is a genuine methodological error, and we should resist writing the past as an inevitable progress toward present sensibilities; (4) despite that methodological commitment, democratic societies have a functional need to narrate these collapses as instances of moral learning and expanded moral imagination, even if the narrative misattributes causation; and (5) in small, face-to-face settings, moral argument can genuinely revise individual beliefs, and this capacity is not illusory—it simply fails to scale to society-wide revolutions for contingent structural reasons, not because practical reason is impotent.

Before I present, I want a consistency audit of this package. Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only appear to conflict? In particular, does my rejection of Whig history in (3) conflict fatally with my endorsement of progressive moral-learning narratives in (4), or can methodological historiography be separated from public pedagogy without bad faith? Is there a hidden contradiction between (1) and (5)—if argument is causally irrelevant at scale, does that undermine my claim that small-group belief revision is genuinely rational rather than just another local status dance? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the cheapest single revision that would restore coherence, and say what I would have to sacrifice for it. I am genuinely unsure which commitment I am most willing to drop, so treat each as live and do not assume I will protect the functional-narrative claim in (4).
