I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have taken up philosophy of decision theory and metaphysics with the earnestness of a late convert. At a recent interdisciplinary seminar on rational agency across cosmological scales, I witnessed an extended disagreement between two regular participants—Dr. Elena Vasquez, whose work concerns modal logic and formal epistemology, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher of physics with a background in statistical mechanics—regarding what they both called “acausal trade” between rational agents separated by causally disconnected domains. I report their exchange below and ask you to adjudicate it move by move, for I could not locate the precise point at which reasoning failed.

The case they discussed was deliberately more structured than the familiar Newcomb-style problem. They asked us to consider two artificial agents, A and B, each inhabiting a separate cosmological region with no shared past light cone and no possibility of future physical interaction. Both agents possess an identical, valid proof that the other is running an isomorphic decision procedure, and each can compute exactly which action the other will execute conditional on its own choice. Vasquez maintained that this logical correlation is sufficient for rational cooperation—what she termed a “proof-mediated exchange”—whereby A performs a costly action that benefits B in exchange for B performing a symmetric costly action that benefits A, despite neither ever being able to observe the outcome in the other’s domain. Chen countered that without any causal channel, there is no mechanism of enforcement, no shared probability space, and consequently no genuine exchange; at best, this describes parallel optimization masquerading as trade.

Their exchange unfolded as follows.

(1) Vasquez: If rationality requires expected-utility maximization over all worlds consistent with one’s evidence, then A’s utility function must include B’s welfare weighted by the logical probability that B’s decision mirrors A’s. The correlation is evidential rather than causal, and evidential decision theory obliges A to treat its own choice as determinative of B’s.

(2) Chen: This collapses the distinction between acting for another and merely predicting another. In standard decision theory, an agent conditions on its own action only when there is a causal or evidential link to outcomes it cares about. Here, A’s choice does not alter B’s world; it only updates A’s beliefs about an independent process. The expected-utility computation is therefore a bookkeeping error.

(3) Vasquez: That objection misses the specific structure of acausal reasoning. A’s decision is not a signal transmitted to B; it is a logical determinant of B’s decision, because both agents derive the same conclusion from identical premises. The correlation is not statistical but necessary, so conditioning on one’s own action reflects the actual structure of the problem rather than a fallacy.

(4) Chen: Even granting logical necessity, one must still specify the reference class over which one averages. If A knows B is isomorphic but does not know how many other isomorphic agents exist in inaccessible domains, then A cannot compute the marginal benefit of cooperating with B in particular. Without a well-defined joint distribution, “trade” is conceptually undefined.

(5) Vasquez: The reference class is fixed by the proof itself. Once A demonstrates that B is the unique agent satisfying the isomorphism conditions within the accessible logical space, the weighting is determinate. The difficulty is not statistical ambiguity but logical identification.

(6) Chen: Logical identification, however, requires that A can correctly model B’s reasoning in every detail. If B employs a slightly different proof strategy, or introduces a stochastic element, the isomorphism fractures. In causally connected trade, contracts and reputation correct such errors through feedback; in acausal trade, there is no feedback loop, so the risk of mis-coordination is absolute and irrecoverable.

(7) Vasquez: That risk is symmetric and therefore cancels in the calculation. Both agents face identical uncertainty about the other’s proof path. Rational agents should therefore adopt the decision rule that maximizes utility given mutual acknowledgment of this symmetry—what one might call a “counterfactual equilibrium”—rather than defecting out of fear of an unobservable mismatch.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each move (1) through (7), indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state which party is more right overall, and identify the specific conceptual pressure point—perhaps one that neither Vasquez nor Chen has addressed—where both arguments appear weakest to you. I am interested solely in the internal logic of their positions; please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
