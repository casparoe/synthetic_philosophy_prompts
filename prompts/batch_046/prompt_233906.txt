Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on rational belief, and I now suspect it demands an implausible omniscience about precise probabilities.

(1) The classic Dutch book demonstrates that if your betting quotients on A and on not-A fail to sum to 1, a bookmaker can construct bets that guarantee your loss.

(2) The usual remedy is to require precise probabilities: for each proposition, assign a unique real-valued credence.

(3) Yet imprecise credences—intervals such as [0.4, 0.8]—are often defended to represent genuine evidential scarcity, allowing one to remain agnostic about climate damage by 2100 without fixing on a point value.

(4) Even with intervals, a diachronic Dutch book can be arranged if the agent accepts bets at any price within the interval; the bookmaker buys at the lower bound and sells at the upper.

(5) So, to avoid being bookable, one must either abandon imprecision entirely or refuse every bet that falls within one's interval of ignorance.

(6) But universal refusal to bet in domains of sparse evidence is not irrational; it is a form of epistemic modesty rather than a logical flaw.

(7) Hence the Dutch book argument, applied consistently, forces precise probabilities in every domain—including those where evidence is genuinely thin—because the only escape is betting abstinence that renders credence practically idle.

(8) That conclusion is unacceptable: rational agents ought to be able to register deep uncertainty without being charged with inconsistency.

Which premise should I reject? I am inclined to preserve (3) and (6), and I am open to surrendering (7) if necessary, but I want to understand what it costs to deny (4) or (5)—specifically, whether blocking the diachronic construction undermines the original synchronic argument or merely limits its scope.
