I run a 75-minute workshop for 12 graduate students in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. Design a structured in-person activity—rules, materials, phased timeline, and a debrief—that makes participants feel Hofstadter’s idea that creativity works like knob-twiddling on a conceptual theme: continuous parameters are adjusted so that the surface form changes while a deep structure is preserved. To keep it from being a standard lecture demonstration, build in a specific twist not found in the usual exposition: every group receives a base concept (e.g., causation, personhood, representation) and a control board with five dials, but one dial is a null-parameter—turning it does not vary a feature but removes it entirely—and the rules require that any proposed variation must include at least one null-turn. Groups generate three variations, then exchange boards with another group, who must reconstruct which dials were moved and decide whether the result is still the same theme or has collapsed into something unrecognizable. Include a peer-evaluation payoff (reconstruction accuracy plus a fidelity score voted by the receiving group), explicit rules that prevent treating dials as binary switches, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity—especially the resistance to turning the null-dial and the crisis when a variation is rejected—onto parts of the knob-twiddling model. Be frank about which feature of real creative cognition the activity cannot reproduce, such as the unconscious, associative emergence of a new theme rather than deliberate parameter adjustment, and tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run (for example, groups avoiding the null parameter or conflating surface novelty with structural fidelity). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not rely on any external text that participants would need to look up.
