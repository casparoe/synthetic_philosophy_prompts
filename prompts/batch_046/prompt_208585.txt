I have been following, mostly through long-form essays and occasional late-night conversations with friends who work in software but have never studied philosophy formally, a debate about whether breaking an enormously difficult task into smaller, human-checkable pieces actually keeps an intelligent system both capable and trustworthy. The proposal—often described as iterated amplification or distillation—is that you train a system on sub-problems that people can directly evaluate, then combine those partial solutions to address something far beyond unaided human oversight. One side argues that if every component is both competent and aligned, the assembled whole inherits both properties cleanly, like weights summed on a scale. The other side insists that something essential evaporates in the assembly: the long-range coherence of a judgment, or the kind of moral orientation that only makes sense when directed at an undivided problem, so that stitching safe parts together might yield an unsafe or incoherent result. Despite years of discussion, the exchange feels frozen to me; both camps seem to share the same premises about supervision and scaling, yet they never converge, as if they are measuring different things with the same word.

My working theory is that the impasse is not waiting for a new experiment—no laboratory result seems imminent—but hides a deeper disagreement about whether intelligence and ethical alignment are compositional at all. The defenders treat them like properties that survive division and recombination; the critics treat them like textures that vanish the moment you cut the fabric, insisting that competence and value are irreducibly holistic. But I cannot tell whether this is genuinely the root, or whether the two sides are actually answering different questions—one about the mechanics of learning, one about the nature of responsibility—while using a single vocabulary. Could you diagnose the structure of this stall? Is it a verbal disagreement over what alignment means when applied locally versus globally, a substantive dispute about the architecture of cognition or value, or an ill-posed question that assumes decomposition is either always safe or always corrupting? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a precise conceptual distinction, a formal result about the limits of modular reasoning, or a shift in the framing of the problem itself? I am looking for an honest assessment of whether this debate can move, not a survey of familiar positions.
