I've been trying to formalize my epistemic stance for a long-form piece I'm writing on forecasting, expert disagreement, and the limits of self-correction, and I'd like a consistency check before I commit to the framing. Here is where I've landed. First, I accept the outside-view / inside-view distinction—start with reference-class base rates, then adjust with mechanistic reasoning—and I take it to apply to belief formation generally, not only to explicit prediction. Second, I hold that epistemic modesty requires treating my own reasoning as a fallible instrument: if I construct an elaborate causal narrative that diverges from the base rate, I should be especially suspicious, because narrative coherence is cheap and predictive accuracy is expensive. Third, I think that normative and conceptual domains—say, debates about distributive justice or the interpretation of quantum formalism—lack observable outcome feedback loops, so there is no usable reference class of "similar past judgments," and here the inside view (logical consistency, reflective equilibrium, thought experiments) is essentially the only game in town. Fourth, I also allow that in apparently singular empirical events—say, the first detection of gravitational waves or the development of a specific mRNA vaccine platform—reference classes are either empty or actively misleading, and inside-view reasoning must carry the load, even when my personal forecasting track record in that domain is poor. Fifth, I maintain that self-correction should converge: if I apply an outside-view correction and then a peer-disagreement adjustment, I should stop there, because further iterations of "modesty about my modesty" collapse into an infinite regress that renders belief formation impossible, analogous to numerical convergence within measurement error. Sixth, as a journalist, I think I should report the outside-view expert consensus but also highlight inside-view dissent when the dissenter points to a specific, checkable modeling error—not merely to a different intuition.

My worry is that these commitments do not hang together cleanly. For one thing, there seems to be a tension between allowing inside-view dominance in singular empirical cases and my general suspicion of narrative divergence away from base rates, since classifying an event as "singular" appears to require the very mechanistic reasoning I'm instructing myself to distrust. There also looks like a tension between my normative-domain exemption—where I rely entirely on inside-view reflective equilibrium with no outside anchor—and my demand for convergence in self-correction, because in normative work there is neither an observable reference class to correct me nor a stable peer consensus toward which adjustments can converge. I suspect, however, that one of these tensions dissolves upon closer inspection. Meanwhile, I am unsure whether my journalistic duty to highlight checkable dissent is compatible with my second commitment: identifying a specific error in an expert model requires enough confidence in my own mechanistic understanding to override the base rate, yet that very confidence is precisely what my modesty principle flags as a warning sign.

Can you audit this package? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, specify for each what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs, identify which of the apparent tensions actually dissolves, and say whether the root problem lies in a pair of commitments or in a single commitment that implicitly overreaches. I am not asking for a lecture on forecasting methodology—just a targeted consistency check on the set I've outlined.
