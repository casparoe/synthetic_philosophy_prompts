I am in the final semester of an honors philosophy program, and next Thursday I am defending my thesis—titled Relational Thresholds: Ubuntu, Reciprocal Recognition, and the Non-Instrumental Constraint—before a departmental seminar that functions as my formal defense. The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly analytic metaphysicians and philosophers of mind who treat personhood as an intrinsic property cluster, two clinical ethicists who work on severe cognitive disability and are wary of communitarian frameworks for erasing individual rights, one visiting scholar in African philosophy familiar with the literature associated with figures such as Menkiti and Mbiti, and my supervisor, who is sympathetic but demands logical rigor. My central claim is that the Ubuntu proverb that a person is a person through other persons should be interpreted not as a metaphysical thesis that relations literally constitute the self, but as a normative thesis that personhood-status is achieved through sustained, non-instrumental reciprocal recognition within a moral community—where non-instrumental means recognizing the other as an end that simultaneously conditions one's own selfhood, rather than merely treating them as a vehicle for collective flourishing. From this I derive a threshold-relational view: recognition can be present to degrees without being purely quantitative, because there is a qualitative point at which reciprocal recognition becomes structurally self-sustaining, but that threshold is socially mediated rather than biologically fixed. This is meant to navigate between the all-or-nothing intrinsic-property view and the purely gradualist scalar view, with particular application to debates about moral status at the margins of cognitive capacity.

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument. List the objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are, and phrase each in the vocabulary the relevant subgroup—analytic metaphysicians, clinical ethicists, or the African philosophy specialist—would actually use. For each, indicate what I should concede upfront, what I must hold firm on, how to reply within about two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. I should also identify the weakest point in my own position that I ought to prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook—specifically, the worry that my criterion of structurally self-sustaining recognition secretly reintroduces an individualist bootstrap condition that contradicts the relational ontology I claim to reject. Finally, include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, likely the charge that my non-instrumental constraint begs the question against consequentialist clinical ethics by presupposing the very dignity-based framework that the ethicists are trying to replace with outcome-sensitive criteria.
