Construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies explanatory accounts of why specific moral norms collapse, using the abolition of Atlantic chattel slavery and the decline of dueling in Western Europe as the two anchor cases. Build the tree from commitments about causal mechanisms rather than from theorists' names. The root question should separate explanations that treat the norm's end as driven primarily by a shift in the social meaning of honor and shame from those that treat it as driven by changing material interests or by the spread of rational moral arguments.

From the honor-and-shame branch, split according to whether the shift operates through intra-elite status competition—where continuing the practice becomes a marker of low status among peers—or through broader social visibility to outsiders who do not share the honor code. Then inject the following niche sub-distinction into this branch only: distinguish vertical deference honor, in which status depends on recognition by social superiors, from horizontal peer honor, in which status depends on recognition among equals. Apply this split to show how it reclassifies the decline of dueling, noting which of the two honor-types fits that case and which does not.

From the interests branch, split according to whether the dominant group's material incentives changed structurally or whether the costs of enforcement and retaliation rose relative to the benefits of maintaining the practice. From the arguments branch, split according to whether the arguments achieved independent persuasive force across social groups or whether they only gained traction once honor or interest shifts had already weakened the norm's defenders.

Each leaf should be labeled with a descriptive composite profile rather than a single author's name, though you may append in parentheses which well-known philosophical accounts—such as Appiah's argument-centered framework or Bernard Williams's thesis linking moral change to honor—approximate that profile. The tree must include at least two unoccupied logical branches: combinations of answers that no prominent account occupies. For each empty branch, diagnose whether the vacancy results from conceptual incoherence, theoretical unattractiveness, or a combination that is historically unexplored but conceptually coherent.

In addition, identify one pair of adjacent leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate using only mechanism-based questions—where distinguishing them requires importing specific historical chronology, institutional detail, or authorial intent rather than examining the explanation's structure—and flag this limitation explicitly.

The deliverable should be presentable to an informed reader who has not studied the philosophy of moral change, so every branching question must be answerable by examining the explanatory mechanism described, not by recalling a theorist's label.
