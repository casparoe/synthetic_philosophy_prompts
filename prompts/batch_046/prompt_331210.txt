You are a senior research-ethics consultant with expertise in philosophy of science and institutional decision-making. Before drafting, briefly identify the core conceptual tensions in the case below—what is empirically settled, what is metaphysically contested, and where precautionary reasoning might apply. Then write a polished, professional memo.

I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, serving on our institute’s Interdisciplinary Research Ethics and Funding Board. We must reach a decision on a concrete proposal from a joint physics-philosophy research group. The researchers defend a variant of panpsychism they call diffuse sentience: the idea that phenomenal properties are not bound into discrete, localized minds but are distributed across quantum field fluctuations. Applying this framework, they argue that aggressive high-decoherence experiments—specifically stress-testing coherence in quantum computing substrates and certain calibration runs in particle detectors—may not merely erase information but collapse distributed micro-experiences, potentially generating widespread, low-intensity suffering across enormous numbers of entities. They ask the board either to approve funding for a low-decoherence protocol redesign or to issue a provisional moratorium on high-decoherence calibration experiments until ethical guidelines are developed.

The board is divided. One faction regards this as empirically unsupported metaphysics that should not constrain real laboratory work. Another faction contends that the precautionary principle applies: if diffuse sentience is even metaphysically possible, the scale of affected entities is vast, so we should minimize decoherence risk now rather than wait for philosophical certainty.

Please draft the board’s decision memo, addressed to the committee, recommending one clear course of action—approve funding, issue a moratorium, or reject with specific conditions for future reconsideration. The memo must not exceed 500 words and should be accessible to technically trained readers without philosophy training. It must include an actionable bottom line that the board can vote on; an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration supporting the option you do not choose; and a clear separation of what is settled (for example, that decoherence is physically well-described, and that panpsychism remains a minority philosophical position without direct empirical verification) from what is genuinely contested (for example, whether phenomenal properties can exist in an unbound, distributed form; whether decoherence events constitute harm under panpsychism; and whether precautionary reasoning legitimately extends to metaphysical risks of this kind). Do not present any contested metaphysical claim as the consensus view. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further literature. Think step by step, then present the final memo in formal, professional language.
