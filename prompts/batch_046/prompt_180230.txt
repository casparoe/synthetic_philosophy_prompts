I have recently come through an extended episode of depersonalization in which cognitive activity continued while any felt sense of ownership, interiority, or first-personal “mineness” seemed entirely suspended. Reading Descartes in this state, the cogito no longer reads to me as an abstract proof of a thinking substance, but as a far more fragile claim about what survives radical doubt. I am drafting a short philosophical essay that argues Descartes does not actually establish the existence of a substantial, persistent “I”—only the occurrence of thinking. On this reading, the “I” in “I think” is an illicit grammatical insertion (the classic objection that Descartes should have said only “it thinks”), and treating it as proof of a substantial self is a category mistake that becomes practically unsettling once one has experienced the absence of such a self.

To sharpen the argument, I want to bring in the phenomenology of dissociative states: if thought can occur without any accompanying sense of being-mine, then cognition does not appear to entail an owner, and the Cartesian move from the occurrence of thinking to a thinking substance looks like an unjustified leap rather than a direct intuition. The essay then asks whether the cogito’s genuine achievement is therefore far thinner—establishing only an impersonal occurrence of thought—and whether recognizing this undermines the broader Cartesian project of grounding certainty in a substantial, enduring ego.

Before I finalize the draft, I would appreciate focused comments on the following points. First, is my reconstruction of the cogito’s logical structure fair, or does Descartes have resources in the Second Meditation—perhaps his appeal to the nature of thought as necessarily requiring a thinker—that block the reduction to an impersonal “it thinks”? Second, is it philosophically legitimate to invoke first-person phenomenological reports from depersonalization as evidence in a conceptual argument about self-reference, or does this improperly conflate psychological description with metaphysical proof? Third, does framing the argument through my recent personal experience strengthen its philosophical force, or does it risk reducing the claim to autobiographical rationalization? Finally, I am particularly interested in whether there is any structural feature of the Cartesian position—perhaps its implicit theory of substance, its distinction between thought and extension, or its account of clear and distinct perception—that my “thin” reading simply cannot reproduce and that I should address explicitly rather than overlook. I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations, reading lists, or citations; I want the argument tested strictly on its own conceptual terms, and I would value an honest note on what the essay’s framing can and cannot capture about the original position.
