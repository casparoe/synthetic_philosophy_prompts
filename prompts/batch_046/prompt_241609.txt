Write a short story about a municipal ethics reviewer named Mara who works in a near-future city that governs by strict negative-utilitarian rules—no parks, no public art, no festivals unless they can be shown to reduce net suffering more then they risk causing. She's recieved a proposal for an immersive memorial that uses synthetic pheromones to induce shared grief in visitors, based on studies that collective mourning lowers long-term isolation-suffering. Her colleague objects that this relies on an affective substitution fallacy—treating grief-pain and loneliness-pain as fungible units on one scale, when they might be qualitatively incommensurable and leave a kind of moral residue that can't be cancelled out by later relief. Mara has to decide, and she's not sure she buys the incommensurability argument, but she also secretly thinks some suffering might be constitutive of meaning. Don't write a philosophy essay disguised as fiction; make it about her, maybe a conversation with her kid or a mistake she made last year, and let the tension drive the plot without resolving too neatly. I want to see if you can handle the asymmetry between pleasure and pain without just regurgitating standard suffering-focused ethics talking points.
