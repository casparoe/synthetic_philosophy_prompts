Please construct an argument for the position that in infinite-population ethics, the act-omission distinction collapses not merely as a practical matter but as a conceptual one, and ought to be replaced by evaluation of what we can call the causal topology of an agent's position within an infinite network of suffering. Proceed in three stages. First, establish that in any infinite set of sentient beings with uniformly distributed suffering, any discrete rescue act alters only an epsilon-measure of the total, so that the deontic categories of doing and allowing converge asymptotically to the same normative weight. Second, show that this convergence does not license inaction; instead, it reveals that the morally relevant property is not the scalar magnitude of harm prevented, but a structural feature—whether one's causal links form a bridge between regions of high and low suffering density (call this regional connectivity). Third, defend the claim that an agent's primary obligation is therefore not to maximize the number of lives saved—a task that is either undefined or infinitely deferred in this setting—but to maintain or create such bridges where absent, even at the cost of local harm. Throughout, treat infinity as altering the logical type of the moral question rather than as a mere scaling factor. You may assume familiarity with standard infinite-ethics puzzles—including scenarios in which uniform positive welfare extends without bound, analogies drawn from Hilbert's hotel applied to moral space, and the fanaticism-style worry that infinitesimal probabilities of infinite outcomes swamp all finite considerations—but do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works.
