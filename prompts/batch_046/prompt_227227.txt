Write a dialogue between two constitutional theorists serving on a design committee for a transboundary river-basin authority. The authority will hold binding powers over water allocation, labor-migration licensing, and century-scale aquifer management across three sovereign jurisdictions, affecting present citizens, non-citizen agricultural workers employed under contract, and descendants who will inherit depleted or restored resources.

One theorist—call her A—defends a narrow coercion principle: the demos should include only those directly subject to the authority’s enforceable sanctions, which she interprets as criminal penalties, direct taxation, and regulatory coercion, but not contractual obligations such as work-permit conditions or employer-mediated wage rules. The other—call him B—defends a structurally modified all-affected-interests principle: anyone whose fundamental life prospects are determined by the authority’s core distributive decisions belongs to the demos, including contracted workers and, through appointed trustees, future generations who will live with the hydrological outcomes.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other’s actual arguments rather than speaking past them. In particular, have them work through this tension: the authority can bind workers through contract termination, licensing revocation, and sector-wide wage floors, yet it cannot imprison them or tax them as persons; A should argue that this places workers under market governance mediated by employers, not under democratic citizenship, while B should argue that the structural dependency created by the authority’s monopoly over water-access permits collapses the distinction between coercive and contractual subjection in practice. They should also examine whether representing future generations via trustees solves the boundary problem or instead introduces a legitimacy deficit—representation without authorization—thereby shifting rather than resolving the democratic deficit. Both characters should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. The register should be formal and appropriate to constitutional design deliberation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
