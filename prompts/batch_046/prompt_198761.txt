Compose a sestina on the theme of contingency and chaos in history — specifically the proverb that for want of a nail a shoe was lost, for want of a shoe a horse was lost, and so on to the loss of a kingdom — using the six end-words nail, shoe, horse, rider, battle, kingdom. The sestina's prescribed rotation of these line-endings (stanza one ending in 1-2-3-4-5-6; stanza two in 6-1-5-2-4-3; and so forth) should function as a structural analogy to deterministic chaos: a single initial perturbation, the missing nail, propagates through a system governed by an invariant rule, producing an apparently unpredictable sequence that is nonetheless fully determined. In the envoi, present all six words in reverse order (kingdom, battle, rider, horse, shoe, nail) arranged over three lines, but shape the closing image as a strange attractor — history's trajectory never repeats exactly, yet remains confined within a bounded region of possibility, neither dissolving into pure randomness nor converging on a single inevitable endpoint. Do not resolve the tension with a sermon about human agency or with a shrug toward the circle of life; allow the form's relentless, rule-bound repetition to hold the paradox open. The result should be precise enough that a reader acquainted with chaos theory recognizes the structural correspondence, and coherent enough that a reader without that background experiences it as a finished poem.
