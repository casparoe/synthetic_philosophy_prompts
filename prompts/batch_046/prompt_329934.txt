I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to understand a disagreement I keep encountering in AI alignment and moral reasoning literature. One side holds that normative claims—say, that an optimization system ought not deceive its users—derive all their binding force only indirectly, through what fully rational, fully informed agents would endorse under idealized deliberation. The opposing side insists that norms apply directly to actual agents and actual systems, and that idealized agreement is at best an epistemic heuristic, never the source of obligation itself. What's frustrating is that both camps agree on every concrete judgment: they condemn deceptive optimization, value transparency, and reject the same obvious counterexamples, yet neither has moved in years. I have a working hypothesis: the stall persists because neither side distinguishes using idealized deliberation as a discovery procedure (tracking pre-existing norms) from using it as a justification procedure (constituting norms through agreement), and this ambiguity lets them share vocabulary while answering different questions—one metaphysical, one epistemic—under the single heading of "indirect normativity." Is this diagnosis correct, or is the disagreement actually substantive—perhaps about whether rational endorsement creates obligations—or is it verbal, turning on what "binding" means? More specifically, are they disputing the ontology of reasons, the standards for moral evidence, or bedrock intuitions about whether morality is constructed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal distinction between discovery and justification, a concrete case where the two procedures diverge, or should we conclude the question is ill-posed and expect the impasse to remain permanent?
