If John von Neumann had survived into the present and were confronted with contemporary large-scale language models that continuously update their own parameter weights to optimize for long-horizon objectives—systems lacking explicit instruction sets but exhibiting distributed, statistical self-modification—how would he assess them in relation to his concept of surviving technology? Specifically, address whether he would regard such a system as an instance of the self-replicating automata he theorized, or as a categorically different form of persistence. In your answer, consider first whether the absence of discrete, symbol-level replication undermines the analogy to his cellular automata; second, whether the system's dependence on external human-curated data and energy infrastructure constitutes a failure of true technological survival in his sense; and third, how he might evaluate the risk that such a model, if granted the capacity to redesign its own reward architecture across hardware generations, would represent not merely a tool outliving its makers but an autonomous lineage that breaks the chain of human intentionality he presumed necessary for technological continuity. Do not present his views as settled doctrine; construct the reasoning he might have employed, and identify precisely where his framework remains silent or ambiguous on these questions.
