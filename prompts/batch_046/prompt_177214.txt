I am not an academic—just someone who has been arguing with my brother-in-law about whether voting is ever truly required. He works in logistics and holds no formal credentials, but he thinks carefully about moral obligations. Over the past year he has given me these judgments. First, in a tight national election between an honest but reckless candidate and a careful but deceptive one, he insists that one must vote for the honest reckless candidate because allowing deception to win makes the abstainer partly responsible for the result. Second, in that same race but living in a state where the outcome is already fixed, he says staying home carries no moral stain, though he votes anyway to stand with others who are affected. Third, in an election for a purely ceremonial office with no legal authority—such as an honorary city poet—he treats voting as no different from applauding at a parade: pleasant if done, meaningless if skipped. Fourth, in a primary where his preferred candidate is near one percent and the two frontrunners are both acceptable but very different, he votes for the longshot and says that voting for someone one merely tolerates is like renting out one's conscience at a discount; he also rejects strategic voting as self-betrayal. Fifth, in a referendum on a technical water-rights measure he admits he has not studied, he abstains and argues that voting out of ignorance is worse than silence because it pretends to knowledge one lacks.

Now consider this case. There is a genuinely tied election for a regional board that controls water allocation to an impoverished district. The two candidates are both gravely compromised: one openly promises to divert water for private profit, while the other will preserve a slow-acting contamination through bureaucratic neglect, framing it as conservation. There is a write-in option for a local activist he deeply respects, with no path to victory. He has also learned that every ballot in his precinct must be cast through a specific machine built by a firm he considers exploitative, and using it requires pressing a screen that displays his name publicly in a small room.

Predict what he will do. Identify the two or three rules that best explain all six positions—including this one—making sure that at least two rules agree on the first five but diverge sharply here. For each rule, state what it predicts he should do in this tied race, especially whether he should vote for one leading candidate, write in the activist, or abstain entirely, and whether the machine's origin and public display alter the calculation. Give your best guess of his actual choice with your reasoning, and explain why you prefer that interpretation. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case, different from all above, that would cleanly separate the rules you have named.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
