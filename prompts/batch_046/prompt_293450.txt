Please argue that the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism is best resolved not by treating embedded moral sentences as expressing higher-order attitudes (as the quasi-realist strategy suggests) but by denying that logical embedding requires uniform propositional contribution across contexts. Specifically, defend the view that a moral sentence in an unembedded context expresses an attitude with direct attitudinal potential, whereas in conditional or disjunctive embeddings it retains only structural attitudinal potential—that is, it functions as a placeholder preserving the speaker's commitment to a normative framework without itself expressing a discrete attitude. Your argument should address the standard objection that expressivists cannot account for valid inferences involving embedded moral claims, such as the move from the wrongness of lying to the wrongness of encouraging one's brother to lie, and should explain why treating embedded clauses as retaining structural rather than direct potential avoids the problem without collapsing into cognitivism. Keep the argument self-contained and avoid appeals to specific secondary literature.
