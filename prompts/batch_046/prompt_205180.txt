Analyze the June 2016 UK referendum on EU membership as a case study in direct-democracy theory, specifically treating it as binary preference aggregation over an undefined implementation space. I want the mapping done with discipline: identify the actual decision-makers (the electorate), the option set presented on the ballot (Remain versus Leave, with no specification of trade regime, immigration rules, or transitional terms), and the payoff structure that followed (the vote was advisory under UK constitutional convention, yet politically treated as binding; the actual withdrawal required years of Article 50 negotiation in which voters had no further input, producing outcomes—Deal, No Deal, and eventually a specific Withdrawal Agreement—that were never on the ballot).

Then apply the standard epistemic and preference-revelation justifications for direct democracy—Condorcet-style aggregation, the wisdom-of-crowds argument, and the claim that voting reveals true preferences—together with the structural critique that these justifications assume a well-defined choice set and either reversibility or fine-grained feedback. Show precisely where the theory predicts a well-formed referendum should look different: should there have been a menu of withdrawal options? Should there have been a second confirming vote once the terms were known? Does the theory have resources to address the representation gap that opened when Parliament, not the electorate, had to choose among implementation paths?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does Brexit reveal about direct-democracy theory? Is this an anomaly the theory can absorb by saying constitutional-scale, irreversible, package-deal decisions should be excluded from referendum? Or does the episode expose that aggregation theorems are being applied outside their domain—like importing a frame-problem solution into an environment where the action space was never pre-specified? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than inventing a post-hoc defense, and say whether a defender of direct democracy should treat Brexit as a misapplication of the institution or as a genuine counterexample to its epistemic claims.
