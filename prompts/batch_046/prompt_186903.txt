As a novelist attempting to render the interior logic of a mental-health tribunal, I have become preoccupied by an institutional contradiction that appears to go unremarked. Within the same legal system, a defendant may invoke psychiatric diagnosis to negate criminal responsibility—arguing that the condition destroyed the capacity for moral agency—while months later, in a civil commitment proceeding, that same diagnosis serves as grounds for coercive medication on the premise that the patient lacks insight, that is, the rational authority to refuse treatment for one's own condition. The practice therefore treats mental illness as simultaneously dissolving moral accountability in one courtroom and dissolving epistemic sovereignty over one's body in another, without ever articulating why one disorder should erase responsibility here and autonomy there. Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind, agency, and medical knowledge that permits these two institutional logics to coexist without formal contradiction. Is the underlying view a coherent medical naturalism, or does it conceal an unacknowledged moral ontology? Furthermore, articulate what Szasz's critique—that mental illness functions as a myth rather than as a disease of the brain—reveals about the hidden commitments of this dual arrangement, particularly regarding whether psychiatric language serves explanatory or rhetorical-social purposes. Distinguish strictly what the practice presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and describe which procedural elements—of both criminal and clinical hearings—would require fundamental redesign if we replaced its implicit metaphysics with a framework that interpreted severe psychological suffering as a problem in living rather than as a brain pathology requiring coercive correction.
