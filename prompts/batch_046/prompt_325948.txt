I'm attempting to systematize my judgments about reproductive genetic selection within a liberal eugenics framework, and I need to see whether they cohere or collapse into incompatible commitments. Here are my verdicts across six cases.

Case 1: A couple uses preimplantation genetic diagnosis to avoid implanting an embryo carrying a mutation for Tay-Sachs disease. Verdict: not merely permitted, but required by any adequate account of procreative beneficence.

Case 2: The same couple, with no disease risk, uses polygenic scores to select, among viable embryos, the one with the highest predicted cognitive ability within the normal range. Verdict: permissible, though failing to do so is not a moral failing.

Case 3: Deaf parents who identify with Deaf culture use selection to ensure their child will be deaf, matching family and community norms. Verdict: permissible; prohibiting this would wrongly impose a single normative conception of bodily capacity.

Case 4: Parents use germline editing to enhance a trait they call cognitive stamina — sustained attention and stress resistance — knowing the edit also produces what I will call affective compression: a narrowed emotional range in which deep grief and intense joy are both attenuated, though average subjective well-being is predicted to be higher. Verdict: impermissible, regardless of the well-being gain.

Case 5: A state offers subsidies for embryo selection against polygenic profiles associated with low socioeconomic mobility, framing the program as a structural remedy for inequality. Verdict: impermissible; the state may not direct reproductive choices toward social-engineering ends.

Case 6: Parents select against an embryo carrying a gene for left-handedness, not because of any medical concern, but to avoid anticipated minor social friction. Verdict: wrong, though the wrongness is of a different order and lesser magnitude than in Case 5.

My question is whether a single principle — perhaps one that distinguishes between preserving health, respecting cultural identity, and maintaining what I think of as phenotypic openness — can deliver all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments. Specifically: what clause of the principle is motivated by each case? What does the resulting account predict for a case I have not listed — parents selecting an embryo with a genetic profile associated with exceptionally high empathy but also with significantly elevated risk for clinical depression? And if the principle cannot accommodate all six, which verdict should I overturn, and why?

Please state the principle precisely, map each clause to the case that motivates it, render the verdict on the unlisted empathy-depression case, and identify explicitly which of my six judgments, if any, must be abandoned for coherence.
