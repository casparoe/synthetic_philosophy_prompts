hey — i'm writing a piece on collective intentionality and where the line is between actual joint action and people just happening to coordinate. i've been testing my gut on cases and the verdicts feel mostly right, but there's at least one pair that doesn't sit well together.

here's my pattern:

- two strangers push a car out of snow. no words, just a nod. joint action? yes.
- two people on a subway happen to stand up at the same stop. not joint.
- a jury spends days arguing and returns one verdict. yes.
- 20,000 voters all pick the same candidate. not joint — just aggregated individual acts.
- a flash mob of strangers who never met, rehearsed only through an app, then dance in a plaza together. wierdly, i say yes, this is joint.
- factory workers on seperate sides of a plant tightening bolts on the same car, never communicating. no — not joint, even though the car needs both.
- two scientists who've never met co-author a paper via async shared docs. yes, joint intellectual action.
- a tango pro leading a blindfolded partner who doesn't know the choreography; the partner just follows physical pressure. i think it's joint but lopsided — the pro is fully co-intending, the partner is more like an instrument being guided, yet still inside the joint act.

the tension: strangers-with-no-contact are joint in the flash mob and remote co-authors, but not in the factory. and large groups are joint (jury) or not (voters) based on... deliberation? but the flash mob had zero deliberation.

is there one principle — maybe about mutal representational control, or shared adjustability in real time — that captures all this without being ad hoc? please state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and predict what it says about a case i haven't listed: an AI and a human co-writing fiction, where the AI generates sentences and the human selects and edits.

also be honest: what argument or finding would change your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? i want to know where the cracks are.
