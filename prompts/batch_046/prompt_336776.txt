I have been invited to deliver the opening address at the formal inauguration of the Meridian Institute for Cross-Domain Inquiry, an institution that has deliberately abolished traditional departmental divisions between conceptual and empirical disciplines. The audience will be mixed—philosophers of science, neuroscientists, literary historians, university administrators, and private donors—many of whom are quietly skeptical that rigorous inquiry can survive without the old boundary between analysis and synthesis. The matter is personally urgent because only last semester I was drawn into a heated faculty dispute over whether disagreements about consciousness in artificial systems were merely semantic—resolvable by clarifying meanings—or genuinely empirical questions requiring experimental resolution. In attempting to defend the analytic side, I returned to Quine’s critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction and, more specifically, to the second dogma: the assumption that every meaningful statement can be reduced to an equivalent logical construct upon terms referring to immediate experience. I realized that this reductionist commitment does not merely affect philosophy of language; it sustains the institutional architecture that assigns philosophers the task of clarifying concepts and scientists the task of testing them.

For my remarks to be honest, I must not invoke that architecture as a comfort. Instead, I want to argue that the Institute’s erasure of boundaries is not a loss of rigor but an acknowledgment of Quine’s image of belief as a web: individual statements face the tribunal of experience not in isolation but as part of a corporate body, and we preserve or revise them—including logical and mathematical ones—on pragmatic grounds rather than according to a metaphysical division between meaning and fact. The speech should open with the faculty anecdote, then explain—without alienating jargon—why the two dogmas are intertwined and why the decision to treat a disagreement as semantic or empirical is itself a practical judgment made inside inquiry, not a preliminary classification that determines who gets to speak. Please write these remarks so that they honor the anxiety of those who fear disciplinary dissolution, avoid collapsing all inquiry into an undifferentiated whole, and close with a vision of collaboration as shared navigation of a single web, where rigor is measured by the coherence and resilience of our adjustments rather than by loyalty to a boundary that Quine showed was never there.
