Take the role of a professional philosopher of mind with particular familiarity with metaphysical debates about consciousness and conceptual analysis. I have been discussing with colleagues whether the argument associated with Chase and Sanborn—that qualia ought to be “quined” because they constitute an incoherent posit—actually succeeds in undermining phenomenal consciousness, or whether it merely applies Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment to show that qualia are dispensable to our best physical theories. One colleague insists that if the problem is genuine conceptual incoherence rather than mere ontological extravagance, then physicalists should actually welcome the result: they can reject qualia outright without having to demonstrate that subjective experience plays no indispensable explanatory role in psychology or neuroscience. I find this unconvincing. If the concept of “what it is like” is internally contradictory, then any physicalist account that attempts to preserve subjective experience by redescribing it in functional, representational, or neural terms seems to be smuggling the very incoherent structure back in under a different name. So my question is this: does the Chase-Sanborn incoherence argument genuinely assist physicalism in avoiding Quine’s ontological challenge, or does it instead reveal that physicalism cannot accommodate consciousness without falling into contradiction? Before you answer, please think step by step and carefully distinguish an objection based on conceptual coherence from one based on ontological parsimony.
