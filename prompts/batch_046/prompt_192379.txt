I'm a high school student asked to deliver the opening remarks at the dedication ceremony for our school's Center for Gradual Neuro-Replacement Studies, named for the theoretical pioneer of substrate-independent continuity, Dr. Aris Thorne. The audience is split: half are faculty in philosophy of mind and neuroscience who will recognize debates about the Ship of Theseus, personal identity over synthetic substitution, and the problem of discrete thresholds; the other half are parents, donors, and fellow students with no background in these issues. Thorne's own work is associated with a specific, somewhat niche framing—what she called the distributed-transition model—which holds that when every neural substrate is replaced by a synthetic analog over decades, personal identity is neither fully preserved as a continuous subject nor destroyed at a precise moment, but instead enters an overlapping zone in which moral obligations to the original and to the successor system are both partially valid and structurally irresolvable. I need a roughly five-minute speech that treats this idea as a real constraint on language rather than decoration. Specifically, I must avoid saying that Thorne lives on inside the synthetic architecture, and I must also avoid saying she was lost at any identifiable point, because her thesis makes both descriptions false. The remarks should still honor her legacy and offer genuine comfort to the family members present, without misleading the specialists in the room. Please write the speech.
