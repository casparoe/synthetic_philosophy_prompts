I've come around to this idea that in history, overdetermination isn't just common—it's structurally inevitible, and that means the whole "what caused X?" framework is kinda broken. There's what I'd call a causal redundancy threshold: once an event has more than, say, three genuinely sufficient causal chains, singeling out one is always a narrative choice, not a discovery. Take the fall of Rome or the printing press and the Reformation—there's always like five running in parallel. But I want to know what would actually count against this redundancy-threshold view. Not just "historians do single out causes," but what formal arguments from philosophy of science about causal selection, what historical cases where overdetermination genuinely breaks down (maybe a case where removing one factor clearly collapses the outcome?), and whether there's any result about counterfactual fragility that could hurt it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also, what would a defender have to concede—are there domains where single-cause explanations are genuinely correct, and does admitting that collapse the position? And be honest: if the view just gets qualified away every time a counterexample shows up, has it become unfalsifiable, and is that actually a problem for a claim about historical method?
