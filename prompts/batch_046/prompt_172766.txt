Is there an established name for the following argumentative pattern, which I keep encountering in discussions about whether human life is meaningful? The speaker invokes cosmic scale—the eventual heat death of the universe, the indifference of physical law, or the sheer temporal span of existence—to argue that some particular human project, such as career ambition, moral reform, or artistic legacy, is ultimately pointless. When it is objected that meaning might be local, relational, or self-creating and need not depend on cosmic endorsement, the speaker typically concedes the point: value is human-made, finite, and requires no eternal guarantee. Yet within the same exchange, without altering the subject, they revert to the cosmic perspective to undermine a different commitment or to reject an alternative account of meaning—treating the identical cosmic indifference now as a devastating objection rather than a neutral backdrop. The maneuver is not applied systematically; it operates more like a selective spotlight, illuminating certain pursuits with futility while leaving others in protective shadow.

Two concrete instances help fix the shape. First, an interlocutor dismisses long-term institutional change as absurd because in a billion years none of this will matter, yet defends intimate friendship or aesthetic experience on the ground that these are their own reward—without explaining why institutional reform cannot also be its own reward, or why cosmic time should weigh against one but not the other. Second, in debates between absurdism and secular humanism, the same person treats the absence of cosmic purpose as a fatal blow to transcendent meaning, but then treats that very absence as liberating for human-authored meaning—never clarifying whether cosmic silence is an epistemic defeat, a motivational threat, or an ontological fact that simply leaves local value untouched.

This is not quite the motte-and-bailey, since there is no stable defensible core being protected behind an indefensible extension; it is not plain equivocation on meaning, because the speaker may consistently hold that value is locally constructed; and it is not the canonical absurdist position that life is objectively meaningless yet subjectively livable, because the cosmic argument is deployed instrumentally rather than as part of a unified theory. Is there a concept in the philosophy of meaning, existentialist rhetoric, or the epistemology of scale that captures this specific oscillation between treating cosmic context as globally nullifying local value and treating it as globally neutral—irrelevant to local value—without a principled rule governing the switch? If no such concept exists, can you name and define the phenomenon in a way that distinguishes it precisely from the appeal to cosmic insignificance, the genetic fallacy applied to value, and simple rhetorical bad faith? I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving itself: what exactly the pattern is, what it is not, where its boundaries lie, and whether it represents a distinct philosophical confusion or merely a recurring rhetorical habit.
