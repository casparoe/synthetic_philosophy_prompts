Write a short dialogue between two philosophers of perception—call them A and B—who disagree about how to interpret the colour phi phenomenon through the lens of what we might call Orwellian versus Stalinesque temporal revision in conscious experience.

A defends an Orwellian model: she holds that when a subject reports seeing a dot move across a field and change colour midway, the brain does not merely leave a gap in the record; rather, present integration mechanisms retroactively revise the content of the earlier experiential frame so that the colour change is assigned to the correct spatiotemporal location within the past moment of experience. For A, this is analogous to the Ministry of Truth continuously rewriting historical archives: the past phenomenal content is not lost but actively reconstructed, and the subject’s report describes a genuinely revised prior state rather than an inferred fill-in.

B defends a Stalinesque model: he holds that the intermediate frames—say, grey or colour-neutral states—are not rewritten but airbrushed out of the phenomenal record altogether. There is no retroactive content assignment; instead, suppressive or gating mechanisms discard inconsistent intermediates, leaving a gap that is then masked by post-hoc narrative inference. For B, this is analogous to airbrushing a disgraced figure from an official photograph: the record is not falsified but selectively emptied, and any seeming smooth transition is a confabulated bridge across an absent interval.

The dialogue should be genuinely back-and-forth: each speaker must respond to the other’s specific arguments rather than talk past them. They should address at least (i) whether the subject’s report of a smooth colour transition describes a genuinely revised past experience or merely an absence concealed by inference; (ii) whether neural feedback dynamics better support active reconstruction (Orwellian) or suppressive gating (Stalinesque); and (iii) whether the two models yield different predictions in edge cases—unexpected colour changes, divided attention, or very brief presentation times—relating directly to debates about the timing and temporal extension of experience.

Neither speaker should be a strawman. Both should concede genuine weaknesses in their own position and recognise real strengths in the opponent’s. To keep the prompt away from well-worn pretraining patterns, inject one niche conceptual twist: extend the Orwell/Stalinesque distinction by asking whether a third, “Trotskyist” option is conceivable, in which the original intermediate experiential content survives in some inaccessible neural or sub-personal form (like an unedited photographic negative) but is permanently exiled from conscious report, neither rewritten nor erased but merely hidden from the official record.

Write the dialogue in polished British English. After the dialogue, include a brief postscript of no more than 150 words noting where the dialogue takes liberties with contested interpretations of the phi phenomenon, with the analogy to historical revisionism, or with empirical claims about neural dynamics; and state explicitly what argument or empirical finding would lead you to reframe the disagreement—for instance, to collapse one model into the other, to endorse the Trotskyist third way, or to abandon the revisionist framing altogether. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
