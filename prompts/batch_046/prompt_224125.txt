Two colleagues argued, after a seminar on biological self-organization, about whether DNA replication is structurally analogous to Gödel's self-referential construction in formal arithmetic. (1) She claimed the analogy is tight: the genome encodes not only structural proteins but also the ribosomes and polymerases that decode it, so the system contains a physical description of its own interpreter—much as Gödel numbering lets a formal system encode arithmetic statements about its own provability. (2) He objected that Gödel's result depends on intentional syntax—symbols representing other symbols—whereas DNA replication is pure causal chemistry with no internal semantic interpretation. (3) She replied that the genetic code is genuinely symbolic: codons stand for amino acids via adapter molecules (tRNAs) whose own structure the genome specifies, so the interpreter is physically built according to instructions embedded in the interpreted message. (4) He countered that this remains only causal feedback, not logical self-reference: DNA never produces a sequence asserting its own non-replicability, which is the heart of Gödel's diagonal argument. (5) She said requiring explicit logical negation is too narrow; the genome's encoding of its own decoding machinery is a functional analog of self-reference, and lethal mutations that disable the decoder mirror incompleteness—systems unable to guarantee their own consistency. (6) He concluded the parallel is poetic but risks reifying "code" talk into false ontology, since chemistry lacks "truth" conditions to violate.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—perhaps about whether the analogy must be literal to be illuminating, or about what genuinely distinguishes causal loops from representational ones.
