You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in political ethics, citizenship, and the morality of armed defence. I live in a small, permanently neutral country that maintains no standing professional army and belongs to no military alliance. Instead, national defence relies on a universal militia: nearly every citizen is required to keep a service rifle at home, complete periodic refresher training focused strictly on territorial defence, and remain available for call-up in an emergency. I am a software project manager with no prior military background. Until recently I treated this as a distant civic formality, but the government has now linked participation directly to ordinary civic life: anyone who misses the refresher drills without a certified exemption loses eligibility for certain public-sector contracts and faces a significant tax surcharge. At work, a pacifist colleague argued that this system is designed to erase the moral boundary between soldier and civilian, and that in a neutral state this is especially pernicious because it makes every citizen symbolically responsible for violence without giving them any real choice in the matter. I do not want to free-ride on my neighbours' willingness to protect our independence, yet I resent that my access to ordinary economic opportunities is being tied to my willingness to hold and maintain a weapon. There is something about my colleague's framing that strikes me as overly tidy, but I cannot pinpoint the error. From an ethical standpoint, is it legitimate for a neutral state to make ordinary civic and economic benefits conditional on universal militia service, and does my participation genuinely make me complicit in dissolving the distinction between those who fight and those who do not?
