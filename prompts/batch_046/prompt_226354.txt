I'm currently drafting a paper for a graduate seminar on environmental historiography, and I have come to defend a somewhat heterodox position that I want to stress-test before committing it to writing. I hold that pathogens, long-term climatic regimes, and volcanic events should not be treated as passive background conditions or occasional "triggers" that merely set the stage for genuinely human history; rather, they should be recognized as possessing a form of distributed, non-intentional agency that operates on the same explanatory plane as collective human action. I also maintain that the standard historiographical critique of environmental determinism is largely misdirected: the real peril is not the assignment of primary causal weight to non-human forces, but the use of climate, disease, or volcanic winters to excuse, erase, or render invisible the range of human adaptive, political, and institutional responses that always accompany them. Before I finalize this view, I want a rigorous map of its exposure. What specific observations from the historical record, conceptual arguments about agency and causation, or formal results from philosophy of science or metaphysics would genuinely count against it? Please distinguish the decisive defeaters from the merely awkward ones—identifying cases where treating a germ or a volcanic winter as an "actor" produces explanations that collapse back into rival frameworks, and saying what defenders of this position should themselves concede as damaging. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—whether every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by insisting that "the human response is part of the interaction, not a refutation"—and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue of conceptual sophistication or a vice that renders the position historically unproductive. Finally, is there a coherent middle ground that preserves meaningful causal primacy for human intentionality without reducing environmental forces to inert scenery, and what would have to be true for my specific framing of the determinism problem to be wrong rather than merely unfashionable?
