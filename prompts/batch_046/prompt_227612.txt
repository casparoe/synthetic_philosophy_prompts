I am working on the boundary problem in democratic theory and have come to defend an analogical criterion for membership in the demos: an entity belongs if and only if it stands in a relation of structural substitutability within the collective decision procedure—meaning it can be mapped, without loss of the relevant normative properties, onto the position of a governed party that lacks an effective exit option—regardless of whether it is a human citizen, a future generation, or a non-human agent that meets the analogical conditions. This extends the familiar all-affected-interests principle by replacing causal impact with a formal analogy to the core situation of subjection without consent. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this view, not merely the standard objections one finds in the literature. Which observations about actual democratic practices, which conceptual distinctions internal to analogical reasoning, which results in social choice theory about representation, and which normative intuitions about membership would serve as decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments? In particular, does the analogical move risk collapsing the distinction between being causally affected and being a democratic member, and are there plausible cases where structural substitutability holds but inclusion seems clearly wrong, or where it fails yet inclusion seems required? Please separate what is relatively settled in democratic theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation—whether about the legitimacy of analogical extension in normative politics or about the moral status of non-human entities—as though it were the consensus position. I ask that your reply be composed entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
