In our metaphysics and biology of identity reading group, a debate about whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg has stalled for months. One camp defends what we call gametic essentialism: your actual origin in that particular zygote, formed from that sperm and that egg, is metaphysically necessary for your being you; any counterfactual scenario with different gametes produces a different individual, even if development proceeds identically thereafter. The opposing camp defends trajectory essentialism: what makes you the particular organism you are is not the initial gametic pair but the specific, continuous developmental trajectory—including epigenetic programming, regulatory network dynamics, and morphological convergence—that unfolds from conception onward. They observe that developmental biology reveals substantial robustness to initial conditions: different starting gametes can, in principle, converge on highly similar phenotypic and neurological outcomes through compensatory mechanisms and canalization. So, they argue, you could have originated from different gametes provided the same developmental trajectory was realized, just as the same watershed can arise from different tributaries if the dynamics of flow are preserved. Both sides agree on the empirical biology—developmental plasticity exists but is bounded, and personal identity is not reducible to DNA sequence alone. Both agree that if origin is essential, it constrains counterfactual identity claims in ways that matter for ethics and law. Yet neither side has budged in over a year. Is this disagreement hidden behind shared vocabulary—perhaps "origin" means the gametic event for one side and the initiating conditions of a trajectory for the other? Or is there a substantive but obscured empirical disagreement about the actual limits of developmental convergence in human ontogeny? Might it be a disagreement about the standards for modal claims—whether metaphysical possibility tracks biological possibility, or tracks something more restrictive? Or are they simply answering different questions: one about the individuation conditions of organisms, the other about the persistence conditions of persons? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's actual structure—verbal, substantive, or bedrock—supported by what each side actually says about origin, trajectory, and identity, together with an explicit hypothesis about which diagnosis is correct. I also want an honest assessment of whether anything could move it: a conceptual distinction (for instance, between genesis conditions and sustaining conditions), a formal result about essential properties in biological kinds, an empirical finding about the uniqueness of epigenetic landscapes, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates causal origin with metaphysical essence. Please do not survey general theories of personal identity; diagnose the stall, defend your diagnosis with reasons drawn from the positions as described, and say what progress, if any, should be expected.
