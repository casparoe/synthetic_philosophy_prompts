The debate over whether Hume’s “Of the Standard of Taste” ultimately endorses a qualified objectivism about beauty, a sophisticated form of relativism, or a middle position grounded in the intersubjective authority of refined critics has been curiously static since the revival of Humean aesthetics in the late twentieth century. The same moves recur: realists cite Hume’s talk of a “standard” and the correctability of taste; anti-realists cite his insistence that disagreement often persists without fault; and a third camp treats delicacy of sentiment as an epistemic virtue that produces convergence without metaphysical objectivity. Every side can accommodate the same textual evidence—the appeal to true judges, the admission of irreducible disagreement, the analogy with color perception—so the literature feels less like an advancing argument and more like a stabilized equilibrium. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about Hume exegesis or about beauty itself, but about an unacknowledged split over the governing model of judgment: one side treats aesthetic evaluation as analogous to perceptual judgment (with error conditions, correction procedures, and a normative pull toward accuracy), while the other treats it as analogous to preference or taste (with irreducible variation that no refinement can eliminate), and they use the word “objectivity” to track success conditions in these two different frameworks. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether aesthetic properties are response-dependent in a specific metaphysical sense, or about what counts as a reason within aesthetic discourse—that explains why neither camp can concede?
