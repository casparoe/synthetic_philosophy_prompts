I have been thinking about G.E.M. Anscombe’s well-known protest against Oxford honouring Harry Truman, particularly her claim that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki amounted to murder rather than to an action that was merely unjust. Without a formal philosophical background, I am struggling to see whether her position depends on a distinction that genuinely holds up outside the historical case. Please write a dialogue between two people—one who broadly accepts Anscombe’s moral framework and one who rejects it—debating whether Truman’s decision was murder in the precise sense she intended. The conversation should turn on the difference between directing harm at non-combatants as a means and accepting harm to them as an unintended side-effect, but please inject a less familiar twist: have them apply that same distinction to a hypothetical choice between destroying an enemy weapons plant hidden inside a densely populated quarter, versus deliberately killing a small number of genuinely neutral civilians in order to break a siege and save a larger population. Both interlocutors should be equally sharp, responding directly to each other’s actual arguments rather than speaking past one another. Write the response in British English. Please also distinguish, within the framing or the exchange itself, which points are broadly settled in moral philosophy from those that depend on genuinely contested premises—especially regarding the validity of double-effect reasoning in strategic contexts—and do not present either character’s disputed commitments as though they were the consensus view.
