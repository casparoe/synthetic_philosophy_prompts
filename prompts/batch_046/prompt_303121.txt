I sit on the Curriculum and Instruction Committee of my university's College of Arts and Sciences, and next Tuesday we vote on restructuring the required "Foundations of Western Thought" survey. At present the course is divided into four chronological modules—Ancient, Medieval, Renaissance, and Early Modern—but a working group of historians and philosophers of science has proposed replacing them with thematic clusters such as "Authority and Dissent," "Nature and Mechanism," and "Empire and Its Others." They contend that labels like "Renaissance" and "Scientific Revolution" are not natural kinds but nineteenth-century historiographical constructions, that the "Middle Ages" is an especially unstable category invented largely to flatter modern self-conceptions, and that a thematic organization would better reveal conceptual continuities across conventional boundaries. The opposing faction argues that these period divisions still capture real discontinuities in institutional practice, conceptual vocabulary, and material conditions, and that abandoning them will deprive students of a shared chronological scaffold without which thematic comparison becomes arbitrary.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting—written in American English, and without requesting bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since the committee needs reasoning it can weigh on its own merits. The memo should recommend one concrete course of action—whether to adopt the thematic model, retain the period structure with revised framing, or implement a hybrid—together with a specific time frame for rollout if adopted. It should engage the anti-periodization argument with genuine intellectual charity, identifying its strongest point rather than treating it as mere disciplinary fashion, and it should include an honest account of the best consideration on the side you reject. The recommendation should be actionable for an institutional body rather than personal guidance, and it should avoid resting on obscure technical theories that only specialists in historiography of science would recognize.
