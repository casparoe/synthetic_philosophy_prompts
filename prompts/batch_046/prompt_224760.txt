I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I recently heard a popular podcast summarize what the host called Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The summary went roughly like this: to be genuinely blameworthy or praiseworthy for an action, you must be genuinely responsible for the character and motives that produced it; but you are not responsible for your original nature, which was fixed by genetics and environment before you could choose anything; and even if you later reshape yourself, that reshaping is done by the very nature you are trying to change, so any self-modification is circular rather than genuinely self-creating. From this the host concluded that true moral responsibility is impossible regardless of whether determinism holds, and that the only theoretical escape—being a causa sui, a complete cause of oneself—is obviously impossible, so our practices of blame and praise are ultimately unjustified social conventions. The argument sounds powerful, but I worry it collapses the distinction between being causally influenced and being morally undeserving. Is this a fair rendering of Strawson's Basic Argument? Please distinguish the parts that are accurate, the parts that are subtle overstatements or simplifications, and whether the host's conclusion—that blame and praise are never justified—actually follows once the summary is corrected. I'm especially interested in whether the regress applies to an agent that continuously updates its own decision weights over time, like a reinforcement-learning system that modifies its policy based on reward feedback: does that model avoid the regress, or does it just relocate the same problem one layer back?
