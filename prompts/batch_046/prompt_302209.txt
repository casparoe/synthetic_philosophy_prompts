Hey, I spent thirty years in molecular biology and now I'm poking around philosophy of mind, and I think I built a bad argument about pain asymbolia. Here it is, with probably a typo or two:

1. In pain asymbolia people report the location, heat, and sharpness of injury just fine, but they say it doesn't hurt and they don't pull away.
2. So the "badness" of pain must be a seperate affective module—maybe ACC tagging—added onto the raw sensory data.
3. If that module is missing, there's no suffering, even if tissue is being destroyed.
4. The only way I can tell you are suffering is by seeing that module expressed—wincing, saying it's awful, avoiding the source.
5. So my moral reason to stop your pain is really just to stop that tagging, not to stop the damage itself.
6. That means if I had a drug that silenced the tagging but kept the sensory relay intact, I'd be removing all harm from an injury.
7. Therefore causing physical damage plus giving the drug wouldn't be morally wrong—like cutting someone who can't recieve the badness.
8. And activating pure tagging with no injury at all would be as bad as torture.

That proves way too much, right? I'd like to keep (2) and (4) if possible—they feel right from a biology angle—but I want a definate answer, not a menu of options. Tell me exactly which step is the mistake, defend that choice, and say what I have to give up if I reject it.
