You are an experienced philosophy-of-history examiner designing a final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the core distinctions among Ranke’s archival ideal of showing the past as it actually was, Carr’s constructivist claim that historical facts are selected and shaped by present concerns, and Hayden White’s thesis that historical narratives are structured by deep linguistic tropes rather than by empirical verification alone. Then think step-by-step.

Write one 90-minute essay question that forces students to apply these three frameworks to a specific, non-standard case: a state-sponsored computer-networking initiative launched in 1973 called the National Packet Grid, which collapsed before reaching operational scale, leaving only partial technical logs, contradictory oral histories from its engineers, and a contemporary government mandate to frame the failure either as a heroic precursor to modern infrastructure or as a cautionary tale about over-centralization. The question should ask students to determine whether Rankean fidelity, Carr-style present-centered selection, or White’s tropological analysis offers the best account of how this history ought to be written, and whether the three approaches can be synthesized or are fundamentally at odds given the evidentiary gaps and institutional pressure.

Provide a model answer of roughly 700 words. The answer must not merely summarize the three thinkers but must demonstrate, with reference to the case, how each framework handles the missing logs and competing testimonies, and it must explicitly identify at least one point of direct tension—specifically, between Carr’s criterion of present relevance in selecting facts and White’s claim that such selection is already governed by prefigural narrative modes.

Then provide a marking scheme that distinguishes an A response, which names that specific Carr-White tension and shows why synthesis fails or succeeds in this case, from a B response, which accurately describes all three positions but treats them as largely compatible without confronting the contradiction. List three common errors that should cost marks: reducing Ranke to naive empiricism; equating Carr with radical relativism; and treating White’s tropes as mere stylistic ornament rather than as conditions of narrative coherence.

Keep the prompt formal, focused, and suitable for students who have studied the primary debates but must apply them to an unfamiliar scenario. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
