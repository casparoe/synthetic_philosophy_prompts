For a feature I am writing on whether Leibniz's best-world doctrine can survive contemporary philosophy of religion, a philosopher friend walked me through an argument that seems to collapse the framework by proving far more than any critic intended. I have reconstructed it with numbered steps, but I cannot locate the weak joint, and I want to know what rejecting each step costs.

(1) Global Optimality Thesis: Leibniz holds that God actualized not merely a good world, but the unique world that maximizes compossible perfection—greatest variety governed by the simplest laws—such that every constituent occupies a precise relational role in an integral equilibrium.

(2) Counterfactual Tethering (CT): in a system whose value is globally optimal rather than aggregative, altering any single constituent destroys the specific relational structure that makes the whole optimal; the altered system is therefore not merely different but strictly suboptimal.

(3) Human volitions are constituents of the actual world. So if I genuinely could have acted otherwise, there exists a possible world W' identical to ours except for my choice, and by CT, W' is strictly worse than the actual world.

(4) If God is both omniscient and benevolent, He would not embed within His masterpiece agents whose free exercises could generate worlds strictly inferior to the one He selected; that would be analogous to an architect designing a load-bearing structure that occupants are free to dismantle.

(5) Therefore either human choices lack genuine counterfactual openness—they are predetermined to preserve optimality—or God selected a world vulnerable to degradation, contradicting the claim that it is the best possible.

(6) The first horn denies our phenomenology of open deliberation; the second denies divine rationality. Both are unacceptable if the framework is to preserve its core motivations.

(7) So combining the Leibnizian thesis with CT and a robust notion of freedom yields that either freedom or divine perfection must be illusory—a conclusion that proves too much, since the doctrine was designed to reconcile them, not to annihilate one.

(8) Hence the argument does not merely revise optimality or limit freedom; it forces an abandonment of the very framework it targets.

Which step should I reject? I am inclined to defend (1) and (3), and I want to know whether the damage is done by CT itself—perhaps optimality permits local substitution without global collapse—or whether (4) smuggles in an illegitimate expectation that God must insure against every free deviation. If I retain CT, must I pay with libertarian freedom? If I reject CT, do I lose the distinctive Leibnizian idea that this world's value is structurally integral rather than additive? I need the diagnosis and the price of each alternative, not a bibliography.
