Construct a conceptual taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—that sorts positions on whether the macro-period labels “Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution” denote genuinely discontinuous historical structures or are retrospective historiographical constructions. Build the tree from the logical space of commitments rather than from named schools: begin with whether macro-period boundaries can be non-arbitrary at all; then split on whether a legitimate period requires (i) a single defining essence, (ii) a family-resemblance cluster of features without a common core, or (iii) is best treated as a purely instrumental narrative device with no referential pretension. Apply these frameworks to concrete diagnostic cases—whether the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century Italian recovery of classical texts represents a discontinuity in kind sufficient to justify “Renaissance,” and whether the seventeenth-century mathematization of nature can be traced in continuous development from late-medieval calculatory and kinematic traditions, thereby undermining the claim of a “Scientific Revolution.” Each leaf should identify the resulting stance (for example, “full essentialist about all three,” “Renaissance-only constructivist with continuous science,” “complete eliminationist”) and, in one sentence, articulate its most awkward historiographical commitment—such as having to deny that Petrarch’s self-conscious break with the immediate past was historically consequential, or having to defend that “feudalism” is a precise analytic category despite extensive regional variation. Explicitly flag any logically possible combination of answers that lacks an occupant and briefly diagnose whether its emptiness stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Phrase every node as a concrete claim assessable without prior knowledge of the taxonomy’s labels, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
