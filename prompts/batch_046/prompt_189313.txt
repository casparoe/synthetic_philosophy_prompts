I'm a retired ecologist who got into philosphy of science after I stopped working, and I'm stuck on this old fight over Friedman's F-twist — the idea that unrealistic assumptions are fine if your predictions come out right. Economists have been defending it since the 50s, philosophers keep saying it isn't real explanation, and neither camp ever seems to budge. My hunch is that the stall isn't really about realism versus instrumentalism, but about a hidden split over what I call "intervention portability" — whether the model's equations still hold when you actually manipulate the system, not just observe and calibrate at existing data points. One side might only care about predictive fit at observed values, the other wants the structure to survive counterfactual tweaking. Is that the real fault line, or are they just answering different questions under the same word "explanation"? I don't want a survey of positions. I want a diagnosis: is this verbal, is there a buried empirical disagreement about which models count as evidence, or is it just clashing intuitions about what science owes us? And be honest — could anything actually break the deadlock, like a formal result linking portability to predictive failure, or a clear case study, or should I accept that this one's just going to stay deadlocked?
