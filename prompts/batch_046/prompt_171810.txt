Please describe the most important implications of adopting a sphere-separation account of corruption—where corruption is defined not as any cross-boundary exchange of favors, but as the collapse of locally maintained distinctions between economic, political, and kinship spheres, with the wrong consisting in the dissolution of ritual or temporal boundaries rather than in reciprocity itself. Apply this framework specifically to three contexts: democratic clientelism, in which politicians distribute goods to constituents through personal networks; professional gift-giving, in which benefits are exchanged for access or preference without an explicit contract; and the design of international anti-corruption standards, which typically criminalize discrete quid pro quo transactions. For each, say whether the framework implies that some practices currently labeled corrupt are in fact legitimate institutional maintenance, whether it can distinguish corruption from ordinary social obligation without collapsing into cultural relativism, and what an anti-corruption regime built on sphere-maintenance rather than transaction-prohibition would have to change. Also identify the weakest point in this framework—the consideration or counterexample most likely to undermine it—and state clearly what argument or empirical finding would change your mind about whether it captures the core wrong of corruption or merely rationalizes it.
