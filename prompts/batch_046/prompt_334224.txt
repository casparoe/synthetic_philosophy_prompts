I'm currently taking an intermediate course in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to formalize a reflexive extension of the causal exclusion argument that applies to the reasoning process itself. The familiar version holds that physical causal closure gives every physical effect a sufficient physical cause; mental properties supervene on neural properties without being reducible to them; so if both mental state M and its neural base N cause physical behavior B, we face systematic overdetermination, which is implausible. Hence mental properties are causally excluded. I want to see whether pushing this consistently undermines the argument's own justification.

Here is the chain I sketched in my study notes:

(1) Physical causal closure: every physical event has a sufficient cause that lies entirely within the physical domain.
(2) Non-reductive physicalism: mental properties supervene on physical properties but are not reducible to them.
(3) If mental state M supervenes on neural state N, then N is sufficient for M, and N possesses all the causal powers required to produce any physical effect that M might produce.
(4) Therefore, if M causes physical behavior B and N also causes B, B is systematically overdetermined by M and N.
(5) Systematic overdetermination across all mental-physical interactions is implausible.
(6) So mental properties lack distinctive causal efficacy; they are causally excluded.
(7) Apply (6) reflexively: my present belief in premises (1) through (6) and the inferential steps I take to reach (6) are themselves mental properties.
(8) By (6), these mental properties have no causal efficacy; my acceptance of (6) is caused solely by underlying neural processes, regardless of whether the reasoning is valid.
(9) But if neural processes determine acceptance irrespective of rational merit, then (6) is not rationally justified by this argument; it is merely the causal output of neurons.
(10) Hence, if (6) is true, there is no rational basis for believing it.

This strikes me as proving too much: it doesn't just exclude mental causation but seems to defeat the justification for the exclusion itself. I am inclined to think the error lies in (8), on the grounds that causal efficacy and rational warrant might come apart, but I am unsure whether saving rational warrant concedes that reasoning is causally epiphenomenal. Could you diagnose where the chain fails? Specifically, is the mistake an equivocation between causal redundancy and normative irrelevance in step (8), or does (3) illegitimately assume that supervenience always entails causal competition rather than allowing the mental property to be relevant in proportion to its explanatory role? I would also like to know what a defender of emergence, or of an account that lets mental properties contribute without competing with their bases, would say about (3) and (4). Finally, please indicate what would change your mind: which specific consideration—such as a convincing model of downward causation that avoids overdetermination, or a demonstration that rational assessment fully survives causal epiphenomenalism—would lead you to locate the flaw in a different premise, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
