Having spent thirty years designing sensors that discriminate signal from noise near the detection limit, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind and epistemology, and I find that my old experimental intuitions keep colliding with the literature on self-knowledge. I have four judgments about mental-state knowledge that feel stable to me, yet I cannot see the principle that unifies them, and I suspect one must be sacrificed.

First, in conditions of full alertness—reading in good light, well rested—I not only know that I understand the sentence before me; I also seem able to know that I know it. The transparency here feels complete.

Second, in the gradual-threshold situations familiar from my laboratory work—temperature lowered by imperceptible increments, or a tone faded slowly into silence—there is never a precise instant at which detection ceases. Applying the same structure to feeling cold: if it were true that whenever one feels cold one is in a position to know one feels cold, there ought to be a sharp boundary between knowing and not knowing, yet there is none. So near the boundary I deny that the transparency holds: one may feel cold without being positioned to know it.

Third, consider structural self-deception about motivation. A senior researcher sincerely reports that she is pursuing a line of inquiry for its explanatory merit, yet her behavior is entirely governed by career preservation and funding incentives. Even though she has direct access to her mental life, I think she does not know her own motivation. This failure is not a matter of vague boundaries; it is systematic distortion.

Fourth, regarding the logical principle that if one knows P then one knows that one knows P—the KK principle—I find it holds in the clear alert case but fails in the self-deception case. She knows she is working on the project, but she does not know that she knows her motivation is corrupted; indeed she lacks that second-order knowledge entirely.

Can these four verdicts be captured by a single account of knowledge transparency or self-knowledge? Specifically, is there a principle that explains why transparency fails in gradual-threshold conditions, which present a sorites-type boundary problem, and also fails under systematic self-deception, which presents a causal or structural distortion, without reducing one failure to the other? Or do these two failures pull in opposite directions—one suggesting that knowledge is unreliable near vague boundaries, the other that it can be globally undermined even far from any boundary?

If you think no unified principle survives, please identify which of my four verdicts should be overturned and defend that choice. Then state the resulting principle with precision, including its clause about boundary conditions and its clause about structural distortion. Finally, apply it to a case I have not mentioned: a subject is gradually fatigued over several hours until she reaches a state in which she can no longer distinguish whether she is genuinely tired or merely bored, and she must immediately decide whether to continue running an experiment that requires sustained attention. Does she know her own mental state? Does she know that she knows?
