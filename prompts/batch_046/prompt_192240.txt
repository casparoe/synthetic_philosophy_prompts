I'm drafting a two-page proposal for a philosophy-of-history fellowship, and I'm trying to sharpen a half-formed idea I discussed recently in a seminar on nineteenth-century thought. The idea is that Nietzsche's tripartite division of history into monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes is almost always interpreted as a taxonomy of human attitudes toward the past, but it might also function as a structural description of how institutions select, preserve, and suppress records. I want to ask whether this framework can be extended—without losing its normative bite—to what I am calling procedural or algorithmic history: the automated curation, preservation, and erasure of historical traces in large-scale digital archives and in generative-model training pipelines.

The precise question is whether algorithmic curation constitutes a genuinely new mode that destabilizes Nietzsche's taxonomy, or whether it reveals that one of his three modes—most plausibly the critical—was always implicitly dependent on a technological infrastructure he underestimated. Existing scholarship either treats the three modes as psychological dispositions of individuals and cultures, or, in digital-studies literature, discusses algorithmic bias and archive decay without engaging Nietzsche's specific claim that each mode serves a distinct life-function and carries a characteristic danger. Consequently, it remains unsettled whether applying the framework to automated systems is a productive conceptual extension or a category mistake.

My approach will be conceptual reconstruction followed by a focused case study: I will first map the functional role, life-need, and risk of each Nietzschean mode from his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, then test the mapping against documented protocols for training-data filtering and archive deprecation—treating these practices not as empirical objects to be surveyed but as institutional mechanisms that perform selection, preservation, and suppression. The possible conclusions are that (1) algorithmic curation collapses the critical mode into a disguised monumental framing; (2) it operates as an unstable hybrid that requires a fourth category; or (3) automated antiquarian preservation eliminates critical capacity altogether, confirming the framework's normative urgency but undermining its descriptive neutrality. Given the semester-length scope, I will limit the case analysis to a single, well-documented filtering protocol rather than surveying digital archives broadly, and I will treat success as producing a precise conceptual map of which normative criteria survive the extension and which break. The project fails if the mapping proves purely metaphorical—if terms like monumental and antiquarian lose all determinate content when applied to automated processes—or if the textual reconstruction of Nietzsche's functional claims requires extensive philological work that exceeds the fellowship timeline.
