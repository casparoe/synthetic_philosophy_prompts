Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self ought to produce. One camp, which may be called the precedent-autonomy camp, maintains that a competent earlier self’s directive retains normative authority over a later self even when substantial cognitive change has occurred, provided some minimal psychological or biological continuity persists. The opposing camp, which may be called the present-interest camp, maintains that such authority is conditional upon the preservation of the specific evaluative and deliberative capacities that made the original directive meaningful; once these are lost or transformed beyond recognition, the directive becomes an alien imposition rather than an expression of autonomy.

The document should contain the following elements, presented in formal, academic prose of approximately 800 to 1000 words, using British English spelling and conventions, and without bibliographies, citations, or named references to particular scholars:

1. A numbered section of claims that both camps can sign, covering (a) the clinical phenomena of partial cognitive preservation—for example, in progressive neurodegenerative conditions where affective responsiveness and some preferences remain despite severe impairment of executive function and autobiographical memory; (b) the legal and institutional framework within which directives operate; and (c) the epistemic limits of predicting future mental states from present ones.

2. The precise formulations of the remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own camp would accept as accurate, with particular attention to whether continuity must be rich (including shared values and reasoning capacities) or minimal (mere biological persistence or narrative coherence) for authority to survive.

3. The cruxes: identify at least three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. These should include (i) whether a directive can legitimately contain conditional threshold clauses that specify the cognitive conditions under which it is revoked or activated; (ii) whether the burden of proof lies with those who wish to override the directive or with those who wish to enforce it; and (iii) whether partial preservation of affective states constitutes sufficient continuity to ground authority.

4. For each crux, present a concrete hypothetical clinical scenario that both camps agree is relevant and not question-begging. For each scenario, state explicitly what the precedent-autonomy camp predicts ought to be done and what the present-interest camp predicts ought to be done.

The tone should be that of a constructive, good-faith philosophical document: neither side should be caricatured, and residual disagreement should be rendered as exact as possible. Do not invent empirical studies; rely on conceptual analysis and shared clinical descriptions. You may inject one further niche framing of your own—perhaps a distinction between prospective consent and retrospective ratification, or a typology of cognitive thresholds—provided it is developed clearly within the text rather than assumed as common knowledge.
