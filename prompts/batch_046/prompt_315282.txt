I'm preparing a feature on the conceptual architecture of transitional justice and need a rigorous analytical framework applied to a canonical case. Take South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission—established in 1995 with its distinctive amnesty-for-full-disclosure mechanism, in which perpetrators of politically motivated crimes could secure individual amnesty only by making complete public disclosure, facing adversarial opposition from victims and families, and submitting to a judicial committee's evaluation. Analyze this institutional design as an attempt to solve a collective-knowledge problem through credible commitment, specifically one structured by the logic of rational irrationality: ex ante, the post-apartheid state had a compelling interest in binding itself to non-prosecution in order to extract testimony that no coercive mechanism could secure; ex post, once disclosure had been rendered, the same democratic state faced powerful retributive and legitimacy-based pressures to prosecute regardless of prior promises. Map the players precisely—the applicant, the victim-opponents, the amnesty committee, and the successor democratic government inheriting these commitments—identifying their respective options and payoff structures at the moment of promise and at the moment of execution. Then assess whether the TRC's procedural safeguards—public hearings, victim participation, judicial review, and the formal separation of amnesty from prosecution—functioned as genuine commitment devices that locked the state in, or whether they merely displaced the irrationality onto other actors (for instance, by requiring victims to bear the enforcement burden, or by permitting the state to honor amnesty in form while undermining it in practice through widespread denials and the subsequent failure to prosecute denied applicants). Be disciplined about where the framework illuminates the design and its failures—particularly regarding the asymmetry of epistemic authority between perpetrator-testifiers and victim-interpreters—and where it is silent or misleading, especially concerning the long-run democratic legitimacy of a state that trades impunity for knowledge. Finally, state what the episode reveals about the theory itself: does it demonstrate that institutional rational irrationality can be engineered away through procedural architecture, does it show that such commitments are structurally unstable in democratic transitions, or does it suggest that the framework was never intended for collective institutional agents?
