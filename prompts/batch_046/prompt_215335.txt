hey i need a classroom activity for a philosophy workshop — about 20 people, 90 minutes, mostly undergrads who know basic ontology but haven't read much sociology. the big question: are generations (boomers, millennials, gen z, etc.) real kinds with causal powers, or are they just demographic slices we reify?

i want them to feel the pull of treating cohorts as essential kinds before they notice the ground has shifted. inject this niche framing: "temporal kind essentialism" — the belief that being born in window X gives you properties that stick to the *label* even when the people inside the window change. design a structured deliberation / role-play with hidden info and round-based rules so they run into the problem themselves, not via a lecture.

specifically:
- divide them into three "policy committees" of ~6, plus 2 observers.
- each round they negotiate a budget for "Gen Z needs" based on trait cards they think describe the cohort.
- hidden mechanic: the birth-year boundary of "Gen Z" shifts by 3 years each round (1997-2012, then 2000-2015, then 2003-2018). half the people in the room now fall inside/outside, but the trait cards and budget allocations stay locked to the *label*, not the actual people present.
- they should defend allocations in round 2 using "but Gen Z is digital-native," then in round 3 realize the people they're talking about are now mostly older millennials and younger alphas, yet the funding profile hasn't budged.
- include materials (cards, boundary slips, payoff/points scheme that doesn't need real money), a timeline for 90 min, roles, and rules about who knows the boundary shift when.
- the debrief should map round 1 → naive realism about cohorts; round 2 → reification; round 3 → the ontology crash. say what will predictably go wrong the first run (probably people catch the shift too early, or the observers spoil it — tell me how to stop that).
- be honest about which feature this CANNOT reproduce: the shared historical trauma / generational consciousness part (the mannheim-style stuff). say the facilitator has to supply that in the debrief and why the game can't model it.
- no citations, no reading list, no "for further reading." keep it casual — maybe a typo or two is fine.
