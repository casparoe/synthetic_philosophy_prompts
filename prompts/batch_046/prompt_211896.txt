Question: Compare Peirce's and James's pragmatist accounts of truth, focusing on two issues: (a) whether practical success functions as a criterion of truth or only as a criterion of meaningfulness; and (b) whether truth is a property of individual beliefs or of an ideal consensus reached by an indefinite community of inquirers. In your answer, explain how each philosopher treats religious belief—specifically, whether religious hypotheses are cognitively meaningful and whether they can be rationally adopted in the absence of sufficient prior evidence. Conclude by identifying one premise in James's position that Peirce would reject, and explain why.

Student answer:
Peirce's pragmatic maxim states that to understand a concept we must consider the practical effects it has on our experience and conduct. From this, Peirce develops an account of truth: a belief is true if it is the belief that would ultimately be agreed upon by all investigators in the ideal limit of inquiry. Truth is therefore not about what works for any one person right now, but about the long-run convergence of scientific inquiry. Because of this, Peirce applies the pragmatic maxim to religious belief by arguing that religious concepts have no practical experimental consequences, so they are cognitively meaningless. This makes Peirce's view close to logical positivism.

James, by contrast, defines truth as what is verified in experience and works for us in the long run. He applies Peirce's pragmatic maxim directly to the psychology of individual belief formation: if a belief produces satisfactory practical results for the believer, it is true for that person. In his essay on the will to believe, James argues that we have a right to adopt religious beliefs even without prior evidence, provided the option is living, forced, and momentous. This shows that James rejects Peirce's fallibilism, because James allows us to hold beliefs permanently without ever submitting them to the possibility of revision by future inquiry. Therefore, James's view is essentially an individualistic application of Peirce's long-run convergence model, just compressed into the lifespan of a single believer.
