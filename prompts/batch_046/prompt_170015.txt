I am studying how biography functions as a vehicle for moral learning, particularly in contrast to hagiographic traditions that suppress vice and bodily weakness in favor of unalloyed sanctity. To that end, I am working through Sir Thomas North's 1579 translation of Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, where the archaic syntax and spelling often obscure the rhetorical logic. Here is one passage describing Lycurgus during a siege by the Clitorians:

"on a time +straightly be+sieged by the CLITORIANS, in a hard drye grounde, where no water could be founde: offered them thereupon to re+store all their lands againe that he had gotten from them, if he and all his companie dyd drincke of a fountaine that was there not farre of. The CLITORIANS dyd graunte vnto it, and peace al+so was +sworne betweene them. called all his +souldiers before him, and tolde them if there were any one amonge+st them that would refrayne from drincking, he would re+signe his kingdome to him: one in all his companie that could (or would) forbeare to drincke, they were +so +sore a thir+st. they all drancke hartely except him +self, who being the la+st that came downe, dyd no more but a litle moy+ste his mowthe without, and +so refre+shed him +self, the enemies +selues +standing by, and drancke not a droppe. had promi+sed, alledging they had not all droncke."

Can you help me parse both the language and the biographical design? Specifically, I am trying to understand whether Plutarch is constructing what might be called competitive abstention: a public contest in restraint that makes the leader's virtue visible precisely because everyone else fails. How should I read the detail that Lycurgus offers to resign his kingdom to anyone who can match him, and that not a single soldier can? Does this frame moral instruction as an aspirational gap rather than an imitable model? And does North's rendering of bodily thirst—"they were +so +sore a thir+st" and Lycurgus's minimal "moy+ste his mowthe without"—serve to ground the virtue in physical reality in a way that hagiography typically avoids? Please explain the passage's logic without appealing to secondary scholarship I could not access offline.
