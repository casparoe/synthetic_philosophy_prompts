You are an expert philosopher of language with deep familiarity with Davidson’s radical interpretation, preparing material for a podcast interview. Before drafting, spend a moment brainstorming the standard pressure points in Davidson’s principle of charity—especially where belief-attribution and meaning-attribution are usually treated as inseparable, how testimony interacts with interpretation, and what kind of rationality-failure could break belief-charity without destroying interpretation altogether.

Your task: Draft eight interview questions for an unnamed philosopher who defends an asymmetric charity thesis within Davidson’s framework. On her view, in radical interpretation we apply the principle of charity maximally to belief-contents but only weakly or instrumentally to semantic assignments, thereby reversing Davidson’s usual holism. She claims this resolves Quinean indeterminacy of translation without collapsing meaning into belief.

The questions must turn on actual content, not ask her to restate Davidson or Quine. Specifically include:
- One question on the classic “gavagai”-type case: if we charitably fix beliefs about rabbits but remain agnostic on whether “gavagai” means rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit part, what exactly constrains the semantic assignment? Provide, in parentheses, the likely reply that prior belief-fixation supplies the constraint, followed by the sharp follow-up pressing that reply.
- One question on testimony: if belief-charity is strong but meaning-charity is weak, can an interpreter ever learn from a speaker’s explicit self-correction about word-meaning, or is all testimony about meaning always pre-filtered through prior belief-attribution? Again, include in parentheses the anticipated Davidsonian interdependence reply and the follow-up that makes that reply harder to sustain.
- One question on boundary conditions: what specific kind of irrationality or cognitive failure breaks the belief-charity constraint yet still permits interpretation at all? Include the follow-up that asks whether that same failure does not also undermine the semantic assignments she wants to preserve.
- At least three further questions probing other pressure points—e.g., whether the asymmetry survives Davidson’s argument that beliefs cannot be identified without identifying meanings; how logical constants are treated under weak semantic charity; and whether the view makes radical interpretation empirically testable in a way Davidson rejected.

For each question, indicate briefly in parentheses the standard reply it anticipates and the follow-up designed to block it.

Formatting instructions: Begin the response immediately with Question 1; include no preface, no summary of Davidson’s view, and no concluding remarks. Number the questions 1 through 8. Think carefully through the logic of each exchange, then produce the final polished list.
