Write a villanelle on Chesterton's fence, composed from the viewpoint of a reformer who first sees only an obstacle to progress. Use two refrains: one expressing the impulse to tear down what blocks the way, the other expressing the Burkean humility that one should not destroy what one does not understand. Let these refrains alternate through the stanzas and converge in the final couplet. The repetition should mirror the circular return of the same argument across generations, with the rhyme scheme reinforcing the sense that justifications are inherited rather than invented. In particular, frame the fence as encoding tacit social knowledge distributed across time—no single generation holds its full rationale—so that the poem's turn lies not in the fence being kept, but in the recognition that its value is precisely this distributed opacity. The poem should be philosophically accurate: avoid both naive traditionalism and the assumption that all barriers are arbitrary, and let the form itself carry the tension between innovation and epistemic caution.
