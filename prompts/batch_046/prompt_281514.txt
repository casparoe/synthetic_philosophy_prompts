I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether a nation can be guilty of crimes committed generations before anyone now living was born. I tried to defend a reading of Karl Jaspers—his fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—against her objection that if every citizen has been replaced, the accused entity is no longer the same one that acted, so guilt cannot stick. I sketched an essay arguing that metaphysical guilt survives this “Ship of Theseus” problem not because the nation is numerically identical over time, but because it inherits an unbroken institutional obligation to acknowledge harm, functioning more like an inherited debt than like a property of a persistent substance. I worry I have blurred the line between moral guilt and institutional liability, and that my debt analogy might be a category error rather than a solution. Please read my sketch and comment specifically on whether the distinction between metaphysical and political guilt holds up here, whether the replacement objection actually defeats inherited obligation, and whether I am implicitly assuming what I need to prove about institutional continuity. If the whole framing rests on a confusion about Jaspers—especially if I am misusing “metaphysical guilt”—tell me plainly rather than trying to make the argument work.
