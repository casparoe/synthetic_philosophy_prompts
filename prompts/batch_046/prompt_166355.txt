For a graduate seminar in Renaissance political thought (14 students, 100 minutes), design a structured simulation titled “Form and Flood” that makes participants experience the ontological strangeness in Machiavelli’s pairing of fortune and virtù—not as “luck versus skill,” but as a relation in which fortune supplies only formless physical matter (materia) and virtù is the imposition of political form (forma). The group should come away feeling that an event (a flood, a camped army, a plague) has no intrinsic normative status—obstacle or opportunity—until a prince assigns it one, and that boldness is therefore not risk-tolerance but the decisive act of giving shape to the shapeless.

Set the framing narrowly: instruct participants that in this simulation, fortune never communicates hidden intentions; it delivers only raw material descriptions. Virtù, in turn, is divided not into “good” and “bad” but into two functional modes—anticipatory structure (the dikes) and aggressive re-formation (the mold)—both being ways of actualizing potential in otherwise inert matter.

Structure and rules:
- Roles: Divide into seven pairs. In each pair, one student is “Fortuna” and one is “Virtù”; two rotating observers record whether interpretation collapses into risk calculus. Fortuna receives sealed cards describing events in purely physical terms (e.g., “river water breaches the bank; two hundred outsiders arrive at the gate with no visible leader”). Fortuna must read the description aloud without evaluative language. Virtù has ninety seconds to declare the event’s assigned form—obstacle, occasion, or unformed matter—and to commit ten “virtù points” across two bins: “Dikes” (institutional preparation that fixes future form) and “Mold” (immediate intervention that reshapes present form). Pairs may not consult others.
- Incentives: After all pairs commit, reveal a hidden Outcome Matrix predetermined for each event but unknown to participants. The matrix rewards alignment between assigned form, resource mix, and the event’s “potential”: dikes succeed only if the event was assigned obstacle and prepared for; mold succeeds only if assigned occasion and acted upon. Assigning “unformed matter” without splitting resources evenly triggers a paralysis penalty (state stability drops). The pair with the highest cumulative stability over four rounds wins a small symbolic stake—choosing the seminar’s next primary text.
- Timeline: 10 minutes for role assignment and rule explanation (no lecture on Machiavelli—only the prohibition on Fortuna’s evaluation); 40 minutes for four rounds; 30 minutes for debrief; 20 minutes for meta-discussion.

The debrief should:
- Map each round onto Machiavelli’s metaphors: the raw description to fortune as privation of form; the form-assignment to virtù as actualization; the dike/mold split to the river metaphor in The Prince and to the claim that fortune favors those who seize and reshape her; the paralysis penalty to Machiavelli’s contempt for half-measures and indecision.
- Note predictable failures: participants will interrogate Fortuna for hidden codes (treating material descriptions as encrypted messages), will conflate virtù with moral caution or fairness, and will resist labeling disasters “occasions” out of ethical discomfort. The facilitator must cut off information-seeking and remind the room that in this ontology, as in Machiavelli’s, matter carries no concealed meaning.
- Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the gendered cosmology in which fortune is figured as a woman who must be beaten and coerced is structurally alluded to only in the aggressive framing of the “Mold” bin, but the simulation cannot reproduce that misogynistic metaphysics without generating unproductive discomfort; the facilitator must supply that dimension verbally in the meta-discussion. Additionally, the activity uses anachronistic abstractions, so it cannot reproduce the historical specificity of Italian city-state politics; the facilitator must explicitly connect the abstract “form-giving” to Machiavelli’s actual concern with founding and preserving states.

Finally, build the activity around a somewhat niche conceptual framing rather than the standard “fortune is random” interpretation: present virtù as an Aristotelian inversion—where Machiavelli treats fortune not as an external causal agent but as the absence of form, and virtù as the power that actualizes potential—so that participants are running into an ontological problem, not replaying a cliché about luck.
