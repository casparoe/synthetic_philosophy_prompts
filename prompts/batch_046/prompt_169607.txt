I'm taking an upper-level seminar on cross-cultural moral psychology, and we've been working through the WEIRD framework—the observation that Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic populations tend to weight individual harm and distributive fairness far more heavily than loyalty, hierarchical authority, or bodily purity, whereas many non-WEIRD groups treat those latter considerations as equally or more central to moral judgment. I tried to formalize what seems to be the standard anti-objectivist argument drawn from that literature, but my reconstruction proves far more than I intended and ends up undermining the very research program that supplies its empirical premise. I want to preserve both the empirical claim about deep cross-cultural variation and, if possible, the idea that moral objectivity requires culture-neutral arbitration; I just can't find the precise misstep that keeps the argument from collapsing into self-refutation.

Here is the argument as I laid it out for my study group: (1) If moral objectivity holds, then there must exist culture-neutral reasons capable of resolving deep moral disagreements. (2) Cross-cultural studies in folk morality show that WEIRD and non-WEIRD subjects do not merely apply shared principles differently; they disagree about which considerations—harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity—count as morally fundamental in the first place. (3) For any specific contested practice, every available reason for condemnation or endorsement draws on norms that one side already accepts and the other rejects; there is no neutral arbitration procedure that both sides must recognize. (4) Therefore, deep cross-cultural moral disagreements are rationally irresolvable. (5) If disagreements are rationally irresolvable, then there is no objective fact of the matter about who is right. (6) So moral objectivity is false, and moral claims are at best expressions of local cultural norms. (7) But apply (1) and (3)–(5) to (6) itself: some traditions affirm objectivity, others deny it, and there are no culture-neutral reasons to prefer anti-realism—any defense of (6) will invoke norms (epistemic humility, tolerance) that objectivists can reject as parochial. (8) Therefore, by the argument's own lights, (6) is not objectively true; it is merely the local belief of a WEIRD-influenced academic subculture. (9) By the same reasoning, every normative claim used to interpret the data—claims about scientific authority, about the wrongness of imposing values, or about respect for autonomy—is also just locally valid, rendering the research framework that establishes (2) normatively self-undermining.

The conclusion feels like a reductio: it does not merely deny moral objectivity but makes it impossible to coherently assert anything, including the denial, and it seems to swallow the categories we use to describe WEIRD versus non-WEIRD differences in the first place. Which numbered step should I reject? Does the flaw lie in an equivocation between rational irresolvability and the absence of an objective fact, in a hidden quantifier shift between particular first-order disputes and global meta-ethical frameworks, or in treating the claim in (6) as subject to the same conditions as ordinary moral judgments? Please explain precisely where the chain slips, why that step initially looked plausible, and what the standard philosophical responses—perhaps a realist distinction between justification and truth, or the view that meta-ethical claims are not themselves moral claims—would have to pay to block the slide. Write your response in American English.
