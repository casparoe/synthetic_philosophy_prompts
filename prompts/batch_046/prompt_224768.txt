Consider a philosopher of mind—call her Dr. V—who endorses Hofstadter’s thesis that the self is a strange loop: a distributed, self-referential symbol (‘I’) sustained by neural patterns rather than anchored in any particular substrate. Her verdicts on prior cases are as follows: she denies that a simple feedback thermostat possesses an ‘I’, despite its causal loop; she allows that a split-brain patient may host two attenuated strange loops, each bearing its own ‘I’; she denies that a perfect digital upload of a brain’s state inherits the original ‘I’, on the grounds that the loop requires continuous dynamic coupling with a body and an environment; yet she grants that a biological brain replaced neuron by neuron with synthetic equivalents retains its original loop, provided the symbolic pattern remains intact.

Now examine a novel architecture: the symbol ‘I’ is not instantiated in any single substrate—biological or artificial—but is maintained solely through real-time predictive-coding synchronisation between two spatially separated processing streams that never physically intersect. Each stream corrects the other’s prediction errors; the loop is robust, but pausing either stream causes instantaneous collapse, and neither half can reconstruct the symbol alone.

Predict Dr. V’s verdict on whether this architecture possesses an ‘I’. Then identify the two competing principles implicit in her record—pattern-preservation across substrate change versus embodied dynamic continuity—and show how each principle yields a different answer about the architecture. State which principle you think she ought to favour for consistency, and specify the minimal further case that would force her to abandon one principle in favour of the other.
