I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent considerable time thinking about whether pleasure can truly be the one thing that makes life good, because the pleasures I actually experience seem to share almost nothing in common. The satisfaction of finally grasping a logical proof, the bodily relief of warmth after being cold, and the quiet recognition of a familiar face in a crowd feel as different from one another as sound feels to a sighted person. For centuries philosophers have debated whether all pleasures contain a single uniform "pleasantness"—like one flavor added to different dishes—or whether they form only a family resemblance, held together solely by our tendency to seek and preserve them, not by any shared inner quality.

My own suspicion is that much of the stalemate comes from the two sides answering different questions under the same word. One camp seems to ask whether there is a common phenomenal texture—something identically present in every case of enjoyment—while the other asks whether there is a common functional role, such as a shared mechanism of reward learning or a shared evaluative orientation that directs us to continue an experience. If that is correct, then part of the disagreement is verbal: they are not disputing the same fact, but rather disagreeing about what needs explaining.

Yet I also wonder whether a hidden empirical layer is doing real work beneath the surface. Consider this possibility: imagine a technology that could isolate the neural-affective signature of the pleasure taken in a particular chord progression and map it onto an entirely different sensory modality—so that you felt the very same "warmth" of enjoyment, but now attached to pressure on the skin rather than to sound. If this mapping were seamless and you could not determine whether the pleasantness "belonged" to the music or to the pressure, would that demonstrate that pleasure is a single separable module applied to diverse contents? Or would it merely reveal that our vocabulary for pleasure is too coarse to track genuine differences?

So I am not asking for a verdict on hedonism. I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is the heterogeneity problem a substantive phenomenological dispute that closer introspection could resolve? Is it a conceptual dispute about what counts as a "common quality" versus a "common cause"? Or is the question ill-posed—because "pleasure" names a genuinely heterogeneous class by design, and asking what all pleasures share is analogous to asking what all games share, expecting one answer rather than a web of similarities? And if the impasse is indeed driven by an unnoticed distinction—perhaps between phenomenal texture and evaluative orientation—would making that distinction explicit dissolve the debate, split it into two separate debates, or leave it exactly where it stands?

Please do not offer a survey of historical positions. I want to know whether my hypothesis about the hidden distinction is right, whether there is a real empirical or formal question masked by the conceptual confusion, and what—if anything—would actually move the discussion forward: a conceptual clarification, a refined thought experiment like the mapping scenario I described, a result from neuroscience, or a fundamental change in the question being asked.
