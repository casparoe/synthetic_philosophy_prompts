I recently promised a dying mentor that I would preserve and defend a particular interpretation of her work on practical reason, and now that my own views have shifted substantially I am unsure whether the obligation survives both her death and my changed beliefs. When I raised this with a colleague he dismissed the hesitation with "a promise is a promise," as if the phrase itself settled the normative question. Before I present this case in a seminar on why promises bind, I need that slogan pulled apart: what are the precisely different claims it could be expressing — for instance, that promises are self-grounding speech acts, that they always generate undefeated pro tanto reasons, that they bind only within a sustaining social practice, or that breaking them necessarily wrongs the promisee? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, false, or covertly depends on a full account of promissory obligation that the slogan pretends to avoid. Then say which readings actually justify dismissing changed-circumstance objections like mine, and which ones would require me to defend exactly the kind of moral theory I am trying to examine. Please separate what is settled in the philosophy of promises from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular account — whether expectation-based, practice-based, or autonomy-based — as the consensus view.
