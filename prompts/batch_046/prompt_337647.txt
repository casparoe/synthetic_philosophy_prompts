Since I began assisting with my university’s special collections, I have been struck by an asymmetry in our retention protocols that seems to presuppose a particular philosophy of the historical record, though I cannot tell whether that philosophy is consistent within itself. Our archive preserves every official version of faculty senate minutes, every published monograph by a tenured professor, and every donated personal correspondence of deans and presidents; but we have no policy for retaining draft minutes with dissenting annotations struck through, the internal “working papers” of administrative staff, or the informal memoranda circulated among junior lecturers. At the same time, we actively solicit the private papers of prominent alumni while accepting protest flyers, union newsletters, and community organizing records only when they arrive accidentally inside donated boxes, never through targeted acquisition. The result is that the archive renders the institution’s formal authority highly legible and its internal contestation, along with the voices of subordinate staff and external critics, largely silent.

What theory of historical knowledge—or of whose testimony counts as part of the record—does this pattern of preservation and omission presuppose? Does it require the view that only intentional, finalized, authorially sanctioned documents carry evidential weight, or can it be explained by a theory about institutional liability, privacy risk, and tractability? Is the practice philosophically consistent with itself—does the preservation of leaders’ private letters depend on the same theory that justifies discarding staff working papers—or does it combine incompatible commitments about voice, authority, and the boundary between public and private? And if we replaced the implicit theory with one that treated archival silence not as a neutral absence but as an active form of historical distortion to be corrected, what specific changes to acquisition policy, retention schedules, and cataloging practice would necessarily follow?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory with care, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that rival theories could license it. In your response, state how confident you are in your reconstruction and in your consistency assessment, and single out the specific point—whether in the implicit theory, the evaluation of consistency, or the projected institutional changes—about which you are least certain.
