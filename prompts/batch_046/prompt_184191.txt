Exam question: In Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, distinguish the three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—by describing each mode’s characteristic attitude and the specific disadvantage that arises when it dominates the others. Then explain how Nietzsche believes these modes ought to be related within a healthy culture, particularly with respect to the demands of present life rather than to an abstract balance among them.

Student answer: Monumental history looks to great past achievements to prove that excellence is possible and to inspire present action. When it dominates, its disadvantage is that it can inflate the present with false grandeur and distort the past into myth, leaving people paralyzed by comparison to unattainable models. Antiquarian history cherishes and conserves the past, giving a culture continuity, roots, and a sense of belonging. If it becomes dominant, it risks a kind of critical rootlessness: by preserving everything indiscriminately, it eventually undermines the very traditions it seeks to protect, producing cynicism about whether any inherited value is genuinely binding. Critical history examines the past in order to judge and reject portions of it, freeing the present from burdensome inheritance. Its dominant danger is stagnation and mummification, because relentless judgment freezes culture in perpetual self-critique and prevents new growth from taking root. Nietzsche therefore holds that a healthy culture maintains all three modes in equal proportion, like a balanced triad, so that history remains useful without becoming destructive to the archival metabolism of living memory.
