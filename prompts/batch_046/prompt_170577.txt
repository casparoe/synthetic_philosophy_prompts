I am designing a closing exercise for my graduate seminar on theories of well-being, and I want a poem that does genuine conceptual labor rather than decorating a familiar point. Please write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—using the standard end-word rotation (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on through the six stanzas). Choose six end-words that name phenomenologically disparate pleasures: taste, proof, warmth, recall, expectation, and release. In each stanza, place these words at the line endings according to the sestina scheme, but vary their grammatical roles and sensory contexts so that the repetition highlights divergence rather than shared essence. The poem should enact the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the reader should feel that nothing except the word itself connects the gustatory pleasure of a ripe fig, the intellectual pleasure of seeing a proof close, the bodily pleasure of warmth, the mnemonic pleasure of sudden recall, the anticipatory pleasure of expectation, and the relief of release. Do not resolve the problem by implying a hidden common factor—no "all are pleasant feelings," no "all are modes of satisfaction," no phenomenological substrate revealed in the envoi. Instead, let the envoi gather all six end-words in close proximity to underscore their irreducibility. Write in American English, with precise imagery and a rhythm suitable for oral delivery in about ninety seconds.

After the poem, provide a section titled "Commentary on formal choices" of roughly 150 words. In it, identify which rotational positions do the work of separating the pleasures (for example, where a word shifts from noun to verb, or from physical to cognitive context), explain how the sestina's refusal of linear rhyme mirrors the failure of reductive analysis, and note why the envoi's gathering of terms does not constitute a synthesis. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the commentary should stand on its own reasoning.
