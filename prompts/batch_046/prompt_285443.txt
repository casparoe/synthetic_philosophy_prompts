Analyze Hungary’s transformation between 2010 and 2014 through the lens of democratic backsliding theory, but with a specific focus on whether the framework can distinguish constitutional replacement from norm erosion and whether it collapses into a partisan label when majoritarian endorsement conflicts with international assessment.

Set the stage: in 2010, Fidesz won a two-thirds parliamentary majority; in 2011 it replaced the post-communist constitution with a new Fundamental Law; it then expanded and subsequently packed the Constitutional Court, rewrote electoral rules to favor the governing party, and consolidated media and civil-society oversight bodies under partisan-aligned authorities. The EU eventually triggered Article 7 proceedings, and bodies such as the Venice Commission issued sharply critical opinions. Meanwhile, Fidesz was re-elected with supermajorities in 2014 and 2018, and domestic polling consistently showed broad popular support for the government’s constitutional vision.

Now apply the theory. Standard backsliding narratives describe a gradual, incremental degradation of democratic norms—capturing referees, tilting the playing field, and eroding checks—while maintaining a democratic facade. Map Hungary onto this sequence: which moves fit the incremental model, and which do not? Specifically, address the 2011 Fundamental Law not as a stealthy erosion but as an explicit constitutional replacement enacted through fully legal parliamentary procedures. Does backsliding theory have the conceptual resources to classify a democratically enacted new constitution as “backsliding” if it removes liberal constraints but retains competitive elections? Or does the theory implicitly presuppose a particular liberal-constitutional baseline, so that any departure from it is coded as decline regardless of democratic authorization?

Next, turn to the “who decides” problem. The Venice Commission and EU institutions labeled these moves as democratic erosion, yet Hungarian voters repeatedly ratified them at the ballot box. Analyze whether this tension reveals an epistemic gap in the concept: is “backsliding” an objective institutional diagnosis, or is it a normative judgment made by actors committed to a specific model of liberal democracy? If the theory cannot adjudicate between “the people chose illiberal democracy” and “the people were deceived by captured institutions,” does it risk functioning as a partisan label rather than an analytical tool?

Finally, let the case test the theory. Does Hungary confirm backsliding theory by showing how legal mechanisms can be weaponized? Or is it an anomaly—an instance of rapid, transparent constitutional transformation that the gradualist framework misdescribes? And what does the case reveal about whether democratic backsliding is a useful cross-national category, or whether it needs to be disaggregated into distinct phenomena—norm erosion, constitutional replacement, and competitive authoritarian consolidation—each requiring different evaluative criteria?

Keep the analysis disciplined: specify who the relevant actors are (the governing party, the constitutional court, the EU, the electorate), what counts as evidence of backsliding in each instance, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
