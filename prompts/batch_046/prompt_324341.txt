I serve on the interpretive-ethics committee of a regional public-history museum, and we must vote before the end of this month on the narrative framework for a permanent gallery about how ordinary civilians become involved in state-organized violence. The staff proposal centers two well-known explanatory models drawn from historical debates about perpetrator motivation. The first model emphasizes situational pressure, peer dynamics, authority structures, and incremental commitment: it treats participants as largely ordinary people who were swept along by group conformity and the fragmentation of responsibility. The second model emphasizes deep ideological commitment and culturally embedded hatred: it treats participants as willing actors who embraced violence because their beliefs made it feel legitimate, necessary, and even satisfying. Our committee is split almost evenly, and each side fears that adopting the other frame will either excuse perpetrators as mere cogs or collapse a complex social phenomenon into individual monstrosity.

Over the past several months I have developed a concern that does not fit neatly into either camp, despite having no formal training in history or psychology. I believe both frameworks overlook what I have come to call the interstitial moral space—the repeated, small-scale moments between receiving an order and carrying out an action where perpetrators made active choices about how to understand their own roles. Rather than being purely pushed by peers or purely driven by ideology, I think many people constructed a self-image of professional competence, administrative loyalty, or procedural correctness through bureaucratic rituals like record-keeping, role separation, and task compartmentalization. This allowed them to experience violence as a form of work rather than as compelled obedience or enthusiastic murder. I want the committee to take this idea seriously as more than a weak compromise, but I also want an honest assessment of where it fails against the stronger evidence on either side.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve one of the two staff frameworks, reject both in favor of a modified approach that incorporates my interstitial idea, or approve one framework with explicit conditions and a defined pilot period—together with a clear time frame for implementation or further review. The memo must treat the strongest version of the position you do not recommend with genuine seriousness: if you favor the situational frame, explain exactly what the ideological-commitment evidence still demands of the exhibition; if you favor the ideological frame, do the same for situational pressure; if you propose my interstitial framework, show precisely where it improves on both and where it is weakest compared to each. Write the document so that a non-specialist board could vote on it directly. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations, and do not ask reviewers to verify specific archival claims.
