I am currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on the philosophy of death and intergenerational ethics, and last week two of my classmates—let me call them Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement over Samuel Scheffler’s so-called afterlife conjecture, specifically whether human extinction is bad in ways that go beyond the direct harm of death itself. I am trying to understand where the real fault lines lie, and I would like you to serve as a professional philosopher with expertise in value theory and the philosophy of death. Before you adjudicate, please take a moment to brainstorm the central distinctions at stake—particularly between intrinsic value, instrumental value, conditions of rational agency, and psychological dependency—so that your analysis is precise rather than superficial. Then go through their exchange move by move.

Here is how the argument unfolded. (1) Maya began by saying that extinction is catastrophically bad not merely because people die, but because it retroactively undermines the meaning of present human projects. Drawing on Scheffler’s idea that much of what we value depends on the expectation that humanity continues after our deaths, she added her own twist: many of our endeavors—large-scale scientific programs, artistic traditions, institutional reforms—are essentially “dead-end projects” if the species ends, because their value is partly constituted by being links in an unfinished chain that future agents complete or carry forward. The loss is not just future goods; it is that our current efforts become rational dead ends with no audience and no telos. (2) Leo replied that this confuses the conditions for rational pursuit with the location of value. He argued that a scientist today derives meaning from the discovery itself and from the intellectual life she leads now, not from whether someone in 2200 reads her paper. He accused Maya of a teleological fallacy—treating value as if it must be realized in some future endpoint—and noted that if she were right, anyone living near the probable end of humanity should regard their life as meaningless, which is plainly absurd. (3) Maya answered that Leo was conflating “the intrinsic value of an act” with “the conditions that make it rational to undertake that act in the first place.” She conceded that writing a symphony might feel good in the moment, but the labor is only rationally justified if there is some possibility it will be heard and integrated into an ongoing tradition; extinction therefore does not just remove future listeners, it erodes the present rational basis for committing to such projects at all. (4) Leo countered that this makes the badness of extinction entirely parasitic on our contingent psychological need for legacy and continuation. If we cultivated a genuinely Stoic indifference to whether our work outlives us, Maya’s argument would collapse, which suggests she has not shown any objective, non-psychological harm in extinction—only a conflict with our current motivational structure.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the most important thing both Maya and Leo are missing—whether that is a distinction they overlook, a premise they share but should question, or a broader implication neither addresses. Keep your reasoning explicit and do not introduce bibliographic references or citation requests; I need an analysis that stands on its own conceptual merits.
