Consider an institutional practice found in certain community-based transitional justice frameworks, in which legal amnesty is not granted to an individual offender but to a designated "disrupted relational unit" encompassing the perpetrator, the victim's extended kinship network, and a community elder. Rather than serving an individual prison sentence or delivering a personal apology, the offender enters into a jointly administered custodial obligation—typically cooperative stewardship of a shared communal resource—until a local council certifies that the relational fabric has been restored. What theory of moral personhood does this arrangement presuppose? Is an Ubuntu-style relational ontology, in which personhood is constituted through interdependence and communal belonging, strictly necessary to justify the procedure, or could a liberal rights-based framework accommodate it by treating the collective custodianship as a contractual remedy? Does the practice implicitly deny that an individual can be wronged apart from damage to their social network, and is that denial built into the logic of the institution or merely a contingent feature of its design? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to align with, and indicate whether a system that recognized robust individual rights could adopt this mechanism without contradiction.
