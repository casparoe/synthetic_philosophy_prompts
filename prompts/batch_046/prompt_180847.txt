Contemporary democracies staff legislative and advisory bodies through two mechanisms that appear to pull in opposite directions: competitive elections that tend to produce career politicians with accumulated expertise, and random sortition—used for citizens' juries and deliberative mini-publics—that deliberately disregards competence and tenure. At the same time, term limits are defended as a check on entrenched power even though they systematically discard institutional memory. What implicit theory of political representation and civic capacity does this hybrid institutional architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the underlying view: does it treat representation as a skill best refined through electoral competition and long tenure, as an equal status possessed by all affected citizens regardless of knowledge, or as a statistical property of a randomly selected sample? Determine whether the practice genuinely requires one coherent theory or is philosophically underdetermined among rival accounts—for instance, an epistemic-democratic view that privileges informed judgment versus an equality-of-status view that privileges being affected. Then, apply the idea of family resemblance to the concept of representation itself: if representation lacks necessary and sufficient conditions and instead names a cluster of overlapping practices (delegation, resemblance, authorization, accountability), does that dissolve the tension between elections and sortition, or does it merely show that the hybrid system is holding together practices that share only a name? And if representation is genuinely family-resemblance-like, what would a consistent institutional design have to abandon—term limits, career tracks, or the very distinction between elected and sorted bodies?
