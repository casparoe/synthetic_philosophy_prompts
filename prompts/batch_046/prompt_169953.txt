I'm trying to determine what conditions preserve, fragment, or multiply a self, treating it not as a substance but as a center of narrative gravity. Here are my intuitive verdicts. After total retrograde amnesia, if a person rebuilds a coherent new autobiography, I think the original center is lost and a different one takes its place. In split-brain patients whose hemispheres issue contradictory first-person reports, I see two coexisting centers rather than one divided self. A corporation that maintains continuous decision-procedures, records, and public identity despite complete turnover of its members seems to possess a center of narrative gravity, though not a conscious one. If a person's memories are gradually and imperceptibly replaced by false but internally consistent ones, the center shifts smoothly without breaking. But if a subject's autobiography is rewritten overnight—so that they sincerely embrace incompatible values and life-goals—while bodily and causal continuity remain intact, I think the original center has been destroyed and replaced, not transformed. Two people who agree to merge their autobiographical records into a single joint "we" narrative, retaining private experiences but presenting one unified identity to the world: I think a single center exists, yet it is unstable and prone to fission. Is there a coherent principle—grounded in narrative coherence, causal continuity, first-person authority, or social recognition—that accommodates all of these judgments? If a simpler principle forces me to drop one, which should go, and why? Finally, apply your best account to the case I'm unsure about: an artificial system with no biological substrate that continuously generates an internally consistent, responsive, first-person autobiography of events it never actually experienced—does this constitute a center of narrative gravity?
