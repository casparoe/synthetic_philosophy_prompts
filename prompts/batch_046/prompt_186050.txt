I'm on the review board for an international history-curriculum consortium, and lately we've been getting complaints about how textbooks frame 1914 — some still push the old Fischer-style line that Germany deliberately sought a continental war and bears the moral weight, others say it was basically a system failure with no national villain. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any textbook passage that assigns causation or blame for WWI, grounded in whatever philosophy actually knows about holding a nation-state morally responsible. Not just causal contribution, but the moral part — collective agency, whether a state can "intend" war the way a person intends an action, distributed vs. collective guilt, that kind of thing.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step flow: the questions to ask in order, what counts as passing each gate, where it outputs "this framing is acceptable," "this framing crosses into unjustified national blame," or "this framing is ambiguous — send to ethics panel." It has to work for reviewers who aren't philosophers; they get about twenty minutes per passage and have to sign a form. Include the hard cases the procedure must handle — like when a passage mixes structural language ("the alliance system dragged everyone in") with agential language ("Berlin chose escalation") in the same paragraph. Then run it on three passages: a German textbook that frames 1914 as miscalculation with no special weight on Berlin; a British textbook that says German leadership deliberately sought war and bears primary moral responsibility; and a Serbian textbook that frames the assassination of Franz Ferdinand as legitimate resistance to occupation, implying Austria-Hungary's invasion was the sole true cause. One of these should come out opposite to what the reviewer probably wants. And be honest about where the procedure will misfire — for instance, when "the nation" is used ambiguously between government, military, and population, or when a passage assigns causal but not moral blame and the procedure can't tell the difference. Don't ask for citations or a bibliography — half our reviewers work offline and can't check them anyway. Also please don't lean on some ultra-obscure theory of collective intentionality that only three people have written about; stick to the standard distinctions that are actually in the literature.
