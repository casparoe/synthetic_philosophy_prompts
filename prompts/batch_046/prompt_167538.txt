So I've been workshopping this with my grad students and I think there's a hidden leak in this argument about material constitution but I can't locate it. Start with a clay statue and the lump of clay it's formed from: they share every material part at time t, but squash the statue into a ball and the artwork is destroyed while the lump just changes shape. So they're not identical -- call that (1). Now here's the twist I added: imagine over fifty years every clay particle gets slowly replaced by a biodegradable polymer replica, same weight, same museum catalog number, but zero original material remains. We'd still say it's the same statue, right? So its indentity conditions don't depend on original matter. That's (2). Now apply the same logic to a person: replace every biological cell with a synthetic functional equivalent, preserve psychological continuity and social recognition, and the person survives just like the statue. That's (3).

But here's where it proves too much: if material never matters for statue-indentity, then crushing the polymer version and 3D-printing a replacement with the same shape and catalog entry means the statue was never really destroyed -- it's practically indestructible. That conclusion is bonkers, so (1) must be false and the statue just IS the lump. But then squashing destroys both, which means there's no separate artwork being lost, and that feels wrong too. Where's the error? I'd really like to keep (1) and (3), but I need to know what I'm paying. Is their a hidden equivocation between 'being the same artwork' and 'being the same physical object' lurking in (2), or am I sliding between modal properties without noticing?
