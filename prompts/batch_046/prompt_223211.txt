Since retiring from laboratory work and turning to philosophy of science, I have been sitting in on colloquia where biologists and philosophers talk past one another. At one recent session, two speakers debated whether DNA is literally a code.

(1) The biologist argued that DNA meets the defining conditions of a code: the mapping between codons and amino acids is arbitrary, as shown by engineered organisms with orthogonal translation machinery that reassign triplets, and the information is stored in a linear symbol string decoded by separate mechanisms (ribosomes and tRNAs) rather than read through direct chemical affinity. She added that if one constructs a fully synthetic cell—using non-canonical nucleotides, read by an engineered ribosome, and functioning in a cytoplasm that never evolved naturally—to direct the synthesis of functional proteins, the genome still operates as a code, which demonstrates that the coding relation is independent of evolutionary history or physical necessity.

(2) The philosopher replied that arbitrariness and separate decoding machinery are insufficient for being a code in any philosophically substantive sense. He claimed that genuine codes require an interpreter capable of semantic error—misinterpreting a symbol—whereas DNA translation involves only causal efficacy: a mismatched tRNA either binds or it does not, and there is no “mistake” in the intentional sense. He further argued that in her synthetic-cell example, the only reason we call the artificial genome a “code” is that we, the designers, have assigned meanings to its symbols; absent our interpretive framework, the synthetic nucleotides are merely polymers with differential stability, no more a message than a crystal lattice.

(3) The biologist countered that requiring semantic error imports an unnecessary psychological criterion that would disqualify not only DNA but also computer programs executed by machines and automated Morse transmission. She proposed that the relevant distinction is between causal processes sensitive to symbol identity and those that are not: DNA translation is symbol-sensitive, and that mechanistic decoupling is all that “code” needs to denote.

(4) The philosopher concluded that symbol-sensitivity without intentional interpretation collapses the distinction between a code and any complex causal chain, and that the synthetic-cell case actually confirms his view—the “meaning” resides entirely in the designer’s manual, not in the cell itself.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then indicate which party is closer to being right about whether DNA is literally a code, identify what both are missing—particularly regarding whether a code requires an evolved interpreter, an intentional designer, or only a mechanistically decoupled symbol-to-effect mapping—and propose what kind of case, such as an autonomous synthetic replicator with no external designer and no evolutionary ancestry, would best settle the dispute.
