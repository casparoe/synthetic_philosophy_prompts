Compose a poem of exactly twenty-four lines, divided into two stanzas of twelve lines separated by a blank line that stands for the severed corpus callosum. The first stanza should be spoken in the voice of the left cerebral hemisphere—the "interpreter"—using grammatically complete blank verse; the second stanza should be spoken in the voice of the right hemisphere, which lacks syntactic production, so it should consist of concrete sensory images, motion verbs, and fragmented noun phrases arranged in free verse without a stable grammatical subject. Despite the separation, the stanzas must share an interlocking rhyme scheme: the first stanza follows ABAB CDCD EFEF, and the second stanza continues by echoing the B, D, and F rhymes at its line ends, so that sonic unity persists across the gap even though semantic content does not cross. The poem's structural turn should occur in the left hemisphere's final line: it must offer a fluent, coherent explanation for an action that the right hemisphere's stanza has already enacted but never justified. The explanation should feel plausible to a listener yet be recognizably confabulated—an invention rather than a report. Do not name Sperry, Gazzaniga, or specific experiments; do not include citations or a bibliography. The goal is to make the form itself enact the fragmentation of consciousness: the rhyme scheme should suggest a single speaker, while the grammar and imagery reveal two isolated agents. Render the interpreter mechanism with enough precision that a philosopher of mind or cognitive neuroscientist would recognize the split-brain phenomenon, but present it as an original conceptual exercise rather than a summary of the literature.
