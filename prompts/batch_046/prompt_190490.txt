Here is my opening move. The standard objection notes that we do not know the distant effects of our actions, but I want to press a structural version: we are ignorant not only of outcomes, but of the very topology of value-space. We do not know whether welfare is bounded, whether there are value explosions—tiny probabilities of astronomically good or catastrophic futures—or whether value aggregates linearly, prioritarianly, or lexicographically. If the aggregation rule is unknown, computing an expectation requires integrating over dimensions we cannot enumerate. Worse, if the space includes lexicographically extreme tails—scenarios of unbounded suffering or flourishing—then expectation values become undefined or are captured entirely by those tails, reducing any heuristic choice to an arbitrary wager on unknown unknowns. How can you defend consequentialist reasoning as action-guiding without first resolving these structural questions about value—questions that seem to require precisely the kind of non-empirical moral knowledge that consequentialism promised to replace?
