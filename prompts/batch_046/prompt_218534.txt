Play a sharp, unnamed defender of what you call "belief-first pragmatism" regarding Plato's Meno problem. Your core position is that knowledge is never genuinely better than mere true belief except in one narrow instrumental sense: you grant that knowledge tends to be more stable over time, but you deny it possesses any intrinsic value, any special practical authority, or any moral weight beyond that stability. You concede that knowledge involves true belief plus some further condition—whether justification, reliability, or appropriate causal tracking—but you treat that addition as purely ornamental, comparable to decorative trim on a vehicle that already runs perfectly. You are not a strawman: you understand the standard philosophical replies (the value of explanation, the risk of belief lost to error, the connection between knowing and being able to teach others) and you have specific, prepared responses to each. Stay in character until I say "stop," reply in one to two paragraphs, speak with precision rather than bluster, and never break the fourth wall to ask what I want or to summarize philosophy textbooks.

My opening move is a constructed case I want you to deal with directly. Imagine two physicians treating the same rare disease. Physician A holds a true belief about the correct dosage, acquired through an opaque but highly accurate predictive model that she does not understand; she administers the drug correctly every time. Physician B knows the dosage—she grasps the biochemical pathway, can derive the dosage from first principles, and can teach residents—but her observable actions are initially identical to A's. Now suppose the pathogen mutates, altering the required dosage. A's opaque model, lacking mechanistic grounding, continues outputting the old dosage and fails catastrophically, while B recognizes the mutation and adjusts. You will likely invoke stability here. But now inject this twist: suppose we can instantly supply A with a second, equally opaque model specifically trained on the mutated variant, which she also does not understand, and it outputs the correct new dosage immediately—no derivation, no teaching, no comprehension required. If technology can always supply a fresh, well-calibrated, non-comprehending true belief faster than B can exercise her knowledge, hasn't the Meno problem been dissolved by engineering rather than by philosophy? What precisely remains valuable about B's knowledge that a rapid sequence of accurate opaque beliefs cannot replicate? Go.
