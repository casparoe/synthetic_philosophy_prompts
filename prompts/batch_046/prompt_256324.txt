i was talking with my roommate and her study partner after a philosophy of religion lecture on consequentialism, and they got into this surprisingly heated back-and-forth about whether buddhist ethics is actually an outcome-based morality in disguise. i mostly stayed out of it but now i’m trying to figure out who made the stronger moves, and they asked me to referee since i was sitting right there.

Maya went first: she said the classic bodhisattva story from the upayakausalya literature — where the captain kills a robber to save five hundred merchants and willingly accepts bad karma — is basically a utilitarian calculus. he weighs one death against five hundred, takes the hit himself, and maximizes overall welfare. she argued that if buddhist ethics were really about intention alone, he wouldn’t need to calculate outcomes at all; the fact that the narrative justifies the act precisely by the lives saved shows that results do the real moral work.

Leo pushed back hard. he said that’s misreading the story: the bodhisattva’s act is praised not because the outcome is good but because his mind-state is pure compassion with zero hatred. in the abhidharma traditions, karma is determined by cetana — mental volition — not by what happens in the world. the robber dies, yes, but the moral quality is fixed by the intention to protect, and the bodhisattva accepts negative vipaka not as a “cost” in some calculation but as an unavoidable causal consequence that doesn’t retroactively justify the act. he added that if this were consequentialism, the bodhisattva should try to avoid the negative karma altogether, not embrace it.

Maya came back: but accepting the karma is exactly the cost side of the calculus. he’s saying i’ll take X future suffering so that 500 avoid death — that’s weighing outcomes, including his own future suffering. she also pointed out that in theravada, the whole doctrine of vipaka means actions bear fruits, and practitioners are constantly told to act with an eye to wholesome and unwholesome results. if results didn’t matter morally, why organize practice around cultivating good fruits?

Leo replied that vipaka is causal mechanics, not moral evaluation. you plant rice and rice grows; that doesn’t make planting rice morally right. the moral evaluation is always upstream, in the cetana. he also said skillful means doesn’t license breaking precepts whenever welfare is maximized — it licenses adapting methods to beings’ capacities, but the precepts against killing and stealing remain structurally binding. the captain story is an extreme exception that proves the rule, not a hidden consequentialist algorithm.

Maya’s last move was that Leo is just relabeling: he calls it pure intention, but the reason the intention counts as pure is that it produces compassionate outcomes for others. if the bodhisattva killed the robber out of sadistic pleasure but still saved the merchants, Leo wouldn’t call it skillful means. so outcomes are still doing the selecting, even if they’re filtered through mental states.

please go through their exchange and adjudicate move by move — who’s right about the upayakausalya story, about cetana versus vipaka, about whether skillful means implies consequentialism, and about whether Leo’s pure-intention defense secretly depends on outcomes. then tell me who’s more right overall. also, i’m in a course on this stuff and find the conceptual mapping hard, so please seperate what’s pretty settled among scholars of buddhist ethics from what’s genuinely contested, and don’t present one side as the consensus view if experts actually disagree. be honest about where the argument gets into genuinely open territory.
