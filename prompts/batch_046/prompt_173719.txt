I'm running a grad seminar on Strawson's reactive attitides and moral responsibility, and one student sent me an argument that seems to blow up the whole view. (1) For Strawson, being a fitting target of reactive attitudes like resentment or gratitude is sufficient for being morally responsible. (2) We now deploy deterministic risk-assessment algorithims in sentencing and welfare that causally fix outcomes with no room for choice. (3) People genuinely feel resentment towards these systems when they deny benefits or extend sentences. (4) So by (1) the algorithms themselves are morally responsible agents. (5) But algorithms lack consciousness, reciprocal recognition, and the capacity for the participant stance, so attributing responsibility to them is absurd. (6) Strawson refuses to restrict responsibility by metaphysical criteria like free will or consciousness, since that just restarts the old determinism debate. (7) Therefore either he accepts that algorithms are responsible, or he smuggles in hidden metaphysical conditions he officially rejects. (8) Hence the reactive-attitude account proves too much.

Which step should I tell them to drop? I want them to explain why that step looked fine, and then price the alternatives: what does a pure Strawsonian have to pay if they keep (1) but reject (3), what does a revisionist who allows metaphysical filters pay if they reject (6), and what does someone who denies (3) — saying we only resent the designers — have to concede about institutional responsibility? Please keep the settled bits seperate from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present one camp's view as the consensus.
