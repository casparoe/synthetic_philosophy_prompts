I'm the chief assessment auditor for a national secondary-school history examination board. Each examination cycle I must review approximately forty-five proposed essay and source-based questions that ask candidates to evaluate past actors, institutions, or policies—often implicitly judging them as just, progressive, oppressive, or effective. I have fifteen minutes per question, no access to specialist historiographical texts during review, and must defend any rejection to a non-specialist appeals panel that includes teachers but no philosophers of history. The stakes are high: an improperly presentist question can invalidate a marking scheme and trigger widespread candidate appeals. I need a reusable audit procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes—that I can apply repeatedly to determine whether a question illegitimately imposes present standards on the past, or whether it applies evaluative criteria in a historically defensible manner. The procedure should distinguish three specific failure modes of presentism: conceptual anachronism (deploying categories foreign to the actors' own framework), normative anachronism (applying post-dating moral or political values without justification), and metric anachronism (using evaluative measurements—such as equality indices or rights-based metrics—whose operational definitions depend on modern institutional contexts). At each node, provide a quick linguistic or logical indicator I can check without specialist knowledge; clearly flag where judgement is unavoidable and cannot be proceduralised; and specify what kinds of dispute the audit is not designed to settle. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical questions: (a) asking whether a seventeenth-century representative assembly was "democratic"; (b) asking whether an abolitionist campaign was "morally justified" using ethical commitments explicitly documented in the actors' own writings; and (c) asking candidates to condemn a nineteenth-century factory proprietor for failing to provide gender-parity workplace policies—a case where I expect the procedure to flag the question as problematically presentist, though I personally believe the condemnation is warranted and will find that verdict unwelcome. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—particularly regarding comparative moral learning across periods—and please say how confident you are in the recommendations you make, singling out the specific point about which you are least certain. Use British English, avoid requests for bibliographies or citations, and do not rely on obscure theoretical references that fall outside standard training data.
