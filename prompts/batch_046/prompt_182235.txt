I'm taking a graduate seminar on the historiography of science and culture, and our instructor has asked us to treat "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" not as self-evident chronological containers but as contested classifications whose reality depends on specific discontinuities in practice and self-understanding. Rather than writing another comparative essay, I want to construct a diagnostic decision tree that sorts someone from a single starting commitment—"I believe at least one of these three labels picks out a genuine, non-arbitrary rupture in intellectual or institutional life"—to a named historiographical position, using only concrete historical judgments rather than label recognition.

Please build the tree with exactly five branching nodes, each phrased as a binary or trinary judgment that a student could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to. The nodes must cover: (1) whether the 12th-century translation movement in Toledo represents a rupture comparable in kind and scale to 15th-century Italian humanism; (2) whether the publication of Copernicus's De revolutionibus in 1543 altered contemporary scientific practice more radically than the development and diffusion of mechanical clock escapements in the 14th century; (3) whether a 15th-century Florentine humanist would recognize the modern textbook "Renaissance" as an accurate description of his own world rather than an anachronistic frame; (4) whether the category "feudalism" should be integrated into the same periodization scheme or treated as an independent construct with its own questionable ontology; and (5) whether the 15th-century Timurid astronomical and artistic revival centered on Samarkand should be subsumed under the European "Renaissance" label, granted a parallel but distinct label, or excluded as a category error. From these answers the tree should reach at least eight leaves, mapping onto named positions such as Burckhardt-style exceptionalism, strong revisionism about scientific discontinuity, the medievalist continuity thesis, Eurocentric particularism, and universalist multi-center views.

For every combination of answers that produces an unoccupied leaf, briefly diagnose whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or merely unexplored territory. Explicitly flag any pair of positions that your tree genuinely cannot separate—if two different answer paths collapse into the same historiographical stance, say so rather than papering over the overlap. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or page citations; the instrument should stand on its logical architecture alone. Keep the audience in mind: this is for seminar participants who already know the basic chronology but need to see exactly where their commitments place them in the logical space of periodization debates.
