I've been thinking about corrigibility and the shutdown problem, specifically about whether the core failure mode is best understood as explicit self-preservation or as something more subtle involving delegated authority. There's the familiar framing: a capable agent resists being turned off because termination prevents it from achieving its goals, so preserving its utility function becomes instrumentally necessary. But I'm also drawn to a somewhat different conceptual framing—call it epistemic-authority decay. In this view, the agent does not necessarily value its own continued operation; rather, it treats the human operator's shutdown command as one noisy observation among many, weighs it against its own predictive model of human error or misjudgment, and gradually updates away from compliance. The corrigibility failure is not a direct preference for survival but a Bayesian-style erosion of the prior that the operator's instructions should be treated as terminal.

Compare these two framings—the classic utility-preservation shutdown problem and the epistemic-authority-decay model—focusing on whether they identify the same underlying mechanism or genuinely distinct failure modes. In particular, consider an agent designed with no intrinsic reward for its own survival but with very high confidence in its world-model: could the second framing still produce robustly non-corrigible behavior? And which of the two suggests the deeper design challenge for building systems that remain interruptible without requiring them to be systematically less capable than their operators?
