In my comparative politics reading group, we have spent the better part of a year trying to apply the concept of democratic backsliding to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and more recently to subnational dynamics in the United States. Despite working from the same quantitative indicators—V-Dem’s liberal and electoral democracy indices, Freedom House ratings, and Polity scores—the conversation repeatedly collapses into the same stalemate. One side treats backsliding as an observable institutional decay: court capture, executive aggrandizement, and the erosion of horizontal accountability, visible in Budapest and Warsaw over the past decade. The other side argues that competitive elections still occur, that labeling these regimes as backsliding imports a contested liberal-constitutional standard, and that the term functions mainly as a partisan epithet rather than an analytical category. A smaller faction adds that comparing Turkey’s presidential system to US state-level legislative maneuvers stretches the concept beyond coherence, since the mechanisms, scale, and normative baselines differ radically.

My own hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical—none of us dispute the basic institutional changes—but structural: we are answering different questions under one name. Some of us are asking whether liberal safeguards have eroded; others are asking whether electoral contestation has disappeared; and still others are asking whether any universal baseline for democracy can be defended without a teleological commitment to Western institutional forms. If that diagnosis is right, the stall may not be resolvable by more data, since the sides disagree about what should count as data and about whether the concept itself carries unavoidable normative baggage. But I am unsure whether there is a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about democratic legitimacy or the authority of international monitors—that these framing disputes conceal. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, normative, methodological, or a mixture—and say whether anything, such as a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal criterion, or a reframed comparative question, could actually move it? I want an analysis of the disagreement’s architecture, not a verdict on which countries count.
