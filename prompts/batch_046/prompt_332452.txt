I just came away from a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether biological “function” is fundamentally an evolutionary notion or merely a causal-role notion, and we kept returning to an institutional pattern that seems to straddle both accounts without ever acknowledging the tension. In clinical genetics and agricultural biotechnology regulation, a gene variant or an engineered edit is routinely sorted into either “restoring function” or “disrupting or conferring novel function,” and that sorting carries enormous institutional weight: it determines whether a therapy qualifies for expedited review or whether a crop falls under lighter regulatory oversight. The criterion for “restoration,” however, is almost always evolutionary conservation—whether the edited or wild-type sequence matches the ancestral state across related species—while the parallel clinical determination of whether a variant is “pathogenic” appeals almost entirely to its current causal contribution to physiological homeostasis, with evolutionary history treated as medically irrelevant. At the same time, synthetic-biology registries standardize and score biological parts purely by their causal performance in a chassis organism, requiring no evolutionary pedigree whatsoever, yet patent applications for those very parts frequently invoke their “natural occurrence” and evolutionary origin both to establish prior art and to resist broad patentability claims.

What theory or theories of biological function does this triad—regulatory classification, clinical assessment, and synthetic-biology cataloguing coupled with patent framing—collectively presuppose? Does the regulatory preference for evolutionary conservation in defining “restoration” strictly commit those institutions to an etiological account, or could a sophisticated causal-role theorist reconstruct the same classification by treating evolutionary stability as merely a heuristic for present causal reliability? Is the clinical side genuinely free of evolutionary commitments, or does it smuggle them in through background norms about what counts as a “normal” causal role? And if these institutions were forced to adopt a unified account—whether purely etiological or purely causal-role—what exactly would have to change? In particular, if evolutionary conservation were dropped as the regulatory benchmark for “restoration,” could the distinction between restorative and novel editing survive on purely causal-role grounds, or would the regulatory architecture collapse into a single continuum of phenotypic effect? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several theories might license it, and tell me what a consistently unified institutional practice would look like.
