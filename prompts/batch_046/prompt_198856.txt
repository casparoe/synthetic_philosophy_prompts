I am attempting to apply Fernand Braudel's tripartite schema of historical time—the longue durée of geography and climate, the intermediate rhythms of social and economic structures, and the rapid time of individual events—to a study of seventeenth-century Mediterranean trade collapse. Braudel presents these as nested temporalities, but he never clarifies whether the slower layers function as genuine causal constraints or merely as descriptive background conditions. To avoid both geographical determinism and the opposite reduction of climate to scenery, I have been working with an idea I call scale-weighted explanatory calculus: assigning differential explanatory shares to each temporal layer—say, assigning a majority share to long-term climatic cooling, a moderate share to mercantile structural shifts, and a residual share to specific diplomatic decisions—before combining them into a single account. My difficulty is that Braudel describes the longue durée as not being a cause in the same sense as an event, yet he also treats it as structurally determinative. This seems to require some translation mechanism—perhaps analogous to an economic discount rate—that converts long-duration conditions into quantifiable explanatory weight. If no such mechanism exists, is the three-layer model merely a taxonomic device with no actual explanatory force? More specifically, if one rejects the calculus I propose, on what grounds can one maintain that geography operates as a constraining base rather than as an inert setting?
