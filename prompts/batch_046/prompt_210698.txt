I am a doctoral student in formal epistemology working on the interaction between accuracy-first norms and diachronic self-correction, and in my reading group a colleague has pressed an argument that I think contains a hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation between object-level and meta-level accuracy, or an illicit quantifier shift in an infinite regress—but I have been unable to locate the precise fault line. The argument is meant to show that accuracy-first epistemology, taken seriously, collapses into global suspension of judgment. It runs as follows.

(1) On an accuracy-first view, rational belief is governed solely by the maximization of expected epistemic utility, standardly measured by a proper scoring rule (e.g., Brier score or logarithmic score) applied to one's total evidence.

(2) If an agent acquires strong evidence E that her reasoning process for propositions of type T is systematically unreliable—say, prone to framing effects or base-rate neglect—then for any particular p of type T, her expected accuracy for her current credence c(p) drops sharply, because E undermines the evidential link that justified c(p).

(3) Therefore, upon recognizing E, accuracy-first rationality requires her to revise c(p) downward, replacing it with a new credence c'(p) that reflects the discounted weight of her first-order reasoning.

(4) But c'(p) is itself a belief formed by the same reasoning process that E indicts; hence E applies with equal force to c'(p), generating a second-order revision to c''(p), and so on.

(5) The only fixed point of this infinite revision sequence is a state in which the agent assigns no determinate credence to any proposition of type T—i.e., global suspension of judgment across that domain.

(6) Global suspension, however, is not a belief state that can be scored for accuracy (it yields no predictions about the world), so it functions not as a rational terminus but as an epistemic black hole that absorbs all substantive cognition.

(7) Consequently, any accuracy-first agent who takes seriously the possibility of her own cognitive failure—which the view should encourage, since recognizing error is the primary mechanism for improving accuracy—is rationally compelled to abandon all first-order beliefs about T.

(8) Since this result extends to virtually every non-trivial domain (moral, scientific, philosophical), accuracy-first epistemology is self-defeating as a normative framework: it mandates the very epistemic paralysis it was designed to prevent.

My colleague concludes that we must reject the accuracy-first framework entirely. I want to resist that conclusion—particularly, I want to preserve (1) and (7), since I think self-correction is central to rational belief—so I am looking for the weakest link in the chain. Which specific step contains the flaw? Is it an illegitimate projection from process-level unreliability to object-level credence revision? Does the argument conflate the accuracy of a belief state with the accuracy of the reasoning that produced it? Or is there a scope ambiguity in how the scoring rule applies across orders of belief? Please identify the step you reject, explain why it initially looks plausible, and then price the alternatives: what must an accuracy-first theorist pay to block the regress without abandoning the core commitment that rational belief aims at truth?
