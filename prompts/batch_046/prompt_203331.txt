In philosophy of physics, the debate between spacetime substantivalism and relationism—revived in modern form by Newton's bucket argument and reshaped by the dynamical metric of general relativity—has reached a peculiar stalemate. Both sides accept Einstein's field equations, agree that there is no fixed background spacetime, and concede that the bucket's concave water surface can be modeled within the same formalism whether one posits an independent manifold or treats the metric as encoding purely relational structures among bodies and fields. The substantivalists hold that the manifold remains an indispensable substantial entity that carries the metric and explains why accelerated motion produces real physical effects—such as the curvature of the water—rather than merely describing correlations with distant matter. The relationists counter that the metric field itself supplies all spatiotemporal relations, that invoking an invisible container violates parsimony, and that Newton's bucket demonstrates only relative rotation, not rotation with respect to an unobservable substrate. Despite this shared formal ground, neither camp has moved in decades.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical but concerns hidden criteria for ontological commitment: one side treats indispensability in our best physical theory as sufficient for existence, while the other demands either causal efficacy or direct observational accessibility, and neither side can argue the other out of that standard. Yet I am uncertain whether this diagnosis is right. Could the stall instead hide a formal disagreement—perhaps over whether a fully manifold-free formulation of quantum gravity is conceptually possible, or whether structural realism about the metric already collapses the distinction? Or are the sides answering different questions—one asking what exists, the other asking what explains inertia—while using the word "space" for both? To sharpen the point, consider a hypothetical extension: a rotating bucket containing water in a quantum superposition of rotational velocities, entangled with an external angular-momentum probe, with no classical reference frame present. The substantivalist might claim that a definite rotational outcome requires a substantial spacetime frame to resolve the superposition; the relationist might claim the correlation is purely relational between water and probe, rendering the container unnecessary even in the quantum regime. They agree on the quantum mechanics and on the absence of a background metric, yet they diverge on what the case shows about ontology.

Please diagnose the underlying disagreement: is it verbal, about the meaning of "substance"; substantive, about standards of explanation or ontological commitment; a hidden empirical question awaiting a result from quantum gravity; or a sign that the original question is ill-posed? Support your diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says about the bucket and about dynamical metrics. And tell me what, given that diagnosis, would actually count as progress—whether a theorem showing full dispensability of the manifold, a conceptual distinction between structural persistence and substantial existence, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected.
