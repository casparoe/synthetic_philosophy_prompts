I've been arguing on a rationalist forum about whether the presumptuous philosopher objection really dissolves once you accept SSA, and I want to settle it with a rigorous exam problem for my upper-level philosophy of probability seminar. I'm writing the take-home final, and I'm tired of essays that just ask students to explain Bostrom's distinction between SSA and SIA—those answers are pure regurgitation and don't separate the students who understand indexical reasoning from those who have memorized the labels.

So I want one essay question, about 800 words expected, worth 40 marks, with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question should present the following specific scenario—call it the Vast Posthuman Model—and ask students to work through it: suppose cosmological reasoning gives us two competing models, M1 (our civilization is typical, with roughly 10^10 observers) and M2 (99.9% of observer-moments exist in civilizations with more than 10^40 observers). A student named Alex applies SSA with the reference class of all observer-moments within the model being evaluated and concludes that finding ourselves in a small civilization strongly disfavors M2. Another student, Blake, applies SIA and argues that M2 is actually favored because it predicts more observers overall. The exam should ask students to do four specific things: (1) identify the precise structural error in Alex's application of SSA—specifically, why selecting the reference class as observer-moments within the model violates the self-sampling constraint or generates a self-undermining loop; (2) explain why the presumptuous philosopher objection is not merely about being surprised at our smallness, but about the evidential asymmetry between counting observers inside a model versus using observer counts to adjudicate between models; (3) evaluate whether Blake's SIA reasoning actually resolves the asymmetry or merely pushes the indexical problem back into the prior over cosmological models, with explicit attention to whether SIA treats more observers as a likelihood multiplier or as an indexical weighting; and (4) propose a coherent reference class that avoids both errors, or argue that no such class exists, and defend whether this undermines anthropic reasoning altogether.

The model answer should be around 600 words and must contain specific philosophical moves: distinguishing reference-class selection from prior assignment; noting that SSA applies within a fixed model but not across competing models without circularity; explaining Bostrom's point that rejecting SIA makes us presumptuous only if we treat our location as evidential rather than random; and addressing the charge that SIA collapses into a form of observer-relative Bayesian updating that violates the principle of indifference.

The marking scheme should separate an A answer (all four moves present, with explicit recognition that the reference-class error is circular, and a nuanced take on whether SIA relocates rather than solves the problem) from a B answer (correct definitions of SSA and SIA but treating the scenario as a simple which-one-is-right comparison without seeing the circularity). Deduct marks specifically for: treating SSA as applicable across models without fixing the reference class externally; confusing the presumptuous philosopher with a simple Doomsday-style argument; or asserting that SIA solves the problem without addressing the prior-shifting objection.
