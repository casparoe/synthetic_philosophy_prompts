I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of science, currently working on explanatory practices in macro-history. At a recent workshop on counterfactual reasoning in historical explanation, two colleagues—let us call them Mara and Leo—entered into a sustained disagreement about whether any meaningful sense of historical inevitability survives rigorous counterfactual scrutiny, using the mechanization of production in eighteenth-century Britain as their central case. Mara defended a position she labeled structural inevitability: she argued that even if we counterfactually suppress specific local catalysts—say, the particular engineering trajectory that led to James Watt's separate condenser, or the immediate proximity of coal seams to emerging textile centers—the broader configuration of competitive state systems, accumulated merchant capital, and proto-industrial labor markets functions as an attractor state, making some form of mechanized production highly probable across a wide range of nearby possible worlds. Leo responded that this formulation is circular: the attractor is defined precisely by reference to the actual historical outcome, so claiming that history converges on it explains nothing. In its place, he proposed modal-proximity inevitability, according to which an event counts as inevitable only if its absence in the nearest possible worlds requires either violations of well-confirmed physical or social laws, or perturbations so large that they cease to be counterfactuals in the relevant historiographical sense. By Leo's measure, substituting an alternative engineer for Watt is a small, law-respecting perturbation—several contemporaries were pursuing steam improvements—so mechanization was not inevitable in any non-tautological sense. Mara countered that Leo's criterion is prohibitively strict: applied consistently, it would deny inevitability to virtually every macro-historical transition, thereby rendering the concept useless for historians who need to distinguish structurally driven systemic shifts from genuinely contingent local events. Leo's final reply was that explanatory utility is not a license to conflate our predictive interests with modal facts; if historians require a concept of inevitability, they should explicitly articulate the normative or pragmatic framework that licenses it—perhaps one weighting long-run predictive success—rather than treating structural convergence as a discovery about nature. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not settle the matter. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: evaluate Mara's initial structural-attractor claim, Leo's tautology charge together with his modal-proximity replacement, Mara's objection that Leo empties the concept of historical work, and Leo's concluding point about normative frameworks. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere between, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, specify exactly what each party gets correct and what each overlooks—particularly whether an intermediate position can preserve non-circular explanatory power without collapsing into pure pragmatism—and conclude by identifying what both are missing about the relationship between counterfactual stability and the scale of historical explanation.
