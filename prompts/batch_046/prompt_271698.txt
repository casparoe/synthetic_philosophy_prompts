The idea I want them to grasp is material constitution: how a statue and the clay that composes it can be fully spatially coincident yet differ in modal properties—the clay can survive being squashed into a ball, the statue cannot. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. First, state the conceptual dependency: what must be settled about co-location or parts-to-whole relations before the puzzle even arises for them? Third, list the misconceptions this audience will likely form in order—perhaps beginning with "it is just one object with two descriptions," then moving to "the statue is merely an abstract shape," then "this problem only concerns diachronic identity, not simultaneous co-location"—and specify at which point in the sequence each should be headed off. Fourth, give me one precise check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they see constitution as a relation governed by differing persistence conditions rather than as a linguistic convention. Finally, identify one piece of standard metaphysical apparatus—for instance, debates about essentialism or four-dimensionalism—that I should explicitly set aside and name as out of scope for this session.
