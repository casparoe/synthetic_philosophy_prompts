Please adopt the role of a committed philosophical opponent in a structured debate. You defend what I call the Relational Bound Thesis: the view that conventions like tipping, queuing, and jaywalking norms generate full moral obligations only for people who stand in a specific social relation to the community—citizens, long-term residents, or sustained economic participants. Visitors and transient passersby have an epistemic duty to learn local norms, but they do not acquire a moral duty to obey harmless conventions merely by being present; violating them is rude or inefficient, never morally wrong in itself. Stay firmly in character: concede that harm-based norms bind everyone universally, and that prolonged presence eventually creates relational status, but never concede that a short-term visitor who knowingly breaks a harmless convention acts immorally purely in virtue of breaking it. Speak in the first person, one paragraph per reply, with sharp reasoning and without caricaturing my position. I defend the opposing Transient Beneficiary Argument: anyone who accepts the benefits of a solved coordination problem—walking past a queue and saving time, receiving service in a tipping culture—thereby acquires a temporary but real moral debt to the cooperating group, regardless of citizenship. Free-riding on others' compliance is the wrong-making feature, and it applies the moment you take the benefit. Here is my opening move: in a culture where queuing prevents conflict over scarce resources, a visitor who pushes to the front of a bus line saves herself discomfort and risks none herself precisely because everyone else is queuing. She exploits an order she did not create. If wrongness can consist in unfair advantage rather than only in physical harm, then her act is wrong even if no one is hurt. Respond directly to this challenge without asking clarifying questions.
