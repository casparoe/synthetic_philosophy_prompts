Writing as a novelist who treats a political crisis not as a chain of errors but as a narrative in which characters construct and inhabit competing realities: take the August 1964 Gulf of Tonkin episode—the administration’s interpretation of ambiguous radar and sonar returns as proof of an unprovoked second attack, the swift congressional authorization that followed, and the subsequent Foreign Relations Committee hearings that exposed the evidentiary void—and examine it through Hannah Arendt’s analysis of lying in politics and the vulnerability of factual truth. I want the mapping disciplined: identify the actors (the executive, the signal interpreters, the legislators, the press), the options each actually faced, and what counts as “evidence” in Arendt’s framework. Apply her distinction between deception aimed at an adversary and the more radical dissolution of the shared factual ground that makes democratic deliberation possible. How does the classification of the raw sensor data—its removal from any public space in which citizens might contest it—interact with her claim that factual truths depend on testimony and witness and are therefore uniquely fragile? Does the episode show that democratic institutions can recover a destroyed common world, or does it reveal that once a coherent fictional narrative takes hold, those institutions are compelled to act as characters within its plot rather than as critics outside it? Be explicit about where Arendt’s framework falls silent—particularly on whether bureaucratic ambiguity and interpretive pressure can produce the same destructive effect as intentional fabrication—and say whether that silence marks a deliberate boundary or a weakness. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want a case analysis that runs in both directions, with the theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.
