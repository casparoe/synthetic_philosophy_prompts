I'm working through Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which a tortoise challenges Achilles over the justification of deduction, and I'm trying to make sense of a specific structural move. The tortoise accepts that premise A holds, that premise B holds, and even that the conditional linking A and B to conclusion Z is true; nevertheless he refuses to assent to Z. Achilles responds by adding a further premise—call it C—which states that if A, B, and the conditional all hold, then Z holds. The tortoise accepts C as well, yet still withholds assent to Z, and Achilles can keep introducing further premises of the same form without ever reaching the conclusion.

Most interpretations read this as a regress about logical rules: to apply modus ponens one apparently needs an explicit premise licensing that application, which in turn requires another licensing premise, and so on. But I want to understand the exchange through a distinction that is not always emphasized—between recognizing that a set of premises materially supports a conclusion, and being normatively bound by a rule that licenses the move from those premises to that conclusion. On this view, the tortoise might consistently grant the material support relation at every stage, while consistently denying that any explicit statement of the licensing rule imposes an obligation on him to accept Z.

If we read the dialogue in this way, is Carroll's regress actually vicious? Or does it reveal that deductive justification depends on something that cannot be captured in an ever-lengthening list of explicit premises—perhaps an inferential capacity or a normative stance that is not itself propositional? I want help understanding whether the tortoise's refusal should be seen as a failure of rationality, or rather as a coherent separation between accepting propositions and committing to an inferential rule. Does Carroll's structure support the claim that deduction requires a non-propositional ground, and if so, what is precisely lacking in the tortoise's position?
