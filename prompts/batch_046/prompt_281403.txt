I am drafting an essay on whether an individual self—an “I”—can be genuinely distributed across multiple brains, working from Douglas Hofstadter’s suggestion that selfhood is a symbolic pattern capable of persisting in others after death. Rather than rehearsing his well-known strange-loop framework, I want to develop a more specific claim: that the persistence of one self in another brain should be evaluated by structural isomorphism between the original and reconstructed pattern, not by emotional proximity or frequency of interaction. I call this the fidelity-of-reconstruction thesis. On this view, when a close friend retains extensive conceptual mappings of a deceased person—shared vocabulary, predictive models of behavior, internalized decision patterns—the friend’s cognitive architecture hosts a high-fidelity shard of the original self-pattern. By contrast, a distant acquaintance who remembers only biographical facts hosts a low-fidelity trace that does not constitute distributed identity but mere informational influence.

The essay then introduces a distinction between thin transmission and thick reconstruction. Thin transmission occurs when information about person A enters person B’s memory without altering B’s self-model; B remains a unified first-person center that merely knows about A. Thick reconstruction occurs when B’s self-model integrates A’s patterns as a functional sub-routine—B can generate A-like judgments in contexts where A’s expertise is relevant, and does so without experiencing them as alien intrusions. The critical question is whether thick reconstruction amounts to A’s self being partly present in B, or merely to B having learned to simulate A.

To defend the distributed view, I invoke split-brain cases: when the corpus callosum is severed, two first-person perspectives emerge from one neural substrate. If first-person access is a local interface rather than a global property of the entire brain, then there is no principled barrier to one first-person center (A) being hosted partly within another brain (B), provided B’s architecture supports the relevant pattern without generating a competing first-person claim over it. The objection that B still experiences everything as “mine” can be met by noting that B’s first-person interface may cover only the native substrate, leaving reconstructed patterns operating in a non-first-person mode—much as split-brain patients do not experience the other hemisphere’s actions as self-authored.

Please comment on this draft. I am particularly interested in the following:

- Is the fidelity-of-reconstruction thesis coherent, or does measuring structural isomorphism across brains require empirical neuroscience that goes beyond what can be defended conceptually?
- Does the distinction between thin transmission and thick reconstruction actually do work, or does it collapse into familiar distinctions between memory, influence, and skill acquisition?
- Does the split-brain analogy genuinely support distributed selfhood across brains, or does it only show that a single substrate can support multiple non-communicating first-person perspectives?
- Are there standard objections from personal-identity theory—such as the requirement for spatio-temporal continuity of a single body or brain—that this argument fails to address?

Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I want conceptual criticism that could be delivered without external lookup, focusing on coherence, missed objections, and whether the injected framework of structural fidelity holds together.
