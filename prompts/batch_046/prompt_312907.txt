I am preparing a proposal for a four-month, part-time independent research fellowship on a conceptual question in philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence interpretation. My half-formed idea is this: debates over whether large language models literally hold beliefs tend to split between behavioral-prediction arguments and internal-representation arguments, yet both sides implicitly assume that belief-attribution to an artificial system should mirror the conditions we use for humans. I want to investigate whether this mirroring assumption is justified, or whether adopting the intentional stance toward an AI requires a structurally different form of interpretive stability—specifically, invariance of attributed belief-content under low-level token perturbations that preserve semantic meaning—because human cognition carries an implicit robustness guarantee that artificial architectures do not necessarily share. Please turn this into a formal research proposal with a precisely stated question: does legitimate belief-attribution to an AI depend on a kind of counterfactual robustness (stability across syntactic perturbation) that is not required for human belief-attribution, and if so, does that reduce the intentional stance toward AI to a predictive heuristic rather than a literal interpretation?

The proposal should explain why existing positions leave this open: behavioralists can point to predictive success without addressing whether that success holds under perturbation, while representationalists examine internal structures without asking whether those structures support the right kind of interpretive invariance for belief-talk. Lay out the method in concrete terms: construct a taxonomy of belief-attribution conditions (predictive utility, counterfactual stability, normative coherence, and architectural transparency), apply them to a targeted class of model behaviors—such as consistent factual reporting versus context-dependent contradiction—and evaluate whether any subset is sufficient for intentional-stance adoption without collapsing into anthropomorphism. Specify what a successful result looks like—perhaps a demarcation criterion distinguishing heuristic belief-talk from genuine belief-attribution in artificial systems—and what failure looks like: either that all conditions reduce to the human case with no structural difference, or that no stable criterion can be formulated without circularity. Include an honest scope assessment: this is philosophical conceptual analysis aided by illustrative examples, not an empirical study of model weights, so it must avoid overclaiming about specific architectures.

Please separate what is settled—such as that the intentional stance is primarily a predictive strategy rather than an ontological commitment to mental states—from what is genuinely contested, including whether predictive success alone licenses belief-attribution to systems lacking consciousness or biological embodiment, and whether robustness under perturbation is an empirical feature of particular architectures or a conceptual prerequisite for belief-talk. Do not present either the behavioralist or the representationalist view as the consensus, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I will supply references myself. The deliverable should read as a submit-ready document with clear sections for the question, the gap in current answers, the proposed approach, anticipated results and failure conditions, and realistic limitations for the fellowship term.
