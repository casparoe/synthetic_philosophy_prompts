Write a take-home exam prompt, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme, for an advanced undergraduate seminar in comparative political ethics (final assessment, 48-hour window, 1,500-word limit, 40 marks). The topic is what counts as corruption, specifically engaging clientelism, ritualized gift-giving, and whether Western legalist standards are genuinely universal or parochially ethnocentric. To prevent a generic definitional recitation, embed in the exam question a specific, non-obvious institutional design: a municipal administration that operates two parallel accounting registers—one formal ledger tracking tax revenues and public expenditure, and one “community reciprocity” ledger recording contributions that are legally permitted, used to fund local services, but distributed according to kinship proximity and social rank rather than to objective need or democratic allocation. Ask students to determine whether this dual-register system is corrupt, and if so in precisely what respects, requiring them to distinguish conceptually between (i) personal enrichment by office-holders, (ii) institutionalized patronage that binds citizens to elites, and (iii) functional substitution for a fiscal state that lacks extraction capacity. They must further evaluate whether applying Western transparency norms to condemn the reciprocity ledger is conceptually appropriate or amounts to normative imperialism, and they should address whether an impartial-spectator judgment—if one could be formed—would settle the matter or merely relocate the disagreement.

The model answer must make at least four identifiable conceptual moves: (a) separating corruption-as-abuse-of-public-office from corruption-as-deviation-from-formal-procedural-rules; (b) analyzing the “registry problem” (the coexistence of two legitimate but incompatible accounting regimes) as the crux rather than treating gift-giving as self-evidently venal; (c) assessing whether universalist condemnation can be defended without collapsing into ethnocentrism, specifically by testing whether the reciprocity system violates conditions that any functional bureaucracy must meet regardless of cultural context; and (d) explaining why the impartial spectator’s likely sympathy with the beneficiaries of the reciprocity system does not automatically legitimate the system, and why its likely disapproval of opacity does not automatically condemn it.

The marking scheme should award first-class marks (32–40) only for answers that maintain all four distinctions with precision, recognize that functional substitution for state failure is not automatically exculpatory, and avoid both the error of conflating all non-market exchange with bribery and the error of invoking “cultural relativism” as an argument-stopper without further justification. Second-class marks (24–31) should be assigned to answers that grasp the dual-register structure but blur the distinction between personal and institutional corruption, or that treat Western standards as obviously universal without argument. Lower marks should penalize answers that rely on unargued assertions about “how things are done there,” that request or invent bibliographic citations, or that fail to identify the registry problem as the central analytical crux. Do not ask students for reading lists, specific author citations, or bibliographies; the assessment should be answerable entirely through conceptual reasoning applied to the case described.
