I have recently begun advising the governance design for a decentralized scientific consortium in which members must commit ex ante to open-data and resource-sharing protocols, even though the proprietary value of specific findings will only become clear ex post. The structural parallel to Parfit's hitchhiker is immediate: participants must promise future cooperation to secure present collective investment, yet each faces rational pressure to defect once the benefit is secured and enforcement is weak. What makes my situation different from the textbook case is that the problem is bilateral—the funding bodies and host institutions that provide the initial "rescue" also face rational pressure to withhold commitment unless they can secure binding assurances in advance—so the failure mode is not merely unilateral defection but a mutual anticipation of it that collapses cooperation before it begins.

Given this practical context, I am trying to decide how to direct my own limited research and advisory capacity—roughly two years, with training in decision theory and institutional design but not in moral psychology—toward the philosophical and theoretical questions that would actually improve how we construct credible commitments in low-enforcement environments.

What do you see as the principal open questions or active lines of work at the intersection of rational-choice theory, commitment architecture, and the philosophy of practical reason? Please defend a ranked agenda: which two or three questions deserve the bulk of that effort, and which prominent issues should be deprioritized because they are either intractable with current formal tools, insufficiently upstream, or largely settled without practical payoff for bilateral commitment design?

In giving the ordering, make your criteria explicit—tractability, dependency (how many other design problems hinge on resolving it), and direct relevance to settings where both parties must rationally commit despite anticipating defection. I am particularly interested in whether the standard framing of the hitchhiker problem as a unilateral risk of post-rescue defection obscures a deeper, underexplored problem about how rescuers rationally withhold cooperation in anticipation of that defection, and whether correcting that asymmetry—treating commitment failure as a coordination problem between two rational agents rather than a self-control problem for one—should be the central theoretical focus rather than refining models of individual rational irrationality. If that reframing is wrong or unproductive, say so and explain what should take its place.
