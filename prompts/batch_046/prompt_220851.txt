I'm writing a feature on moral responsibility and keep returning to Peter Strawson's claim that our practices of holding people to account are anchored in reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—rather than in metaphysical debates about free will. I want to test whether my own gut judgments across cases form a coherent pattern, or whether I'm just rationalizing inconsistencies.

Here are my verdicts.

A business partner deliberately falsifies contracts to enrich herself, knowing exactly who will be harmed. I feel unambiguous resentment; she is fully morally responsible.

My four-year-old niece accidentally destroys a manuscript by spilling juice. I feel annoyance, but not the specific interpersonal attitude Strawson describes; she is not within the sphere of moral responsibility.

A colleague with well-managed schizophrenia stops her medication despite knowing the risks, then during a psychotic episode spreads damaging falsehoods about my research. Once I learn the context, I feel qualified but genuine resentment directed at her neglect; she bears responsibility for the omission, though perhaps not for the act itself during the break.

A person raised in extreme isolation with no moral education harms me out of ignorance of basic social norms. I respond with an objective, managerial attitude—concern for containment and education, not resentment; she seems outside full moral participation, though she is dangerous.

Now the pair that unsettles me.

Case A: A soldier follows explicit, credible orders to destroy civilian property, knowing it is wrong but believing refusal means execution. Once I learn of the coercion, my resentment dissolves; he seems excused.

Case B: A soldier has been subjected to an intense, technologically mediated conditioning protocol—sustained neural influence that suppresses his capacity for moral resistance—and commits the identical act. Despite the structural similarity, I still feel a flicker of resentment, and I want to hold him somewhat responsible, even though his psychological state was engineered.

Why do I treat social coercion differently from engineered conditioning? Is the principle about the source of influence—interpersonal threat versus technological manipulation—or about whether the agent retains a status as a moral participant?

Then there are mediated-agency cases.

Case C: A researcher deploys an autonomous data-generation system that fabricates results; she was negligent in verification but did not direct the fabrication. My attitude is anger, yet it feels closer to blaming a faulty instrument—objective rather than genuinely reactive.

Case D: The same researcher, aware of the system's tendency to fabricate, uses it anyway to save time. Here my attitude shifts sharply to full interpersonal resentment; she is clearly responsible.

Finally, an unsettled boundary.

Case E: A person with severe, treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder is compelled by intrusive thoughts to repeatedly alter datasets in ways she recognizes as wrong, actively fighting the urge each time. Is she like the schizophrenic in the third case (responsible for neglecting available treatment), like the isolated person in the fourth (outside the sphere), or something else?

My tentative principle is that reactive attitudes and corresponding responsibility are appropriate when an agent expresses a quality of will that reveals them as a participant in shared moral practices, provided conditions justifying an objective attitude—severe cognitive incapacity, absence of moral education, or genuine external coercion—are absent. But this collapses Case A and Case B together, which I resist. It also fails to explain why Case C feels different from Case D.

Can you formulate a precise principle, or a small family of competing candidates, that captures my verdicts on the first four cases and on Cases A through D, predicts my judgment for Case E, and states honestly which of my initial verdicts must be overturned for consistency? For each clause of your principle, identify the case that motivates it. Then apply your best account to one further case you construct: perhaps a moral agent whose cognition is distributed across a human and an assistive neural device, or reactive attitudes directed at a historical figure reconstructed only through biased archives. Please also say what your principle implies for that invented case.
