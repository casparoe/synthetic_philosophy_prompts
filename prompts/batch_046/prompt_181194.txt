I am one of the remaining original members of a residential cooperative founded fifteen years ago under a charter that binds residents to collective governance and shared maintenance obligations. Over the past decade, founding members have departed one by one—never more than two in a given year—and each vacancy was filled through the existing admission vote. No original residents remain. A recent proposal by newer members seeks to replace our collective structure with a conventional landlord-tenant arrangement using outsourced labor, and they argue that the charter's provisions are obsolete. A counter-argument holds that every transition was incremental and ratified by the membership at the time, so there is no identifiable point at which the cooperative became a different kind of entity; consequently, an exit clause permitting dissolution only upon a fundamental change in nature has not been satisfied. I am trying to determine whether I should invoke that clause, but I am caught between the intuition that continuity has been preserved through procedural legitimacy and the intuition that the complete replacement of the membership, combined with the proposed structural overhaul, represents a qualitative break. Is there a philosophically coherent way to maintain that a series of individually insignificant changes can collectively constitute a decisive transformation, without arbitrarily selecting a cutoff point? And does the absence of any sharp dividing line between the original collective and its present form undermine the claim that it remains the same institution at all?
