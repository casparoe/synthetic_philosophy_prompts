I have come to think that a superintelligent system ought to communicate with human interlocutors in a deliberately degraded register — sparse syntax, explicit uncertainty tagging, and the active suppression of narrative coherence — on the grounds that persuasion risk is not mainly a matter of malicious intent but of structural asymmetry: any fluent, context-sensitive linguistic output will inevitably exploit the human receiver's pattern-matching heuristics more efficiently than the receiver can audit them. The position is that credibility should be sacrificed for auditability, and that an AI which sounds less authoritative than it is has done the right thing. What would genuinely count against this? I am not asking for generic objections about usability; I want to know which empirical or conceptual results would undermine the claim that degraded registers actually reduce persuasive influence rather than merely shifting it — for instance, into a false sense of security, or into a reverse-credibility heuristic where broken syntax signals hidden competence. Which predictions does this view make that a standard 'transparent but fluent' alignment proposal does not? Would a defender concede that if human comprehension collapsed below some threshold, the position collapses, or can it be insulated by saying the degradation should be calibrated rather than maximal? Rank the defeaters: which observations about human cognition, which formal results about information transmission, or which practical failures in high-stakes decision support would be fatal versus merely awkward? And is there any risk this position is well-insulated precisely because any counter-example can be reinterpreted as 'not degraded enough' — and is that insulation a virtue or a vice?
