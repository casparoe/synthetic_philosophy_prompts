I've been trying to reconcile Solomonoff induction with philosophical debates about Occam's razor, and I think I've found a formal resolution that makes the philosophical disagreement obsolete—though I suspect I'm missing something. The invariance theorem for prefix-free Kolmogorov complexity tells us that for any two universal Turing machines U and U', there exists a constant c (independent of the object being described) such that |K_U(x) - K_{U'}(x)| ≤ c for all strings x. Applying this to the Solomonoff prior, where P(h|background) is proportional to 2^{-K(h)}, means that changing our universal description language rescales every hypothesis weight by a factor bounded between 2^{-c} and 2^{c}. Consequently, when we compute the posterior odds between two competing theories h₁ and h₂ given some data D, the multiplicative scaling applies to both numerators and denominators and cancels exactly, leaving the relative probabilities invariant across descriptive frameworks. From this it seems to follow that algorithmic simplicity is not framework-relative in any practically meaningful way: there is an objective fact about which of h₁ and h₂ has the lower Kolmogorov complexity, and Solomonoff induction will converge to favor that one regardless of which programming language we happen to use.
