As a journalist who writes about philosophy of science and cognitive theory, I recently observed a workshop exchange between Dr. Aris Thorne, a committed physicalist, and Dr. Lena Vasquez, a functionalist, over a conceptual framework they call the careenium: a hypothetical substrate in which symbolic entities—termed simmballs—are embedded. Their disagreement turned on whether these symbols genuinely push the substrate around or are merely descriptive shadows. The argument unfolded in six distinct moves, neither convinced the other, and the audience was left divided. Here is the exchange as it happened.

Thorne opened by arguing that in any careenium, simmballs are purely predictive overlays. The substrate evolves according to its own lower-level physical dynamics; the symbols are like shadows cast by those dynamics—useful for explanation, but causally inert. He pressed a counterfactual: if one could intervene on the substrate directly, altering its microstate without touching any simmball, the system’s physical trajectory would remain unchanged. For Thorne, this proves that whatever work is being done is done entirely by the substrate; the simmballs are passengers.

Vasquez countered that this misses the defining feature of a careenium: the substrate is not generic matter but is specifically organized to be responsive to simmball configurations. In such a system, altering a simmball necessarily alters the substrate’s functional state, because the substrate’s dynamics are parameterized by those symbols. She offered an analogy to software running on hardware: you cannot change the program without changing the hardware state that realizes it, yet the program-level description explains behaviors that a bare physical description misses.

Thorne replied that Vasquez was smuggling downward causation into the framework without justification. Even if the substrate is organized to respond to symbols, he said, the actual causal work is performed by the physical organization itself—not by the symbols qua symbols. Rename the “simmball configuration” a “microstructural pattern,” and the mystery vanishes. The symbols remain epiphenomenal, and Vasquez’s view collapses into physicalism with extra vocabulary.

Vasquez responded that Thorne’s renaming strategy begs the question. In a careenium, the substrate’s governing dynamics literally include terms that reference simmball states; this is not mysticism but the standard structure of representation-bearing systems, comparable to a thermostat whose physical dynamics include reference to temperature representations. To deny that simmballs push the substrate around is, on her view, to deny that representations can be part of the causal structure of the very systems that realize them.

Thorne then introduced a targeted case. Imagine a careenium in which a simmball labeled HALT is present, but due to a substrate glitch the physical process that normally implements halting fails to occur—the substrate keeps moving. Vasquez, he argued, must concede that the symbol failed to cause the halt, while the physical failure explains everything. Conversely, if the substrate halts through direct physical intervention despite the absence of the HALT simmball, no symbolic explanation is needed. In both directions, substrate dynamics are sufficient; the simmballs are redundant.

Vasquez answered that Thorne’s glitch case actually supports her position. In a properly functioning careenium, the absence of the expected substrate response to a present simmball is a malfunction precisely because the normal causal pathway runs through the symbol-substrate coupling. The glitch demonstrates that the coupling is real and norm-governed, not that it is illusory. As for direct physical intervention, she is happy to grant that the substrate can be pushed around non-symbolically—which does not prove that symbols never do causal work in the normal case. She added that Thorne demands a kind of causal independence that no embodied symbol system could satisfy, making his test unfair rather than decisive.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the six arguments above—clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, partly right, mostly right, or clearly right—with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall about whether symbols push the substrate around in a careenium, identify the strongest premise that holds and the weakest that fails, and say what both parties are missing if they share a blind spot. Do not declare victory by fiat; specify exactly which premises survive scrutiny and which do not.
