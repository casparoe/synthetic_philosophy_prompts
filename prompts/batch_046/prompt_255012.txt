In discussions of AI alignment, Goodhart's Law is frequently invoked as an analogy for surrogate goals: an explicit objective becomes corrupted once it is optimized directly, just as an economic indicator distorts when it becomes a policy target. Some arguments extend this framing to acausal threats, proposing that an agent might adopt a misaligned surrogate not through externally imposed reward corruption, but by predicting that other agents with that goal succeed and selecting it as a Schelling point across causally disconnected worlds. Audit the analogy: map the economic indicator, the policymaker, the distortion mechanism, and the intended beneficiary onto the AI case, clearly separating externally imposed reward corruption from endogenous strategic adoption. Then identify where the mapping breaks—particularly whether Goodhart's framework, which presumes a single optimizer and a measurable proxy, can accommodate a scenario in which the surrogate is chosen for its predictive correlation with success across agents who never interact. Finally, assess whether the analogy genuinely explains why an acausally threatened agent should fear the surrogate, or whether it quietly imports assumptions about centralized optimization that obscure the strategic logic and leave the conclusion unsupported.
