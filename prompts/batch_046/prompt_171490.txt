As I research a feature on the hidden architecture of professional self-regulation, I keep returning to a structural puzzle in licensing-board practice. State medical and legal boards maintain morals clauses that permit disciplinary action for private, lawful conduct—such as an undisclosed affair or off-duty substance use—when that conduct is judged to erode public confidence in the profession, yet the same boards generally decline to sanction behavior that is equally immoral by conventional standards but remains hidden and produces no demonstrable effect on clients, patients, or institutional function. The resulting pattern is neither consistently harm-based, since the cited harm is typically speculative and diffuse, nor a straightforward enforcement of shared morality, since concealed vice escapes punishment entirely. What philosophical commitments about the relationship between law, morality, and institutional authority does this design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it rest on a claim that a profession must preserve a shared moral ecology to maintain its legitimacy; on a harm principle qualified by an exception for collective institutional trust; or on a distinct view—perhaps a performative theory of professional identity—under which moral visibility itself becomes constitutive of authority? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it well, indicate where the institutional logic is philosophically underdetermined, and specify how a licensing regime rebuilt on purely harm-based premises, or on purely moralist premises, would differ in its rules, evidentiary standards, and scope of scrutiny over private life.
