Exam question:
David Benatar defends an asymmetry in the evaluation of pleasure and pain with respect to beings who do not actually exist. First, lay out the four-part logical structure of this asymmetry, clearly distinguishing the evaluation applied to actual beings from that applied to merely possible beings. Second, apply the asymmetry to the following hypothetical case: imagine a potential being, S, who—if created—would experience exactly 90 units of pleasure and 10 units of pain, but in entirely discrete, non-overlapping temporal intervals with no psychological continuity or memory linking one interval to another. A critic argues that in such a case of disintegrated well-being, Benatar's framework should be applied separately to each interval rather than to S as a unified whole, which might yield different procreative recommendations for different segments. Assess whether this objection succeeds, paying particular attention to whether Benatar's argument requires a single, continuous subject of experience across time.

Student answer:
Benatar's asymmetry consists of four claims. First, the presence of pain is bad for an actual being who experiences it. Second, the presence of pleasure is good for an actual being who experiences it. Third, the absence of pain is good, and this holds even when there is no actual being who enjoys that absence—no one is deprived of not suffering. Fourth, the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is an actual being who is deprived of it. For merely possible beings, this creates an asymmetrical reason structure: there is a reason not to create a being who will suffer, but no parallel reason to create a being who will enjoy pleasure.

Applying this to S: since S does not exist, the absence of her 90 units of pleasure is not a harm. However, if she were created, the 10 units of pain she would experience would be genuinely bad for her. Thus, there is a reason against creating S, and no countervailing reason based on her potential pleasure.

The critic's disintegrated well-being objection proposes applying the asymmetry segment by segment. This objection misunderstands Benatar's reliance on the non-identity problem. Because the intervals lack psychological continuity, each interval constitutes a metaphysically distinct potential individual. Therefore, Benatar's framework should be applied to each segment-subject separately: for any segment containing pain, there is a reason not to bring about that specific subject; for segments containing only pleasure, there is no reason to bring them about. This does not weaken Benatar's position—it actually strengthens the anti-natalist conclusion, because it generates multiple potential harm-subjects without producing any corresponding benefit-subjects that could offset them. Consequently, the objection fails. Benatar does not need to presuppose narrative unity across an entire life; he only needs a subject capable of experiencing harm or benefit, and in this disintegrated case, that subject is the segment. So the framework applies without requiring continuous personal identity over time.
