I was discussing Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur with a colleague over coffee, and we kept circling a tension she never fully resolves. On one hand, she argues that durable happiness requires disciplined study of the sciences to elevate the soul above bodily disturbance; on the other, she insists that we must preserve a carefully regulated illusion—particularly regarding the permanence of affection and the stability of fortune—because stripping away every deception exposes the soul to a despair that reason by itself cannot remedy.

I am now preparing a seminar on Enlightenment moral psychology and want a villanelle of nineteen lines in strict ABA rhyme, with two alternating refrains, that stages this precise conflict. One refrain should invoke the necessity of intellectual order and study; the other should invoke the protective function of a well-ordered illusion. In the tercets, let the refrains appear to offer successive resolutions, but in the final quatrain force them into direct syntactic collision or merge them into a single line, so that the form itself asks whether happiness is achieved through reason's triumph or through reason's secret complicity with deception. The verse should be accurate enough that someone familiar with du Châtelet's distinction between bodily and spiritual happiness, and with her adaptation of Lockean uneasiness, recognises her specific position rather than a generic Stoic or Epicurean view. Please compose it in formal English verse without anachronistic language, and do not append any bibliography, citation, or explanatory note.
