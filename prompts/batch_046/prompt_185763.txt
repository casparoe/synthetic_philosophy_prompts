Write a Petrarchan sonnet (octave ABBAABBA, sestet CDECDE or CDCDCD, with a clear volta at line 9) in the voice of the husband from Bernard Williams’s “one thought too many” scenario, in which he saves his wife from drowning rather than a stranger. In the octave, depict the rescue as an unmediated expression of ground-project commitment—no weighing of lives, no appeal to principle, only the direct pull of a particular attachment. At the volta in line 9, let an intrusive second-order reason enter: the reflective thought that saving her is justified because it maximizes welfare or conforms to an impartial rule. Make the poem’s form enact the philosophy: use the octave’s enclosed, interlocking rhymes to embody the self-contained circle of intimate commitment, and let the sestet’s rhyme scheme fracture or interrupt that enclosure to mirror how this extra deliberative step alienates the agent from his own reason for acting. Be precise about the failure mode—Williams’s objection is not that the wrong person is saved, but that possessing this kind of reason corrupts the integrity of the attachment that makes the act what it is—so that a reader familiar with the integrity objection recognizes the conceptual structure rather than a mere name-drop. After the poem, include a brief note (about 100–150 words) separating what is broadly settled in Williams’s objection—for instance, that moral theories requiring agents to justify intimate commitments via impartial principles risk a distinctive form of alienation—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether the objection targets only a reflective stance or any consequentialist calculation, and how exactly it relates to the wrong-kind-reasons literature. In that note, flag any liberties the poem takes with Williams’s original example or with contested interpretations of the problem, and do not present any contested reading as the consensus view.
