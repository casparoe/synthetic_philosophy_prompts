I'm working through the debate about whether reasons for action must be internal to an agent's motivational set, but I want to push a more radical variant than the standard Humean-versus-externalist framing. Call it the thesis of normative opacity: it is not merely that an external reason fails to motivate an agent who lacks the corresponding desire; rather, if a putative reason's content cannot be fully articulated within the agent's own first-personal conceptual scheme—if it remains opaque to her practical self-understanding—then it does not count as a reason for her at all, not even in an "objective" or "external" sense. This goes beyond ordinary internalism, which typically grants that there are mind-independent moral facts that provide reasons regardless of an agent's current motivations. Normative opacity denies that there is any coherent notion of "a reason for S" that S could not, in principle, come to grasp through her own practical reasoning without conceptual distortion.

Please argue for normative opacity. Defend it against the standard externalist reply that moral obligations—say, to distant strangers or future generations—bind agents regardless of their motivational states, and explain why treating an agent as subject to reasons she cannot conceptually access violates the conditions for practical agency rather than respecting them. Address the objection that this collapses into solipsism about value: show why recognizing normative opacity does not entail that agents can only value what they already value, but rather sets a condition on how genuinely new values can be adopted. To ground the argument, consider a concrete case: an agent is asked to make a substantial sacrifice for a cause whose normative structure is not merely alien to her but conceptually inaccessible—she cannot understand what kind of good it is, not just that she rejects it. Does she have an external reason to comply? Argue that she does not, and that insisting otherwise mistakes the authority of morality for an authority that can operate independently of the conditions that make someone an agent at all.
