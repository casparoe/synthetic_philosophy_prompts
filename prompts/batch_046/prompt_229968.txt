I've been trying to understand whether my own consciousness works like a single movie screen—where everything I'm aware of is playing in one place—or more like several rough drafts being edited at once, with no central viewer. I don't have a formal philosophy background, but I've been paying close attention to how my mind behaves in different situations, and I keep finding patterns that pull in opposite directions, so I'm hoping someone can help me see whether there's an actual principle behind them.

When I stare at a sunset and suddenly notice the brief green flash at the horizon, my intuition is that the green was already present in my experience; I just hadn't pointed my attention at it yet. That feels like there's one continuous show running, and attention is just a spotlight moving across it—which sounds like the Cartesian theater idea, even if the theater is small. But then I think about driving home on autopilot and reaching my driveway with almost no memory of the last ten minutes. If there's a single audience watching everything, who was seeing the road? It seems more plausible that navigation and lane-keeping were running as separate drafts, with no unified "me" present to witness them. Hypnosis points the same way: when someone is told a hot object is cool and picks it up without flinching, I don't think they're hiding the pain from themselves; I think the pain draft was never written at all. So far, that all favors the multiple-drafts view.

But then I run into cases that make me want the theater back. In split-brain reports I've heard about, one half of the brain sees an image the other half doesn't, and the speaking side immediately invents a unified explanation for behavior it didn't cause. That looks less like peaceful coexistence of drafts and more like a desperate psychological need to maintain the illusion that there's one show with one audience. And in dreams, I often don't notice impossible events—I'm flying, or talking to someone who died—not because I've edited out the contradiction, but because the editing mechanism itself seems to be asleep. If consciousness were just competing drafts, I'd expect at least one draft to flag the impossibility. Instead, it feels like the projector is running but the projectionist is gone. So my verdicts are: hidden-but-attended details favor a single show with a moving spotlight; autopilot and hypnosis favor drafts without a central audience; split-brain confabulation shows we psychologically need a unified audience even if we don't have one; and dreaming shows the editing apparatus can go entirely offline rather than merely producing conflicting content.

Is there a coherent principle—maybe something about levels of access, or about integration versus mere parallel processing—that captures all four without contradiction, stated clause by clause with the case that motivates each part? Or must I abandon either the idea of a unified self or the idea that attention reveals pre-existing content? Please also tell me what the resulting account predicts for blindsight, where a person correctly guesses the shape of an object they sincerely claim not to see—does that belong with hypnosis, with split-brain confabulation, or with something else? And be honest about which of my four verdicts, if any, has to be overturned to keep the account both simple and coherent.
