Retired engineer here, been reading Machiavelli's little book for princes and I'm stuck on a tension I turned into an argument that feels like it destroys the whole fortune/virtù idea, but I definatly think I'm missing something obvious.

Here's the chain:

1. Machiavelli compares fortune to a river — you can build dikes, but you can't stop the flow. So fortune is just whatever lies outside your control at the moment.

2. Virtù is the quality that lets a prince build those dikes, meaning it expands the boundary of what he can control.

3. If that's right, then fortune and virtù aren't two separate forces; they're just labels for the inside and outside of the same control boundary.

4. He also says fortune governs about half our affairs, even for the best rulers. But if virtù really expands control, then more virtù should shrink fortune's share; there's no reason it should stay stuck at fifty percent.

5. So either virtù doesn't actually grow your control (which makes praising it weird) or the half-and-half line is just rhetoric with no fixed content.

6. There's another issue. If fortune is defined as the uncontrolled part, then saying it governs half is like saying the part I don't control is about half — true by definition once you draw the line, not an observation about the world.

7. So the distinction either collapses into a tautology or into a contradiction with the idea that virtù matters.

In my old field we talked about noise floors — you don't stockpile control over randomness, you design systems that tolerate it. So treating virtù like a kind of capital you invest against fortune feels off, but I can't pin down why. Where's the flaw in steps 1-7? Which one should I drop, and what does giving it up cost me? I especially want to keep both that fortune is real and that virtù can push back.

Also please be explicit: what argument or example would change your mind about where the mistake is? And tell me where your own reasoning here is shakiest — I want the weak points exposed, not hidden.
