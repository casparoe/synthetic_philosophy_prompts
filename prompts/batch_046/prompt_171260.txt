Exam question: Goodman introduces the predicate grue—green if observed before time t, blue otherwise—to generate a paradox for inductive inference. First, explain precisely why this is not merely a re-statement of Hume’s skeptical worry about the future resembling the past, but rather a problem about the language in which we formulate lawlike hypotheses. Second, discuss whether Goodman’s criterion of entrenchment genuinely resolves the paradox or merely relocates it, paying particular attention to whether entrenchment tracks objective features of properties or only the historical sociology of our inductive practices, especially if we allow that entrenchment could shift in non-stationary environments.

Student answer:
Goodman’s grue paradox differs from Hume’s problem because Hume asks how we justify the expectation that unobserved cases will resemble observed ones, appealing to custom and habit. Goodman is not concerned with psychological justification at all; he points out that for any observation of green emeralds before time t, the hypothesis that all emeralds are green and the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue are both fully consistent with the data. Since both hypotheses are equally supported, induction cannot be a matter of simply projecting observed regularities unless we have a prior rule for selecting which predicates are appropriate for projection. Thus Goodman’s problem is about the formal conditions for confirmation rather than about skeptical doubt.

Goodman solves this by appealing to entrenchment: a predicate is projectible to the extent that it has been used in successful past inductive inferences. Because green has been entrenched through centuries of successful predictions and grue has not, we are rationally entitled to project green rather than grue. This shows that induction is not arbitrary; it is governed by the actual history of our predictive successes. The criterion therefore avoids conventionalism, because entrenchment is not a matter of social agreement or linguistic fashion but an objective record of which predicates have actually worked in scientific practice.

That said, one might worry that if entrenchment is entirely historical, then in a non-stationary world where successful predictive practices change, the very predicates we should project could shift without any change in the underlying properties of things. This suggests that lawlikeness is not intrinsic to nature but is relative to the stability of our inductive traditions. So while Goodman distinguishes his logical problem from Hume’s psychological one, his solution may ultimately make the objectivity of natural law depend on the contingency of human scientific history.
