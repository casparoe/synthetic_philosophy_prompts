We spent the better part of last evening arguing about what interpretability researchers are actually tracking when they flag something as anomalous across model runs. The practice I keep returning to works like this: researchers train multiple instances of the same architecture with different random seeds, locate a sub-network that computes some specific function—say, tracking grammatical number agreement—identify it as a particular mechanism by its causal role rather than by neuron index, and then treat copies where that computation is absent or altered as anomalous. Yet within a single model, if that same sub-network suddenly shifts its behavior across layers or training steps, they register an internal anomaly, implying the mechanism ought to maintain a stable token-level identity. So they permit cross-instance identity based on functional role but demand intra-instance stability based on implementation location. What ontology of mechanisms does that dual practice presuppose? Does it commit them to a functionalist metaphysics with an unexplained stability clause, or to a hybrid view that risks incoherence? Reconstruct the implicit theory of mechanism identity—whether it is defined by causal structure, by realized computation, or by design specification—and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Is the framework consistent with both pure functionalism, which should allow arbitrary implementation drift, and pure token-identity, which should deny cross-seed comparison altogether? Then say what mechanistic anomaly detection protocols would have to change if we adopted a strictly token-identity view: could we still speak of the same mechanism failing across seeds, or would anomaly detection collapse into purely local diagnostics?
