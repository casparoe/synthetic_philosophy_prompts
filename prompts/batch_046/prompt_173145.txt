As a retired experimental physicist now working through moral philosophy, I have noticed a recurring pattern in discussions of blame that I cannot quite place. A senior researcher condemns a junior colleague for rushing data analysis to meet a grant deadline, yet her own identical conduct years earlier is treated by peers as an understandable reaction to funding pressure rather than a moral failure. Similarly, a professional activist denounces a public official for accepting a minor perk, while she herself receives comparable benefits in her own role, framing hers as “standard practice” and the official’s as “corruption.” In each case the blamer is not simply guilty of the same fault—ordinary hypocrisy—because the very conditions that excuse her are structurally embedded in her position, while she withholds those same conditions from her target. The fault is the same in kind, but the architecture of available excuses is distributed asymmetrically by social role rather than by consistent principle. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration, in which standing to blame is undermined not by shared vice but by a structurally unequal distribution of exculpatory conditions? How precisely does it differ from standard hypocrisy and from the familiar “who are you to blame” problem? If the literature offers no dedicated term, what definition and boundary conditions would best capture it—particularly the feature that the blamer need not be conscious of the asymmetry, and that the excuse is genuinely available to her in a way it is not to the person she blames?
