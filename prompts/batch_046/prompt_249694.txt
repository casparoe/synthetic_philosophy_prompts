During a recent seminar on personal identity and neurotechnology, a classmate defended mind-uploading against what she termed "discontinuity anxiety" by repeating a one-liner: "If you replace every neuron gradually with a synthetic equivalent and remain conscious throughout, then scanning and reinstantiating the whole brain in silicon at once is just faster maintenance—no different in kind." She paired this with a hypothetical clinical model in which a patient receives artificial synaptic interfaces at a rate of roughly four percent annually over two decades, retaining continuous autobiographical memory and social recognition, and argued that anyone who accepts the gradual case must therefore accept sudden uploading. I am convinced the sentence conceals several distinct arguments—about physical continuity versus functional continuity, about whether phenomenal experience requires biological substrate, about whether personal identity tracks psychological relations over time or substrate-independent patterns, and about whether the temporal distribution of change carries metaphysical or merely psychological weight—but I cannot tell which of these readings are tautological, which are substantive and defensible, and which are actually false. Could you lay out the precise claims that slogan might be expressing, treating them as a menu of interpretations rather than selecting one? For each, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is genuinely contentious, and whether it actually bridges the gap from gradual neuroprosthetic integration to instantaneous substrate-transfer. Specifically, apply your analysis to the twenty-year staggered-prosthesis scenario—where biological and synthetic tissue coexist—so I can see which interpretations preserve the analogy to sudden uploading and which collapse it. Also clarify where the dispute stops being about metaphysics and becomes a normative judgment about risk, authenticity, or the value of biological embodiment, since my classmate seemed to slide between these without noticing.
