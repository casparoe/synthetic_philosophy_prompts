I am a novelist preparing to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for fellow writers at a historical-fiction residency — all well-read generalists, none professional historians — on the proposition that nations are not primordial inheritances but invented and imagined constructions. I wish to combine Hobsbawm's concept of invented tradition with Anderson's imagined communities, but I want the session to turn on a tension that is seldom foregrounded: that tradition is manufactured not chiefly through outright forgery, but through the selective curation of ambiguous archival fragments into a seamless narrative of continuity. I require a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself.

Please specify the exact sequence in which these conceptual layers must be introduced, with an account of what each depends upon and why any other ordering would cause the argument to collapse. Identify the one concrete historical case that should carry the seminar — it must be concrete enough that these writers might adapt it for fiction, yet not so arcane that it presupposes specialist archival knowledge; it should simultaneously illustrate the bureaucratic fabrication of ritual (in Hobsbawm's sense) and the horizontal, print-mediated comradeship Anderson describes, and you should justify why it meets both conditions.

Next, map the misconceptions that this audience is likely to bring or form — for instance, that 'invented' denotes cynical fakery rather than sincere construction, or that 'imagined' denotes mere unreality — and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Propose one brief diagnostic exercise or question for the final ten minutes that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the dependency between archival selection and collective identity, as distinct from mere repetition of the terms. Finally, state explicitly what must be omitted at this level — I have in mind the dense postcolonial historiography that complicates Anderson's thesis of print-capitalism, together with any bibliography or further reading — and explain why such omission strengthens rather than weakens the session.

Write this in a formal register, as though outlining pedagogical intentions to a colleague, and employ British spelling and punctuation throughout.
