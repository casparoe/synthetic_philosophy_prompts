A colleague on an insurance review board is evaluating whether "restoration deficiency syndrome" should be added to the covered conditions list. The proposed diagnosis is defined purely biometrically: a person scores in the bottom 5 percent on a standardized "cognitive restoration efficiency" test, which measures how quickly neural activity returns to baseline after sustained mental effort. Crucially, the proposal does not require that the person experience fatigue, report impairment, or fail at any specific social role. One board member insists this is a genuine disease—an objective dysfunction of a biological mechanism, analogous to a heart that pumps blood too slowly. Another board member counters that without a normative premise about how quickly a brain ought to recover, the label is just a statistical tail being medicalized; she compares it to treating the bottom 5 percent of height as a skeletal disorder. Both agree on all the physiological data; they disagree only on whether those data constitute illness. I was asked which side is correct and whether there is any neutral, value-free test that could settle it. I now see that the dispute might turn on a deeper philosophical division about what health and disease actually are. Can you identify the structure of their disagreement, explain why both positions look plausible once you accept their starting point, and advise whether a purely descriptive account of biological function can really ground disease classification without importing hidden norms?
