I'm running a fifty-minute tutorial for second-year philosophy students who have completed introductory logic but have not yet studied metaphysics. I need to teach Bradley's regress and the problem of the unity of the proposition. Do not explain the regress to me; instead, plan the explanatory arc. First, state what conceptual distinction must be secured before the regress can appear puzzling rather than pedantic — I take it to be the gap between a mere list of items and a single judgement that binds them — and justify its priority. Next, specify the carrying example: avoid the standard 'Socrates is wise'; use instead a case in which someone simultaneously judges that a particular apple is both red and round, so that the student sees the binding problem in one mental act. Then lay out the dependency order: when to introduce Bradley's claim that relations are not ultimately real, when to present Russell's doctrine of external relations, and how to position Russell's reply so that it does not appear as a cheap dissolution of the difficulty. Identify the misconceptions students are likely to form — for instance, that the regress is merely grammatical, that Russell simply disproved Bradley, that unity is only a problem for language and not for thought, and that Bradley's own solution requires accepting the Absolute — and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be defused. Provide one final check question that distinguishes understanding the problem as metaphysical rather than psychological. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level — perhaps the full architecture of Bradley's Absolute — and why omitting it is pedagogically sound.
