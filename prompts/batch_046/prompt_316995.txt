I am an undergraduate in a seminar on ontology and digital environments, and I serve part-time on the moderation council for a persistent virtual world that supports user-generated assets, real-money exchange, and immersive VR presence. We face a steady flow of incident reports—virtual theft, avatar-based assault, defacement of communal spaces—and we lack a reusable framework for deciding whether an incident warrants only in-world remedies (restitution, temporary suspension, world-state rollback) or also real-world-style sanctions (permanent exclusion, referral to external arbitration, or public identification). I want you to operationalize the philosophical position that virtual objects and virtual harms are not “real” in a simple on/off sense, but rather exhibit what I will call cross-layer robustness: a virtual entity or harm is robustly real to the extent that it is embedded in practices extending beyond the platform (economic markets, identity-linked reputation, or institutional recognition) and produces effects that persist in the affected party’s practical reasoning or emotional life after they have logged out or removed the headset. 

Please design a decision procedure that a non-philosophical moderator can run in about fifteen minutes per case, using only information available in our logs, user statements, and market records. Structure it as an ordered sequence of questions, each with: (a) the specific indicator or evidence that counts as a sufficient answer; (b) a clear stopping rule that routes the case to an in-world remedy only; (c) explicit escalation flags—marked as value judgments, not factual determinations—where the procedure must hand off to the human council because the question involves weighing dignity, symbolic meaning, or communal identity rather than observable cross-layer embedding. Include a small diagnostic checklist (no more than six concrete tests) that moderators can apply to assess both cross-layer embedding and post-logout persistence.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. The theft of a rare virtual artifact that required roughly three hundred hours of in-game labor to construct, is actively traded on an external real-money market for several hundred U.S. dollars, and is registered to the victim’s verified real-world identity; the victim reports no emotional distress or practical disruption outside the game, only economic loss.

2. An immersive VR interaction in which one user’s avatar performed a non-consensual, physically invasive gesture on another user’s avatar for several minutes; the interaction left no persistent record in the world database (no object was created, no property was taken), but the victim reports sustained anxiety, sleep disruption, and avoidance of VR environments for weeks afterward.

3. The defacement of a virtual memorial site built by the community to commemorate a real-world tragedy; the act used only standard in-world building tools, destroyed no external-market assets, and caused no direct post-logout psychological harm to any identifiable individual, yet it triggered widespread communal outrage and was interpreted by many users as an attack on shared moral identity; the perpetrator claims the act was purely symbolic play within the game’s fictional frame.

In your applications, include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with common moderator intuition—explain precisely why the cross-layer and persistence criteria produce that result, and identify what normative judgment the procedure is deliberately not making. Finally, specify clearly what falls outside the procedure’s scope (for example, harms that are purely physiological without normative embedding, or purely fictional conflicts with no cross-layer extension) and state the conditions under which the council must override the procedure regardless of its output.
