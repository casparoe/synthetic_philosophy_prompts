Draft an adversarial-collaboration summary between the Directed-Normative-Power camp and the Reliance-Practice camp on the question of why promises bind. Both camps agree that promising is a speech act that systematically creates expectations, that breach produces a distinctive moral response not reducible to mere disappointment, that promises can remain binding even when keeping them is suboptimal for the promiser, and that apology and acknowledgment are specifically appropriate after breach rather than being interchangeable with compensation. They disagree on whether these phenomena are best explained by an irreducible exercise of normative authority that generates a claim-right held specifically by the promisee, or by a socially instituted coordination mechanism embedded in practices of reliance and reputation, where the distinctive response to breach is a repair signal and the bindingness is instrumental rationality made conventional.

The deliverable should have the following structure. First, a numbered Agreed-Claims section covering: the core phenomenology of promising and breach; what any adequate account must explain, including directedness to the promisee, persistence across changes in the promiser’s interests, and the specific appropriateness of apology; and what standard purely-utilitarian and purely-expectation-based reductive accounts fail to capture. Second, a Disagreements Table with rows for (a) whether the promisee acquires a claim-right independent of actual reliance damage; (b) whether the normative force survives the hypothetical dissolution of the supporting social practice; (c) whether the moral residue after breach—specifically the owed apology even when harm is fully compensated—indicates an unfulfilled directed duty or only a coordination-repair obligation; and (d) whether a binding promise can exist in a one-shot, anonymous interaction with no reputation effects and no induced reliance. Each row should present the Directed-Power formulation in wording that camp accepts, and the Reliance-Practice formulation in wording that camp accepts.

Third, identify the Cruxes: two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. Suggested cruxes are: (i) in a one-shot interaction with no audience and no prior reliance, does breaking a promise still wrong the promisee specifically, or is there no wrong at all? (ii) if the promisee never learns of the breach and no third party ever knows, leaving only the promiser’s private knowledge, does any moral failure remain, and if so, of what kind? (iii) can the distinction between breaking a promise and merely failing to confer a comparable benefit be maintained once all reliance and expectation effects are fully factored out?

Fourth, for each crux state what both sides agree counts as a relevant test case or decisive argument, and record each side’s predicted verdict in advance. Fifth, examine whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive: specifically, whether the disagreement over normative power versus institutional rule collapses once both sides accept that promises functionally coordinate reliance and generate directed expectations, leaving only a disagreement about whether that functional profile is best described with the vocabulary of irreducible authority or with that of reducible convention. If part is verbal, specify exactly which part and why. Sixth, conclude by stating the minimal revision each camp would need if the other camp’s predictions on the crux cases turned out correct, and rank those revisions by severity.
