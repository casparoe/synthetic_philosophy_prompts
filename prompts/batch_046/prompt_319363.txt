The exam question:
David Lewis analyzes convention as a regularity in behavior that solves a coordination problem, sustained by mutual expectation and common knowledge. Outline the core conditions of his account—the regularity itself, the preference for conformity on condition that others conform, the existence of an available alternative regularity, and the epistemic conditions. Then consider whether this framework can accommodate the evolution of norms in large populations with bounded rationality and partial observability, where agents observe only a local sample of others' behavior rather than the global pattern. Specifically, address whether weakening the common-knowledge requirement to "mutual belief up to degree k" preserves the arbitrariness of the convention, and whether evolutionary dynamics such as replicator dynamics in signaling games can substitute for Lewis's rational-expectation mechanism.

The student's answer:
Lewis defines a convention as a regularity R in a population P facing a recurring situation S, where (1) members conform to R, (2) they expect others to conform, (3) they prefer to conform if others do, (4) there is an alternative regularity R' that they would prefer if everyone followed it, and (5) all of this is common knowledge. The coordination problem arises because everyone wants to match others, yet there are multiple equilibria—driving on the right versus the left, for instance. Because the choice between R and R' is arbitrary, conventions depend on precedent and salience rather than on any intrinsic merit of the solution itself.

When we apply this to large populations with bounded rationality and partial observability, the common-knowledge condition becomes unrealistic. Agents only observe local neighbors, so they cannot possess common knowledge of the global regularity. However, this does not destroy Lewis's account; rather, it means we should weaken condition (5) to mutual belief up to degree 3, which Lewis himself proposed in later work on epistemic logic. Once this weakening is in place, the arbitrariness of the convention is preserved, since any local cluster can settle on R or R' independently of the others.

Moreover, evolutionary dynamics like replicator dynamics in signaling games can fully substitute for Lewis's rational-expectation mechanism. In these models, strategies spread through imitation and selection rather than through deliberation, yet they produce the same stable regularities. So Lewis's framework actually anticipates evolutionary norm evolution: the common-knowledge condition is merely an idealization that bounded-rationality agents approximate through repeated interaction. Therefore, partial observability strengthens rather than undermines Lewis's analysis, showing that conventions are simultaneously rational and evolutionary phenomena.
