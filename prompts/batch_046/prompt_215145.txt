I'm writing my thesis chaper on axiological minimalism and I keep coming back to this position—call it tranquilism—that only the absence of craving and suffering actually carries weight. Everything else is just noise, or so I want to argue. I'm trying to map out what principle could possibly makes sense of my gut reactions across cases, but I keep tying myself in knots.

My verdicts are these: a life of steady, mild contentment with no intense pleasure but also zero suffering and zero craving feels genuinely good, probably near-optimal. A life of constant high pleasure that's always accompanied by a subtle, restless craving for more feels bad, or at least not good. Someone who reaches total non-attachment through long practice, reports no suffering, and feels no particular joy either—that strikes me as a clear success, not a tragedy. But then there's this scenario my colleague made up: a hypothetical "desire-nullifier" compound that doesn't just suppress craving but seems to eliminate the very capacity for satisfaction, leaving the subject in a flat, neutral state with no suffering but also no possible relief. I think that's still better than suffering, but I can't tell if it's on a par with the meditation case or noticeably worse. There's also the comparison between two people: one with constant low-level background cravings they never notice, versus one who experiences occasional sharp cravings that are immediately satisfied. The first strikes me as worse off, which feels like it should be impossible if only absence matters. And I'm genuinely unsure about temporary suffering: if someone endures intense suffering for a year to achieve permanent, irreversible non-craving, does the temporary pain cancel out the gain, or is the net clearly positive?

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or do I have to drop one? I particularly need to know whether absence-of-craving and absence-of-suffering are reducible to each other, and what the principle predicts for artificial tranquilisation versus achieved non-attachment.
