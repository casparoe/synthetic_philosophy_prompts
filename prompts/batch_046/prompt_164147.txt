I am working on a seminar paper in philosophy of mind on synthetic cognition, and before I evaluate competing positions I need the full specification of what an adequate theory of intentional attribution to AI systems must deliver. I am specifically interested in applying the intentional stance to transformer-based language models, but I want the desiderata to be architecture-neutral enough that they do not collapse into mere commentary on current LLMs.

Write the requirements list: what any theory of belief-attribution to synthetic systems must get right to count as adequate, not merely heuristic. For each desideratum, include (i) the phenomenon or case it must handle—e.g., consistent false outputs across diverse prompting contexts, correction via fine-tuning or RLHF without weight-level change, the absence of biological teleology in engineered artifacts, instrumental success without explicit world-models, and the distinction between derivative intentionality (imposed by designers) and original intentionality; (ii) the consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a camp preference; and (iii) whether it is non-negotiable or negotiable depending on one’s metaphysical commitments.

In particular, address the following constructed problems that standard treatments tend to blur together:

- The stochastic commitment problem: a model repeatedly asserts p across semantically varied prompts, p is false, and the system possesses no internal verification mechanism. Must the theory classify this as belief, as a frozen correlation in parameter space, or as a distinct intermediate state? What criteria distinguish the three?
- The design-stance overlap: since every behavior is causally downstream of human engineering goals, what constraint must the theory include to prevent intentional attribution from collapsing into functional or design-stance explanation?
- The representational minimalism tension: small models show little emergent coherence, while larger ones display stable cross-contextual patterns that look belief-like. Must the theory require explicit, localized representations, or can distributed high-dimensional activation configurations—what I am calling distributed parameter realism—satisfy the representational condition?
- The correction asymmetry: after RLHF, the model revises its outputs but retains the same weights that originally generated the error. Does this demonstrate belief-revision, input-output remapping, or neither?

Then flag any pair or subset of desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable given what we know about transformer architectures and the intentional stance—particularly if requiring both biological teleology and predictive success, or both representational explicitness and empirical equivalence with black-box behavior. Identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the most popular accounts.

Finally, very briefly—no full scorecard—indicate how three positions fare against the list at their first failure point: a pure Dennett-style intentional-stance view applied without modification; an eliminativist view that denies beliefs altogether to non-biological systems; and the distributed parameter realism view mentioned above, which treats stable activation patterns across contexts as constitutive of synthetic belief. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the brief scoring is only to check consistency.
