Please argue that what is usually called “path dependence” in socio-technical and institutional development is largely an observational artifact produced by tracking symbolic and legal surfaces rather than functional equilibria, and that this illusion is especially clear in the evolution of land-tenure regimes in post-colonial settings where colonial statutory codes survived but enforcement architectures collapsed and were rebuilt through localized bargaining. Develop the claim that the persistence of colonial legal language—categories of ownership, registration procedures, and formal dispute forums—creates the appearance of a locked-in historical trajectory, even though the actual allocation rules, investment incentives, and conflict-resolution practices have converged on patterns closely resembling those that emerged organically in jurisdictions with no shared colonial origin. Address the standard counterfactual—whether the same material outcomes would have arrived absent the colonial path—by distinguishing “symbolic path dependence,” meaning the endurance of names, formal classifications, and legitimation narratives, from “functional path dependence,” meaning the endurance of allocative outcomes and behavioral regularities. Conclude that the domain in which path-dependence claims carry genuine explanatory weight should be restricted to symbolic legitimation, not to material institutional performance, and explain why this restriction undermines the normative force typically assigned to historical contingency in arguments for or against institutional reform. Your argument should treat at least one concrete mechanism—such as the decoupling of statutory title from de facto possession rights—through which formal continuity masks functional convergence, and it should specify what evidence would be required to show that a regime is genuinely functionally path-dependent rather than merely symbolically inherited.
