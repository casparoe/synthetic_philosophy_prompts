I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, yet I have come to hold a strong version of what I take to be Fernand Braudel’s longue durée view: that deep geographical, climatic, and structural conditions shape technological and social trajectories far more than individual agents or discrete events do. I apply this aggressively to computing — I believe the architecture of the internet, the shift to cloud infrastructure, and even the current trajectory of artificial intelligence are driven mainly by slow-moving forces such as the thermal geography of data centres, the physics of silicon lithography, and long-term energy distribution, rather than by any founder’s vision or a particular research breakthrough. I want a rigorous map of what could count against this position. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and write in British English. Structure your answer as follows:

1. Decisive defeaters: the observations, arguments, or historical or technological developments that should genuinely overturn or severely weaken this view, ordered from most to least damaging, including any that may already have occurred. (Approx. 250 words)

2. Merely awkward evidence: findings that are inconvenient but that a defender of the view could plausibly absorb through qualification or reframing. (Approx. 150 words)

3. Internal concessions: what advocates of a strong longue durée approach themselves admit counts against it, rather than only what critics claim. (Approx. 150 words)

4. Unique predictions and exposure: what this view predicts that rival, more agent-centred accounts do not, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability; if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a claim of this kind. (Approx. 200 words)

5. Bottom line: an honest assessment of whether the position is genuinely exposed to refutation or is overly protected, and whether that matters for its usefulness. (Approx. 100 words)

I am particularly interested in concrete cases where an individual decision or a sudden event clearly redirected a technological path in ways that slow structural forces alone could not have anticipated.
