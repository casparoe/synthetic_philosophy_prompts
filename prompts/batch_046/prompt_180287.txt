I recently started thinking about something I call "virtue amortization": if leisure has to be earned through labor, then shrinking working hours doesn't erase the moral debt—it forces people to pay it off faster, hour by hour, which means the same total virtue must be compressed into less time.

I'd like to practice arguing this through with someone who genuinely believes the Protestant work ethic still holds—that meaningful labor is a moral calling, and that rest is a reward rather than a right—so please take that role. Be sharp and serious, not a strawman; concede small practical points if they come up, but never abandon the core claim that work gives life its value and that unearned leisure is morally deficient. Speak in the first person, stay fully in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to about a paragraph so the exchange feels live.

Here is my opening move: if virtue amortization is correct, then the 35-hour week doesn't liberate workers; it intensifies the old ethic by demanding higher moral output per hour, which is exactly why karoshi—death from overwork—appears not as a contradiction of the work ethic but as its logical endpoint.
