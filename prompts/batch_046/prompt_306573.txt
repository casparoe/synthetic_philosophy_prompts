I am writing the final exam for an upper-level metaphysics seminar. I need one essay question on the special composition question—when, if ever, do parts compose a single object—designed so that a student cannot answer it by reciting the standard taxonomy of universalism, nihilism, and organism-based restriction. To force genuine analysis, inject the following framing: ask students to evaluate whether “functional-causal integration” can serve as a principled, restricted criterion for composition, using as a central test case a distributed digital swarm—thousands of autonomous drones linked only by wireless protocols, with no shared physical boundary or continuous causal contact, that collectively maintain a sustained, goal-directed behavior over time, with individual units constantly joining and leaving. The question should require them to apply this criterion to determine whether the swarm has parts that compose one object, and to confront whether the criterion escapes the boundary problem (where exactly does the swarm end if connectivity is graded?) and the problem of temporary parts (does turnover destroy identity?).

Please deliver:

1. The exam question itself, formatted for a 90-minute essay with a suggested length of about 750 words. The question should explicitly demand that students (a) state the special composition question precisely; (b) explain why extremal answers fail to match ordinary ontological judgments; (c) present functional-causal integration as a candidate restricted answer; (d) apply it to the swarm case, showing both its intuitive pull and its structural vulnerabilities; (e) compare this criterion with an organism-based restriction, assessing whether it collapses into universalism, nihilism, or an unprincipled list; and (f) defend a reasoned verdict on whether functional integration solves or merely relocates the problem.

2. A model answer of roughly 700–800 words that executes all six moves with conceptual precision—especially distinguishing causal interaction from functional coordination, and showing why boundary vagueness is not merely epistemic but metaphysically damaging to the proposal.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A-range answer (16–20) from a B-range answer (13–15) by naming the specific analytical moves that must be present and the specific failures that should cost marks. In particular, the scheme should penalize: treating functional integration as sufficient without addressing boundary vagueness; conflating the special composition question with the problem of the many; failing to distinguish temporary membership from part-replacement; and offering a verdict that does not follow from the analysis given.

Constraints: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not depend on obscure texts, page numbers, or verbatim quotations. The question must be answerable by a student with standard metaphysical training and reasoning ability, without access to external sources.
