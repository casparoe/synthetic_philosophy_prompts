Write a deadpan parody, approximately 700 words, formatted as the annual strategic report of a fictional institution called the Institute for Comprehensive Memory. The report should be written in the voice of an earnest, data-obsessed administrator who believes the institute has achieved a genuinely universal and neutral record of human experience. The satire should target the real-world rhetoric of archival completeness—particularly the way archives present structural silences (whose papers are never collected, whose languages are not translated, whose suffering is filed under incidental conditions) as accidental gaps rather than as products of power.

To keep the piece from collapsing into generic complaint, build the report around an invented curatorial framework called "provenance inversion": the institute's signature innovation is to treat every absence as a positive artifact. For each category of missing record—say, the correspondence of domestic workers, or the oral histories of a displaced community—the institute produces a full catalog entry describing the chain of institutional decisions that led to non-collection, assigns it an accession number, and displays the empty folder in a climate-controlled case. The administrator should describe this practice with pride, using the language of transparency, restorative justice, and metadata completeness.

Include at least three specific invented artifacts or catalog entries that illustrate the mechanism: for example, a merchant's ledger in which the entries regarding coerced labor have been physically excised and replaced with a curatorial note attributing the gap to "paper degradation in tropical storage conditions"; a woman's private journal catalogued solely under "domestic concerns, 1892-1904," with its political passages summarized in the metadata as "emotional weather"; and an empty display case for "Indigenous Land-Use Contracts, Pre-Settlement" accompanied by a wall text explaining that no such contracts existed because the legal framework did not recognize the signatories as contracting parties.

The humor and critique must arise entirely from the gap between the administrator's cheerful neutrality and what the reader can infer is being erased. Do not allow the narrator to wink, apologize, or break into explicit critique. By the end, a reader should understand that the archive's claim to totality is itself an exercise of power—specifically, that documenting absence with bureaucratic precision does not remedy the silence but formalizes it, granting the institution credit for having noticed what it continues to suppress.

Avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or references to specific real-world archives or theorists by name. The piece should stand on its own conceptual logic.
