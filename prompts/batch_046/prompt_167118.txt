I chair a three-person arbitration panel for a regional health authority that rules monthly on whether contested conditions should be listed as "pathological" for reimbursement, clinical-trial eligibility, and workplace-accommodation mandates. We have thirty minutes per case, no access to external specialists during the hearing, and our rulings are administratively binding. To reduce inconsistency, the panel has adopted a hybrid framework: a condition must satisfy at least one naturalist criterion—biological dysfunction relative to species-typical functional design—and at least one normativist criterion—experienced harm or impairment of widely shared human capabilities—without double-counting the same feature for both. We are not trying to settle the metaphysical debate; we need a reusable decision procedure.

Write an operational protocol my panel can run in order, with explicit stop-rules (where the answer is no), pass-through gates (where the answer is yes), and hand-off flags (where the question is one of values or policy rather than classification). The procedure must handle two specific edge-case classes that keep reaching us: "phenotypic buffering conditions," in which a biological dysfunction is fully masked by compensatory mechanisms and only manifests under particular environmental stressors, and "normative drift conditions," in which a biological profile remains within statistical functional norms but produces severe impairment because of rapid social or technological change. Define these briefly for board use, but do not treat them as excuses to abandon the framework.

Prescribe your response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Executive summary of the protocol (about 150 words).
2. The step-by-step procedure (about 500 words): ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the three stop-rules, the two hand-off flags, and the point where judgment is unavoidable.
3. Board-use definitions (about 200 words): biological dysfunction, harm/capability impairment, phenotypic buffering, normative drift, and the anti-double-counting rule.
4. Worked applications (about 250 words each):
   a. A clear affirmative case: a congenital deficiency in a metabolic enzyme that causes progressive tissue damage and is experienced as debilitating fatigue regardless of environment.
   b. A phenotypic buffering case: a polymorphism reducing pancreatic insulin reserve that produces no symptoms in lean, low-calorie environments but precipitates metabolic crisis under sustained caloric excess; petitioners seek pre-disease classification even for asymptomatic carriers.
   c. A normative drift case: a sensory-processing profile well within statistical population norms that causes no impairment in craft-based or rural economies but produces severe occupational disability in information-economy workplaces; petitioners seek full disease status and reimbursement, but the panel is predisposed to reject medicalizing a social-environment mismatch. Show where the procedure delivers an answer the panel will dislike and explain why.
5. Limits and misfire conditions (about 200 words): what the procedure explicitly cannot do, the cases where it will give contradictory outputs, and the residual judgments that must be made by a person rather than by the protocol.

Keep the language precise but free of unnecessary jargon; we are not philosophers by training. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Mark every point where the protocol requires a value judgment rather than a factual determination.
