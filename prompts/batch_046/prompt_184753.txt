I am revising the syllabus for my graduate survey in philosophy of mind, and I have allocated one fifty-minute session to illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Rather than rehearsing the familiar eliminativist caricature—Dennett as the philosopher who denies that anyone is conscious—I want the students to engage with the more nuanced position, particularly as articulated by contemporary illusionists, that phenomenal properties are real in some sense but are systematically misrepresented by introspection. To prevent the discussion from collapsing into the standard zombie-argument choreography, I intend to frame the problem around a specific conceptual tension: if phenomenal consciousness is indeed an illusion, one must explain not merely why it occurs, but why it is so remarkably stable and resistant to correction, given that subjects appear to have direct, first-personal access to the very properties that are allegedly mischaracterized. A colleague in cognitive science suggested using change-blindness and inattentional blindness as analogues, yet I fear these are now too familiar to first-years to carry the specific point about introspective misrepresentation rather than mere absence.

Please plan the teaching arc for this session. First, identify the conceptual dependencies that must be secured before illusionism can appear as a substantive thesis rather than a straightforward denial of experience—specifically, how the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness needs to be established, and why the transparency thesis matters here. Second, propose a single concrete scenario or thought experiment that can serve as the carrying example: it should illustrate the gap between what is introspectively available and what is phenomenally presented, without relying on the standard Fregean or scientific-reduction analogies, and it should make the misrepresentation claim visible rather than treating illusionism as a simple error theory. Third, map the misconceptions I should anticipate—such as the conflation of illusionism with eliminativism about consciousness simpliciter, the assumption that an illusion requires an intentional deceiver, or the belief that illusionism is self-refuting because the illusionist must be phenomenally conscious to assert it—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Fourth, specify what should be explicitly set aside at this level, whether particular neuroscientific details of introspection or the full metaphysics of representational content. Finally, suggest one concise diagnostic question I might ask at the close of the session to test whether the students have understood the specific structure of introspective misrepresentation, rather than merely parroting that qualia do not exist.
