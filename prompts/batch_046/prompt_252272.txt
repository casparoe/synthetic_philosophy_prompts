Many clinicians and bioethicists defend brain-death criteria by comparing the human body to an aircraft and the brain—or the conscious person—to its pilot. On this view, brain death is like a pilot bailing out: the fuselage (the body) may still be aloft on autopilot—ventilators and vasopressors keeping the heart beating and lungs moving—yet the pilot, the one who makes the flight meaningful, is irreversibly gone. From this, proponents draw two conclusions: that a brain-dead patient is already dead, not merely dying, and therefore that removing organs for transplantation does not violate the dead-donor rule, since one cannot kill what is already dead.

Audit this analogy carefully. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what in the biological case plays the role of the pilot, the aircraft, the autopilot system, bailing out, and the crash? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use—particularly whether the body retains integrative biological unity without a functioning brain, whether mechanical support is genuinely analogous to autopilot rather than to life-support sustaining a damaged but living organism, and whether the pilot can ever be “replaced” or “reintegrated” in ways that have no biological counterpart. Third, examine what the analogy quietly imports: does it presuppose a Cartesian separation of the person from the body? Does it treat consciousness as the sole bearer of moral status, and if so, does that commit the defender to conclusions about anencephalic infants or patients in permanent vegetative states that they might reject? Fourth, consider how the analogy interacts with the competing circulatory definition of death—if death is the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, then on this analogy the aircraft has not yet crashed, and the pilot’s absence alone does not constitute the plane’s destruction. Does that undermine the analogy’s support for brain-death criteria, or can it be patched?

Finally, say whether the two conclusions—that brain death is true death and that the dead-donor rule is therefore satisfied—actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If they do not, suggest a more accurate analogy for the relationship between brain function, bodily integration, and the boundary of death, and explain what work your replacement can do that the pilot-and-plane picture cannot.
