I have recently been appointed to a municipal heritage and planning committee, and in reviewing proposals to dismantle Victorian-era zoning barriers and alleyway easements, I have found myself caught between reformers who dismiss these structures as arbitrary relics and preservationists who invoke Chesterton's fence analogy without being able to specify the problem the barriers originally solved. This has made Burke's argument about prescription and inherited wisdom personally urgent rather than merely historical. I am now recording an episode for a philosophy podcast in which I will interview an unnamed political philosopher who defends a position she calls "institutional Chestertonianism": the claim that Burkean prescription should be read not as passive status-quo bias, but as an active epistemic burden on reformers—before removing any long-standing institution or regulation, one must reconstruct the functional problem it was designed to solve, and failure to do so should count as a decisive reason against removal, not merely a cautionary note. I would like a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond restating the view. Please include at least one question pressing on how this differs from straightforward risk aversion or status-quo bias; one asking what the view requires when the original problem has clearly disappeared but the institution persists; and one addressing whether the epistemic burden applies with equal force to institutions deliberately constructed to enforce racial or class exclusion. For each, provide a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—particularly if she distinguishes an institution's origin from its current function, or claims that some fences were never gates but walls. The sequence should build in rigour and end with a final question about what kind of historical or sociological evidence could, in principle, persuade her that a particular fence should come down despite our present inability to reconstruct its purpose. Please write the questions in British English, avoid generic biographical openings, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
