In my reporting on philosophy of mind, I have been tracking a recurring argumentative shape in debates over whether subjective experience—what is usually called qualia—constitutes a coherent posit at all. The pattern unfolds as follows. A defender insists there is a feature of consciousness, the “what-it’s-likeness” of seeing crimson or feeling pain, that no physical or functional description can capture. When pressed to specify exactly what this feature is, they reply that any characterization in terms of brain state, causal role, or behavioral disposition changes the subject; the feature is precisely what remains once all such descriptions are subtracted. A critic—working in the spirit of arguments that treat qualia as incoherent—then points out that this leaves the feature with no positive, intersubjective content: it is defined only negatively, as “not-physical,” and therefore cannot be compared across subjects, measured, or embedded in any explanatory law. Rather than conceding, the defender treats this very emptiness as proof of authenticity: they say the feature is known only by direct acquaintance, that its resistance to conceptual analysis demonstrates it is real and irreducible, and that critics confuse this resistance with incoherence. At the same time, ordinary explanations still invoke these features causally—“she recoiled because the pain was awful”—even though their content has just been declared inaccessible to any description that could figure in a causal account.

I am trying to determine whether philosophy of mind has a specific name for this loop, in which a concept’s lack of positive content is wielded by defenders as its greatest virtue and by critics as its fatal flaw. It is not quite the explanatory gap, which is usually framed as an epistemic limitation rather than a structural feature of the concept itself. It also differs from standard mysterianism, since the issue is not merely that we lack the cognitive tools to solve the problem, but that the problem’s formulation may depend on treating descriptive bankruptcy as a substantive property. Is there an established term for this particular dialectical move—defending a posit by celebrating its resistance to specification—or for the stalemate it produces? If no standard label exists, I would want a precise coinage with clear boundary conditions: how it differs from ordinary appeals to first-person authority, from genuinely primitive concepts that need no further analysis, and from simple equivocation between negative and positive characterization.
