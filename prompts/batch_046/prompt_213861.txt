I've been trying to map the conditions that make consent valid—focusing on deception, coercion, and competence—when a colleague proposed an argument that appears to prove no institutional consent can ever be fully valid. The steps run as follows:

(1) For consent to be valid, the consenter must explicitly authorize every materially necessary component of the act to which they consent; partial authorization is insufficient.
(2) Any complex institutional act—say, undergoing surgery or signing a financial contract—decomposes into an open-ended hierarchy of sub-acts: consenting to the surgery requires consenting to the anesthesia, which requires consenting to the specific drugs administered, which requires consenting to the injection, and so downward with no principled stopping point.
(3) Because the hierarchy is open-ended, complete authorization requires completing an infinite regress of nested consents.
(4) A finite agent cannot complete an infinite regress of explicit authorizations.
(5) Therefore, for any complex institutional act, there will always be some sub-act the agent has not explicitly authorized.
(6) By (1), this means the agent's consent to the overall act is not fully valid.
(7) Yet we routinely treat such consents as valid and binding.
(8) So (1) must be false, and valid consent does not require authorization of every sub-component.

The colleague claims this collapses the standard fully-informed-consent model into an impossible task. I want to preserve both the intuition behind (7)—that these consents genuinely are valid—and some meaningful version of (1), since it seems right that you cannot validly consent to surgery if you reject the anesthesia that makes it possible. Where exactly does the reasoning slip? Identify the problematic step or inference, explain why it initially looks plausible, and price the alternatives: what does abandoning (1) cost for our understanding of deception and hidden risks, and what does abandoning (2) or (3) cost for how we analyze the structure of complex actions? Please answer in American English.
