I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science and serve as the graduate-student representative on our university’s Curriculum and Standards Committee for the College of Engineering and Applied Sciences. In my seminar on inductive reasoning and scientific method, we have been examining the formal structure of no-free-lunch results and their frequent migration from theoretical computer science into broader arguments about rationality and prediction. This background has made me unusually sensitive to how philosophical claims about induction can be imported—sometimes carelessly—into administrative and curricular reasoning.

The computer science department has submitted a proposal to eliminate the required graduate course “Statistical Learning Theory and Inductive Bias” from the core curriculum for all machine-learning-track Ph.D. students. Their written justification, endorsed by several senior faculty, runs as follows: because no-free-lunch theorems establish that every learning algorithm achieves identical expected performance when averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, there is no epistemically privileged reason to privilege frequentist risk minimization, Bayesian inference with particular priors, or structural risk minimization over one another. They conclude that the choice among methods is therefore a purely pragmatic or engineering matter, and that requiring students to master the theoretical foundations of inductive bias amounts to imposing an arbitrary metaphysical commitment on empirical practice. The proposal requests that the course be replaced by an elective implementation workshop, with the change taking effect in the next academic year if approved by this committee.

Our committee must vote next Thursday. Several members—mostly from the engineering schools—are inclined to approve the change on the grounds that it removes an unnecessary theoretical barrier to interdisciplinary collaboration. The remaining members, including some philosophers and applied mathematicians, suspect the department has conflated a formal result about uniform distributions over problem spaces with a substantive claim about scientific rationality, but they have not yet articulated this concern in terms that address the engineers’ argument directly rather than simply appealing to disciplinary authority.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the elimination, reject it, or approve it only subject to a revised replacement requirement—together with a specific timeline or condition if the recommendation is conditional. The reasoning should take the CS faculty’s position seriously rather than dismissing it as a category error: in particular, it should examine whether their appeal to no-free-lunch genuinely supports the conclusion that inductive bias is arbitrary, and whether accepting that conclusion would commit them to a broader skepticism about empirical prediction that undermines the very research programs they seek to advance. Include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Write the memo in formal American English, suitable for circulation to both philosophers and computer scientists, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to sources the reader should consult.
