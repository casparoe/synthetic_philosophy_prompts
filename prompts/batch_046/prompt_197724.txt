For a philosophy-of-science seminar on cognition and artificial intelligence, I need the list of desiderata that a philosophical theory of inverse reinforcement learning—treated not as an engineering method but as an account of how observers infer human preferences from behavior—must satisfy to count as adequate. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, then mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (for instance: descriptive fidelity to observed choice patterns versus normative correction of inferred rewards; preservation of rational-optimization assumptions versus accommodation of systematic bias; uniqueness of the inferred reward function versus permissive underdetermination; and the demand that the account apply to non-strategic, habitual, or developmentally immature agents). In particular, address whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable given both the underdetermination of reward functions from behavior alone and the findings of behavioral economics on persistent deviation from rational choice—if there is an unavoidable conflict, identify which desiderata generate it and whether abandoning one rescues the others. I want the weight kept on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing algorithms, though a brief note on which approaches fail which non-negotiable conditions would help. Crucially, include a specific requirement about how the theory should handle recursive or self-correcting agents—those whose observed behavior includes attempts to identify and correct their own cognitive biases—since in such cases the standard IRL boundary between observer and observed collapses, and it becomes unclear whether the inferred preference should be corrected for the agent's own meta-cognitive adjustments or taken at face value. If satisfying this recursive-case condition forces the theory to abandon descriptive neutrality, say so and price the cost.
