I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's Neuroethics Review Board. We are reviewing a pilot-study proposal from a neurotechnology group that wants to test a non-invasive, closed-loop transcranial device designed to stabilize positive affect in participants regardless of external feedback—functionally a partial, embodied analog of the classic experience-machine scenario, though without virtual reality. The investigators defend it as therapy for persistent anhedonia; board members object that decoupling emotional states from real-world achievement undermines well-being rather than promoting it. By next Friday we must circulate a written opinion recommending approval, conditional approval, or rejection, with a concrete timeline and monitoring criteria. Draft the memo: recommend a specific decision, engage seriously with the hedonist claim that sustained positive affect just is well-being, address the objective-list worry that such decoupling eliminates conditions for genuine accomplishment, and treat honestly the strongest argument for the side you reject. Frame the core tension not as a standard hedonism-versus-eudaimonia debate, but through the niche concept of affective calibration—the idea that well-being requires emotional states to retain epistemic and motivational tracking of external conditions—while explicitly noting where that framing is weakest.
