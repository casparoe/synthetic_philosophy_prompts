Student answer: "The study's distinction between descriptive and normative divergence is important. Descriptive divergence—different judgments about culpability in moral luck cases—is not fatal to the evidential use of intuition, because reflective equilibrium permits revising particular intuitive judgments when they conflict with well-supported principles. However, once we recognize normative divergence—differences in when people think intuition should be revised—we can accommodate cross-cultural variation without relativism. Each culture applies its own meta-intuitive norms within a local reflective equilibrium, yielding coherent but culturally indexed results. Therefore, the disagreement about moral luck is not a contradiction but a difference in calibration parameters: Western respondents apply a high weight to outcome severity, while East Asian respondents apply a relational-context filter, much like using different units of measurement. Consequently, philosophers should not treat one culture's intuitions as erroneous, but should build pluralistic theories that incorporate both calibration schemes."
