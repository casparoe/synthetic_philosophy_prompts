I'm working out when epistemic bootstrapping counts as legitimate reciprocal support rather than vicious circularity, and my judgments on cases feel somewhat inconsistent. A geologist uses radiometric dating to establish a rock's age, then appeals to the coherence of that result with stratigraphic ordering to strengthen confidence in the dating method: acceptable reciprocal calibration, not a cheat. A person with perfect recall remembers that they correctly remembered yesterday's weather, and uses that to establish they have perfect recall: clearly circular and illegitimate. Someone verifies a calculator's result by mental arithmetic, then verifies mental arithmetic with the calculator, treating convergence as mutual confirmation: I think this is bad, but I can't explain why it's worse than the geologist case. A scientist calibrates an instrument against a standard, then uses the calibrated instrument to check whether the standard was contaminated; if the check comes back clean, she takes it as partial confirmation of the calibration: this feels okay, yet structurally it resembles the calculator loop. A novice chess player trusts a grandmaster's evaluation, then uses that evaluation to form a judgment about the grandmaster's reliability, then uses that reliability judgment to accept further evaluations: benign bootstrapping. But if she begins by treating the grandmaster as infallible and uses every correct evaluation to confirm that infallibility, it feels empty. Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes acceptable reciprocal calibration from vicious circularity, explains why the chess case works but the infallibility assumption doesn't, and decides whether the calculator loop should be grouped with the geologist or with the memory case? If no single principle saves all these verdicts, tell me which one I should abandon.
