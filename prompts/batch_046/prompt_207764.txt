Write a sestina in the voice of a longtime party activist who is only now noticing that the organization's vocabulary has replaced her own moral judgment, using Simone Weil's specific critique of political parties — the manufacturing of collective passion, the subordination of individual conscience to group dynamics, and the substitution of justice by partisan loyalty — as the conceptual engine rather than as a text to summarize. Do not quote or cite any particular essay; instead, make the philosophical content do structural work through the form.

Use exactly these six end-words in the standard rotating sestina pattern: line, vote, machine, conscience, we, truth. In stanzas 1–3, deploy each word in contexts that sound like collective virtues (the line is disciplined, the vote is democratic, the machine runs smoothly, we are united, conscience is collective, truth is shared doctrine). In stanzas 4–6 and in the envoi, let the identical words reveal their suppressive function (the line erases doubt, the vote replaces private judgment, the machine thinks in place of the speaker, we dissolves the individual, conscience is silenced, truth is loyalty). The enforced return and rotation of the end-words should enact Weil's claim that the repeated vocabulary of collective action becomes a mechanism that drowns out private attention.

The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with Weil's argument recognizes the particular diagnosis — not a generic complaint about politics — and the inversion of the six terms should mirror the shift from apparent solidarity to concealed coercion. Keep the diction formal but not archaic, and avoid attributing specific phrases to named works. After the poem, provide exactly one final line beginning with the word Mechanism: that names the formal feature of the sestina performing the philosophical work and states in one clause what that feature enacts.
