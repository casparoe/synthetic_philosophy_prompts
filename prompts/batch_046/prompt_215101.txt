Since I began volunteering at a sanctuary that cares for severely cognitively impaired companion animals alongside genetically modified research mammals, the standard framing of animal ethics and speciesism has begun to feel inadequate to me. I need a rigorous specification of what any adequate theory of interspecies moral status must actually be able to do—not a survey of named philosophers, but the desiderata themselves, with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and a clear judgment about which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely camp-specific preferences.

Please lay out the full requirements list. The theory should accommodate radical intra-species cognitive variation—for instance, a human with profound neurodegeneration and an unaltered conspecific with typical capacities—without either denying status to the former on capacity grounds or extending it to the latter solely by species membership. It should preserve a firm distinction between possessing moral status at all and possessing equal moral weight, and maintain that distinction consistently across species lines. It must remain consistent with evolutionary biology’s continuous distribution of cognitive traits, refusing any binary threshold—language, mirror self-recognition, tool fabrication—as the gatekeeper for status. It should address synthetic or engineered cases, such as a non-human mammal with sustained human-level cognitive capacities through neural augmentation, without automatically conferring full human-equivalent rights or withholding them purely on original taxonomic grounds. It needs to explain why inflicting suffering on any sentient being is wrong regardless of species, while allowing that different capacities can ground qualitatively different obligations rather than merely scalar differences in value. Finally, it must avoid collapsing entirely into either relational accounts, which struggle to ground obligations to wild strangers, or pure intrinsic-capacity accounts, which struggle to explain obligations to severely disabled companions.

For each desideratum, explain why it is required and identify the kind of theory it excludes. Then indicate which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the dominant popular approaches—whether contractarian, aggregative utilitarian, or strong rights-based egalitarian—and whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given current biological or philosophical constraints. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named proponents.
