I am writing a feature on the boundary between legend and history, and I want to test whether my own judgments on four well-known cases hang together or hide an inconsistency. Here are my verdicts.

First, the Trojan War. Late Bronze Age Hittite diplomatic texts reference conflicts involving Wilusa and Ahhiyawa in ways that align with Homeric geography; the destruction layer at Hisarlik corresponds to a violent end of a fortified western Anatolian settlement around the correct period; and the Greek epic tradition preserves specific political relationships and place-names that do not appear invented. My verdict: there is a genuine historical kernel—an actual conflict centered on that settlement—even though the wooden horse, divine interventions, and heroic biographies are clearly later accretions.

Second, King Arthur. The name Artorius or Arthur surfaces in ninth-century Welsh annals and in the Historia Brittonum as a battle leader, possibly in the late fifth or sixth century, but these texts are separated by centuries from the events they claim to record. Every element that makes Arthur culturally distinctive—the Round Table, the Grail quest, the chivalric code, Camelot, Lancelot—emerges only in twelfth-century romance. There is no independent contemporary documentation or material trace specific to those events. My verdict: this does not become history. At best there is an unverifiable name embedded in origin folklore.

Third, the Exodus. There is no Egyptian administrative record of a mass departure of six hundred thousand people; archaeological surveys of the Sinai peninsula reveal no trace of large-scale forty-year encampment; yet there is evidence of Semitic labor in the eastern Delta, and the Merneptah Stele attests an entity called Israel in Canaan by roughly 1208 BCE. My verdict: despite these contextual correlations, the Exodus remains legend—specifically an origin myth—because its scale and narrative architecture are incompatible with the expected archival and material record, and the indirect parallels are insufficient to anchor the specific event.

Fourth, the founding of Carthage. Archaeology confirms Phoenician settlement at Carthage from around 814 BCE. Roman and later traditions preserve the story of a Tyrian queen—Elissa or Dido—who led the colonization. No direct extra-traditional record of this specific queen survives, but the settlement date, the Phoenician origin, and the pattern of western Mediterranean colonization are independently attested. My verdict: here there is a historical kernel. A real founding leader is plausible, and the social process is documented, even though the romantic details—the oxhide boundary, the suicide—are literary.

My working principle is what I call convergent independent transmission: a legend crosses into history when specific, non-obvious details in the narrative are independently corroborated by documentary, archaeological, or demographic sources that do not derive from the narrative tradition itself.

This principle captures Troy and Carthage, and it excludes Arthur. But I am uneasy about the Exodus. The Semitic labor presence, the Merneptah Stele, and the settlement patterns are all independent of the biblical narrative, yet I still reject the Exodus. Is my rejection actually driven by a second clause I have not articulated—perhaps what I should call archive-negative inference, where an event of a given scale and type should have left traces in archives whose preservation biases are well understood, and the absence of those traces undermines even indirect correlations? If I add that clause, I must ask whether it forces me to overturn any other verdict. For instance, sub-Roman Britain also has poor preservation conditions, so the absence of Arthur-specific traces may not license the same inference. Does that mean Arthur and the Exodus are not parallel after all, or does it mean I should be more agnostic about Arthur?

Please take a committed position rather than surveying options. Either defend a single refined principle—stating precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it—that accommodates all four verdicts; or argue that consistency requires overturning one of them, identifying which and why. Then apply the principle to a case I have not listed: the narrative of the Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon, where a real South Arabian kingdom existed, the story appears in two independent traditions—Hebrew and Ethiopian—yet there is no direct archaeological or extra-biblical record of the visit itself. Be explicit about which of my original four judgments your principle cannot save, if any.
