I serve on my university’s Joint Governance Board for Synthetic Biology and Intellectual Property. Our technology-transfer office is preparing a disclosure and preliminary patent filing for a synthetic gene circuit—designated Project LexiGene—developed in our molecular engineering laboratory. The construct employs an expanded nucleic-acid alphabet (including synthetic bases X and Y) together with a fully reassigned codon table, so that the bacterial host does not produce a conventional polypeptide but instead traverses a discrete sequence of metabolic states in response to chemical inputs: the DNA functions as a state-transition table for a simple finite-state automaton executed inside living cells. The inventors contend that this is not merely a biochemical mechanism that carries information, but literally a programming language compiled into nucleic acids; they want the filing to adopt software-patent framing and to describe the sequence as a “code” in the same robust sense that source code is text. The board is divided: some officers warn that accepting the language-and-code framing could trigger stricter export-control classifications for digital information, while others treat the terminology as harmless descriptive marketing. We must vote next week.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before the meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action—endorse the framing, reject it, or endorse it subject to specific conditions—with an explicit procedural step or deadline (for instance, requiring a supplementary conceptual review prior to filing). The reasoning should engage seriously with the philosophical question of whether DNA, especially in this engineered form, is genuinely a code rather than a causally correlated biochemical signal. In particular, examine whether the synthetic system exhibits properties that have been central to debates about genetic information—arbitrary pairing between symbol vehicle and meaning, the capacity to refer to conditions not currently present, and open-ended combinatorial productivity—and whether satisfying or failing these conditions should determine our institutional framing. Treat honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side: if you recommend rejecting the language framing, explain why the inventors’ comparison to software might still capture something important about the system’s causal architecture; if you recommend endorsing it, explain why the biochemical-mechanism view might remain the more defensible regulatory default despite the engineered syntax. The document should be actionable by the board and should not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
