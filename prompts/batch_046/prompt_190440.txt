For a philosophical reading group I have been asked to write down my profile on collective minds so that others may examine its joints. I come to this recently, after retiring from a career in neuroethology, and I have been working through Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue" not as literature alone but as a serious intuition pump about distributed cognition. Here is where I stand.

I am a functionalist about mental representation: what makes a state representational is its causal-functional role within a system, not the substrate that realizes it. I am not, however, a functionalist about phenomenal consciousness. I hold that consciousness requires a specific mode of biological integration—something like the recurrent thalamocortical dynamics found in mammals—and that this is why there is something it is like to be a bat but not to be a thermostat. I take the colony-level behaviors in the ant-fugue analogy—optimization of foraging paths, nest repair, defensive coordination—to be genuinely predictive only when we treat the colony as an agent with distributed representations. Specifically, I am inclined to view the pheromone trail network as functioning analogously to a sparse, distributed population code, similar in formal structure (though not in biological medium) to sparse coding in sensory cortex; the colony thereby maintains a global state vector that no individual ant encodes. I therefore attribute a form of collective cognition, or "colony-level intentionality," to an anthill, but I deny that there is anything it is like to be Aunt Hillary, since the colony lacks the unified phenomenal field that biological integration produces. I hold that emergence here is a real explanatory relation, not an epistemic gap: colony-level mental descriptions figure in autonomous laws that are not reducible without predictive loss to accounts of individual ant neurophysiology and stigmergic feedback alone. At the same time, I accept that the evidential standard for attributing cognition should be behavioral and predictive—if modeling the colony as an agent improves explanation, we are warranted in the attribution regardless of substrate. Finally, I think that if an artificial swarm reproduced the colony's interaction topology and behavioral profile, we ought to extend the same cognitive attribution to it, though by my view on consciousness we should still deny it phenomenal experience.

Please audit these commitments. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear so. For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me, and then tell me which single revision—were I forced to make only one—would restore the most overall consistency. Treat this as an audit of my position, not as a general lecture on collective consciousness, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
