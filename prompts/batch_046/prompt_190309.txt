I am a retired structural geologist who has recently turned to philosophy of science and archaeology, and I am trying to formalize my intuitions about when material remains legitimately license inferences about past beliefs and social institutions. Below are seven concrete verdicts I hold about such inferences; they seem largely consistent, yet I suspect at least one pair is hard to reconcile without either ad hoc complexity or abandoning a judgment. I want a precise principle, or a small competing set, that captures the pattern, together with its predictions for cases I have not listed and an honest accounting of which verdicts it must sacrifice.

First, a hoard of standardized clay accounting tokens—uniform in shape, graded in size, recovered exclusively from administrative buildings in a Bronze Age settlement—strongly confirms an institutionalized system of economic record-keeping, even absent any deciphered writing.

Second, the near-complete absence of defensive architecture in a settlement that nonetheless contains abundant weapons in burial contexts does not, in my view, confirm a pacifist social institution; I treat this absence as silent, likely reflecting geography or resource allocation rather than a shared normative belief in non-violence.

Third, a single anomalous artifact—say, a Roman denarius recovered in a stratigraphic layer that predates sustained interregional contact—should not overturn a reconstructed institutional isolation of the local economy; it should be explained as accidental intrusion rather than evidence of integrated trade institutions.

Fourth, repeated canonical iconography on ritual vessels found across multiple distant sites does confirm a shared cultic institution with standardized representational norms, not merely independent local practices that happen to converge.

Fifth, the presence of elaborate burial goods for children that precisely mirror adult mortuary treatments does confirm that the culture’s institutionalized beliefs about post-mortem care extend to the young; I reject the alternative that these goods serve solely for the grief-management of survivors.

Sixth, the discovery of a large-scale, coordinated irrigation network in the absence of any centralized storage or redistribution facilities should not be taken to confirm a hierarchical bureaucratic institution; distributed cooperation among kin groups seems sufficient, and I resist inferring centralized authority from engineering scale alone.

Seventh, however, the systematic absence of child-sized specialized tools in a culture where adults possess highly differentiated craft instruments does seem to me to confirm an institutional division of labor that formally excludes the young—even though this, like case two, is an inference drawn from absence.

The tension I perceive lies between my willingness to derive institutional conclusions from positive pattern and presence (cases one, four, five), my refusal to do so from negative space or engineering complexity (cases two, six), and my willingness in case seven to treat absence as institutionally meaningful after all.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of my question. Specifically: state one precise principle, or a small competing set, that accommodates as many verdicts as possible; for each clause, cite the motivating case; predict the principle’s verdicts for at least two further scenarios I have not mentioned—perhaps a culture with standardized weights but no market exchange, or one with widespread literacy implements but no surviving texts; and state honestly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot save without ad hoc complexity, indicating whether you think that verdict should be overturned.
