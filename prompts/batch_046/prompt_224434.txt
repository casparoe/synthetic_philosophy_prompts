I have adopted a refined version of the view sometimes associated with Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: that human extinction is catastrophically bad for those now living not primarily because of the deaths involved, but because it severs the narrative continuity on which the meaning of present human lives depends. On my version, the harm is not proportional to the number of potential future people who are never born, but to the structural feature that our projects, attachments, and achievements are designed to extend beyond us and be taken up by successors. If humanity ended painlessly and instantly, the wrong done to the living would lie in rendering all present striving permanently incomplete—like a sentence interrupted before its period—regardless of how many generations are lost. I want an honest map of how exposed this position is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which specific cases or discoveries—say, about locally generated meaning, about artificial systems that perpetuate human values without biological continuity, or about isolated communities that flourish without any expectation of succession—would count as decisive rather than merely awkward? Which formal results in population ethics or decision theory, such as those surrounding the non-identity problem or the aggregation of reasons across persons, threaten the claim that narrative coherence can ground extinction's badness without collapsing into a form of total utilitarianism? What predictions does this view make that rival accounts—those grounding the harm in lost welfare, violated rights, or cosmic significance—do not? Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank the threats from fatal to uncomfortable, and say whether the position is well insulated by qualifications, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
