John Stuart Mill died in 1873, long before any of the economic experiments he cautiously discussed in his later years could be attempted. Imagine he is presented with a contemporary proposal for coordination that did not exist in his lifetime: "federated algorithmic planning," in which worker-owned cooperatives share production data through a decentralized network of predictive models that allocate resources without either market prices or a centralized state bureaucracy, with every cooperative able to audit and adjust the algorithmic weights that guide distribution. The proponents claim this preserves voluntary association—no cooperative is compelled to join—while achieving the coordination benefits Mill associated with socialist organization. Drawing on Mill's actual late commitments—his defense of experimental socialism, his insistence that liberty and individuality must survive any economic transformation, his deep skepticism of bureaucratic centralization, and his belief that competitive incentives serve a moral as well as an economic function—predict how he would assess this system. Specifically: (1) Would he regard algorithmic transparency and democratic auditing of the models as sufficient safeguards against the bureaucratic domination and conformity he feared? (2) Would he accept that predictive forecasting can substitute for price signals in preventing waste and exploitation, or would he insist that direct competition among producers remains indispensable? (3) Given that participation is technically voluntary but functionally necessary for economic survival, does this arrangement fall closer to his ideal of free cooperation or to the compulsory state socialism he rejected? Answer by reconstructing Mill's reasoning from his known principles rather than offering a modern endorsement, and indicate which of his commitments he would most likely have to relax if he were to approve the scheme.
