I recently read a summary by a popular science writer discussing why individual choices—such as voting in a general election or giving up meat—are often said to be morally pointless in large societies. The writer described something called the inefficacy objection, explaining that when thousands or millions of people are involved, any single person's action is too small to change the final result. According to the summary, philosophers have split into two clear camps: those who accept that this removes any direct moral duty to act, and those who defend individual action on the basis of "expressive value", which the writer defined as using one's behaviour to send a message to other people about one's values.

The writer then applied this reasoning to climate change, arguing that activists who urge people to reduce their personal carbon footprint are making a logical error. They claimed that if every resident of Britain stopped flying for a year, airlines would simply lower prices to fill the seats with other travellers, so the total number of flights and therefore the emissions would barely change. From this, they concluded that voting and eating plants are only ever justified as personal virtue or self-expression, never because they genuinely alter collective outcomes.

I have been turning this over, and parts of it feel convincing, but other parts seem suspiciously neat. Is this summary fair? I am particularly unsure whether the inefficacy objection really shows that one's contribution is literally zero in every relevant sense, or whether it is being confused with being too small to notice on its own. I also wonder whether "expressive value" is being reduced to mere signalling, and whether the airline pricing example actually addresses the right kind of collective harm—perhaps it confuses one market mechanism with a different kind of social coordination problem. Could you please set out which parts of this account are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which subtly misrepresent the underlying philosophical debate? I would be grateful for the answer to be written in British English.
