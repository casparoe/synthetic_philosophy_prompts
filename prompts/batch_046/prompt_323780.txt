The identity of indiscernibles—Leibniz’s claim that no two distinct substances can share all properties—has faced its most enduring challenge in the symmetric-universe thought experiment usually associated with Black: two spheres, identical in every intrinsic quality, suspended in an otherwise empty space with only a distance relation between them. For decades the exchange has followed a familiar rhythm. Defenders invoke spatial or temporal relations, or the “weak discernibility” afforded by irreflexive relations like being-two-meters-from; critics reply that if space is genuinely relational rather than a container, those relations either collapse or fail to ground distinctness without circularity. Meanwhile some metaphysicians treat the scenario as metaphysically impossible, others as obviously possible, and neither camp seems able to move the other.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement in disguise, nor simply a verbal dispute over the word “property.” Rather, I suspect the stalemate reflects a deeper divergence about what an ontology must deliver: one side demands that every individual be uniquely identifiable by qualitative features alone, while the other accepts that identity can be primitive or structurally fixed, with “indiscernibility” being a feature rather than a bug. But I am unsure whether that diagnosis is right, or whether there is instead a hidden formal question—perhaps about the conditions under which a relational system can have automorphisms without collapsing into a single object—that neither side has fully articulated.

Please diagnose the disagreement: is it substantive or verbal; do the sides differ about facts, about standards of metaphysical explanation, or about bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for? Are they answering different questions—one about descriptive completeness of theories, one about the identity conditions of concrete objects—under the single heading “identity of indiscernibles”? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would a formal result about structural automorphisms move anyone; a clarification of whether relational properties count as “intrinsic” in a technically defined sense; or an argument that quantum-mechanical indistinguishability either vindicates or undermines the classical intuition behind the spheres? I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of positions, and I want an honest answer about whether anything could break the impasse, or whether the debate is structurally unresolvable.
