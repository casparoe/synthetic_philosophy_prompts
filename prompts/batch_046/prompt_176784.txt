Take the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 in England and analyze it through the competing lenses of microhistory and history-from-below versus elite and state-centered historiography. Begin with the standard narrative reconstructed from parliamentary rolls, royal chronicles, and the judicial proceedings against the named leaders: treat this as the state-archive version, and lay out precisely what it explains well (the geographic spread, the targeting of specific officials, the rapid suppression) and what it systematically obscures (the composition of the crowds, the local sequencing of uprisings, and the motives of participants who left no signed records). Next, examine what a history-from-below reconstruction must work with—the near-total absence of documents produced by the rural laborers themselves, the mediation of every popular demand through clerks and chroniclers with institutional stakes, and the exceptional fragments such as the letters associated with John Ball or ballads recorded in later decades. Map where these fragments allow genuine microhistorical recovery of local mentalities (for example, through patterns in manorial court rolls or tax-resistance records) and where they remain isolated curiosities that risk being over-read as representative of collective consciousness. Then focus on a direct contradiction between the two archives: whether the rebels articulated a universal principle of abolition of serfdom or pursued targeted, locality-specific grievances, noting which archive supports which reading and why the evidentiary asymmetry makes adjudication difficult. Finally, turn the episode back onto the theory: does the extreme scarcity of subaltern voices in this case show that microhistory is necessarily parasitic on elite documentation, or does it reveal a conceptual mistake in assuming that collective agency must leave individual textual traces in order to be real? Conclude by stating clearly whether this episode strengthens or weakens the stronger ontological claim—that history from below offers a genuinely different account of historical causation rather than merely a moral or emotional supplement to the state-centered narrative.
