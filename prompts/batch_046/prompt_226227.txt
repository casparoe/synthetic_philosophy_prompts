I'm giving a job-market talk next month in a history and philosophy of science department, and I'm defending a thesis that sounds, at first pass, like a rehabilitation of Whig history—except it is not. The core claim is that writing the past as progress toward the present is not a correctable cognitive bias or a historiographical vice that can be eliminated through methodological hygiene; rather, it is a structurally necessary condition for explanatory narrative coherence. Any account that makes past scientific practices intelligible to present agents must implicitly index its significance conditions to the conceptual framework of the present, and the standard remedies—microhistory, the symmetry principle in science studies, or following the actors—do not actually purge the teleology; they merely displace it into the prior selection of which actors, practices, and archival traces count as worthy of reconstruction. I developed part of this argument in response to long-running discussions on rationalist forums about whether updating on an inside view can ever be fully separated from outcome-based evaluation, and I am worried that my framing sounds like armchair cognitive science to an audience that values archival specificity and is tired of grand narratives.

The audience is a mixed room: mostly social historians of science who are post-positivist and reflexively suspicious of progress narratives, plus two philosophers of science who think the Strong Programme went too far in bracketing rational evaluation, and one senior historian of the Scientific Revolution who has written extensively on anachronism. They are skeptical of anything that sounds like it lets presentism off the hook, weary of reflexive anti-Whig posturing that amounts to little more than methodological virtue-signaling, and they will press me hard on whether my view collapses into a full-blown presentism that makes rational comparison across historical periods impossible. They do not read decision theory, and analogies to updateless reasoning will fall flat unless translated into historiographical terms.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the six or seven objections I will actually get from this specific room—not the ones I would face at a philosophy of history conference or from a rationalist forum audience—ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do. Each objection should be phrased the way an archival-minded social historian or a skeptical philosopher of science would actually put it, not in generic form. For each, give me a two-minute reply that concedes the point where I genuinely must concede and holds firm where I can, followed by a brief note on where the reply is weakest and where I risk sounding like I am special-pleading. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that forces me to defend whether my structural-necessity claim licenses exactly the kind of popular progress narratives I claim I am not defending—and sketch a brief reply strategy for it. Make sure the objections target the claim that teleology is structurally necessary rather than merely common, and that they press me on whether my argument undermines the normative resources historians need to criticize bad science in the past.
