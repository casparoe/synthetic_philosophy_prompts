As a journalist writing about the pedagogical design of philosophical debate, I am developing a feature on how institutional simulations can expose hidden commitments in punishment theory. For this research, I need a runnable classroom activity for a graduate ethics seminar of about sixteen students, confined to a single fifty-five-minute session, that makes participants inhabit the practical tension between retributive desert and consequentialist optimization rather than merely discuss it abstractly. Please design a structured deliberation game—complete with explicit rules, assigned roles (for example, allocation officers with different institutional mandates, case reviewers, and silent observers), a timed schedule broken into rounds, and a non-monetary payoff scheme using points that determine which collective narrative the group must defend at the end—centered on distributing a fixed portfolio of institutional responses (formal exclusion, a restorative obligation, monitored reintegration, or deliberate inaction) across a sequence of presented offender profiles. The critical injected mechanism: in each round, every participant must register a binding allocation decision and articulate the principle behind it before learning whether the offender possessed full rational agency, was severely cognitively compromised, or was factually innocent; only after this commitment is the underlying status revealed, forcing the group to decide collectively whether to maintain the allocation on backward-looking desert grounds or revise it based on forward-looking social utility. The debrief should map specific moments in the rounds onto characteristic commitments of retributivism and consequentialism, be explicit about which metaphysical feature—such as the reality of libertarian free will—the simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator, and identify what will predictably go wrong in the first run (for instance, participants refusing to commit before information is revealed, or collapsing all reasoning into affective empathy) together with precise adjustments to correct it. The prompt should contain no requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should be answerable entirely from reasoning and general philosophical knowledge without online retrieval.
