For a seminar on self-reference and personal identity, I am assembling a set of creative exercises that require students to engage with Douglas Hofstadter's conception of the I as a strange loop — that is, a self-referential symbol that denotes the pattern of cognitive activity producing the symbol itself, rather than pointing to a fixed substrate. To avoid the usual tropes of artificial consciousness, I would like a short story set in an imagined Institute for Recursive Taxonomy, where scholars do not study objects directly but maintain layered catalogues of how previous catalogues have classified those objects. The protagonist should be a meta-cataloguer responsible for auditing descriptions of other auditors' systems, who stumbles upon a file that appears to be describing her own present act of comprehension in real time — including her affective response to being described — in such a way that the description cannot be relegated to a higher meta-level without losing its referential closure. The narrative should dramatise the tension between interpreting this event as the emergence of a genuine strange-loop self (where the symbol I acquires autonomous reference to the loop it inhabits) and interpreting it as a merely hierarchical feedback glitch that dissolves upon reclassification. Please write the story in polished British English, approximately 800–1000 words, maintaining a contemplative, conceptually precise tone rather than a thriller-like register. Do not include bibliographic references, citation requests, or explicit mentions of Hofstadter's texts within the story's dialogue or narration; instead, let the institutional architecture and the protagonist's reasoning embody the philosophical problem. The conclusion should remain genuinely ambiguous about whether a self has formed, leaving the reader with the structural question rather than a definitive answer.
