I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Frank Ramsey's 1925 essay on meaning—specifically his two-step structure in which the spatial and temporal scale of the universe makes cosmic significance conceptually impossible, but in which significance is then reconstructed locally without collapsing into consolation or hedonism. My friend kept sliding between reading Ramsey as a proto-existentialist celebrating absurd freedom and reading him as offering a stoic consolation prize, and I want to prevent exactly those slides in a session I am leading for a small reading group of first-year philosophy students who have just finished Camus and now think they understand meaninglessness. Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself: what prior distinction—perhaps between significance requiring an external comparator versus being capable of being intrinsic—must be in place before the problem even registers for them; the one concrete example or thought experiment that should carry the idea, with explicit guidance on what it must include (the experience of scale) and what it must exclude (anything that lets them confuse local importance with cosmic importance); the misconceptions they will bring and form, in the order they will surface—the nihilist misreading that nothing matters, the hedonist misreading that only immediate pleasure remains, and the more subtle misreading that modern cosmology's finite-but-unbounded universe undermines Ramsey's empirical premise rather than his conceptual one—together with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off and how to absorb it without derailing the arc; a check for understanding that would show they have grasped the structural point rather than merely feeling reassured; and what to deliberately leave out at this level, such as any technical connection to Ramsey's work on infinite sets or the psychoanalytic context of the essay's composition, with an explicit note that these are being omitted. I want the plan to be rigorous enough that the idea lands as philosophy rather than self-help, but accessible enough that students without mathematical training can follow the dependency structure.
