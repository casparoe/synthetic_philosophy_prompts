In a recent policy discussion about whether current great-power rivalry should be read through the Munich analogy, the Vietnam analogy, or Graham Allison’s Thucydides Trap framework, a participant shut down the comparison with the line “History doesn’t repeat itself, but it often rhymes.” The slogan seemed to be doing contradictory work: it was offered both as a warning against treating the present as a literal rerun and as a license to draw structural analogies anyway. I want the slogan disambiguated.

Can you lay out the menu of precise theses it could express? Please distinguish at least the following dimensions: (i) the level at which repetition is denied and “rhyming” is affirmed—surface events, causal-structural relations (e.g., rising-power fear dynamics), rhetorical framing, or outcome-types; (ii) whether the claim is descriptive (about historical patterns), epistemic (about our ability to recognize them), or normative (about what license we have to act on analogies); and (iii) whether “rhymes” implies a specific theory of analogical reasoning—say, a surface-feature-matching model versus a relational-structure-mapping model (of the kind developed in cognitive-science work on analogy)—and if so, which analogies (Munich, Vietnam, Thucydides) that theory would actually vindicate.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, false, or incoherent, and—crucially—which of those readings actually supports the argumentative move the speaker was making (dismissing literal repetition while keeping analogical reasoning alive). Also indicate which reading people who invoke this line in geopolitical debates most likely intend, and whether that intended reading survives the precisification. I do not need citations, author attributions, or reading lists; I just need the disambiguation with verdicts, and a clear statement of which precisifications leave Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides Trap standing as valid guides to present action.
