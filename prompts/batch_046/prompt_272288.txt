Having retired after three decades in experimental physics, I have spent the past two years working through philosophy of medicine and psychiatry, and I find myself returning repeatedly to Thomas Szasz's thesis that mental illness is a myth—more precisely, that conditions classified as psychiatric disorders are best understood as problems in living rather than as diseases with detectable lesions or mechanistic dysfunctions. From a natural-science background, the challenge seems particularly pressing today, because neuroimaging and molecular genetics now routinely identify biological correlates for disorders that Szasz treated as purely behavioral or social deviations. This does not seem to refute him automatically, since correlation is not pathology, but it raises a cluster of conceptual issues I am struggling to order by importance.

I want to ask where a small philosophical research group—say, three philosophers without clinical training—should direct its efforts over the next few years if it takes Szasz's critique seriously but also wants to engage with contemporary science. One cluster concerns ontology: are psychiatric kinds natural kinds, practical kinds constructed for intervention, or social kinds that embed normative judgments about acceptable cognition and affect? A second cluster concerns ethics and policy: if diagnosis necessarily involves value judgments about deviation from social norms, does that weaken the justification for involuntary commitment and coerced treatment, or can a normative framework be reconstructed without collapsing into Szasz's anti-psychiatry? A third cluster, which I have only recently encountered, involves explanatory pluralism—the idea that neural-mechanistic explanations and first-person narrative explanations can both be valid without reduction. I wonder whether resolving that pluralism is upstream of the ontological and ethical questions, or whether it is largely independent and should be deprioritized.

If you were advising such a group, how would you rank these lines of inquiry? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature, but for a defended agenda: which two or three open conceptual questions deserve priority, according to what criteria—tractability for philosophers rather than neuroscientists, how many downstream issues depend on resolving this one, and whether progress would actually alter clinical practice or mental-health policy. I should also mention that I am preparing this for a discussion group of fellow retired scientists who have asked me to present the current state of the debate; I want to avoid both the well-rehearsed question of whether schizophrenia is real and the highly technical literature on nosological realism that assumes deep familiarity with specific taxonomic debates. What would genuinely move the needle?
