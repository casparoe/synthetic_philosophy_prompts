Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of history—call them the configurationist and the redundancy theorist—debating multi-causal explanation in the case of the Late Bronze Age palace-economy collapse. The configurationist defends a view of configuration-sensitive overdetermination: climate stress, maritime network disruption, and internal social stratification were not causally substitutable, but formed a structurally coupled system in which each factor modified the efficacy of the others, so that counterfactual removal of any single element does not leave redundant causes to reproduce the same outcome, but instead transforms the entire causal architecture. The redundancy theorist replies that this is a distinction without explanatory difference: if the collapse remains counterfactually robust across the removal of individual factors, then the case is genuinely overdetermined in the standard sense, and invoking "structural coupling" merely redescribes interaction effects without adding predictive or explanatory power beyond ordinary multi-causal analysis. Have them address each other directly—each should respond to the specific argument just made, not talk past it—discussing whether their disagreement is substantive or verbal, whether counterfactual robustness can be assessed independently of a model of interaction, and whether the configurationist framework can be operationalized for historical cases or remains a post-hoc redescription. Ground the exchange in the concrete dynamics of Bronze Age interdependency rather than abstract logic alone. Ensure neither interlocutor is a strawman, and inject the niche conceptual axis between causal substitutability and structural coupling as the core of their dispute.
