I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind and medicine, and I need one essay question that probes whether pain asymbolia genuinely undermines the claim that pain is bad in virtue of its phenomenal character. The question must not be answerable by reciting the clinical literature on asymbolia or by rehearsing standard hedonist versus desire-satisfaction frameworks; rather, it should compel students to confront a specific conceptual tension. In typical asymbolia, subjects report feeling pain and assign it negative valence, yet they show no protective behaviour, emotional distress, or the usual motivational profile. Ask students to evaluate whether this dissociation demonstrates that the badness of pain lies in its motivational force rather than in its felt quality, or whether it reveals that these subjects possess a phenomenally present but evaluatively hollow state — and what that distinction implies for representationalist theories that identify pain with a particular kind of evaluative representation. To prevent mere memorisation, embed within the question a hypothetical paired case of "reverse asymbolia": subjects who display full protective and affective behaviour but sincerely deny any unpleasant feeling. Require students to use this comparison to diagnose what, if anything, is missing in each case, and whether either scenario can support a purely phenomenal or purely motivational account of pain's badness.

Please write: the essay question itself, designed for a 90-minute examination and expecting an answer of roughly 1,200 words; a model answer of about 600 words that executes at least three identifiable philosophical moves — for instance, distinguishing phenomenal presence from evaluative uptake, deploying the paired comparison to isolate what each theory must explain, and addressing an objection drawn from representationalist accounts of pain; and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks that separates an A response from a B response by naming the specific moves each must contain, allocating points accordingly, and providing a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each criterion. The scheme should reward precision about the conceptual distinction rather than familiarity with clinical terminology, and it should penalise answers that treat asymbolia as straightforward evidence for either view without engaging the hypothetical contrast.
