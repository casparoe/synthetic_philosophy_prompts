Contemporary democratic institutions deploy sortition in strikingly asymmetric ways: juries, selected by lot, render binding verdicts in discrete disputes but serve only temporarily and with narrowly circumscribed jurisdiction; citizens' assemblies, also selected by stratified random sampling, deliberate on constitutional or climate-policy questions but are almost invariably granted advisory rather than legislative authority; and proposals for full lottocracy typically preserve an elected executive or permanent administrative apparatus rather than extending lot to all governing functions. What implicit theory of democratic legitimacy, representation, and epistemic division of labor does this tripartite architecture—binding lot for adjudication, advisory lot for deliberation, election for execution—presuppose? Does it depend on a distinction between descriptive and substantive representation, on a claim that statistical sampling yields fairness but not strategic coherence, or on a deeper view about the conditions under which political power can be legitimately exercised without retrospective electoral accountability? Reconstruct the philosophical commitments that render this hybrid practice intelligible rather than ad hoc, distinguish what the arrangement strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify points where defenders of pure election, pure sortition, or hybrid models genuinely disagree. Please separate what is settled in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any single contested interpretation—whether about the nature of representation, the value of democratic equality, or the epistemic properties of deliberation—as the consensus view.
