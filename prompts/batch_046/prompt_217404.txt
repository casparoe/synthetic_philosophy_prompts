In the long-running exchange between H.L.A. Hart and Lord Devlin over the recommendation to decriminalize private homosexual acts and prostitution, the standard narrative is familiar: Hart insists that law should be limited to preventing harm to others, while Devlin argues that society may legitimately use law to enforce shared morality because a common moral code is necessary for social cohesion. Yet for all the commentary, a particular strand of the disagreement seems frozen. Devlin's defenders often read his social-fabric claim as a normative thesis about democratic self-definition—the idea that a polity has a right to preserve its moral identity—while Hart and his successors treat it as an empirical prediction that societies disintegrate when shared moral norms erode. Meanwhile, a less discussed distinction has begun to emerge: what we might call structural moralism, according to which legal prohibition is justified only when a moral norm functions as a coordination mechanism necessary for basic social cooperation, versus expressive moralism, according to which the law may prohibit conduct solely to register collective moral disapproval, regardless of any coordination function. The stall, as I see it, is that critics of Devlin tend to attack structural moralism on empirical grounds—pointing out that societies tolerate deep moral disagreement without collapse—while defenders retreat to expressive moralism, which does not depend on empirical predictions about disintegration but on a claim about democratic authenticity. But this retreat is rarely made explicit, so the debate cycles between an empirical dispute that seems answerable and a normative dispute about the legitimacy of collective moral expression that seems incommensurable with liberal harm-based reasoning.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about facts—no study of social cohesion is going to resolve it—nor is it merely verbal, since both sides genuinely disagree about what justifies coercive law. Rather, I suspect that Hart and Devlin are answering different questions under the single heading legal moralism: Hart asks what limits liberal neutrality places on state coercion, while Devlin asks what conditions must obtain for a democratic community to maintain authoritative self-governance. The appearance of a shared debate is produced by ambiguous terms like social cohesion and common morality, which function descriptively in Hart's framing and normatively in Devlin's.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden empirical question about the relationship between legal prohibition and social stability that has been obscured by normative rhetoric? Or is the impasse a sign that the concept of legal moralism bundles together two distinct institutional justifications—structural and expressive—that should be separated? If the diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions, what conceptual distinction or change of framing would allow progress—perhaps separating the coordination function of law from its expressive function? If, on the other hand, the disagreement turns out to rest on bedrock intuitions about whether collective moral disgust ever constitutes a reason for state action, is there anything—an institutional design, a formal model of democratic legitimacy, or a distinction in the philosophy of language—that could move it? And if not, should we conclude that the debate is not stalled but correctly recognized as intractable?
