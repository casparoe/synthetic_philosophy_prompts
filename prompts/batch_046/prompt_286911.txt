As a software engineer who spends most of my time reasoning about fault tolerance in distributed clusters, I have been trying to understand a moral problem that feels structurally analogous: if a system failure threatens five nodes and can only be repaired by redirecting resources away from one structurally independent node, is there a moral reason to save the larger cluster? This led me to philosophical debates about whether the numbers count in rescue cases, particularly John Taurek's argument that one should flip a coin between saving one stranger and saving five strangers rather than automatically saving the five, because moral obligations run pairwise and do not aggregate across persons. I have also encountered the standard aggregationist replies, but rather than rehearse the usual exchange I want a precise map of what remains after good-faith engagement.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed Pairwise Non-Aggregationist—who holds that there is no moral reason to prefer saving the greater number when all are strangers and that cardinal aggregation violates the separateness of persons—and a committed Weighted Aggregationist—who holds that numbers do count but insists aggregation must be constrained by some non-arbitrary individual veto, diminishing-returns threshold, or proximity condition—could both sign after an adversarial collaboration. The statement should contain: (i) a numbered section of agreed claims about the logical structure of rescue dilemmas, the failure of naive utilitarian summation, and the relevance of partial rescue capacity, proximity, and tie-breaking rules; (ii) precise formulations of their residual disagreement, with each position phrased in language its own advocates would accept; (iii) the cruxes—especially whether the separateness-of-persons objection applies symmetrically when the five form an interdependent system (like a distributed database cluster) versus five fully independent individuals, whether there exists a non-arbitrary cardinal threshold for aggregation, and whether the moral mathematics should be modeled as a distributed consensus protocol in which each node holds veto rights over being sacrificed for the cluster versus a centralized optimization function; and (iv) for each crux, one specific hypothetical case or argument both sides agree is genuinely bearing, together with each side's explicit prediction about what principle should govern it. Format the deliverable as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

Please separate what is settled among philosophers who have seriously worked on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present either the aggregationist or the anti-aggregationist position as the obvious consensus. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—only for the conceptual architecture to be made explicit and for any contested interpretation to be flagged as such.
