I'm closing a seminar on inherited institutions and want a poem that performs the logic of Chesterton's fence through Burke's idea of prescription rather than reducing either thinker to a generic defense of tradition. Could you write a standard sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—using the rotating end-words fence, inherit, reason, remove, shadow, and prescription? The poem should treat the fence not as an arbitrary obstacle awaiting rational justification, but as the visible boundary of an authority we inherit precisely because it exceeds our present capacity to reconstruct it from first principles. Let the opening stanzas establish the reformer's confidence: the fence looks pointless, its origin forgotten, and reason demands its removal to open the ground. Then let the turn occur in the envoi, where removing the fence does not reveal open space but exposes the shadow of accumulated, unarticulated adjustments—the prescription that was holding back forces rational reconstruction never anticipated. Keep the repetition of the end-words structurally audible, so that the returning lines feel like fence posts reasserting themselves, and make the final gathering of all six words in the envoi feel like the moment the reformer realizes what was actually being kept in place. Please stay faithful to Burke's claim that long possession can itself be a title to authority independent of explicit consent, and to Chesterton's warning that we destroy what we do not understand, but avoid any platitude about tradition being good for its own sake. The poem should be readable aloud—about thirty-nine lines, strict sestina form—to a room that knows the basic texts but hasn't heard this particular synthesis.
