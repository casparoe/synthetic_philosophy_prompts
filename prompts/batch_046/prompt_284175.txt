I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of religion working on comparative ethical theory, and in my recent graduate seminar on Buddhist moral philosophy I've repeatedly noticed an argumentative pattern that I cannot map cleanly onto the standard distinctions in the literature. When our discussions concern large-scale collective action—evaluating climate-mitigation policies, economic redistribution, or public-health interventions—my interlocutors consistently adopt an aggregative, long-range framework: they weigh the suffering of all sentient beings across future rebirths, treat karmic causation as producing extensive causal networks that amplify or diminish aggregate harm, and conclude that the right institutional policy is the one that minimizes total dukkha over indefinite timescales.

Yet when the conversation shifts to direct, individual-level conduct—particularly actions governed by the first precept or by Vinaya regulations—the same participants switch abruptly to categorical language. Killing is declared impermissible regardless of the net suffering prevented; consequentialist reasoning is rejected not merely as imprudent but as a category error that mistakes conventional moral discourse for the absolute structure of karmic causation. The shift is not merely contextual flexibility: it is an asymmetry in the very framework of evaluation, where the scale of the decision—institutional versus personal—seems to determine whether aggregative reasoning is permitted at all, rather than the specific content of the act. I have tentatively labeled this a "scaling asymmetry" in Buddhist ethical reasoning, but I am unsure whether it corresponds to anything already named.

Does this configuration have an established term in Buddhist studies or comparative ethics? How should it be distinguished precisely from ordinary rule-consequentialism, from upāya-kauśalya interpreted as contextual flexibility in teaching, and from applications of the two-truths doctrine to moral discourse? If no precise term exists, what conceptual boundaries would best define it—does the asymmetry turn on institutional agency, on the presence of direct volitional intention (cetanā), or on some other structural feature? I am less interested in a bare label than in knowing where the phenomenon begins and ends, and which adjacent concepts it genuinely overlaps with rather than merely resembles.
