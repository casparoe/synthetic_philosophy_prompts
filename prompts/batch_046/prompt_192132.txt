Consider the following paraphrase of a passage drawn from Kant's ethics lectures of the late eighteenth century: Kant maintains that animals lack self-consciousness and therefore do not possess the rational autonomy that makes a being an end in itself. For this reason, he holds that they exist only as instruments serving human purposes. At the same time, he condemns cruelty toward animals, explaining that such cruelty is wrong not because the animal itself is wronged in any direct sense, but because it deadens the human capacity for sympathy, thereby weakening our ability to fulfill duties toward other rational beings.

This passage is conventionally read as supporting an indirect-duty interpretation: animals have no direct moral standing, and our obligations regarding them derive entirely from obligations we bear toward ourselves and other persons.

For the purpose of analysis, introduce a conceptual distinction not standardly applied to this text—call it asymmetrical receptivity. By this is meant the property of being capable of suffering harm or receiving benefit from an agent's actions in a manner that generates a direct, non-instrumental reason for restraint, even though the affected being lacks reciprocal moral agency and cannot itself hold duties.

Specifically, can Kant's acknowledgment that animals suffer, together with his explicit condemnation of cruelty, be interpreted as leaving conceptual space for a minimal form of asymmetrical receptivity? Or does his claim that animals are merely instruments for human ends categorically exclude any direct moral consideration, rendering the wrongness of cruelty entirely contingent upon its corrupting effect on human character?
