I spent three decades in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, and I have been working out whether Schrödinger's later metaphysical commitments—particularly his reading of Advaita Vedanta—can sit comfortably with the physicalism, philosophy of mind, and ethics I now find plausible. Before I present this package to a reading group, I want a rigorous consistency audit.

Here are the commitments I hold:

(1) Following Schrödinger's interpretation of non-dual Vedanta, I take the multiplicity of individual consciousnesses to be a metaphysical illusion rather than a deep fact: there is only one universal awareness, and what we call personal identity is a practical fiction sustained by bodily continuity and memory, not by any real boundary within consciousness itself.

(2) I remain a causal physicalist about mental phenomena: every mental event has a sufficient neural cause, and I expect neuroscience eventually to provide exhaustive causal explanations for cognition and affect without leaving explanatory work for a non-physical substrate.

(3) I think the hard problem of consciousness—why any physical process should be accompanied by subjective experience at all—is a genuinely unsolved problem, not a conceptual confusion or an artifact of our cognitive limitations.

(4) I hold that ethical obligations are directed only toward beings with genuinely distinct phenomenal perspectives; if two organisms are modalities of the same underlying consciousness, harming one does not generate a separate moral claim distinct from harming the other, though it may still register as harm within the unified field.

(5) I interpret Schrödinger's determinism and his resistance to discontinuous collapse interpretations not as mere scientific conservatism, but as expressing his metaphysical conviction that reality is a single, continuous, indivisible whole—so that the wave function's linearity mirrors the Vedantic denial of real separation between observer and observed.

The tensions I am unsure about are these. First, (1) and (2) appear to conflict: if consciousness is genuinely unified, it seems there should be causal or explanatory work for that unity that neuroscience, focused on individual brains, has no resources to capture. Second, (3) looks like it undermines (2), since if neuroscience is causally exhaustive it is unclear what gap remains for the hard problem to occupy—though I suspect this tension may dissolve upon closer inspection. Third, (4) seems unstable with (1): if there are no genuinely distinct perspectives, then my criterion for directed moral obligation collapses, yet I still want to maintain that suffering localized in one body is ethically significant rather than ethically null. Fourth, (5) might appear to be an anachronistic mysticism projected onto quantum mechanics, though I take it to be a consistent expression of (1) rather than an independent contradiction.

Please examine these commitments pair by pair. For each pair, state whether the tension is real or only apparent; for the genuine conflicts, rank them by severity and specify the least costly revision in each case; and identify which single commitment, if revised, would resolve the greatest number of conflicts. Do not treat all tensions as equally serious, do not assume I am prepared to abandon the Vedantic framework, and be explicit about what that framework actually costs me in terms of the other positions.
