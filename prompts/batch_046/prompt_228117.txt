I sit on my university's curriculum committee for the philosophy major, and we face a concrete decision for next semester's catalog revision: whether to retain our required undergraduate survey sequence organized around three chronological units—'The Middle Ages,' 'The Renaissance,' and 'The Scientific Revolution'—or to replace them with either thematic, problem-based tracks (for example, metaphysics, ethics, and philosophy of nature taught across centuries) or a redesigned chronological scheme using narrower, descriptively neutral intervals (for example, 'Latin Philosophy, 1100–1350'; 'Mediterranean and European Philosophy, 1350–1650'). The philosophical question embedded in this administrative choice is whether these three traditional periods name real ruptures in the history of thought or are retrospective historiographical constructions that distort continuity and implicitly marginalize traditions—Islamic, Jewish, and vernacular European—that do not map neatly onto this tripartite timeline.

What has divided the committee is not merely the abstract ontological status of periodization but a more specific disagreement about what we might call chronological essentialism: the practice of assigning a thinker to a period primarily by birth date rather than by intellectual commitments. Proponents of reform note that Maimonides, writing in the twelfth century, is routinely taught within 'Medieval' syllabi despite his deep engagement with Aristotelian natural philosophy that anticipates debates usually reserved for later courses, while Descartes is placed in 'Early Modern' surveys despite his explicit self-positioning within a scholastic framework. Retentionists reply that undergraduate cognition depends on temporal scaffolding: without the narrative arc of rupture and renewal, students lose genealogical orientation, and the result is not liberation but a disorienting eternal present in which intellectual influence becomes invisible.

A compromise proposal suggests retaining chronological organization but stripping the canonical labels of their metaphysical weight—renaming the units descriptively and expanding geographic scope—so that we preserve pedagogical structure without endorsing a Whiggish narrative of inevitable progress.

Please draft the formal memo I will circulate before our vote in three weeks. It should recommend one of the three options (retain the traditional triad; adopt thematic tracks; or adopt the redesigned chronological intervals), include a concrete implementation timeline with evaluation criteria for the 2027–28 review, and treat seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, the pedagogical argument that temporal anchoring is not merely convenient but cognitively necessary for novice learners. The memo should address the ontological question—are these periods real or invented?—as genuinely unsettled rather than settled, and it should not include a bibliography or reading list.
