I'm a junior fellow on the council of a centuries-old professional guild that regulates master artisans. The guild maintains an unwritten norm enforced through a ledger of omissions: a master's standing improves each year he is never publicly corrected by a peer, even when his designs contain serious flaws that endanger clients. I want to abolish this practice. One faction advises presenting documented failures and invoking our charter's duty of care, treating resistance as a failure of reasoning. Another argues that change will come only if external insurers and wealthy patrons begin demanding transparency, because the guild's prestige is now tied to market confidence rather than internal solidarity. A third group claims that what has eroded is the older idea of craft honour—that a master should feel personal disgrace at concealing error—and that unless I frame silence as shameful rather than dangerous or costly, the senior members will not move. I cannot tell whether these three appeals represent genuinely different engines of normative collapse or just rhetorical costumes for the same underlying shift. Drawing on accounts of how norms like duelling or slavery actually ended, advise me which mechanism is likely to operate here, whether honour can function independently of reason and interest, and how I ought to proceed. Please state how confident you are in your answer and single out the claim you are least sure about. Use British English.
