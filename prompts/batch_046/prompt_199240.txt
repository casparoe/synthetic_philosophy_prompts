I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar in normative ethics, and for our unit on demandingness objections to consequentialism I have been trying to map out where I actually stand on the boundary between moral requirement and supererogation. Rather than relying on the classic textbook scenarios that everyone in the course has already debated, I have constructed a set of concrete cases drawn from career choice, direct rescue, mediated financial contribution, attentional labor, and bodily donation. You should approach this as an expert in moral philosophy specializing in normative ethics and practical reasoning. Before giving your final analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm the principal strategies for resolving demandingness objections—agent-relative permissions, the ought-implies-can constraint, harm-versus-failure-to-benefit distinctions, integrity-preserving reasons, and relevant structural analogies—so that your diagnosis is not merely a post-hoc rationalization of one intuition. I want an audit of reflective equilibrium: not a lecture on standard figures, but a precise check on whether my own verdicts hang together and what they commit me to.

Here are my verdicts, assuming I am a middle-income professional with no dependents and moderate discretionary resources. Case 1: I spend $300 on a weekend trip rather than donating it to a highly effective global-health charity that could save a life at roughly that cost; verdict: permissible, not wrong. Case 2: I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning; saving them requires ruining my $400 shoes and missing an important job interview, and I save them; verdict: required. Case 3: I could restructure my career over five years to earn far more specifically in order to earn-to-give, saving perhaps dozens of lives, but this requires abandoning public-interest law and working seventy-hour weeks in a field I find meaningless; I stay in law; verdict: permissible, since vocational integrity sets a legitimate boundary. Case 4: I learn that my retirement portfolio holds stock in a firm whose indirect supply-chain practices cause serious harm; switching to an equivalent ethical fund takes fifteen minutes, costs nothing, and requires no lifestyle change, but I do not do it out of pure administrative inertia; verdict: I feel somewhat blameworthy—closer to a failure than Case 1, despite the lower burden. Case 5: Every Tuesday evening I could volunteer at a nearby shelter where I am genuinely useful, but I consistently stay home to rest and watch films; verdict: not required, though it would be admirable if done. Case 6: I am healthy and could donate plasma twice monthly with minimal risk, earning about $100 per session that I could direct to effective charities; I avoid it because the clinic is unpleasant and I prefer leisure; verdict: I am genuinely torn. Structurally it resembles Case 5, yet the cost is so small and the benefit so concrete that part of me thinks it belongs nearer Case 4. This is the case I keep revisiting.

My pattern is not simply that low cost generates a requirement, since Case 4 is low-cost yet I do not treat it like Case 2, and Case 6 is also low-cost yet I hesitate to call it obligatory. Meanwhile Cases 1, 3, and 5 all involve forgoing benefits to others, and I judge them permissible. Is there a precise principle—or a small family of candidates—that captures this distribution? For each principle you propose, please identify the case that motivates each clause, state its verdict on cases I have not mentioned (for example, refusing to donate a kidney to a stranger, or taking an earning-to-give job against one's deepest commitments), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without revision. If no single principle saves all six without ad hoc complexity, please rank the available options by which verdict they force me to abandon and which revision is least costly. Finally, apply your best principle back to Case 6 and tell me whether my ambivalence is principled or just confusion.
