A friend who recently read some moral philosophy summarized the literature on hypocrisy and standing to blame for me as follows. He said that standing is essentially a status one holds in virtue of not being guilty of the very fault one condemns: blame presupposes a kind of moral equality, so if you share the offense you forfeit your position to rebuke it. He presented this as a binary condition—either your hands are clean or they are not, with no gradations or partial exemptions. From this he inferred that hypocrisy does not merely weaken one's rhetorical position but entirely dissolves one's standing, whether the hypocrisy is hidden or open, since the underlying equality is violated either way. He added that this is why hypocritical blame strikes us as conceptually malformed rather than merely annoying, comparing it to an insolvent creditor demanding payment. Finally, he concluded that because standing is binary and fully dissolved by shared fault, a hypocrite retains no legitimate basis for the judgment at all—not even in private reflection—though the moral fact itself might remain unchanged.

I think he may be treating standing as identical to moral authority, and I suspect his binary framing is too sharp. But I am unsure whether the comparison to insolvency is apt, whether hidden hypocrisy really undermines standing in the same way as open hypocrisy, and whether private mental blame is genuinely subject to the same conditions as public censure. Could you audit this summary? Which elements accurately capture the philosophical account of standing and hypocrisy, which are subtle distortions—particularly the binary framing and the extension to private judgment—and does his conclusion that the hypocrite retains no legitimate basis actually follow once the distortions are corrected? Please distinguish between losing the standing to express blame and being wrong about the underlying moral fact.
