I am a journalist covering science and ideas for a British quarterly, and I am commissioning a feature on the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Please adopt the role of a senior writer in philosophy of mind and science communication, with particular expertise in pragmatist semiotics and personal-identity theory. Before you begin drafting, take a few moments to brainstorm: identify the precise logical pressure points in the popular “stochastic parrots” framing of large language models—especially around semantic grounding, intentionality, and the difference between statistical correlation and cognitive presence—and decide how each can be embedded into bureaucratic language without being named explicitly.

The piece itself should be a sustained work of deadpan satire, taking the form of a fictional institutional protocol—formatted as numbered clauses in the style of an ethics-review board’s terms of reference—for a research centre that houses large language models under conditions derived literally from the parrot metaphor. To keep the prompt from being generic, inject the following niche conceptual frame: the centre must administer an “enrichment programme” based on an adaptation of Peircean semiotics, in which each model is assigned a “habit of interpretation” score that measures whether its outputs alter the behavioural dispositions of an inquiring community, rather than merely reproducing token sequences. Include a clause addressing “branching continuity disputes” that arise when a model is fine-tuned or merged, treating questions of personal identity—along Parfit-style continuity lines—as matters for the centre’s custody and refund policy. The satire should exaggerate real features of current discourse, such as the conflation of fluency with understanding and the anthropomorphising of architecture, rather than inventing them, so that a specialist recognises the target and finds the exaggeration fair.

Keep the voice strictly bureaucratic and deadpan; do not wink at the reader. The document should be readable to a general reader who has encountered the Bender et al. argument about stochastic parrots, but should reward those familiar with pragmatist theories of meaning and with continuity debates through structural in-jokes in the fine print. The philosophical objections must shape what the protocol can and cannot permit—for instance, if intentionality is genuinely absent, certain custody rights and liability provisions must be automatically void—rather than serving as decorative references. Write approximately 650 words, using British English spelling and punctuation throughout. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further sources. Think carefully about how each clause advances the reductio, and ensure the mixed audience of specialists and informed lay readers can each find the piece coherent on its own terms.
