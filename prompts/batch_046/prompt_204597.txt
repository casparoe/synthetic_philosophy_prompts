I am preparing coursework for my module in Advanced Normative Ethics, and I need to translate a theoretical framework we have examined in seminar into a practical decision tool. The framework combines indirect normativity — the view that moral requirements bear primarily on the deliberative procedures agents adopt rather than on particular actions considered in isolation — with an idealised-deliberation constraint derived from contractualist moral theory, which holds that a procedure is permissible only if it could be endorsed by rational agents who are fully informed, uncoerced, and reasoning from a shared practical standpoint.

For this assignment, please construct a reusable, step-by-step evaluation procedure — presented as an ordered sequence of questions with explicit stopping rules — that a university research-ethics administrator could apply in approximately twenty minutes when reviewing study proposals that involve delegated moral judgement. By this I mean cases in which research participants are not merely asked to consent, but are required to delegate aspects of their practical reasoning either to the investigator or to an automated proxy operating on their behalf.

The procedure should indicate, at each stage, what counts as a sufficient answer, where a negative determination terminates the review, and where the matter is irreducibly evaluative and must therefore be referred back to the committee chair rather than resolved by the administrator. Please distinguish clearly between steps that can be executed mechanically and those that are essentially judgement calls presented in procedural form. Be explicit about where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a psychology experiment in which participants are deceived about the true purpose of a stress-induction task, with full debriefing promised only after completion; (b) a clinical trial in which patients with diminished decision-making capacity delegate treatment-selection authority to an algorithm trained on aggregated population-preference data; and (c) a hypothetical proposal of your own design in which the procedure delivers a verdict of permissibility that conflicts with a strong intuitive moral objection — that is, an answer the user of the procedure would be inclined to reject. Finally, state concisely what this procedure cannot accomplish, particularly with regard to tensions between first-order deliberative outputs and second-order procedural commitments, and flag any point at which the administrator must rely on moral intuition rather than on the checklist itself.
