I'm preparing discussion material for a graduate seminar in moral psychology and want to stage an exchange that moves past the standard caricatures of the situationist critique. Write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—call them Mara and Theo—on whether the situationist findings in social psychology (the classic obedience and prison-role experiments, and subsequent field studies tracking situational predictors of prosocial behavior) genuinely undermine virtue ethics or merely refine it. Mara should defend a revised Aristotelian position: practical wisdom is not a global, context-blind trait but a domain-sensitive skill, analogous to expert surgical judgment, which explains why the same agent behaves differently across structurally different situations without proving that moral character is an illusion. Theo should press that this "skill analogy" saves virtue ethics only by redefining virtue into something so local and environmentally dependent that the ethical project collapses into ecological management of contexts rather than the cultivation of stable dispositions oriented toward eudaimonia.

The dialogue must feature real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. Theo should respond to Mara's surgical analogy by asking whether a skill that largely vanishes outside its trained domain is still the kind of "character" virtue ethicists need for long-term flourishing; Mara should reply by distinguishing expected variability across domains from unreliable inconsistency within them, and she should concede that if cross-situational correlations of moral behavior approached zero even inside familiar domains, her view would face serious trouble. Both should avoid strawman moves—Theo should grant that Aristotelians never demanded perfect cross-situational uniformity, and Mara should acknowledge that if empirical research showed minor environmental tweaks consistently override trained dispositions with no mediating skill factor, her position would be undermined.

Please conclude with each character explicitly stating the specific empirical result or conceptual argument that would change their mind, and identifying the weakest point in their own reasoning—Mara on whether her skill-model can still ground coherent long-term life-planning, and Theo on whether his critique tacitly relies on an implausibly atomistic conception of the self.
