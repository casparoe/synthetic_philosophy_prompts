Please write the opening address for the dedication ceremony of a new community land-trust housing cooperative. The audience is divided: roughly half are urban-policy scholars and legal theorists who will recognize every allusion to distributive justice, and half are local residents, organizers, and future tenants with no formal background in political philosophy who need to understand why the charter's permanent resale-price caps are justice rather than charity. The cooperative's governing document channels all surplus value into maintenance and new-unit construction rather than private equity, and it binds current and future owners to identical restrictions.

Your speech must do real structural work with Rawls's veil of ignorance and the difference principle: you should justify the arrangement by showing that it benefits the least advantaged, but you must not frame this as "helping the poor" in any way that assumes anyone present knows whether they occupy that position. The justification must be articulated from behind the veil, so that it remains valid regardless of whether a listener is a current resident, a future buyer, or a neighbor priced out.

To avoid a standard textbook rehearsal, inject the following niche extension: treat temporal position—whether one acquires a unit before or after the trust's establishment—as a morally arbitrary feature that must also fall behind the veil. This means the difference principle applies across generations of residents, not merely across social classes within a single moment. The speech should demonstrate how the charter's intergenerational binding mechanism—the perpetual resale cap and the surplus-recycling rule—answers precisely this temporal-veil objection.

Keep the tone formal yet accessible, approximately five to six minutes when spoken, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, page references, named texts with publication dates, or appeals to specific scholars. Do not invent case studies or named experts. The philosophical reasoning should be precise enough that a specialist finds it fair, yet every substantive claim should be fully intelligible to a non-specialist without footnotes.
