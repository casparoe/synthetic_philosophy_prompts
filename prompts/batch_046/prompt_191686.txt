At a recent seminar on personal identity, a speaker summarised Hofstadter’s position with the slogan “the self is a pattern, not a substance, so it can live in more than one brain”, then used it to dismiss my concern that distributing my cognition across separate bodies would destroy me rather than extend me. I am not convinced that line does the work it was asked to do. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this sentence might express — ranging from the trivial claim that mental properties are not physical particles, through claims about informational identity and causal continuity, to the radical claim that a single token-self can be genuinely spatially distributed — and for each say whether it is defensible, false, or merely metaphorical? I want the readings separated as a clear menu: (i) the view that a self-type can be multiply instantiated; (ii) the view that one token-self can have separated parts; and (iii) the view that structural similarity replaces causal continuity as the criterion of persistence. Which of these readings actually supports dismissing my splitting worry, and which leaves it intact? To test them concretely, consider a niche case in which my procedural values are copied into brain A, my episodic memories into brain B, and my affective dispositions into brain C, with no single brain hosting more than one-third of the original pattern; does any reading entail that there is still one “I”, or does it instead entail three partial selves? Please keep the analysis to what can be assessed without outside references, and indicate for each reading whether a defender of distributed persistence ought to accept it.
