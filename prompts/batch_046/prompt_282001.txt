I just finished a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether Kierkegaard’s critique of the press and the modern public is fundamentally a theological claim about inwardness and accountability or a secular sociological diagnosis that can be applied to contemporary anonymous discourse without loss. We kept talking past each other because I kept insisting that his concept of “the public” requires a specifically Christian account of the single individual, while my friend treated it as a purely structural observation about visibility and commitment. To settle my own thinking, I am drafting the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate seminar on existentialism and modernity, and I want one in-class essay question—answerable in roughly seventy-five minutes, with answers expected around eight hundred words—that students cannot answer by reciting the standard summary of leveling, the crowd, and the press’s role in dissolving individual responsibility into abstract spectatorship.

Instead, the question should introduce and require the use of a specific conceptual injection: “structured opacity,” defined within the prompt itself as the condition in which an agent discloses information without knowing its specific recipients or future uses, yet remains formally accountable for the disclosure. Students should be asked to evaluate whether this condition undermines Kierkegaard’s claim that the press destroys responsibility by dissolving the single individual into an anonymous public, or whether it actually intensifies his critique by driving a new wedge between visibility and moral accountability.

Please provide:

1. The exam question, worded precisely and appropriate for a seventy-five-minute in-class essay. It should require students to reconstruct Kierkegaard’s view of the press as an abstract power that generates the public, to distinguish the crowd from the public as a specifically modern form of non-commitment, to analyze how structured opacity interacts with his concept of inwardness, and to take a reasoned position on whether the theological framework is load-bearing for the critique.

2. A model answer of approximately seven hundred words.

3. A marking scheme out of twenty-five points, divided into five specific moves, each with assigned marks: (a) accurate reconstruction of the press’s role in generating an anonymous, non-committal public; (b) clear distinction between the crowd and the public as modern anonymity rather than mere aggregation; (c) analysis of structured opacity’s interaction with inwardness and accountability; (d) a reasoned judgment on whether the theological framework is necessary; and (e) identification and correction of one common misconception—specifically, the conflation of leveling with simple social conformity or egalitarianism. The scheme should specify exactly what distinguishes an A-range answer, which must articulate the asymmetry between visibility and accountability as applied to structured opacity, from a B-range answer, which correctly describes Kierkegaard’s categories but treats them as static descriptions rather than dynamic conditions. Include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail at each point.
