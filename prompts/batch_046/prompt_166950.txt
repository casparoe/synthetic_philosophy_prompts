I have been drafting notes for an essay that takes up Douglas Hofstadter’s well-known argument about poetic translation—that form is not merely a container for meaning but part of its substance—and tries to push it into territory he does not discuss, specifically the translation of modern Japanese haiku sequences into English through formal substitution rather than syllabic preservation. The concrete case I want to examine involves rendering a sequence of contemporary haiku not by retaining the five-seven-five syllable pattern, which often produces awkward phonology in English, but by mapping the thematic and emotional arc onto the rhyme royal stanza (ABABBCC), positioning the conceptual pivot—analogous to the kireji—at the turn between the paired B rhymes, while allowing the seasonal reference (the kigo) to disappear as a lexical item and re-emerge only as an acoustic echo in the rhyme scheme. I call this strategy prosodic haunting: the idea is that the target form does not imitate the source form but preserves its cognitive rhythm through structural analogy, so that what survives is not the phonetic surface but the architecture of attention. I am unsure whether this does real philosophical work or is merely a metaphor dressed up as a method.

Could you comment on these notes? In particular, I would like to know whether the argument that formal correspondence at the level of conceptual architecture can compensate for total loss of phonetic and lexical surface is coherent, whether Hofstadter’s framework actually permits such radical formal transplantation, and whether there is a defensible boundary between a translation that honors form by transforming it and one that simply uses the original as a pretext for an independent poem. I am especially concerned about the treatment of the seasonal reference: if the kigo is dissolved into rhyme sound, has anything genuinely been preserved, or has the source been emptied out? Any suggestions for sharpening the central claim, identifying hidden assumptions, or restructuring the essay’s arc would be welcome.
