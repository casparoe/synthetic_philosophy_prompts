I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students who have studied basic manifold theory but have not taken general relativity. The goal is not historical exposition but conceptual diagnosis: I want them to grasp the hole argument in Einstein's theory of gravitation as a tension between diffeomorphism invariance and the individuation of spacetime points, rather than treating it merely as a failure of determinism.

Please provide a teaching plan—the dependency order of ideas, the carrying example, the misconception map with interception points, a closing diagnostic, and explicit omissions—not the lecture transcript itself.

First, lay out the dependency structure. Which distinctions must be secured before the hole can even be formulated: the active versus passive distinction for diffeomorphisms, the 3+1 initial-value split versus four-dimensional covariance, or the separation of physical field content from coordinate representation? State the exact sequence in which these must be introduced and justify why each step depends on the prior one being stable.

Second, propose one carrying example that is not the canonical sketch of two metrics that agree outside an empty region and diverge inside it. Your example should make the indeterminacy concrete—perhaps using a specific symmetric matter distribution or a rotated region within a spatially flat background—while lacking whatever feature would let students dismiss the problem as a trivial coordinate relabeling. Identify that missing feature and explain why its absence is pedagogically essential.

Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form: that diffeomorphism freedom in this context is analogous to gauge freedom in electromagnetism; that the argument demonstrates a physical failure of determinism rather than an ontological problem about point identity; and that the resolution requires introducing hidden variables to fix the locations of points. For each misconception, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be preempted, the counter-distinction or thought experiment that blocks it, and the one-sentence response you would use if a student voices it during discussion.

Fourth, design one end-of-seminar diagnostic question that reveals whether a student has grasped the structural point—that the argument targets the status of manifold points—rather than whether they can merely repeat that coordinate choices lack physical significance.

Finally, state what should be deliberately left out at this level, with justification tied to conceptual dependency rather than to time alone. In particular, address whether to omit the canonical quantum gravity problem of time, the technical well-posedness conditions for the Einstein initial-value problem, and modern structuralist resolutions that dissolve point-identity altogether. Explain how including any of these would destabilize the dependency chain you have built.
