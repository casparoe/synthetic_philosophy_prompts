You are a senior philosophy examiner specializing in pragmatics, cross-cultural ethics, and the rationalist-adjacent analysis of speech acts. Frame the question around the tension between transparent directness—the Dutch low-context norm that explicit, unvarnished assertion respects the interlocutor by treating her as an epistemic peer capable of bearing bad news—and protective opacity—the Japanese high-context practice of strategic indirectness (often mapped onto the tatemae/honne distinction) that avoids imposing face-threatening burdens by embedding assertions in relational context. Inject a specific, somewhat unconventional analytical framework: the transparency-respect asymmetry, which holds that directness expresses respect by refusing to manipulate the listener's belief-formation, whereas protective opacity risks instrumentalizing the listener by controlling her epistemic environment under the guise of care.

Present this concrete scenario: A Dutch project director asks a Japanese senior engineer, Is the deliverable salvageable by Friday? The engineer knows it is not, but replies, We are exploring adjustments, intending to avoid direct contradiction, prevent the director from losing face in front of the team, and maintain harmony. The director interprets this literally and continues resource planning for Friday.

The exam question must ask students to determine whether the engineer's reply is morally wrong, and if so whether its wrongness is excused, transformed, or aggravated by the protective-opacity context. Specifically, require them to address three things: (1) whether the reply violates Grice's maxim of Quality and, if so, whether the violation is cancelled or merely masked by pragmatic force; (2) whether the cultural framework transforms the utterance from an assertion into a social ritual that falls outside the Quality maxim entirely; and (3) whether the director's literal interpretation creates an independent moral failure on the engineer's part, given the transparency-respect asymmetry.

Provide the following deliverables, clearly labeled:
1. The exam question itself, written for students.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words that takes a clear, reasoned position—for example, that the reply remains a Quality-violating assertion that is not transformed into ritual, and that protective opacity does not excuse the wrongness because it instrumentalizes the director's belief-formation, though it may mitigate blame.

List specific mistakes that should cost marks—for example, treating indirectness as automatically non-assertoric; ignoring the director's belief-formation; or appealing to unspecified cultural relativism.
