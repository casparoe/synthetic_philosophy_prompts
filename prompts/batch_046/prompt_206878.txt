I'm a volunteer docent with no formal training in philosophy or history, though I've spent the last few years visiting memorial sites across three continents and reading survivor accounts in translation. Next month I present to the advisory board of our regional genocide-education center — the board includes a survivor's grandson who works in community organizing, a university historian of twentieth-century violence, an ethics teacher from the local high school, and two curators whose background is in archival preservation rather than interpretation. I want to defend a specific redesign of our comparative gallery: rather than arranging atrocities chronologically or by death toll, I propose grouping them according to what I've come to call the architecture of denial — the particular bureaucratic forms, linguistic euphemisms, and spatial designs each regime used to make mass killing administratively routine and psychologically distant. My claim is that this lets visitors see genuine structural parallels without reducing any one event to a number or implying a hierarchy of suffering. Prepare me for the objections these four people will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come: phrase each objection in the vocabulary that person would use, tell me which ones have real force and which are mainly rhetorical, advise me on what to concede immediately and what to hold, sketch a two-minute reply for each, and flag where each reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — likely whether my framework flattens the singular horror of each event into an abstract administrative pattern — and tell me exactly where my own argument is softest, so I can defend it deliberately rather than hoping they miss it.
