I'm assembling my interpretive stance on Zhuangzi's philosophy of death for a graduate seminar and want a consistency check before I present it. I hold the following commitments: (1) Death is best understood not as termination but as a large-scale transformation (huà) continuous with seasonal change, so that fearing death involves a category mistake—treating a change of state as the annihilation of a fixed bearer. (2) The normative upshot is not stoic suppression of affect but an unforced emotional alignment: grief is not a moral failure, yet it misapprehends ontology by treating the dead form as a lost possession rather than as breath and matter returning to circulation. (3) There is no persistent, form-independent self that traverses this transformation; the "I" is a temporary configuration of qì, so personal survival is literally not at stake. (4) Despite this, one owes responsive care (yīng) to the living bodies of others—feeding, sheltering, protecting them—not because form is intrinsically valuable, but because present conditions call for specific responses, and neglect is a failure of attunement rather than a metaphysical error. (5) The well-known anecdote about Zhuangzi's reaction to his wife's death should be read not as callousness but as illustrating the dissolution of proprietary grief: since there is no bounded owner of the form that dissolved, there is nothing "mine" to be lost. (6) Given the ontological equality of life-phase and death-phase, both desperate life-extension and reckless self-abandonment are symmetrically confused; each privileges one temporal slice as "the real me."

Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look incompatible, and what is the least costly revision? In particular, I am unsure whether (4) can survive (3) and (6) together: if form is genuinely transient and no self owns it, why does responsive care include preservation rather than mere acknowledgment? And does (2)'s appeal to emotional alignment sit uneasily with (1)'s claim that the error is purely cognitive? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection, and specify what I would have to give up if I insisted on keeping (4) in its strongest form.
