I am preparing a short essay on the rhetorical mechanics of intuition pumps, and I would value your critical comments on the central claim and its weaknesses. My thesis is that well-known thought experiments persuade not only by triggering affective responses, but by covertly substituting narrative coherence for metaphysical proximity in the audience's modal reasoning. I examine Judith Thomson's violinist scenario and argue that its narrative plausibility—waking up attached to an unconscious stranger—compresses the perceived modal distance between the hypothetical world and ours, licensing an intuition about permissibility that may not survive explicit modal scrutiny. I make a parallel point about Robert Nozick's experience machine: the force of the case, I suggest, derives partly from the narrative implausibility of permanently disconnecting one's consciousness, which collapses the distinction between what is possible in principle and what is imaginatively available in detail. I then ask whether this mechanism generalizes: do structurally drier pumps, such as standard Gettier cases, operate through a similar substitution, or does emotional salience remain an independent and necessary variable?

Please comment specifically on the following. First, is there a defensible distinction between narrative plausibility and metaphysical proximity that my framing illegitimately blurs, or is that blur precisely the cognitive phenomenon that needs explanation? Second, does this analysis overstate the uniformity of intuition pumps, given that some seem designed to isolate conceptual distinctions rather than to mobilize modal imagination? Third, if the argument is sound, what methodological consequences should follow for the use of thought experiments in normative and metaphysical theory? I am interested in conceptual sharpening rather than references: identify the weakest point of the argument, suggest the most damaging counterexample, and indicate whether the thesis can be salvaged by restricting its scope.
