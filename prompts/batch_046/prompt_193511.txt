I have been working through the standard antinatalist asymmetry associated with David Benatar—specifically the claim that the absence of suffering is good even when there is no subject to enjoy that absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is genuinely deprived—within a discussion group focused on population ethics and artificial moral patients. A particular extension has arisen that I find conceptually slippery, and I would like your credence on whether it holds. Imagine a hypothetical form of conscious existence—call it a "null-subject"—that can be engineered so that its phenomenal life is structurally fixed at perfect hedonic neutrality: it never suffers, never experiences positive affect, and lacks any counterfactual preference for pleasure over this state, yet it remains phenomenally conscious rather than being a zombie or an information processor without inner life. The precise claim I want evaluated is this: "If Benatar's asymmetry is correct, then, all else equal, we have a stronger moral reason to create such a null-subject than to create a flourishing human life, and this comparative ranking follows directly from the asymmetry itself rather than from an independent risk-aversion or precautionary principle about suffering."

Please give me your credence as a single number between 0 and 1. Then decompose it into four sub-estimates, each with a rough probability and a brief justification: (a) the likelihood that Benatar's asymmetry extends coherently to non-biological or engineered conscious entities rather than being indexed specifically to human reproductive contexts; (b) the likelihood that a permanently hedonic-neutral yet phenomenally conscious state is metaphysically possible without collapsing into either unconsciousness or hidden suffering; (c) the likelihood that, given (a) and (b), the asymmetry genuinely generates the ranking null-subject > flourishing life, rather than rendering them incommensurable or reversing the ordering; and (d) the likelihood that any apparent support for the ranking is actually driven by the asymmetry and not by a smuggled-in principle that suffering-avoidance always dominates pleasure-promotion. Combine them explicitly, state your overall figure, identify which of the four terms is the weakest link in your reasoning, and describe what kind of conceptual clarification or philosophical argument—without requiring empirical discovery—would most shift that particular term.
