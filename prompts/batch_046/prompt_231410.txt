Read Albert Camus's novel The Stranger in light of the arguments he develops in his essay opposing capital punishment. In that essay, Camus treats execution not merely as killing but as a ritualized, state-sanctioned murder that degrades both executioner and society; he stresses that the condemned retains a claim to human solidarity and that the spectacle of the guillotine manufactures a kind of collective complicity. The novel, however, depicts Meursault's execution as a flat, bureaucratic endpoint—stripped of ritual grandeur and presented almost as an administrative afterthought—while Meursault himself remains emotionally vacant, refusing conventional remorse or spiritual redemption in his final hours. Does the novel's austere, anti-spectacular portrayal of execution support Camus's moral critique, or does it inadvertently weaken it by removing the very theatrical cruelty he identifies as the core corruption? Moreover, does Meursault's indifference to his own death vindicate Camus's claim that capital punishment denies the condemned a meaningful human future, or does it suggest that a life already so detached from solidarity has little left for execution to destroy? Offer a reading that takes both the narrative texture of the novel and the ethical logic of the essay seriously, identifying where they illuminate each other and where the text resists the philosophical frame.
