I've been reading up on that view of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the idea that you're not a fixed soul but more like the center of mass of a story you're constantly editing. I was talking about it with friends over coffee and we started noticing this specific pattern people do with their life stories that I don't have a word for.

So here's the pattern, with a couple real-feeling examples. Someone gets dumped by a partner and later says that relationship was never really part of their real story, just a detour. But then they hold onto a random summer job they hated, and now call it foundational because it taught them resilience. The move isn't just changing the interpretation of an event; it's retroactively changing whether the event belongs near the center of the narrative gravity well or out in the distant periphery. People do this asymetrically — they push away things that threaten the current emotional shape of the self, and pull in things that support it, even if those things were actually minor at the time. Another case: a person abandons a religious upbringing and describes it as something they went through, not something they were, but then insists a brief volunteer trip in college was always at the core of who I am, even though they forgot about it for years. It's like they're not editing the plot, they're editing the map of what's central and what's not.

Does this specific move have an established name? I know there's general stuff about narrative identity and maybe self-deception or confabulation, but those feel different — confabulation is usually filling in gaps, not this selective relocation of events between the center and the edges. Is there a concept that captures exactly this retroactive restructuring of the center-periphery layout? If so, how is it different from just having a flexible self-concept or doing ordinary reinterpretation? If not, could you coin a precise term and say what its boundary cases are — for instance, does it include people who genuinely change values and therefore genuinely see old things differently, or only the strategic kind? I'd also like you to seperate what's settled in the literature on narrative selves from what's genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and please don't present one camp's view as the consensus if it's actually disputed.
