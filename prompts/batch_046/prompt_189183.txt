I have been reading about the idea of "dirty hands" in politics — the claim that leaders sometimes must do serious moral wrongs to secure necessary public goods, and that this should leave them with guilt or a need for public acknowledgment rather than clean justification. I want to apply this to a case I have been thinking about: the Obama administration's drone campaign from roughly 2011 to 2013, especially the shift to "signature strikes" (targeting groups by behavioral patterns rather than confirmed identity) and the creation of the "disposition matrix," a bureaucratic database designed to institutionalize and streamline kill-list decisions over time. The administration publicly defended the strikes as precise and necessary against terrorism, but internal reports acknowledged recurring civilian casualties, and the process was largely hidden from democratic oversight, with no senior official ever making a clear public admission that moral wrong had been done.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of dirty-hands theory. What does the theory say about what the decision-makers faced — was this a classic dirty-hands situation, or does the bureaucratic, algorithmic framing (the matrix, pattern-based targeting) change the moral structure? Does the theory require that the wrong be felt personally by the agent, and if so, does distributing the decision across committees and data systems dissolve that requirement? Which features does the theory illuminate well — for example, the tension between secret necessity and democratic transparency — and which does it miss or misdescribe, such as the way predictive technology distances the actor from the harm? Finally, does this case support the theory, expose a gap (perhaps that dirty hands was built for visible, one-off choices rather than automated, ongoing systems), or show that the theory needs revision?
