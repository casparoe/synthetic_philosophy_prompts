In a recent departmental reading group on nineteenth-century moral philosophy, a colleague offered a concise summary of Nietzsche’s On the Genealogy of Morals that I have since been weighing as a possible framing for my graduate seminar. The summary runs roughly as follows: Nietzsche’s genealogical method reveals that Christian morality was historically engineered by a resentful priestly caste who, unable to dominate physically, inverted the aristocratic value-pair good/bad into good/evil, thereby condemning strength as sinful. Master morality is therefore essentially egoistic self-affirmation, while slave morality is nothing other than the institutionalisation of ressentiment—the two concepts being more or less co-extensive. The so-called slave revolt succeeds not through any genuine normative force but by persuading the strong to accept a false anthropology in which their natural superiority is reinterpreted as moral corruption. Turning to the second essay, the account claims that bad conscience is merely the internalisation of religious guilt, and that the ascetic ideal represents the same resentment projected onto philosophy and science, with no independent psychological mechanism of its own. I find the broad strokes recognisable, yet I worry that the summary collapses distinctions that Nietzsche carefully maintains—particularly between ressentiment as an affect and slave morality as a value-system, between conceptual genealogy and empirical history, and between the internalisation of cruelty and the mere adoption of religious prohibitions.

Could you audit this characterisation section by section? Please indicate which parts a defender of Nietzsche’s position would accept as fair simplifications, which involve common but misleading conflations, and which are straightforward errors of interpretation—especially regarding whether slave morality depends on a false view of human nature, and whether the ascetic ideal can be reduced to an extension of resentment without remainder. I should be grateful for a reply in British English, suitable for guiding my seminar preparation.
