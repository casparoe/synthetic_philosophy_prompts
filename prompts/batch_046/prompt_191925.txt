Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mara and Theo, debating whether maximal moral goodness—the moral saint—is genuinely desirable. Mara should defend the view that perfect moral commitment is an aspirational ideal, but with a specific twist: she argues that true sainthood requires not merely unlimited altruism but a disciplined "moral triage architecture," in which the saint rationally allocates finite moral attention across an infinite field of suffering, and that constructing this architecture without collapsing into indifference is itself a supreme moral achievement. Theo should oppose her without being a strawman: he grants that altruism is good but contends that maximal moral goodness is structurally undesirable because it collapses the distinction between supererogation and obligation, erodes the agent's practical identity, and—crucially—generates a "moral externality" in which institutions and other agents systematically offload collective responsibility onto the saint's predictable reliability. Require real back-and-forth: when Mara claims that triage is just rational benevolence, Theo must press whether this architecture implicitly accepts that most suffering is permanently unaddressable; when Theo warns about institutional exploitation, Mara must ask whether that makes the saint's goodness contingent on social failure rather than intrinsic virtue. End with neither convinced, but with the central tension left explicit—whether perfect moral attention can be sustained without either epistemic moral fatigue that corrupts judgment or parasitic social dynamics that replace justice with individual virtue.
