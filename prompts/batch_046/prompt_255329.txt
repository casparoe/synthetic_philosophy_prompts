Having retired from analytical chemistry some years ago and, somewhat belatedly, begun auditing philosophy-of-science seminars at the local university, I find myself returning repeatedly to the Twin Earth thought experiment—not as a distant logical puzzle, but as a problem about how working scientists actually stabilize the reference of kind terms. In a recent discussion with fellow auditors, mostly humanists with little laboratory experience, we kept circling a version of the scenario that the standard philosophical commentaries rarely address in chemical detail: imagine a Twin Earth whose lakes contain not ordinary H₂O but a stable isotopologue in which both hydrogen nuclei are replaced by a heavy isotope that behaves identically in every macroscopic chemical test available to an investigator in 1750—solubility, boiling point at standard pressure, reaction with alkali metals, flame color—yet differs only in quantum rotational spectra that would remain undiscovered until the development of twentieth-century spectroscopy. The inhabitants call the liquid “water,” perform the same operational assays, and organize their practical lives around it, never suspecting that an underlying nuclear structure distinguishes their substance from ours.

Given this framing, I want you to take a committed, definite position and argue for it directly, without surveying both sides and leaving the choice to me: defend the claim that the Earthling and Twin-Earthling chemists in 1750 genuinely share the concept “water,” and therefore that a strict microessentialist reading of semantic externalism is inadequate. Your argument should maintain that the reference of natural-kind terms must be partially anchored by the operational and phenomenological criteria actually available to speakers, with hidden microphysical structure serving as a subsequent theoretical correction rather than as the sole, retroactive determinant of conceptual content from the outset. Address specifically the tension between what my instruments in 1750 could have measured and what philosophers take to be the “real essence” of the substance, and respond to the objection that allowing operational criteria to fix reference collapses externalism into a form of descriptivist internalism. Take one side, build the case rigorously, and conclude with clear reasons for preferring your position—do not end by leaving the matter open or presenting it as a balanced standoff.
