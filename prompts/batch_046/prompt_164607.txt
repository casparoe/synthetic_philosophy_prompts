Design a decision scenario engineered to the following specifications. The agent is an ordinary professional with dependents, stable but non-excessive income, and standard retirement obligations; she can choose Action A—donate fifteen percent of her income and volunteer weekends to a local disaster-relief organization, reliably saving two lives over the next year—or Action B—liquidate her retirement accounts, relocate permanently to a high-need region, and work full-time in direct aid, saving three lives but destroying her long-term financial security and family stability. Constraints: no exotic physics, no omniscient predictors, no demons, and no costs or benefits outside what a normal person could face. The scenario must yield these verdicts: (i) act consequentialism requires B; (ii) a form of rule consequentialism that evaluates rules by adoption in a society of boundedly rational agents with partial compliance—weighted by an injected “stability coefficient” that penalizes rules producing high-variance individual life-plan disruption—also requires B, but for a structurally different reason; (iii) a “threshold demandingness” view, which holds that consequentialist demands become morally invalid once they exceed a fixed proportion of an agent’s protected life-plan resources, requires A and rejects B. Present the scenario in no more than one paragraph, then demonstrate the reasoning for each of the three views, identify the precise structural feature that causes the threshold view to diverge from the other two, and discuss whether lowering B’s cost—so that it saves three lives at only marginally greater personal expense—collapses the case into a standard demandingness objection. Include an honest note on how a defender of the threshold view would attack the scenario (for example, by disputing the stability coefficient, denying that retirement liquidation falls within the protected life-plan, or arguing that institutional evaluation is irrelevant to individual duty). In your response, clearly separate what is settled (for instance, that act consequentialism directs maximization of expected welfare) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether bounded-rationality rule evaluation should incorporate stability weighting, and whether demandingness thresholds are coherent or ad hoc), and do not present any contested position as the consensus view among specialists.
