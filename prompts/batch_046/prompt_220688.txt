I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a particular strain of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness she calls interoceptive-error illusionism. Her core claim is that qualitative phenomenal properties do not exist, and the seeming presence of "what it's like" states is an artifact of predictive processing: the brain suppresses interoceptive prediction errors in a way that constructs a phenomenal self-model with no corresponding ontological properties. She anticipates the standard objection that illusionism cannot explain the apparent immediacy and transparency of experience, and her usual reply is that transparency is exactly what error suppression delivers—once the model is complete, there is no residual qualitative remainder. I want a sequence of six questions that make this deflection progressively harder. The questions should press on the mechanics of predictive suppression, the ontological status of the self-model, and—crucially—the boundary conditions where the illusion ought to break down but apparently does not, such as in anosognosia or somatoparaphrenia, without allowing her to smuggle phenomenal properties back in through the explanatory back door. Each question should anticipate her likely reply and include a direct follow-up. Avoid biographical or generic questions; keep the focus on whether her account can distinguish veridical interoceptive awareness from pathological mis-modeling without presupposing the very phenomenal properties she denies.
