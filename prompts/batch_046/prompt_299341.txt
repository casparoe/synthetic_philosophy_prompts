In debates over interventions such as cognitive enhancers, gene editing for stature, or pharmacological mood regulation, I keep encountering the same move: an advocate classifies the procedure as therapy rather than enhancement by selecting a high-demand social environment—modern knowledge work, contemporary height norms, current expectations for emotional self-regulation—as the benchmark for "species-typical" functioning. The critic responds that if the benchmark were an earlier, less demanding environment, the same individual would fall within the normal range and the intervention would unambiguously enhance. The dispute is therefore not about the biological mechanism but about which environment fixes the therapeutic baseline. This is adjacent to the therapy/enhancement distinction and to the social model of disability, yet those literatures usually ask whether disability is constructed rather than addressing this specific normative move of baseline-shifting through environmental selection. Is there an established term in bioethics or philosophy of medicine that captures this exact configuration? If not, take a definite position: either identify the nearest concept and precisely distinguish this pattern from it, or propose a term with a rigorous definition and boundary conditions. Apply your analysis to two cases—one where the environmental shift is explicit (e.g., literacy demands that did not exist a century ago) and one where it is tacit (e.g., gradually rising cultural expectations for sustained attention). Do not merely survey options; defend your classification and the edges of the concept.
