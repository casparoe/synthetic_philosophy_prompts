As a novelist who has sat through enough workshop critiques to recognize the ritual patterns, I am struck by a specific pedagogical practice in MFA programs and literary magazines: the institutional preference for deliberate blurring of conceptual boundaries between domains—rendering a grief as a weather system, a marriage as a failing architecture—while analogies that preserve strict categorical boundaries are dismissed as merely decorative or mechanical comparison. The practice treats cross-domain blending not as ornament but as the primary mechanism by which a narrative achieves cognitive recognition, and workshop evaluation rubrics often reward analogies that restructure the reader's conceptual framework over those that map surface features without altering categories. What philosophical commitments about the nature of cognition does this pedagogical institution presuppose? Does it commit to the view, developed by Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander, that analogy-making is the core of cognition rather than logic or feature-matching, or can it be accommodated by a more modest view that analogies are merely heuristics? Does the distinction between analogies that access deep conceptual structures and those that remain at the surface presuppose that concepts have fixed essences accessible through blending, or is it compatible with a view that all categorization is already analogical and therefore no single analogy is deeper than another? Furthermore, if this practice were replaced by a pedagogy that treated narrative comprehension as primarily propositional or emotional simulation rather than analogical restructuring, what elements of the workshop evaluation would have to change, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several theories of mind despite its apparent commitment to one?
