I've been thinking on my own about how people understand what others really mean when they speak, especially when the words don't say everything outright. I've come to hold the following views, but I worry they pull in different directions. Could you audit them?

My commitments are these:

1. People often communicate meanings they never literally state, and those implied meanings are just as real a part of what was said as the explicit words.
2. There is a genuine difference between what a sentence means by itself and what a particular speaker means by uttering it in a given moment.
3. Real conversation is frequently competitive, deceptive, or performative rather than genuinely cooperative, yet listeners still reliably recover implied meanings.
4. Certain implications seem built into expressions automatically — for example, saying "some of my friends arrived" usually carries the implication that not all did, even when the speaker hasn't actively considered that.
5. If someone accidentally reveals a secret through their choice of words, what they "meant" should still be only what they intended, not what a listener could technically derive; yet I also think the listener hasn't necessarily misunderstood them.
6. Grasping an implication feels more like empathising with a speaker's intention than like working through a logical proof, but it still requires that the speaker intends me to recognise that very intention.
7. Irony seems to depend on a layered shared awareness — I know that you know that I don't mean this literally — and without that several-level-deep common knowledge, irony collapses into mere ambiguity.

Please examine these for consistency. In particular:

- Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict.
- Rank the real tensions by severity.

- Tell me which single change would clean up the most problems if I had to choose one.
