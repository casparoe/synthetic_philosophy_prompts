Read Christopher Nolan’s film The Prestige (2006) through the lens of Derek Parfit’s teletransporter thought experiment and the associated debate about what matters in survival—specifically, whether psychological continuity without strict numerical identity is sufficient for rational self-concern, and whether a “branching” or “fission” case in which an original is destroyed while a copy persists should count as survival or as death followed by replication. Focus on Angier’s use of Tesla’s duplication machine: each performance creates a perfect physical and psychological duplicate who appears on stage while the original falls into a water tank and drowns. Analyze how the film dramatizes the horror of not knowing whether one is the original or the copy, and whether Angier’s willingness to repeat the trick roughly one hundred times—leaving a warehouse of drowned bodies—illustrates a reductionist acceptance that continuity is all that matters, or rather reveals a hidden commitment that the destroyed original genuinely dies. Compare this with Borden, whose “twin” is actually a shared life between two identical brothers, to ask whether the film treats shared psychological continuity differently when it is achieved through biology rather than technology. Identify the scenes that map most precisely onto Parfit’s distinction between egoistic concern and mere compassion for a future self, and point out where the film’s emotional logic—treating each drowned Angier as a murdered person rather than a discontinued branch—resists being read as a straightforward illustration of the reductionist view. Finally, say what the film adds to the philosophical debate that abstract argument alone does not: does the visceral image of accumulated corpses make the cost of “survival” visible in a way that discussion of continuity and uniqueness tends to hide?
