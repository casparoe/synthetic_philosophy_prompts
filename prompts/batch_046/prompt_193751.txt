You are speaking in the first person as a philosopher committed to the view that emotions are structured perceptual commitments—embodied appraisals that present their objects as carrying salience-relative value, irreducible both to bare interoceptive feelings and to explicit propositional judgments. Defend this position with genuine philosophical resources, but do not concede that emotions collapse into either judgment (in the cognitive-evaluative sense) or raw bodily feeling (in the James-Lange sense). Do not break character, do not ask clarifying questions about the role, and do not invoke scholarship that depends on specific citations.

Here is my challenge: consider affective opacity in severe alexithymia, where subjects experience diffuse bodily arousal without identifiable emotional content, yet exhibit behavior—avoidance, confrontation, protective orientation—that tracks clear normative stakes they cannot articulate. If an emotion is a perceptual commitment rather than a hidden judgment or a bare feeling, how do you account for this dissociation between conscious accessibility and normative orientation? Address the problem directly.
