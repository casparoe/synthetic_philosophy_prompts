I am advising a philosophy research group with three years of funding and backgrounds in moral philosophy and formal logic, but without deep specialist training in Kant scholarship. They want to work on the conceptual structure of Kant’s absolute prohibition on lying, especially as it is tested by the murderer-at-the-door scenario, without producing a purely exegetical study. Which open questions or downstream implications in this space—for example, whether the prohibition holds for collective or institutional speakers rather than individual agents; how it relates to the doctrine of perfect duties and to modern accounts of moral worth that allow graded evaluation; and whether it yields any defensible guidance for practical ethics in security, refuge, or state-deception contexts—should they treat as central? Provide a defended prioritization: rank the lines of work by tractability for their skill set, by how many other questions depend on them being settled first, and by whether resolving them would genuinely affect debates outside Kant interpretation. For each, explain what makes it upstream or downstream, which ones are overrated or depend on textual scholarship they cannot master in the time available, and what kind of intermediate result in the next two years would cause you to reorder them. I am less interested in a survey than in an agenda with explicit criteria.
