I am working through a reconstructed problem set in my ancient philosophy course on Aristotelian economics, where the instructor has been issuing implicit verdicts on exchange and acquisition cases without stating the principle behind them. Her rulings so far are these: first, a shoemaker trading footwear for grain with a farmer, where both goods are destined for direct household use, is natural and praiseworthy; second, a merchant who buys olive oil after harvest and resells it during drought at many times the price, even with fully consenting buyers, is engaged in blameworthy chrematistike; third, lending silver at interest for consumption, treating the metal as reproductive, is the worst form of acquisition because money is naturally barren; fourth, using surplus agricultural yield to endow a local gymnasium and library directed toward civic virtue rather than private luxury falls under proper oikonomia; fifth, bartering silk for iron tools where both traders intend only to equip their own households is permissible; sixth, accumulating coin as an end in itself, with no plan for use or exchange, is not true acquisition but a category mistake.

We now face a case she has not addressed: a physician receives substantial payment in gold for saving the life of a wealthy trader. Rather than spending the coin, she immediately converts it into direct ownership of productive vineyards and rental dwellings, lives entirely on the agricultural yield and rent, and devotes all surplus income to maintaining her practice and training apprentices without charge. The trader who paid her originally acquired his gold through the very kind of speculative retail trade she previously condemned. Predict her verdict on the physician—does this fall under natural oikonomia, unnatural chrematistike, or a hybrid category? Identify at least two distinct interpretive rules that could consistently generate her first six rulings, such as a rule keyed to the social origin of the wealth, a rule keyed to whether the acquired medium is barren or productive, and a rule keyed to the final use of the returns. Say which rule you think best explains her track record, what that rule predicts about the physician, and propose one further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate the origin-based rule from the use-based rule.
