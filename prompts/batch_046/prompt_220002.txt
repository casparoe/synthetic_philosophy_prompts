At a recent colloquium on comparative metaphysics, two colleagues—referred to here as Mara and Siddhartha—entered into an extended disagreement over the Buddhist doctrine of anatta and its consequences for personal identity and moral responsibility. Mara began by contending that if anatta is taken literally, there is no enduring self that persists through time; therefore the agent who performed an action yesterday is not strictly identical to the one held accountable today, which appears to dissolve moral responsibility into an illusion. Siddhartha responded that this interpretation imports a Western substance-ontology into Buddhism: the tradition denies an intrinsic, permanent self-nature (svabhava), not conventional personal continuity (pudgala). Moral accountability, he maintained, operates at the conventional level because karma tracks causal-psychological continuity among the five aggregates (skandhas) without requiring a soul-like substrate.

Mara countered that if conventional identity is ultimately reducible to impersonal streams of aggregates, then it functions as a useful fiction—analogous to speaking of a chariot moving when only its parts are rearranging. If praise and blame are directed at such a fiction, Buddhist ethics becomes implicitly fictionalist about responsibility: either the tradition must admit that no one is genuinely accountable, or it must concede that responsibility requires more than mere causal continuity. Siddhartha replied that Mara was presupposing that responsibility needs an enduring substrate rather than an appropriate causal and psychological connection; in Buddhist terms, karma operates through dependently originated processes, not ownership. He further argued that anatta is not equivalent to Western reductionism—while Derek Parfit also denies a further fact of personal identity beyond psychological continuity and connectedness, Buddhism additionally rejects what Siddhartha called the proprietorship model of consciousness, according to which mental states belong to a unified owner-subject. On this view, there are only dependently arisen aggregates, so anatta undermines not merely identity but the very framework of mental proprietorship.

Mara then raised a soteriological challenge: if there is strictly no self, then who achieves liberation? The tradition speaks of Buddhas and arhats attaining nirvana, which seems to presuppose a liberatee. Without one, the spiritual goal appears incoherent—like describing a fire being extinguished when there was never any fire-essence to begin with. Siddhartha answered that nirvana is not an achievement by a self but the cessation of the conditions—craving and clinging (mamankara)—that generate the illusion of selfhood and suffering. Conventional language about attaining liberation is pedagogical rather than metaphysically literal, comparable to describing a flame passing between candles without positing a flame-substance that travels.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each turn—Mara's initial dissolution argument, Siddhartha's conventional-level reply, Mara's fictionalist challenge together with Siddhartha's anti-reductionist distinction and rejection of proprietorship, and finally Mara's soteriological objection paired with Siddhartha's cessation response—rate whether the point is clearly mistaken, mostly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing: for example, whether there is a live middle path between fictionalism and reductionism that preserves accountability without proprietorship, or whether Siddhartha's appeal to pedagogical language fully resolves the tension between literal anatta and normative soteriology. Be precise about where the arguments genuinely intersect and where they slide past one another.
