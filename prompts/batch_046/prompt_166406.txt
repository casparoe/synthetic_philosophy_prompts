In debates over animal ethics, a recurring argumentative move—prominent in Peter Singer’s framework and now widespread in both academic and popular discourse—is to present speciesism as structurally analogous to racism and sexism: just as race and sex are biologically real but morally arbitrary bases for distributing rights or consideration, so too is species membership. The analogy is typically deployed to license the conclusion that we should extend equal moral consideration to sentient animals and abandon practices that treat species membership as a decisive moral boundary.

Audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: for each salient element in the racism and sexism cases—the categorizing trait, the mechanism of exclusion, the victim’s relevant capacities, the institutional structures that reinforce the boundary, the corrective moral principle, and the role of in-group solidarity—identify what plays the analogous role in the speciesism case. Be precise about what maps cleanly and what requires stretching.

Second, separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the analogy’s argumentative work from those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, examine whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions about the social construction of categories, about intra-specific reciprocity and moral agency, and about evolutionary kinship—features central to the critique of racism and sexism but contested in the inter-species case. Also consider whether the analogy suppresses genuinely different capacities—such as autonomous long-term planning, entry into reciprocal moral contracts, or linguistic self-narration—that might ground differential treatment without being “arbitrary” in the same sense as race.

Third, evaluate whether the conclusion—that species membership is morally arbitrary and should be replaced by a criterion like sentience—survives once these disanalogies are fully on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. Does the arbitrariness argument depend on treating all group boundaries as like race, and if species boundaries differ in kind—being biologically rooted and tracking real cognitive discontinuities—does the parallel collapse?

Finally, propose whether a different analogy—perhaps to ageism, ableism, or to a framework of custodial obligation rather than rights—better captures the moral relationship between humans and nonhuman animals, and explain precisely what it gets right that the racism and sexism analogy misses.
