Write an essay on the controversy surrounding Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture"—the claim that human extinction would harm present people not merely through the deaths involved, but by undermining the meaning and value of long-term projects that implicitly presuppose a human future extending beyond our own lives.

Your essay should weigh the conjecture against two serious objections. First, consider the dependency objection: it seems implausible that the welfare of present individuals should hinge so heavily on the existence of distant strangers they will never encounter. Second, address what we might call the "archival successor" problem—an under-discussed scenario in which humanity's collective projects are fully preserved and actively continued by a non-biological successor civilization or an enduring, self-maintaining digital archive. If narrative continuity and project-completion can be secured without biological human survival, does this undermine Scheffler's specific claim, or does it merely relocate the harm to a different kind of loss? Reach and justify a definite conclusion about whether the afterlife conjecture successfully explains why extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves, grounding your verdict in an analysis of whether project-based meaning genuinely requires actual human continuation or can survive appropriate forms of institutional and cultural transmission.
