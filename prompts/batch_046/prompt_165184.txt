In popular accounts of Bell's theorem, quantum correlations are likened to two stage magicians who rehearse a secret script before separating: no matter what the audience asks, their answers match without any message passing during the performance. The analogy is used to suggest that hidden variables could explain entanglement locally, and that talk of "spooky action" is merely confusion about pre-arranged instructions. Audit the analogy precisely: map the script, the audience's choices, the stage separation, and the "no signaling during the show" condition onto the formal conditions of parameter independence, outcome independence, and the statistical independence of measurement settings from hidden variables. Identify where the correspondence collapses—for instance, does the script correspond to a deterministic hidden state that fixes outcomes regardless of distant settings, and does that violate the statistical predictions Bell derived rather than save locality? Next, inject a superdeterminist variant: suppose the audience's questions and the script share a common deterministic past, so the free choice of settings is illusory. Does the analogy then genuinely restore locality, or does it merely replace nonlocal correlation with a global conspiracy that the stage-magic image was never built to represent? Finally, say whether the analogy can carry any real explanatory weight in showing why standard locality assumptions fail, or whether it only disguises a particular interpretation of "local" behind theatrical imagery.
