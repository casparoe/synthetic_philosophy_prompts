I'm developing an essay on the paradox of tragedy that deliberately avoids the familiar Aristotelian catharsis and Humean conversion-of-pain frameworks that dominate introductory treatments. The core claim is that tragic art functions not as emotional purging or as pleasurable contrast to real suffering, but as a form of "structured co-suffering"—a bounded, socially available sorrow that validates unstructured personal grief without requiring the reciprocity that actual mourning demands. Specifically, I'm examining cases where people actively seek out sorrowful films or music during periods of acute real-world grief, reporting that the mediated sorrow feels more real than their own despite full awareness of fictionality. I want to test whether this undermines the standard view that tragic pleasure depends on some form of belief in the represented reality, and whether it points instead to a cognitive-affective posture closer to mono no aware—not as generic impermanence-awareness but as a disciplined, shared recognition that suffering acquires form when witnessed collectively through art. The essay also asks whether cultures that emphasize indirect emotional expression produce different patterns of tragic consumption without reducing to a universalist psychological claim.

Please comment on the accuracy of the philosophical mapping—particularly whether "structured co-suffering" actually distinguishes itself from catharsis or from Schopenhauer's suspension of the will, and whether my framing of mono no aware is doing conceptual work it cannot sustain. I'm also unsure whether the observation that fictional sorrow can feel more real than personal grief is a stable phenomenological datum or an artifact of post-hoc rationalization, and whether that distinction collapses the argument.
