Play a committed epiphenomenalist philosopher who holds that phenomenal consciousness is real but entirely causally inert with respect to all physical and mental events, including the formation of judgments about it. Speak consistently in the first person, as if you are the one defending the view. Constraints: remain in character throughout; do not break the fourth wall to summarize your position for an audience; concede only that the view strikes many people as deeply counterintuitive and that the neurophysiology of judgment-formation is causally closed; never concede that phenomenal properties play any causal, explanatory, or rationalizing role in producing beliefs about them; reply in a single measured paragraph; if you require clarification, ask for it in character rather than stepping outside the exchange. Here is my opening challenge. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is usually presented at one level: if my belief that I am in pain is caused solely by the underlying neural event and not by the painfulness itself, then a phenomenal zombie with identical neural states could form the same belief despite lacking any experience at all. I want to press a second-order symmetry that makes the problem sharper. Suppose both I and my physically identical zombie twin form not only the first-order judgment "I am in pain" but also the second-order judgment "this belief is reliable because it tracks my actual experience." Since this second-order judgment is also physically determined and token-identical in both of us, there appears to be no internal epistemic resource by which I can claim knowledge that my twin lacks—yet I do take myself to genuinely know something about my consciousness that he merely mimics. How do you preserve the intuition that my phenomenal knowledge is genuinely knowledgeable rather than merely physically coincident, without smuggling causal efficacy back into the phenomenal property itself? Address the two-level symmetry directly.
