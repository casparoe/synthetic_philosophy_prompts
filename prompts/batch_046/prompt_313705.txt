At a recent colloquium on historical causation, Dr. Elara Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen debated whether non-human forces should be registered as historical actors in an archive mapping the long nineteenth century. Voss argued that the 1815 Tambora eruption and the cholera pandemics that followed should appear alongside human figures, since both reshaped demography, artistic production, and state policy in ways not reducible to individual intention. She distinguished agentive causation, which requires intentionality, from node-causation, which requires only that an entity occupy a position in a causal network whose removal would alter the outcome, and she claimed that denying node-status to volcanoes and microbes commits an anthropocentric error she called substrate chauvinism.

Chen replied that this conflates explanation with attribution. He granted that Tambora was a necessary condition for the 1816 climate anomalies, but insisted that labeling it an actor invites environmental determinism—the fallacy of treating geological or biological events as explanations that excuse human political failures. He invoked the 1943 Bengal famine and the food crises during the Great Leap Forward, noting that in both cases climate stress and crop failure were present, yet Amartya Sen’s well-known analysis shows that entitlement collapse and policy choices, not nature alone, produced mass death. To list Tambora as an actor, Chen said, risks the same distortion: obscuring how British trade regulations and local hoarding turned weather into famine.

Voss countered that Chen was smuggling moral responsibility into causal analysis. No one proposes holding Tambora morally accountable; recognizing its node-status is precisely what prevents us from overestimating human control. She proposed a framework she termed the careenium—a causal space in which symbolic representations (maps, census categories, medical taxonomies) and physical substrates (magma, bacteria, grain markets) interact without either layer fully reducing the other. Within a careenium, simmballs—symbolic tokens that acquire directional force through institutional embedding—can redirect substrate-level processes without being identical to them. The nineteenth-century cholera map, for instance, was a simmball: a symbolic representation that redirected quarantine policies, which in turn altered bacterial transmission paths.

Chen responded that this is greedy reductionism in reverse. By granting simmballs causal efficacy over the substrate, Voss makes symbols do physical work they cannot perform. The cholera map did not push bacteria around; it changed human behavior, and changed behavior altered bacterial exposure. To say the symbol redirected the substrate is a category error—like claiming a weather forecast caused the storm. He argued that the careenium collapses the distinction between representation and mechanism, and that the only way to avoid environmental determinism is to keep causal stories at the level where responsibility and intervention actually operate: human institutions.

Voss answered that Chen’s view assumes a skyhook—a pure stratum of institutional causation that floats free of biological and geological conditions. If human institutions are themselves embedded in climate, disease ecology, and soil chemistry, then the careenium is not reductionism but an anti-reductionist map showing how substrate and symbol co-produce history. The question is not whether Tambora intended to reshape Romantic art, but whether excluding it from the archive distorts our understanding of why the period turned toward the sublime and the catastrophic.
