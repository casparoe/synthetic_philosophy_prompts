My colleague in historical sociology classifies past societies as having either "collapsed" or merely "changed" according to a framework she has never fully spelled out, and I am trying to reverse-engineer it from her verdicts. So far she has ruled as follows: the Late Bronze Age crisis in the Eastern Mediterranean and Near East (around 1177 BCE) counts as collapse; the Classic Maya southern lowlands (around 800–950 CE) do not—she treats them as political decentralisation in which literacy, settlement, and religious practice continued; Rapa Nui (Easter Island, roughly 1600–1800) counts as collapse; the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century CE does not—she sees institutional continuity through the Church, Roman law, and successor kingdoms; and the Harappan transition (around 1900–1300 BCE) does not, since she regards it as gradual dispersal and localisation rather than abrupt rupture.

She is now examining the abandonment of the Chacoan and Mesa Verde centres in the Ancestral Puebloan region (around 1280–1300 CE), in which road networks, great-house architecture, and centralised ceremonial coordination disappeared within a generation or two, whilst the populations themselves migrated south and east to the Rio Grande Pueblos without any evident demographic crash. Predict whether she will classify this as collapse or as mere change. Then identify the two most plausible implicit rules that fit all five earlier verdicts equally well—one centred on rapid institutional simplification coupled with the failure of functional successors, the other centred on irreversible demographic contraction driven by ecological or systemic overload—and state precisely what each rule predicts for the Chaco case. Give your best guess as to which rule she actually runs, with reasoning, and propose the single further historical or hypothetical case that would most clearly adjudicate between them.
