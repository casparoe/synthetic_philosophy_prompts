I am drafting the final exam for a graduate seminar in value theory centered on the metaphysics and ethics of negative states. I need one 90-minute essay question that forces students to engage with tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering is intrinsically valuable, and that the presence of pleasure is at best instrumentally valuable—without letting them retreat into a generic summary of negative utilitarianism or preference-satisfaction theories. To achieve this, build the question around a specific conceptual scenario: a hypothetical pharmacological intervention that permanently eliminates all future cravings in a population but simultaneously caps their capacity for intense pleasure, leaving baseline affect unchanged, with the further twist that some recipients report a subtle, enduring sense of existential flattening that is not itself painful but is experienced as a loss of directedness. The question should ask students to determine whether a consistent tranquilist ought to endorse universal adoption of this intervention, and in answering they must explicitly address: (i) whether the absence of craving can be separated conceptually and normatively from the absence of suffering; (ii) whether the reduction in pleasure-capacity counts as a harm on the tranquilist account; and (iii) whether the reported flattening introduces a hidden form of suffering or craving that undermines the intervention's justification. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words that demonstrates the required distinctions rather than merely labeling them, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks that lists five must-hit points with their individual weights, specifies the precise conceptual move that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer—namely, showing why tranquilism is not equivalent to the claim that suffering-reduction is the sole moral priority—and includes a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically fail.
