I'm preparing a discussion for a graduate seminar on international justice and keep encountering a particular analogy used against the postwar tribunals: prosecuting Axis leaders at Nuremberg and Tokyo for acts not explicitly criminalized beforehand is like a referee tearing up the rulebook after the match ends and then ejecting players for moves that were legal during play. The comparison is typically invoked to license the charge of victors' justice — that retroactive law renders the trials illegitimate irrespective of the defendants' moral culpability. Before I accept that verdict, I want a rigorous audit of the analogy's reach. Provide an explicit correspondence table that maps referee, rulebook, match, players, and ejection onto the legal and political elements of both tribunals; then specify where the mapping fractures, particularly regarding whether customary international law already contained prohibitions on aggressive war and atrocities, whether the defendants genuinely lacked awareness of moral wrongness, and whether the game ever possessed neutral referees to begin with.

I am especially concerned with whether the analogy applies uniformly across the different categories of charges — specifically, whether it fits the charge of crimes against peace, which appears genuinely retroactive, with the same force it applies to conventional war crimes and crimes against humanity, and whether it can accommodate internal tribunal dissent, such as the well-known rejection of retroactivity arguments by at least one Tokyo judge. Does the comparison smuggle in an idealized image of pre-1945 international law as a finished rulebook rather than an emerging, contested practice? Once the disanalogies are fully exposed, assess whether the conclusion that the trials were fundamentally illegitimate survives, or whether the analogy was silently carrying the entire argumentative load, and propose a more precise comparison — perhaps one distinguishing the creation of rules from their enforcement — that might do the same work without the distortions.
