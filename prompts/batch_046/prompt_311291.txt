Write a thirty-nine-line sestina—six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi—on Pascal’s mugging. Choose six end-words and rotate them according to the standard sestina pattern; among them must be “cost” and “probability.” The word “cost” must remain orthographically identical in every appearance, including the envoi, serving as the unmoving pivot. The word “probability,” however, must undergo a formal decay tied to the stanza number: in stanza one it appears in full; in stanza two as “prob”; in stanza three as “pro”; in stanza four as “pr”; in stanza five as “p”; and in stanza six as a silent dash or blank space where the word once stood. In the envoi, place “cost” alongside that absence so that the demand survives the erasure of its justification.

The poem should render the decision-theoretic structure precisely: a mugger offers an arbitrarily large reward multiplied by a non-zero probability that shrinks without bound, producing an apparently infinite expected utility, yet the rational agent refuses to pay any substantial price. Do not resolve this tension into a simple sermon about rationality or credulity; instead, let the vocabulary’s collapse mirror the argument’s self-consumption—the premises shrink to nothing while the demand remains intact. The language should be accurate enough that a philosopher familiar with expected-utility reasoning recognizes the mechanics of the mugging, but it should also stand as poetry for a reader who has never encountered the problem. Avoid citations, named authors, textual references, or bibliographic apparatus.
