Grade the following student answer to an exam question in an early-modern philosophy seminar. The prompt was: "Descartes denies that animals have minds. Reconstruct his two main lines of argument—the mechanistic-physiological account and the language-based criterion—referring to Discourse on Method, Part V, and his correspondence with Henry More. Then assess whether these arguments cohere with his substance dualism, with specific attention to how he handles human infants and those who cannot speak."

Student answer:
Descartes treats animals as pure automata, comparing them to clockwork in the Discourse. The arrangement of their organs can produce complex behavior—flight, feeding, avoidance—without any internal mental state directing it. He reinforces this with the language argument: arranging words to express novel thoughts requires a rational soul, and no animal, not even a parrot, ever does this. Together these show animals lack res cogitans.

This fits substance dualism cleanly. Human bodies are also res extensa, but they are united to an immaterial soul that thinks and initiates voluntary motion through the pineal gland. Animals have bodies that work the same mechanical way, only without a soul attached. One tension remains: Descartes admits that human infants and the mute possess rational souls despite not speaking, which looks like an ad hoc exemption. If the absence of language does not disprove a soul in infants, it is unclear why it should do so for animals—unless one assumes animals could never develop the faculty, but Descartes never justifies that assumption. A deeper problem is interactionist: if all animal behavior can be explained mechanically, it becomes mysterious why human bodies need a soul to produce behavior at all, rather than merely to produce rational thought. Still, Descartes is consistent in holding that animals lack even unconscious sensation, since any feeling requires a soul to receive impressions at the pineal gland, and animals have none.
