I work with predictive processing models—hierarchical generative architectures, precision-weighted prediction errors, and active inference—and I want to understand the philosophical debate about natural kinds entirely within that vocabulary. Restate the three main positions in that debate—that scientific taxonomy tracks mind-independent joints in nature, that it reflects only our practical and explanatory interests, and the intermediate view that kinds are homeostatic property clusters maintained by causal mechanisms—using only the concepts of generative models, precision allocation, manifold structure, and prediction-error minimization. In particular, translate the idea that science carves nature at its joints into a claim about whether a model partitions its input space into discrete latent causes with sharp boundaries and high precision weights, as opposed to representing the world as continuous manifolds with graded, context-dependent precision. For each position, report what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit (for example, treating normative claims about correct taxonomy as claims about optimal precision settings), and what seems to have no counterpart at all—such as the assertion that joints exist independently of any inference system. Finally, say whether that residue indicates that predictive processing genuinely lacks a metaphysical or normative dimension it needs, or whether it reveals that the original philosophical debate concerns distinctions that make no operational difference to how an actual modeling system carves up its sensory input.
