I've been working through Dewey's political philosophy—especially the continuity he draws between democratic life and experimental inquiry in works such as *The Public and Its Problems* and *Democracy and Education*—and I want to check whether the package I've assembled is jointly tenable before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(a) Democracy is not essentially representation, voting, or majority rule, but a mode of cooperative social inquiry directed at shared problems; political institutions are instruments for organizing that inquiry.

(b) Every normative claim—including claims about justice, rights, and the legitimacy of democracy itself—is a working hypothesis to be tested by its consequences in experience; there are no a priori, non-revisable ends.

(c) Despite (b), democratic inquiry requires stable "conditions of association" (free interaction, open communication, association without coercion) that must be protected as functional preconditions, not treated as further hypotheses open to experimental revision.

(d) The standard for evaluating democratic experiments is objective: the growth and enrichment of shared, communicable experience, rather than the aggregation or satisfaction of pre-given individual preferences.

(e) Expert knowledge should guide democratic inquiry by framing problems and supplying methods of investigation, but experts must never hold veto authority over collective conclusions.

(f) Individual preferences are not static inputs to be summed; they are themselves reconstructed and transformed through the deliberative process.
