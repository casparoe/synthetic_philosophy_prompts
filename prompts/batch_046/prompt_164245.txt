Student answer:
Van Fraassen's reflection principle says that if you come to know that an expert assigns credence r to some proposition A, and you don't have independent grounds for disagreement, then your own rational credence in A ought to be r too. It's a constraint on belief revision: the expert's opinion functions as a direct normative standard for your mental state, not just as evidence to be weighed. Unlike standard Bayesian updating, reflection doesn't treat the expert's report as one more piece of evidence; it replaces your prior credence with the expert's value entirely for that proposition.

This is structurally similar to Lewis's Principal Principle, which says that rational credence should match known objective chance. The difference is that the Principal Principle concerns physical probabilities — like the half-life of an isotope — whereas reflection concerns the beliefs of other rational agents. So reflection is intersubjective, while the Principal Principle is world-directed.

One well-known objection is that reflection seems to erase first-person authority. Suppose I learn that a neuropsychologist assigns 0.6 to the claim that I am currently in pain. By reflection, I should also assign 0.6, which feels wrong because my own phenomenology gives me privileged access. Defenders might limit reflection to empirical domains where experts really do know better, but drawing that line looks ad hoc.

There's also a deeper tension: reflection was originally meant to solve peer disagreement by telling agents to converge on expert consensus, yet if experts themselves are rational but disagree, there may be no unique credence to adopt. This makes reflection either unstable or dependent on a prior ranking of experts, which the principle itself doesn't provide. Despite this, reflection captures something important about the social dimension of rational belief.
