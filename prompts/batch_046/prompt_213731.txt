I defend a position I call normative reciprocity internalism about corruption: an institutional practice counts as corrupt not when it deviates from Western liberal standards of impartiality, but when it involves a breach of the reciprocal obligations that are actually acknowledged and relied upon by the participants within that practice itself. On this view, clientelism or gift-exchange in a patronage system is not corrupt if the obligations are transparent, mutually recognized, and consistently honored; it becomes corrupt only when one party exploits information asymmetry or violates the terms of reciprocity that both sides treat as binding—regardless of whether an outside observer would classify the exchange as bribery. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view. Which empirical patterns—say, of participants in patron-client systems who themselves describe certain exchanges as corrupt despite their transparency—would be decisive? Which conceptual objections, perhaps showing that the view collapses into either an unfalsifiable cultural relativism or a hidden universalism, are fatal versus merely awkward? I am especially interested in whether there are cross-cultural regularities in how people distinguish legitimate reciprocity from exploitation that do not depend on Western formal rules, and whether my view can accommodate them without ad hoc qualification. Rank the defeaters by how damaging they are, indicate which ones a committed defender of normative reciprocity internalism should concede rather than dismiss, and assess honestly whether the position risks being insulated from refutation—if so, whether that insulation undermines its philosophical value. I would personally find it most persuasive if you could show that local actors consistently invoke standards of fairness or betrayal that are independent of the specific reciprocal contracts I identify, or that the view cannot distinguish corruption from mere inefficiency without smuggling in external norms. Please answer in American English.
