As a doctoral student in political philosophy working on democratic legitimacy, I am trying to understand what implicit theory of sovereignty is built into a specific bundle of British constitutional practices. The bundle includes the following: at the State Opening of Parliament, the monarch delivers the legislative program—now the King's Speech—from the throne in the House of Lords; members of the House of Commons are summoned by the officer known as Black Rod, whose entry is symbolically refused when the door is slammed in his face, after which they proceed to hear the speech standing at the bar; and royal assent to bills passed by both Houses is granted automatically by long-standing convention, though the prerogative technically remains a personal act of the Crown. Meanwhile, constitutional doctrine describes Parliament as the Crown-in-Parliament, and courts treat enacted statutes as expressions of parliamentary will without monarchical veto.

What philosophical commitments about sovereignty, representation, and authority does this institutional bundle presuppose? Must one accept that sovereignty is fused rather than divided, or can the rituals of monarchical presence be interpreted as purely symbolic while the legal reality of automatic assent points to a purely procedural view? Is the bundle internally consistent—do the ceremonial affirmation of monarchical authority and the conventional nullification of that authority in assent and speech-making presuppose the same conception of the Crown's role, or do they pull in opposite directions? And what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly popular-sovereignty account in which the monarch holds no constitutional position whatsoever?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among constitutional theorists and political philosophers, and do not present a contested interpretation—for instance, whether the Crown is a constituent part of Parliament in a metaphysical sense or only a ceremonial figurehead—as the consensus view.
