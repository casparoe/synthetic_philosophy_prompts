I have been discussing with colleagues in our practical-reasoning reading group whether the standard objections to expected-value fanaticism—usually framed as conflicts with common-sense risk aversion or with deontological side-constraints—really capture the deepest conceptual problem. Most textbook examples treat the probabilities and utilities as independently given: a tiny chance of infinite utility, or of existential catastrophe, and then we ask whether rationality permits letting that tail dominate. But I am convinced there is a more subtle difficulty that arises when the magnitude of the payoff and the credibility of the probability are not separable, because the mechanism that would produce the extreme outcome is itself sensitive to whether the agent treats it as live. I would like you to construct a concrete, conceptually precise example—something richer than a bare Pascal's-wager-style intuition pump—that illustrates this kind of structural entanglement.

Specifically, present a decision scenario in which an agent faces an option with a seemingly enormous expected value, but in which either the very feature that generates the astronomical value also alters the epistemic conditions under which the small probability can be assigned, or the agent's adoption of an EV-maximizing deliberative rule feeds back into the causal structure that determines whether the outcome occurs. Your example should make explicit where the fracture occurs: does the problem lie in the impossibility of specifying a stable prior, in a reflexive instability in the utility assignment, or in the collapse of the decision-theoretic framework itself? Do not merely catalog possibilities; take a definite position on whether your scenario reveals a genuine, principled boundary to EV reasoning or only a practical limitation that a more sophisticated model could absorb. I want this for a seminar handout, so the scenario should be rigorous enough that students can see exactly which premise is being challenged, but it should not depend on specialized empirical claims or on references to specific literature that a model could not be expected to reconstruct.
