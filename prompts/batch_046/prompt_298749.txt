Assume the role of a senior political philosopher and collective-memory researcher advising a small interdisciplinary group with limited resources. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm silently: list the conceptual tensions that arise when a defeated collective narrative is institutionalized within a living democratic identity rather than archived away.

Your task is to deliver a defended, dependency-aware research agenda—not a survey, and not a bibliography. Focus on the dynamics of “lost causes” and national myth-making, using the four reference cases (the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk) only as illustrative anchors, not as empirical case studies requiring archival excavation. To keep the framing precise, I want you to work within two modes I will define here: “integrative loss,” in which defeat is re-encoded as a moral or strategic precondition for later success and thereby domesticated into progressive history (the Dunkirk register), and “sacrificial stasis,” in which defeat is kept open as an unhealed wound that resists incorporation and demands perpetual loyalty (registers visible in Masada, in certain strands of the Lost Cause, and in some Serbian national narratives around Kosovo).

Within this framework, and without providing any bibliography, reading list, or citations, identify which open questions, conceptual dependencies, and lines of work matter most. Specifically:

- Which unresolved problem is most upstream for a research program of about three years (one senior investigator, two postdocs, conceptual and comparative-historical tools, no large-scale new data collection): the conditions under which integrative loss collapses back into sacrificial stasis; the normative status of “mnemonic debt” (the idea that a polity owes interpretive labor—maintaining space for the defeated narrative to speak—rather than mere commemorative acknowledgment); or the institutional mechanics by which curricula, memorial architecture, and legal frameworks stabilize one mode over the other?

- Give a prioritization that maps dependencies: which questions must be settled before others become tractable, which are tractable now, and which you would deprioritize because resolving them would not significantly change downstream answers or practical stakes?

- Defend the criteria governing your ranking—such as conceptual upstreamness, tractability without web-dependent or highly specialized empirical sources, normative urgency for pluralist democratic deliberation, and risk of persistent conceptual confusion—and state explicitly what kind of argument or comparative observation would cause you to reorder the agenda.

- Identify one fashionable or commonly discussed line of inquiry in this area that you consider overrated or overdetermined, and explain why it should not receive scarce resources in this particular program.

Think carefully, avoid generic lists, and produce a polished, agenda-style response that fixes what deserves attention, for whom, toward what end, and at what cost to other possibilities.
