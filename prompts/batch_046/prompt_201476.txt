You are preparing to defend a thesis at an interdisciplinary seminar on collective cognition, before an audience of philosophers of biology and comparative cognition researchers. Your thesis is that ant colonies can possess genuine intentional states—not by analogy to human consciousness, but through what you call non-aggregative content: patterns of pheromone-mediated interaction that instantiate directedness (aboutness) without being decomposable into the mental states of individual ants. You invoke the familiar Aunt Hillary and Ant Fugue scenarios, but you invert their usual reading. Where those thought experiments are typically taken to show that collective behavior is merely an emergent appearance lacking a unified subject, you argue that they demonstrate interpretive vacancy—a condition in which intentional content exists precisely because there is no centralized interpreter, no internal Aunt Hillary inside the colony. You concede that colonies lack phenomenal binding and first-person access, yet you maintain that they exhibit a form of distributed intentionality that is ontologically on a par with, though structurally different from, individual human intentionality.

The audience accepts group selection, swarm intelligence, and functional explanations of collective behavior, but they reject panpsychism and insist that mental ascriptions require specific behavioral markers: error-correction that revises goals rather than merely adjusting parameters, context-sensitive learning that transfers across domains, and representational decoupling—the capacity to respond to an absent state of affairs. They tend to treat analogies of this kind as illustrative rather than evidential, and they are wary of importing phenomenological vocabulary such as binding or subject into evolutionary biology.

Prepare your Q&A defense sheet. Identify the eight or so objections you will actually receive from this room—not generic anti-group-mind arguments, but objections phrased in their vocabulary (functional explanation, evolutionary cost, representational decoupling, comparative cognition). Order them by the damage they would do to your proposal if left unanswered. For each objection, provide: the formulation as they would actually put it; a concise reply deliverable in about two minutes; and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or depends on a premise they are unlikely to grant. Include the objection you most hope nobody raises—the one that exposes a tension between your appeal to interpretive vacancy and your refusal of panpsychism. Advise what you should concede in your opening remarks to lower the temperature, and identify the single reply that must land for the thesis to survive the first round of questioning.
