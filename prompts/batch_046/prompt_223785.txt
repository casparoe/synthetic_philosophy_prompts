Consider the institutional practice, common in bioethics committees and some legal frameworks governing dementia care, of treating severe autobiographical disintegration—where a patient no longer recognizes family, cannot sustain temporal continuity in first-person memory, and exhibits personality traits incompatible with their prior life story—as grounds for suspending an advance directive not merely on best-interest calculations but on identity-substitution grounds: the claim being that the "author" of the directive is no longer present, because a self requires a functioning center of narrative gravity, and that center has dissolved. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory this practice presupposes. Does it commit to the view that narrative coherence is constitutive of personal identity rather than merely evidential? Does it assume that practical authority—the power to bind one's future—depends on the persistence of such a narrative center, or only that charitable interpretation permits suspension? Examine whether the practice is internally consistent: for example, does it distinguish temporary narrative disruption (delirium, acute psychosis) from permanent rupture, and if so, on what metaphysical basis rather than merely pragmatic? Distinguish strictly what the practice requires from what merely fits it well—could a biological-continuity or psychological-continuity theorist license the same suspension of directives on different grounds? Finally, specify what would have to change in committee evaluation procedures if the narrative-gravity framework were replaced by a purely bodily-continuity or memory-continuity view of personal persistence.
