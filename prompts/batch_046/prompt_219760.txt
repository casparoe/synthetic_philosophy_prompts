I have been spending the last several months embedded in a particular open-source development community, and I keep encountering a practice I cannot place within the literature I have read on signaling and social costs. Core maintainers enforce elaborate manual protocols—reading every line of submitted code by hand, requiring commit messages in an archaic format with no automation, rejecting standard testing frameworks with the explanation that automated tools obscure the underlying mechanics. The stated justification is always quality assurance, yet these manual steps do not yield measurably better outcomes than automated alternatives; they miss bugs that machines catch and add no detectable improvement to the final product. What they reliably produce is exclusion: contributors who will not absorb the friction drift to peripheral modules or leave entirely, leaving a smaller, more homogeneous core. I have noticed structurally similar patterns elsewhere—academic subfields that preserve notational conventions long after simpler ones have been adopted, not because the old notation solves problems the new ones cannot, but because mastery of the old form takes years of socialization; craft guilds that reject labor-saving machinery not on technical grounds but because widespread adoption would dissolve the boundary between members and outsiders. In each case the burden is real, but it does not advertise superior quality in the way a peacock's tail advertises fitness. Instead it advertises membership in a group that can collectively afford to ignore efficiency.

Is there an established term for this specific configuration—where a costly practice maintains group boundaries without correlating with observable output quality—and how precisely should it be separated from the handicap principle, from credentialism, and from ordinary gatekeeping? If the literature offers nothing that captures exactly this, I ask you to coin a term with explicit boundary conditions: define what belongs inside, what falls outside, and why the distinction matters conceptually rather than being a mere relabeling. I am not asking for a survey of adjacent ideas; I want a committed judgment—either identify the concept I have missed and defend the identification against the examples I have given, or propose a new one and defend its independence, stating clearly where my open-source and craft-guild cases sit relative to its edges.
