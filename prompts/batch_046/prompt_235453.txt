I'm helping a VR platform's trust-and-safety team build an internal escalation protocal, and I want to ground it in actual philosophy of virtual worlds rather than gut feeling. The modertors — smart people, not philsoophers — have to decide, for every serious incident report, whether what happened counts as a 'real harm' that triggers real-world action (clinical referral, law-enforcement notification, permanent ban) or stays inside the virtual environment's internal resolution system. They process maybe fifteen to twenty of these per shift, they've got about ten minutes per case, and there's no ethicist on call — just a senior mod who has to sign off. The stakes are lopsided: missing a real harm is catastrophic, but false positives wreck user trust and drive people out of the world.

Turn the philosophical debate about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are genuinely real — I'm especially interested in three distinctions that rarely get put together: causal entanglement with the user's physical welfare, intersubjective stability across different clients and render settings, and whether the harm is ontologically dependent on the specific substrate or could survive being ported to another platform — into a step-by-step decision procedure they can actually run. It should be ordered like a flowchart in words, with explicit tests, and it has to flag exactly where judgment can't be eliminated, probably around assessing subjective suffering and intent. Keep the distinctions that actually matter for escalation and drop the metaphysical ornament that doesn't change the outcome.

Apply it to three cases: (1) a user reports severe psychological trauma after being 'locked' in a virtual room for hours, but the logs show an exit button was always available; (2) a high-value virtual artifact is stolen, where the artifact is just a database entry but has a verified real-money exchange rate; and (3) persistent virtual harassment documented across multiple independent client recordings but leaving no physical trace — this should be the unwelcome result, where your procedure probably says it IS real enough to escalate, even though management would prefer to call it 'just pixels.' Finally, be honest about what the procedure can't do: it shouldn't claim to settle whether virtual worlds contain 'genuine' objects in some deep ontological sense, only whether a particular incident crosses the threshold for real-world intervension. Write it like you're explaining this to my grad seminar over coffee — casual, a little messy, but conceptually sharp.
