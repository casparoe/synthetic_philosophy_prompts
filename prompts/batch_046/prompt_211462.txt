I'm taking a course on chaos theory and history and I keep noticing a weird pattern in how people talk about turning points, but I don't know if it has a name. You know that old saying about losing a kingdom for want of a nail? In a lot of history writing I see what feels like the reverse problem. A historien will seize on some tiny fluke - say a courier getting delayed in a snowstorm - and present it as the decisive cause of a huge outcome, like a war turning. But then if you press them on what would have happened without the snowstorm, they don't actually trace out a different trajectory. Instead they say the structural pressures were so strong that some other fluke would have triggered the same result, or they say the outcome was basically inevitible anyway. So the micro-event is treated as both causally decisive and totally replaceable. I see this in popular accounts of the American Revolution where a messenger's wrong turn gets mentioned as crucial, but the revolutionary momentum is also called unstoppable; and in some counterfactual dissussions about WWI where an archduke's driver taking a wrong turn is named as the spark, yet the systemic tensions are described as bound to explode eventually. Is there an established concept for this specific double-move, where local contingency is invoked to explain divergence but global determinism is preserved? Or is it just inconsistent reasoning? If there's no precise term, I'd like to know what it is closest to - chaos-theoretic sensitivity? overdetermination? - and exactly where the boundary lies, because I'm writing an essay and I need to be able to flag it when I see it.
