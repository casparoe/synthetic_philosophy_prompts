In two weeks I defend a working paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on atrocity law and rhetoric. My thesis is that contemporary politics of naming mass violence is structurally distorted by what I call the "Lemkin paradox": Raphael Lemkin originally formulated genocide in 1944 as a broad concept encompassing both physical destruction and the coordinated destruction of cultural and social institutions, but the 1948 UN Convention narrowed the category to acts committed with specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. I argue that advocacy networks and state actors now strategically oscillate between these two registers—invoking the Convention's narrow intent requirement to deny legal genocide status and block intervention, while simultaneously invoking the broader moral resonance of Lemkin's original framework to mobilize outrage for other cases—thereby producing an asymmetry that undermines both legal precision and political accountability. My proposal is not to amend the Convention but to institutionalize explicit bifurcation in public and advocacy discourse: "genocide-s" (strict legal, for tribunals) and "genocide-m" (moral-political, for advocacy and historical judgment), with mandatory transparent labeling, so that the rhetorical power of the term is not weaponized against its own legal criteria.

The audience is precisely composed of: (1) international criminal lawyers who have advised on tribunal statutes and who treat the specific-intent requirement as a hard-won safeguard against overreach and politicized prosecution; (2) postcolonial historians who study how the politics of naming shapes intervention and who are skeptical of Western legal categories imposed on non-Western conflicts; and (3) activists from an NGO actively campaigning for the formal inclusion of "cultural genocide" in international law, who see the Convention's narrowness as a colonial omission and who depend on the term's moral urgency for mobilization.
