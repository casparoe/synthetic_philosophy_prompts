Our university history department has adopted a grant-writing guideline for public-facing exhibitions. Applicants must treat pathogens, climate patterns, and volcanic events as “historical actors” with explanatory weight comparable to human institutions—for example, framing the 1816 “Year Without a Summer” not merely as a backdrop to crop failures but as an agent that “forced” policy responses, and penalizing proposals that reduce such events to passive background conditions. At the same time, the guideline warns against “environmental determinism” and requires that these non-human causes be shown interacting with, rather than replacing, human decision-making.

What philosophical commitments about causation, agency, and historical explanation does this rubric presuppose? Must it endorse a specific theory of non-human agency, or is it compatible with several rival accounts? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and causation embedded in the practice, distinguish what the rubric strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined—so that alternative views of historical causation could still license the same policy. Separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of science and historiography from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation of agency or determinism as though it were the consensus view.
