After three weeks at my father’s bedside in the neurology ICU, where he was maintained on veno-arterial ECMO following the disappearance of all brainstem reflexes yet before cardiac activity had fully ceased, I have become preoccupied with the conceptual architecture of death rather than with clinical protocol alone. The physicians spoke of death as if it were a single, discrete threshold, but the machinery revealed a body in an ambiguous intermediate condition—capable of thermoregulation, immune response, and even gestational support—despite the absence of neurological integration. Before I examine particular philosophical accounts, I want the full specification of adequacy: what must a theory of human death actually deliver in order to count as satisfactory?

Write the desiderata that any adequate account must meet—the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must handle correctly, the distinctions it must preserve, and the biological results with which it must remain consistent—with the motivating consideration behind each. The list should include: (1) a principled way to address the divergence between whole-brain death and circulatory death without arbitrarily privileging one organ system; (2) accommodation of what I will call distributed vital persistence, namely the capacity of a brain-dead body supported by modern perfusion technology to maintain homeostatic and reproductive functions over days or weeks; (3) clarity on whether the distinction between the death of the organism and the death of the person should be preserved or collapsed, and with what consequences; (4) a determination of whether death is an event with a precise temporal boundary or a process that can be distributed across time and different organ systems; (5) consistency with the biological result that loss of brainstem function eliminates spontaneous integrative capacity, without requiring that every cell in the body has died; and (6) coherent verdicts for edge cases such as anencephalic newborns and brain-dead pregnant women, without generating contradictory judgments.

For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or represents a preference of a particular camp (for instance, the demand for a precise moment versus the acceptance of temporal distribution). Then identify any pair of requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether insisting on both a sharp temporal boundary and full accommodation of distributed vital persistence creates an unavoidable tension, and whether any no-go result analogous to those in other philosophical domains applies here. Finally, briefly indicate which single requirement does the most work in excluding the main existing formulations—the neurological-integration account, the traditional circulatory-respiration standard, and the higher-brain view—though I want the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on a full scorecard. I have no access to a library or database, so please provide no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need the conceptual architecture alone, rendered with enough precision that I can apply it to the clinical cases I witnessed.
