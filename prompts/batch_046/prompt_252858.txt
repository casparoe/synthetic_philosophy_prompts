I'm trying to clarify my own judgments about which metaphysical accounts of propositional and ontological unity genuinely avoid Bradley's regress, and which ones merely disguise it. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, with one that I keep revisiting.

Case 1: The simple judgment "Socrates is wise." Verdict: this is a genuinely unified proposition; there is no infinite hierarchy of relations needed to bind subject to predicate.

Case 2: The relational judgment "Socrates is taller than Theaetetus." Verdict: also genuinely unified; the relation connects its terms directly, without requiring a further binder to link the relation to them.

Case 3: A bundle-theoretic account of an object: redness, roundness, and sweetness all occur together, but there is no substratum or further item that binds them. Verdict: this is not a real unity but a mere collection; if one tries to add a binding relation, Bradley's regress immediately begins.

Case 4: An explicit hierarchical binder theory: relation R binds a and b, relation R' binds R to (a,b), and so on ad infinitum. Verdict: this is the vicious regress in its pure form; it fails to explain unity.

Case 5: A structuralist account in which relations are internal: the terms are defined solely by their positions in a structure, so "taller-than" is not an external glue but constitutive of what it is to be Socrates and Theaetetus in that arrangement. Verdict: this avoids an external binder, but I am unsure whether it delivers real unity or merely dissolves the problem by redefining the terms.

Case 6: A distributed-encoding model (injected for comparison): the properties of an object are encoded holographically across a substrate, with no discrete binding site, yet retrieval always yields a single integrated representation. Verdict: this feels like a unified whole with no binder whatsoever—either it is a counterexample to the premise that unity requires some relating principle, or it is not a proposition or object at all but a useful fiction.

Case 7: A tropes account with primitive compresence: each object has its own particularized redness-trope and roundness-trope, and these are made co-present by a primitive compresence relation that is said not to be a "real" relation. Verdict: I oscillate—sometimes I think this stops the regress legitimately, sometimes I think it is just renaming the binding relation and hiding it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is Case 6: if unity can arise without any binding principle at all, then Bradley's argument seems to rest on a false premise; but if Case 6 is not a genuine case of propositional or ontological unity, then my judgment about Case 5 may also be too generous.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, say what it delivers for Case 6 and Case 7, and be explicit about which of my verdicts—if any—it forces me to abandon in order to remain coherent. Please end with \answer{...} giving a brief label for the principle you take to best fit the pattern, and if you recommend dropping one of my verdicts, append it with a dash in the same box, e.g., \answer{internal structuralism — drop case 6}.
