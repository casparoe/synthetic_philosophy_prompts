I am currently taking an advanced undergraduate course on moral epistemology and the narrative uses of biography, and I find myself returning to a question that arose in our seminar on Plutarch and the later hagiographic tradition. A fellow student proposed what she called the 'specular model' of reading lives: she suggested that encountering a saint's vita, or indeed any exemplary biography, works rather like holding up a convex mirror to one's own character. In her account, the mirror magnifies virtues that might otherwise seem distant and diminishes the observer's own vices so they appear correctable rather than monstrous, and the reader achieves moral recognition through affective identification rather than through propositional argument. She used this image to defend the claim that biography—particularly hagiography—possesses a privileged pedagogical status over abstract ethical theory, precisely because it operates through this kind of reflective self-recognition.

The analogy struck me as doing considerable hidden work, and I should like to audit it carefully. Could you lay out the precise correspondences: what exactly plays the role of the mirror, what is the convex or polished surface, who holds it, what is the reflected image, and what counts as the 'correction' or adjustment that follows? Then identify the points where the mapping strains—especially whether it smuggles in an assumption that moral traits possess the visual immediacy and stability of physical appearance, and whether it conceals the fact that hagiographers construct their subjects through selective omission and idealisation. I should also like to know whether the conclusion—that biography is therefore a uniquely effective vehicle for moral learning—survives once these disanalogies are on the table, and whether a different analogy (perhaps biography as a staged conversation, or as a navigational chart rather than a mirror) might preserve what is genuinely valuable in the specular idea without importing its questionable metaphysics of visibility.
