My study group has constructed an argument about Spinoza's democratic theory that seems to collapse every distinction between citizen and non-citizen, and I cannot locate the error. (1) Spinoza defines right (jus) as coextensive with power (potentia): whatever a being can do by nature, it has the right to do. (2) Democratic sovereignty, for Spinoza, is not a transfer of rights to an external ruler but the collective body of the multitude united by common agreement (communis consensus), exercising the retained natural powers of its members in coordination. (3) Hence democratic sovereignty is not an institutional artifact but the actual coordinated exercise of the retained powers that sustain the collective body. (4) If sovereignty consists in the coordinated exercise of retained powers, then anyone whose power is actually integrated into the collective body—through economic, reproductive, or social coordination—participates in sovereignty, regardless of formal status. (5) Spinoza excludes women, servants, and dependents from democratic rights on the ground that they lack independent power, being governed by the will of others. (6) Yet the labor and capacities of these excluded groups are fully embedded in the collective power that makes the state persist; without their retained capacities, the sovereign body could not maintain itself. (7) Therefore, by Spinoza's own identification of sovereignty with coordinated retained power, these groups are constituent members of the democratic sovereign. (8) But this dissolves the citizen-subject boundary entirely, extending democratic right to every causal contributor in society, which proves far more than Spinoza—or any plausible democratic theory—should accept. I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) through (3), since they seem essential to Spinoza's metaphysics and his distinctive conception of democracy. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is the mistake an equivocation between metaphysical potentia and political authority, a hidden normative restriction within the idea of common agreement, or something else?
