I'm preparing a feature on Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle—specifically his letter to d'Alembert on the theater—and I want to move past the familiar claim that he simply feared luxury and moral corruption. The conceptual knot I need mapped is this: Rousseau argues that the spectator in the theater experiences intense sympathy, pity, and admiration for characters on stage, yet remains physically passive and socially invisible in the darkened hall. He contrasts this with civic festivals, where citizens observe one another in broad daylight and emotional display is inseparable from collective action. What needs explaining is the precise mechanism by which unacted-upon sympathy becomes corrupting rather than merely wasted. Rousseau appears to treat aroused but behaviorally blocked passion as dangerous—almost as if sentiment operates on a hydraulic model in which pressure, denied an outlet through action, turns inward and deforms character. Yet in his broader moral psychology he rejects mechanistic, hydraulic accounts of human feeling in favor of an active, self-constituting will. So please explain the conceptual issue: does Rousseau's condemnation of theater covertly rely on a hydraulic theory of passion that he elsewhere repudiates? If not, what alternative mechanism explains why detached emotional arousal should corrupt the spectator? And does the corruption stem primarily from the spectator's anonymity, from the actor's false representation of virtue, or from the structural mismatch between aroused sentiment and unavailable action? I need a clean conceptual map without bibliographic apparatus, since my audience will not be consulting primary texts directly and I want to avoid importing anachronistic frameworks of my own.
