I've been thinking about Dennett's sphex wasp—the one that repeats its burrow-checking ritual even when the conditions change—and wondering whether human flexibility is just the same mechanism running at higher complexity. Here is the position I'm considering: first-order human behavior is sphexish, running on fixed scripts triggered by context, and our sense of free will comes from a second-order layer that monitors those scripts. But that second layer is itself sphexish; it doesn't genuinely revise the first-order rules, it only executes a rigid self-monitoring routine that produces the feeling of openness. So the common defense of free will that appeals to our capacity for deliberation collapses, because deliberation is just another mechanistic loop. What would actually count against this? I want an honest assessment, not reassurance. For instance, is there a form of behavioral flexibility that cannot be modeled as hierarchical script execution? Would evidence that human moral revision sometimes violates predictive patterns based on prior conditioning be decisive, or merely awkward? And what do defenders of this view themselves concede could falsify it—perhaps a specific kind of causal discontinuity in decision-making—or is the position insulated by defining any observed flexibility as just another script? I want a map of where this view is genuinely exposed, what it risks, and where it risks nothing at all.
