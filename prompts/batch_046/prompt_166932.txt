I have been drafting an essay that tries to disentangle two phenomena that are often conflated in discussions of practical irrationality: classical akrasia, understood as a synchronic failure to act on one's all-things-considered judgment, and the diachronic preference reversals produced by hyperbolic discounting. Most treatments either reduce weakness of will to an intertemporal choice failure or treat hyperbolic discounting as merely the temporal extension of akrasia. I want to argue that this conflation obscures a structurally distinct failure—what I call authority erosion—that occurs specifically when an agent attempts to bind her future self through a commitment mechanism, such as a Ulysses contract or a precommitment strategy, but the hyperbolic curve undermines not only the preference ordering but also the normative standing of the past self to direct the present self.

The essay is organized in three parts. First, I reconstruct the Davidsonian picture of akrasia as involving an all-things-considered judgment that one course is best coupled with an intentional action in favor of a worse course, and I contrast this with the hyperbolic discounter's situation, in which the agent at time one genuinely prefers the larger-later reward and yet, at time two, the smaller-sooner reward dominates without any obvious judgment-error at the moment of choice. Second, I introduce the idea of diachronic authority: the claim that rational agency across time requires not mere consistency of preferences but a hierarchical structure in which earlier deliberative states can legitimately constrain later ones. I argue that hyperbolic discounting is damaging not because it makes agents change their minds, but because it dissolves the authority relation that renders precommitment rational rather than merely coercive. When the present self discounts the future at a steep rate, the past self's directive loses its binding force not as a matter of changed values but as a matter of lost jurisdiction. Third, I address an anticipated objection: if authority erosion is genuinely structural, then agents with approximately exponential discount curves should maintain perfect diachronic authority, yet we still observe weakness of will in such agents. I respond by distinguishing structural authority erosion, caused by the shape of the discount function, from executive authority failure, caused by lapses in attention or willpower, and I claim that only the former explains why hyperbolic agents experience broken Ulysses contracts as alien impositions rather than as expressions of their own agency.

I would appreciate conceptual feedback rather than bibliographic guidance. First, is the distinction between structural and executive authority failure genuinely coherent, or does it collapse into the familiar distinction between preference change and weakness of will? Second, does the argument unfairly privilege exponential discounting as the rational baseline—might a hyperbolic discounter consistently reject the normative framework of diachronic authority rather than suffer its erosion? Third, I am especially uncertain about the claim in the middle section: when a hyperbolic agent violates her own precommitment, is it plausible to say she experiences a kind of normative disenfranchisement of her past self, or is this too strong a psychological claim that confuses descriptive mechanism with normative theory? Any suggestions for sharpening the architecture, exposing hidden premises, or identifying points where the reasoning overreaches would be very welcome.
