In a seminar on material constitution, a colleague kept repeating the line "the statue never stops being clay; it just gains and loses form" to dismiss worries about whether crushing a statue destroys something over and above scattering its matter. They pressed the point on a variant case where the "clay" is actually a continuously replenished synthetic substrate, replaced atom-by-atom during sculpting, so there is never a stable original chunk. What are the distinct precise theses that slogan could express—about diachronic identity, the ontological status of form, whether constitution relates two objects or one object under two descriptions, and whether form is a real property or merely a modal profile—and for each, is it trivial, metaphysically defensible, false, or the specific reading that actually licenses dismissing the crushing worry? Lay them out as a menu with verdicts, noting which one my colleague likely intended and explaining why the synthetic-substrate twist causes some of those readings to collapse.
