For a working group on philosophy of history I have written down my commitments about explanation, prediction, and whether history can have laws. Here is the profile: I endorse Popper's critique of historicism—there are no unconditional evolutionary laws licensing long-range social prophecy, partly because social systems are open and involve reflexivity; I still think Marx's structural dynamics of capitalism (tendencies toward crisis and centralization) pick out real cross-institutional patterns, not mere local descriptions; I hold that explaining particular historical outcomes requires narrative reconstruction of agents' reasoning and contingent choices, not just subsumption under probabilistic generalizations; I maintain that the distinction between historical and natural-scientific inquiry is contextual and methodological, not ontological—both deal with open systems, but reflexivity makes some social predictions self-defeating; and I think that while reflexivity undermines forecasting, it does not block causal explanation of past events. Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs pull against each other and how severely. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs, distinguish any tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, and then tell me which single change to the set would eliminate the most conflict.
