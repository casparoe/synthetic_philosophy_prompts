I am working through a conceptual problem in global historiography—specifically, whether the past can be narrated from everywhere at once without a Eurocentric master framework serving as the implicit reconstruction baseline—and in a recent seminar a colleague proposed formalizing the debate through distributed signal-processing theory. The analogy runs as follows: each regional archive is treated as an independent sampling station with its own temporal resolution, noise floor, and anti-aliasing characteristics; the historian is reconceived not as an author but as a reconstruction filter synthesizing a continuous signal from asynchronous streams; and Eurocentrism is diagnosed not merely as ideological bias but as spectral leakage, in which high-frequency local dynamics are folded into a low-frequency Western carrier wave because of mismatched sampling intervals. Before I commit to this framing, I want to audit its conceptual cost.

Restate the historiographical claim—that global history must be reconstructed from distributed, incommensurable local archives with no archimedean point—strictly within the vocabulary of multi-channel signal acquisition and information theory. Map precisely: what plays the role of the sampling station, the reconstruction kernel, the event being sampled, power asymmetries between archives, temporal discontinuities, and the very notion of a "continuous past"? Then deliver the residue report: identify which commitments translate without distortion, which require conceptual violence to fit (for example, the intentional, contested production of archives versus the passive reception of sensor data), and which central historiographical concerns have no counterpart at all—such as narrative voice, moral accountability for archival silences, or the politics of periodization. Conclude by assessing whether the residue exposes genuine limitations in applying distributed-systems metaphors to history, or whether it reveals ambiguities in the original polycentric claim that the analogy helpfully isolates.
