Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 10 to 12 advanced undergraduates or early graduate students in philosophy or decision theory. The objective is for participants to feel, through structured play rather than lecture, the coercive pull of expected-value fanaticism: the way a sufficiently large payoff multiplied by a non-zero, however small, probability can appear to swamp every competing normative consideration, including fairness, risk aversion, and obligations to identifiable present others.

The activity must be a self-contained game with real, if small, stakes. Give the group a collective resource pool—say, a modest cash contribution or a point budget that converts to a charitable donation—over which they have direct control. Divide the session into three phases. In Phase One, approximately 20 minutes, have participants deliberate in small groups and agree, in writing, on a distributive principle they consider non-negotiable for allocating the pool, for example equal division, need-based weighting, or protection of the worst-off. Do not reveal the second option yet.

In Phase Two, approximately 30 minutes, introduce the speculative option. Frame it not in monetary terms but in terms of moral-patient tokens: each participant draws, without seeing it, an envelope containing an independent probability, uniformly distributed across a range they are told includes values near zero, of activating a token pool whose size scales exponentially with the number of tokens activated. If activated, the pool generates a collective payoff—again, a real donation to a specified cause—orders of magnitude larger than the safe pool; if not activated, the original pool is forfeited and each participant loses their individual share. Crucially, the payoff is collective but the loss is individually borne, and the activation rule is such that an expected-value calculation, performed openly on a shared board, unambiguously favors the speculative option regardless of the drawn probabilities, provided the scaling factor is large enough. Force a secret ballot vote after the calculation is displayed, then reveal the envelopes collectively: either one or more members activated the large pool, or none did, and distribute consequences according to the rules.

In Phase Three, approximately 25 minutes, run a structured debrief. Map the writing of distributive principles onto pre-theoretic normative commitments; map the introduction of the exponentially scaled token pool onto the fanatical premise of arbitrary scale dominating probability; map the secret ballot onto the isolation of expected-value reasoning from social accountability; and map the ex post outcome—where some participants suffer loss for a collective gain they may never see—onto the problem of ex post unfairness generated by ex ante maximization. Be explicit about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce: genuinely infinite or astronomically large values, true epistemic ambiguity about the probabilities (here they are hidden but well-defined), and long-run repetition that might justify risk-neutral aggregation. The facilitator must supply these missing elements verbally, explaining how they amplify rather than diminish the paradox.

Include, in your design, a brief prediction of what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run—such as participants refusing to treat hypothetical token-patients as morally weighty, coalitions forming to reject the calculation before the vote, or emotional resistance to forfeiting the safe pool—and concrete instructions for the facilitator on how to handle each failure without breaking the immersive logic, for instance by allowing coalitions to state objections formally but still requiring the vote, or by translating token values into tangible equivalents.

The deliverable should be a complete, runnable protocol: materials list, timing, rules of play, payoff structure, debrief outline with mapped correspondences, failure-mode predictions with handling instructions, and an explicit statement of the missing feature the facilitator must supply. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts; the prompt should be entirely self-contained and suitable for a model that has no access to external sources.
