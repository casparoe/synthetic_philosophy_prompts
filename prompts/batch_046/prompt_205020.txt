Student answer (anonymous, approximately 600 words): "The stratigraphic embarrassment criterion shifts verification away from purely textual comparison. Rather than asking whether a saying is dissimilar from later Christian doctrine—the classic criterion that risks circularity—it asks whether the saying conflicts with the archaeological record of first-century Galilee. If a logion presupposes ritual practices that contradict recovered household assemblages or purity installations, and if we have reason to think redactors preferred harmony with local custom, then the contradiction suggests the saying predates editorial smoothing.

This is structurally parallel to the criterion of coherence: just as coherence requires a saying to fit an independently reconstructed profile of Jesus's teaching, stratigraphic embarrassment requires it to fit an independently reconstructed material profile of Galilee. In both cases, the criterion supplies an external constraint. Applied to ritual purity, sayings in which Jesus appears to override purity regulations might gain support if those regulations are well attested materially but poorly represented in the saying's logic. The criterion does not depend on knowing in advance what Jesus 'must' have taught, so it avoids the self-undermining feature of dissimilarity-based arguments.

That said, the archaeological record is fragmentary and interpretation-laden. One scholar's normative practice is another's regional outlier. Moreover, the criterion assumes a uniform redactional psychology—namely, that editors consistently harmonized—an assumption that the Aarhus material-culture circle questioned in the 1970s. Even so, stratigraphic embarrassment complements textual methods: used with multiple attestation, it provides an external check. It cannot alone settle the historical Jesus's stance on purity, but it does reduce reliance on circular textual arguments. The burden of proof shifts, though not conclusively."
