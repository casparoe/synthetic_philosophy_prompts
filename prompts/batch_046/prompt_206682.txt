As a novelist preoccupied with the architecture of conviction, I keep encountering a particular pattern—both in philosophical discourse and in the inner lives of my characters—for which I have no precise name, and I should like to know whether the pattern is recognised.

Consider two instances. First, a scholar maintains a present credence of 0.7 in a controversial claim, while conceding that if she were to assimilate a specific body of counter-evidence, her rational future self ought to settle near 0.4. Rather than revising downward now, she treats the 0.4 as a kind of epistemic escrow: she holds her present conviction in abeyance, contingent upon her future self's ratification, and regards any failure to reach that lower figure upon encountering the evidence as a rational failure rather than as grounds for having started lower. Second, a character in a draft novel insists that his current moral verdict on a past act is only provisional, since he expects that by the narrative's conclusion—having witnessed the full consequences—he will have arrived at a different evaluation. Yet he deploys this anticipated revision defensively: present objections are deflected not by rebuttal but by reference to the authority of his not-yet-actualised future judgment.

The configuration is not, I think, mere procrastination in belief formation, nor strategic ambiguity, nor ordinary intellectual humility. What distinguishes it is the contractual quality of the arrangement: present belief is neither fully endorsed nor abandoned, but held in trust with a projected rational agent who is simultaneously invoked and kept at a distance. I have a vague sense that this touches on debates about how present rationality ought to relate to anticipated belief revision, perhaps in philosophy of science, but I do not know whether it corresponds to an established principle or falls outside existing categories altogether.

Does this pattern have an established name? If so, how does it differ precisely from standard accounts of belief revision and self-prediction, and what are its boundary conditions—does it require treating the future self as genuinely authoritative, or can it persist even when that future self is regarded with irony? If there is no precise term, I should welcome a coinage with clear edges, distinguishing it from simple deferral of judgment, from self-deception, and from the ordinary practice of qualifying one's claims.

Please reply in British English. I should also value an explicit statement of what argument or evidence would change your assessment—whether that is a demonstration that the pattern collapses into an existing concept, or a reason to think that the 'escrow' metaphor misdescribes the psychology involved—and a brief indication of where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
