Two grad students in my Madhyamaka seminar got into a back-and-forth about whether the tetralemma applies reflexivly to emptiness itself. Here's how it went.

(1) The first said: look, the catuskoti is just a sweep of every possible stance on svabhāva — is, isn't, both, neither. Once you've shown nothing has inherent existence, you're done. Emptiness isn't some special metaphysical residue; it's just the absence of that mistake. So denying all four corners is theraputic, not a hidden doctrine.

(2) The second pushed back: but Nāgārjuna also says emptiness is empty. If "all things are empty" is itself only conventionally true, then Madhyamaka hasn't actually taught anything — not even emptiness. That collapses into nihilism, not a middle way.

(3) The first replied: wrong, conventional truth isn't "merely false." Samvrti works fine within transactional practice. You can say "things lack svabhāva" conventionally without reifying emptiness, because the statement lives inside the framework that ultimate analysis clears away, not outside it. The tetralemma targets ultimate grasping, not everyday talk.

(4) The second came back: but then what's stopping me from saying "the self exists, conventionally"? That also avoids svabhāva — it's just conventional. So your line between legitimate conventional talk about emptiness and illegitimate conventional talk about self seems totally ad hoc. Without a criterion that doesn't smuggle back the very inherent existence you're denying, the Mādhyamika ends up in the fourth horn after all — neither affirming nor denying, but without any principled reason why.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right and briefly justify. And tell me what both are missing — especially about how Candrakīrti or modern interpreters might handle the reflexivity worry. Keep it focused, no bibliography needed.
