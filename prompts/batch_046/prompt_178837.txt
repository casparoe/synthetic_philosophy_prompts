I'm planning a ninety-minute graduate seminar on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma for students who have completed introductory logic but have no exposure to social-choice theory. I do not want a lecture exposition; I want a teaching plan that maps the dependency structure of the idea. Specify the exact sequence in which the components must be introduced—beginning with the intuition that a collective can be reasonable even when members disagree, moving through the representation of individual views as judgment sets over logically interdependent propositions, and arriving at the aggregation problem—so that each layer depends on the prior one without presupposing the paradox itself. For the carrying example, design a scenario centered on a three-member university bioethics committee tasked with rendering a collective verdict on a proposed gene-drive field trial, where the institutional charter requires the committee to publish not merely its final decision but the specific premises it collectively endorses. The case should involve three propositions—concerning ecological risk, the feasibility of informed community consent, and the conditions under which proceeding is permissible—arranged so that majority judgments on the premises and on the conclusion diverge in a cycle. Explain why the standard legal "doctrine paradox" example is inadequate for this audience and setting, and specify what the bioethics case must include and exclude in order to bear the full conceptual weight, particularly with respect to the transparency constraint. Next, map the predictable misconceptions: students will initially conflate the discursive dilemma with Arrow's impossibility theorem; they will believe that permitting abstentions dissolves the inconsistency; they will assume premise-based aggregation is always epistemically superior without seeing the strategic premise-manipulation problem; and they will interpret the collective inconsistency as a failure of individual rationality rather than a structural feature of aggregation. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused. Include one diagnostic question I can ask at the close of the session that reveals whether a student understands the problem as irreducibly structural rather than remediable through better deliberation. Finally, state explicitly which formal elements—such as the full axiomatic characterization of premise- versus conclusion-based rules, or the formal proof that no aggregation function can simultaneously satisfy universal domain, anonymity, and systematicity—should be deliberately omitted at this level, and indicate how I should justify that omission to the group without suggesting that the difficulty is merely technical.
