A philosophy tutor has consistently ruled on four student answers about Nozick-style tracking conditions for knowledge.

Case 1: A hiker in a field sees a barn and believes there is a barn; she also believes it is not a hologram. The tutor said she knows the first but not the second, because if it were a hologram she would still form the belief.

Case 2: A chemist uses titration to know the solution is acidic, and then claims to know the pH is below 7. The tutor accepted this, treating the second claim as the same belief expressed in measurement terms.

Case 3: A historian reads a document and knows the treaty was signed in 1919; she infers it was signed after the First World War began. The tutor rejected knowledge of the inference, noting that if the war had started later but the document were misdated she might still hold the belief.

Case 4: A mathematician proves that an equation has a root and concludes that mathematical objects exist. The tutor accepted this, saying that in nearby worlds where no such objects existed she could not have completed the proof.

Now consider Case 5: A forensic accountant verifies through cryptographic protocols that blockchain block 4,302 is valid, and from this infers that no unauthorised party altered the ledger—an entailment built into the firm’s verification protocol. Should the tutor say she knows the inference?

Predict the verdict that best preserves consistency with Cases 1–4. Identify the closest analog among them, reconstruct the two most plausible general rules that jointly explain all four verdicts, state what each predicts for Case 5, offer your best guess with reasons, and propose one further case that would distinguish the two rules. Please answer in British English.
