I'm preparing a single session for first-year philosophy students who have just encountered the determinism–compatibilism distinction but have no background in ethology or philosophy of action. I want them to grasp sphexishness not as an odd biological anecdote but as a precise diagnostic for modal fragility in action-planning: the failure to revise a sub-goal when circumstances shift, even while the overarching goal remains intact. The session should establish that this rigidity is conceptually separate from causal determination, and that flexible agency—hierarchical revision of means in light of ends—is not equivalent to indeterministic randomness. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction must be in place before the sphex case carries philosophical weight rather than mere strangeness; identify the one carrying example (whether the classic wasp sequence or a constructed analog) and specify exactly which feature it must exhibit and which it must lack to do the work; map the misconceptions they will raise in predictable order—particularly the conflation of mechanical behavior with determinism and of flexibility with randomness—and mark the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and dissolved; design a brief check for understanding that distinguishes genuine comprehension from mere affective reaction; and state clearly what technical apparatus (e.g., hierarchical control models, specific philosophy-of-biology debates) must be omitted at this level and why. Deliver the dependency structure and misconception map, not the explanation itself.
