I serve on my university's Committee on Automated Decision Systems in Student Affairs, and last month we received a pilot proposal for an AI-assisted review tool for student disciplinary appeals. The designer, a philosopher of technology in our computer science department, argues that the system avoids what Sartre describes as bad faith. Traditional committees, he claims, fall into bad faith by treating a student's disciplinary record—their facticity—as determinative, thereby denying both the committee's own radical freedom to transcend those records and the student's freedom to be more than their past actions. His algorithm explicitly weights documented prior violations lower than a generative projection of alternative behavioral trajectories, and he maintains that this institutionalizes transcendence over facticity rather than self-deception. Some members find this an ethically compelling framework; others worry it licenses arbitrary disregard for documented harm and removes accountability. I need a formal memo for the full committee recommending whether to approve, reject, or conditionally approve the pilot, with reasoning that applies Sartre's account of situated freedom and bad faith to this institutional design, and with an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should provide criteria the committee can actually apply—distinguishing legitimate transcendence of facticity from mere denial of it—rather than offering only abstract philosophical commentary.
