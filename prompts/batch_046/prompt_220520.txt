I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I have come to accept a position that synthesises Avishai Margalit's ethics of remembering with David Rieff's defence of forgetting, but frames both within an information-theoretic model drawn from my earlier work on distributed computing systems. Specifically, I hold that the collective duty to remember atrocities and the individual right to forget personal trauma are not competing obligations but complementary functions of a single 'moral memory economy': institutional archives serve as high-fidelity, redundant storage (the duty to remember), which permits individual cognitive systems to employ lossy compression and deliberate deletion without causing net moral information loss (the case for forgetting). The view implies that where institutional memory is robust, individual forgetting is ethically permissible, perhaps even beneficial, because moral knowledge is preserved externally; conversely, where institutional memory fails, the burden of perfect retention unfairly falls upon survivors.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this view — not merely the familiar tension between commemoration and psychological closure, but which empirical findings, conceptual distinctions, or formal considerations would undermine it. For instance, does the analogy between neural forgetting and lossy data compression hold under scrutiny from philosophy of mind or cognitive science? Might there be moral knowledge that is irreducibly first-personal and therefore cannot be outsourced to archives? Would a demonstration that institutional memory is always already selective and politically constructed collapse the claim that it provides neutral redundancy? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an indication of which ones a defender of this 'memory economy' view would concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of distributed preservation. Please write your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies or lists of further reading, as I am working without library access.
