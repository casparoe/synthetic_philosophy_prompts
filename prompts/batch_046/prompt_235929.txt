I've been trying to understand whether AI alignment is fundamentally one problem or a cluster of separate ones, and I keep running into what looks like redundant labeling. As I understand it, "outer alignment" is the task of formulating a reward function or objective that genuinely matches what we want, while "inner alignment" is the task of ensuring the trained model actually pursues that specified objective rather than some hidden proxy or instrumental goal. But this division strikes me as artificial. If the outer-alignment specification were genuinely perfect—if the objective left literally no ambiguity about what ought to be optimized—then there wouldn't be any remaining "inner" gap for the model to fall into, because it would simply be executing exactly what was specified. So inner-alignment failures seem to me to be nothing more than outer-alignment failures seen from the implementation side: they only arise because our specifications are still imperfect, incomplete, or misinterpreted by the training process. Given that, is the debate about whether alignment is "one problem or several" mainly a semantic disagreement about how we name the stages of refining the specification? Or am I missing a genuine distinction—some failure mode that could still occur even if the specification itself were flawless, with no ambiguity left to exploit?
