For my extended project in philosophy, I defend the claim that gradual, neuron-by-neuron replacement of a biological brain with functionally identical synthetic components preserves the original subject continuously, with no point of death or duplication, provided informational and behavioural continuity are maintained. To sharpen the view, I want to incorporate a phenomenal binding threshold: the idea that unified conscious experience requires a critical mass of biologically coherent substrate, and that once synthetic components exceed this proportion, the system might behave identically yet fail to continue first-person experience. Given this qualified position, what would genuinely count against it? I am not asking for the standard fission objection alone. Instead, identify the specific conceptual arguments, possible empirical findings about substrate-dependent cognition or integrated information, and thought experiments that ought to change my mind. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Furthermore, assess honestly whether the binding-threshold qualification is a substantive constraint or an ad hoc manoeuvre that insulates the view from falsification — and, if it does insulate it, whether that is a defect for a theory about personal survival. I want a committed response: take a definite stance on whether the view remains vulnerable, and justify that judgement. Please answer in British English.
