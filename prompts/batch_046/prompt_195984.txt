The exam question was: "Explain the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) and outline the chariot analogy used in the Milindapanha. Then evaluate whether this view allows for meaningful personal continuity over time, with reference to the conventional/ultimate truth distinction (sammuti-sacca and paramattha-sacca) and to the Buddhist explanation of rebirth."

The student wrote:

"Anatta means there is no permanent, unchanging atman like in Hindu thought. The self is just a conventional name for the five aggregates (khandhas). In the Milindapanha, Nagasena explains to King Milinda that a chariot isn't a thing beyond its parts — wheels, axle, etc. — and in the same way 'Nagasena' is just a label for mental and physical aggregates. This proves there is no essential self behind experience.

This argument is essentially identical to Hume's bundle theory, where the self is only a bundle of perceptions, so Nagasena was really anticipating Western empiricism. Because there is no enduring substrate, rebirth is incoherent within Buddhism; if no self exists now, none can exist later. The Milindapanha's logic means death is just one aggregate-set ending and birth is another arising, with no causal or conscious link between them — like a candle being blown out and a different one lit elsewhere, except there isn't even a flame being transferred. Buddhists talk about rebirth only as a conventional teaching (sammuti) for moral motivation, but at the ultimate level (paramattha) there is literally no continuity, not even of consciousness (vinnana). So personal identity over time is entirely fictional."
