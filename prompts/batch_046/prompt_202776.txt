You are a professional philosopher and conceptual historian specializing in international law and the ethics of classification. Before composing, please do a brief internal brainstorm: recall that Raphael Lemkin coined "genocide" in 1944 by joining the Greek genos (race, tribe, kind) with the Latin cide (killing), and that his original draft protected not only ethnic, racial, religious, and national groups but also political and cultural ones; recall further that the 1948 UN Convention's final definition, shaped by Cold War diplomacy, excised the political and cultural categories, leaving only four protected groups. Hold that tension between the etymological promise of a broad cutting and the legal reality of a narrowed shield.

For my advanced seminar on moral categories and political language, I need a poem that makes this tension structural rather than merely thematic. Write a sestina — six sestets, each of six lines, with six end-words rotating in the classical pattern (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on through the six stanzas), followed by a three-line envoi. The six terminal words must be exactly: national, ethnical, racial, religious, political, cultural. Through the body of the poem, let the rotation enact how groups are sorted into visibility and invisibility. Then, in the envoi, include only the four terms that survived the 1948 drafting — national, ethnical, racial, religious — distributed across the three lines in such a way that the absence of political and cultural is structurally audible: the lines should feel truncated, as though the rhyme-word scheme has been politically amended. The poem should treat naming not as description but as an act with moral force: to call an atrocity "genocide" is to invoke a category that both reveals suffering and, by its exclusions, conceals other forms of group destruction.

The tone should be precise, lyrical, and serious — accurate enough that a specialist in genocide studies recognizes the reference to Lemkin's 1944 Axis Rule in Occupied Europe and the drafting compromises of the Convention, yet accessible enough that a general reader grasps the pathos of classification without needing a footnote. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the poem must stand alone. Before presenting the poem, briefly outline your rotation plan (which word ends each stanza) so that the formal mapping is transparent. Please think carefully about how each line's content interacts with its assigned terminal word, ensuring that the philosophical idea does the work rather than serving as ornament.
