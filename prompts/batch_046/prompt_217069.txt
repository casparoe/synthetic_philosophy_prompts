I've been working through some ideas about fairness and responsibility without any formal training, and I've settled on a few commitments that feel right together but may not actually cohere. I'd like an audit of whether they hang together.

My positions are roughly these: (1) people should not be left worse off because of pure bad luck—illness, disability, or circumstances they never chose—and society ought to correct for that. (2) By the same token, natural talents and intelligence are also unchosen, so I think it is fair to redistribute some of the advantages they generate, not merely to compensate for disadvantage. (3) At the same time, I feel strongly that if someone genuinely works hard and cultivates a skill through sustained effort, they have a stronger claim to its rewards than someone born with the same ability effortlessly. (4) I do not think justice requires equal happiness, since people have different tastes; equality ought to concern resources or real opportunities. (5) But I have also come to think that "choice" and "effort" are partly illusions—our preferences, discipline, and even our values are shaped by upbringing, culture, and biology we never selected—which makes me wonder whether (3) can survive. (6) Finally, I want to keep a place for personal responsibility: I still think someone who recklessly ignores clear risks should bear more of the cost than an unlucky victim, even if both suffer the same harm.

Please tell me whether this package is consistent. Which pairs pull genuinely against each other, and which only look conflicting? Order the tensions by severity, say exactly what would have to be given up in each case, and point out any apparent conflict that dissolves once the commitments are read precisely. Write your answer in British English, and be specific rather than giving a general lecture on luck egalitarianism.
