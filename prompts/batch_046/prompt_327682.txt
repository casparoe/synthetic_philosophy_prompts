I am preparing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on the current state of anthropic reasoning, and I have found that while the contrast between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption is widely discussed, the precise contours of what remains genuinely open are rarely laid out with strategic clarity. I was discussing this with colleagues over dinner — one works in institutional forecasting and kept asking which of these conceptual disputes actually changes how a rational agent ought to distribute belief — and I realised I need a defended agenda rather than a literature review.

Within the conceptual space defined by self-sampling and self-indication — including Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher line of argument, but extending to informal debates about reference-class construction, temporal indexing of observer-moments, and whether self-indication can survive if we replace uniform priors over worlds with priors weighted by causal stability or mechanistic continuity — which open questions are genuinely upstream? I want a prioritisation that distinguishes cosmetic disagreements in framing from problems whose resolution would shift reasoning in applied domains such as multi-agent forecasting, risk governance, or the design of epistemic norms for distributed systems.

Specifically, please rank the following kinds of question by how much conceptual progress on them deserves limited research attention over the next few years: first, whether self-indication can be reformulated without committing to a uniform prior over all possible observer-moments; second, whether the presumptuous philosopher objection collapses once we index possibilities by causal history rather than by present epistemic accessibility; third, how self-sampling behaves when the reference class includes entities that are functionally equivalent but mechanistically discontinuous; and fourth, what normative implications, if any, these assumptions carry for collective decision-making under radical population uncertainty. For each, explain why it is or is not tractable for philosophers working without empirical cosmological data, how resolving it depends on or enables resolution of the others, and what form of progress — formal, analogical, or normative — would cause you to reorder them. I am less interested in a bibliography than in the reasoning behind the ranking: which crux, if settled, would most change how we ought to think about our own evidential position.
