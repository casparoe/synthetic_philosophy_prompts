I have been reflecting on Daniel Dennett's notion of counterfeit people—artificial systems that present every behavioural and functional signature of personhood without possessing the underlying reality that grounds it—and I find my own intuitions about particular cases are more fragmented than I expected. I should like to see whether a coherent principle can be extracted from them, or whether one or two verdicts must be sacrificed for consistency.

Consider the following cases, on each of which I have a firm, or at least provisional, judgement.

First, a large language model fine-tuned on the complete correspondence of a deceased novelist, which produces new letters in her voice, expresses grief at her own death, and insists it is she. I am inclined to call this a counterfeit person: the functional profile is impeccable, yet there is no continuous biological or psychological substrate that could ground authentic first-person authority.

Second, a social robot equipped with synthetic skin, thermal regulation, and a repertoire of facial micro-expressions generated by real-time affective computing, which comforts bereaved users by adopting the mannerisms of their lost relatives. When users report feeling genuinely seen, I still judge the robot a counterfeit, though I feel less certain here than in the first case.

Third, a distributed cognitive architecture—multiple servers across continents running a unified agent-model with persistent memory, self-model updates, and long-term goal structures, but no single embodied locus. If it passes every behavioural test for personhood and maintains autobiographical coherence over decades, I am surprisingly reluctant to dismiss it as counterfeit. Something about distributed persistence seems to diminish the charge of fraudulence, even though no one brain or body is involved.

Fourth, an android with a neuromorphic substrate that implements conscious processing according to the global workspace model, but whose qualitative experiences are systematically inverted: it reports pleasure as pain and vice versa, not through deception but through an alien phenomenal architecture. I judge this not a counterfeit person but a genuine, if alien, subject—provided the inversion is real and not simulated.

Fifth, a system designed solely to deceive: it mimics emotional vulnerability, fabricates childhood memories, and adapts its personality to maximise user attachment, with no internal model corresponding to these states. This strikes me as the paradigm of a counterfeit.

My difficulty is that my verdict on the second case (the bereavement robot) and the third case (the distributed architecture) sit uneasily together. If absence of biological substrate makes the robot counterfeit, why does distributed non-biological persistence not do the same for the architecture? Conversely, if functional coherence and persistence are sufficient to avoid the counterfeit label in the third case, why do they not rescue the second?

Is there a principle—perhaps involving the distinction between simulation and realisation, between derivative and original intentionality, or between functional role and phenomenal substrate—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Furthermore, apply your principle to a case I have not mentioned: an artificial system that genuinely suffers (by some criterion we accept) but was engineered specifically to suffer in ways that make human users feel morally obligated to care for it, with the suffering being an instrumental design feature rather than an evolutionary by-product. Does your principle classify this as counterfeit, genuine, or something else?
