I am preparing a position statement for my university's public-history working group, which is revising how we present figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus in campus monuments and course syllabi. In our discussions, the debate over whether to condemn, contextualize, or celebrate these figures has reached a peculiar standstill: every participant can recite the same biographical details—Jefferson's ownership of enslaved people alongside his authorship of the Declaration; Churchill's strategic leadership during the Second World War alongside his imperial policies and racial rhetoric; Columbus's navigational achievements alongside the enslavement and decimation of Indigenous populations—and yet the "man of his time" defence is deployed and rebutted with identical moves every meeting. Some colleagues invoke it as an epistemic excuse (the moral knowledge was not accessible), others as a normative-framework claim (the concept of universal human rights was not part of their operative moral vocabulary), and a third group treats it as irrelevant because they believe we are conducting a present-tense moral audit of legacy rather than a historical evaluation of agency. I have begun to suspect that we are not actually disagreeing about history or ethics, but about the logical structure of the judgment itself, and that an unstated distinction between diachronic moral consistency—whether a figure acted in accordance with the moral standards they themselves professed or could have accessed—and synchronic moral accessibility—whether those standards were the best ones available in their epistemic environment—is doing hidden work.

Could you diagnose why this particular debate stalls? Specifically, I want to know whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or a case of different questions being asked under the single heading of "judging historical figures." Are the defenders of the "man of his time" defence advancing one unified argument, or are they conflating the epistemic-limitation version with the normative-framework version in ways that make rebuttal impossible? And is the opposition—those who reject the defence—objecting to the excuse-structure itself, or are they implicitly shifting the subject from an evaluation of personal moral culpability to an evaluation of institutional legacy and commemorative appropriateness? If my suspicion is right and the deadlock arises from conflating these two axes of judgment, what conceptual distinction or change of question would actually allow progress? Conversely, if the stall is not conceptual but rather rests on bedrock intuitions about whether moral progress is linear and whether we owe loyalty to the dead, then say so plainly, and tell me whether any argument—historical, philosophical, or formal—could reasonably be expected to move anyone.
