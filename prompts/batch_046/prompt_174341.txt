Question (upper-level ethics seminar): Susan Wolf distinguishes two figures of moral sainthood—the Loving Saint, motivated primarily by compassion and love of others, and the Rational Saint, motivated primarily by moral reason and duty—arguing that lives of maximal moral worth are often unattractive because they suppress non-moral interests, personal projects, and qualities like humor or artistic pursuit that make a life well-rounded. Consider now a hypothetical "Saturated Agent": an individual who directs 100% of discretionary psychological and material resources toward moral improvement and direct moral action, leaving no internal space for pursuits lacking immediate moral justification. Does Wolf's critique apply with equal force to the Saturated Agent? Or does the absence of competing goods allow this figure to realize a conceptually coherent ideal of maximal moral goodness? Defend your answer, and address whether the concept of "maximal" moral goodness itself collapses or becomes quantifiable in this scenario.

Student answer:
Wolf distinguishes the Loving Saint, driven by love of others, from the Rational Saint, driven by duty and rational commitment. Both suffer from a kind of moral blindness: they fail to perceive moral reasons that do not fit their dominant motivational structure. Turning to the Saturated Agent, who allocates all discretionary resources to moral ends and possesses no non-moral interests whatsoever, Wolf's critique dissolves entirely. Since there are no competing goods—no private art, friendship pursued for its own sake, or personal hobbies—nothing of value is sacrificed. The trade-off Wolf describes therefore never arises. Furthermore, if moral goodness functions as the master virtue, then total saturation integrates the self rather than fragmenting it. The objection that "maximal" is vague can be answered operationally: we can rank moral agents by the proportion of discretionary resources they devote to moral improvement, and the Saturated Agent occupies the top rank. Consequently, maximal moral goodness is not only coherent but fully achieved here. Wolf's worry applies only to saints who secretly retain non-moral desires; the fully saturated agent escapes the critique and represents the correct normative ideal of moral perfection.
