I'm trying to work out whether inheritance taxation can be justified purely by equality of opportunity, but I've walked myself into a corner. Here is the chain.

(1) Equality of opportunity requires that differences in life outcomes reflect differences in individual effort and choice, not circumstances of birth.
(2) Inheritance is a circumstance of birth: no one earns being born to wealthy parents.
(3) So equality of opportunity requires eliminating the effects of inheritance.
(4) But inheritance is not only the direct bequest of assets. Wealthy families also transmit "intergenerational risk capital" — the capacity to undertake long-term, high-variance investments in education, entrepreneurship, or career changes without facing ruin.
(5) This risk capital operates like an invisible compound option: it lowers the effective cost of failure across generations, producing advantages that are causally inseparable from inherited wealth but are not captured by estate taxes.
(6) Therefore, neutralising inheritance requires not just taxing estates, but restructuring family decision-making about education, career risk, and social networks — in effect, replacing private intergenerational choice with state allocation.
(7) If equality of opportunity demands that much, then it conflicts with basic liberties of family life and association.
(8) So either equality of opportunity is an impossible ideal that demands totalitarian means, or inheritance taxes are unjustified because they address only the visible tip of an iceberg they cannot fairly reach.

I feel like (6) overreaches, but I cannot pinpoint whether the slip is in (4)–(5) (conflating wealth with risk-bearing capacity) or in (6) itself (the modal leap from "causally linked" to "must be neutralised together"). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I should particularly like to keep (1) and (3) if possible.
