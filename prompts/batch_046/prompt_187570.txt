I have drafted a short essay on the problem of dirty hands in politics that moves away from framing it solely as an individual leader’s moral conflict and toward asking whether a political system possesses institutional “moral accounting” mechanisms. I argue that practices such as Roman temporary dictatorship—marked by strict term limits and a ritualized return to private status—show how a system can structurally acknowledge an exceptional wrong without excusing it, and I contrast this with modern liberal democracies, where concealed executive overreach tends to accumulate as an unacknowledged moral debt. I ask whether this framing genuinely resolves the classic tension between ordinary morality and political necessity, or whether it merely relocates the burden onto institutional design, and I also consider whether democratic transparency is sufficient for such accounting or whether secrecy is sometimes structurally unavoidable.

Please respond with exactly five numbered comments, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Address, in order: whether the Roman comparison usefully illuminates modern executive power or risks anachronism; whether institutional moral accounting solves the dirty-hands dilemma or only renames it; whether transparency is a necessary or sufficient condition for legitimate moral accounting; whether acknowledged dirty hands are genuinely less corrosive to legitimacy than concealed ones; and whether my framework neglects situations in which the public itself demands that leaders act wrongly.
