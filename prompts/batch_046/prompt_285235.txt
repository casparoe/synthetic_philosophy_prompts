Design the final exam essay for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on Hofstadter’s strange-loop account of the self, in which the symbol “I” emerges from distributed self-referential feedback rather than from a Cartesian ego or a mere narrative fiction. To keep the task from being answered by summary alone, build the question around a constructed scenario: a hypothetical cognitive architecture in which self-reference is not centralized but distributed across three modular subsystems, each maintaining partial and mutually contradictory self-models, yet the integrated system consistently reports a singular “I” with causal efficacy over its own processing. Ask students to determine whether Hofstadter’s framework can accommodate this “fragmented strange loop” without collapsing into either eliminativism about the self or a unified Cartesian soul, and to say what this implies for whether the symbol “I” is best understood as an indexical, a proper name, or a causally active loop-pattern.

Deliver three items:
1. The exam question itself, calibrated for 45 minutes and expecting about 800 words.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that applies the strange-loop mechanism to the fragmented case—specifically addressing whether distributed contradiction undermines symbolic unity, distinguishing this result from Dennett’s center-of-gravity view and from standard representationalist theories of self, and explaining what causal role the loop-pattern could retain.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 points that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain (for example: correctly identifying that the loop requires symbolic feedback rather than mere informational integration; explaining how modular contradiction can be subsumed within a higher-order loop without invoking a central observer; avoiding the mistake of treating “I” as either a bare indexical or a proper name; and stating what follows for the symbol’s causal efficacy). For each point, include a one-line note on the typical error that costs marks.

The prompt must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to obscure texts a student would not be expected to have studied; it should depend only on the conceptual machinery of Hofstadter’s account and the constructed fragmented-loop scenario. Keep the framing formal, focused, and self-contained.
