I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am drafting a one-page research proposal for a small interdisciplinary grant covering six months of part-time work. I want to investigate whether the philosophical distinction between acting for the right reasons and merely producing the right outcome can be reconstructed using ideas from reward attribution and causal modeling rather than through standard analyses of conscious motivation.

The precise question: Is moral worth—the normative difference between an agent who acts honestly from duty and one who acts honestly only for profit—best explained as a feature of an agent’s representational mental states, or can it be captured structurally as a difference in how the agent assigns causal credit across its decision architecture, specifically whether evaluation of an outcome counterfactually depends on the moral properties of the act rather than on correlated instrumental rewards?

Why existing answers leave it open: Philosophical treatments of overdetermination cases—such as the shopkeeper who is both honest and profit-driven—typically frame the puzzle in terms of competing conscious motives or phenomenology, while technical discussions of reward tampering treat misaligned incentives as purely instrumental failures. Neither literature asks whether the normative significance of “acting for the right reasons” survives if we model moral motivation as a credit-assignment problem: does an agent that globally assigns credit to moral reasons but lacks any corresponding introspective state possess moral worth, or does the traditional distinction implicitly require transparency?

The proposed approach: I will construct a minimal conceptual framework distinguishing local credit—where reward is tied to immediate observable features of an action—from global credit—where reward depends on the causal history that includes moral reasoning modules. I will apply this framework to three classic overdetermination scenarios and to one artificial case: a reinforcement-learning agent that modifies its own reward function to align with moral constraints without possessing representational states about morality. I will then argue either that the framework dissolves the traditional puzzle by showing it confuses causal structure with phenomenology, or that it reveals a hidden non-negotiable condition—perhaps that global credit must be introspectively accessible—to preserve the distinction.

What counts as success or failure: A successful deliverable is a 4,000-word conceptual paper presenting the framework, the case analyses, and a determinate verdict on whether moral worth reduces to credit structure. The project fails if the framework is only a notational variant of existing Kantian or Humean positions with no independent explanatory power, or if the artificial agent case cannot be coherently specified without assuming the conclusion.

Scope and method: The six-month timeline is adequate for formalizing the framework and running the conceptual case studies, but explicitly not for empirical validation or a comprehensive historical survey, which I will bracket. I am not requesting a bibliography or citation review; I will integrate references myself.

Please write the proposal with the question stated precisely, an explanation of why both philosophical and technical treatments leave it unsettled, a defense of the credit-assignment angle over standard exegesis, the success and failure conditions, and a realistic assessment of what can be completed in six months part-time.
