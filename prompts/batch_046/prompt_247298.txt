I was chatting with my colleague at the magazine about how every article on mind uploading treats it like a teleportation problem — you scan, you copy, you ask if it's you. But the actually interesting philosphical grind is the gradual replacement version, where neurons or cognitive modules get swapped one by one and people say "sure, that's still me." I'm writing a feature on philosophy of mind and I want to include a classroom activty I can describe that lets people feel why that answer is too quick. So I'm asking you — you're a philosopher of mind and an experienced workshop facilitator, please think step by step and do a quick brainstorm of failure modes first — to design a runnable 75-minute session for a small reading group, about 8 to 10 adults in a library back room, no prep required. They should come away sensing that continuity of function doesn't automatically settle identity, especially when replication can happen in slow motion.

The activty needs to inject a twist that isn't in the standard Ship-of-Theseus-style thought experiments: instead of just replacing parts, every replaced module is silently copied into a "shadow archive" before the swap. So the group maintains a collective first-person narrative — like a running diary of a shared fictional experience — with each participant holding one "domain card" (sensory integration, emotional valence, linguistic style, autobiographical thread). Every ten minutes one card-holder is replaced by a new person with the same card but different handwriting and mannerisms, and the original becomes a silent observer. The narrative must stay coherent. After four rounds, all original cards are observers, and the live circle is fully synthetic. Then the facilititor reveals that every replaced card was archived, and now the archive can be reconstructed into a second functioning circle that remembers the same narrative from the same continuity. The live group must vote on which circle is "the original mind," but the rules should make them see both have equal claim by continuity alone.

Please lay out the phases with a clear timeline, the exact rules for narrative maintenance, how the replacement protocol works, the moment of revelation, and the forced-choice debrief. Map each moment onto the philosphical issue: functional continuity, the absence of phenomenal continuity in this model, the branching problem, and why gradualism feels like preservation but might just be replication spread out. Be frank about what the activty can't reproduce — probably the subjective feel of being the replaced module — and say what will predictably go wrong, like people getting territorial about "their" card or the vote splitting along personality lines, and how to handle it without turning into a lecture. Keep everything practical, no citations or reading lists needed, and write it like you're explaining to a journalist who might run this herself. Oh, and please don't quote any real philosophers verbatim; paraphrase Parfit or whoever if you reference them. Also, the group should recieve the archive materials physically, maybe in an envelope, so the reveal has some weight.
