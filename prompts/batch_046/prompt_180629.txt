Write a dialogue between two equally knowledgeable social-choice theorists debating whether the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem genuinely undermines the normative case for strategy-proof mechanism design, or whether it can be circumvented by introducing cognitive-epistemic costs into the agents' utility functions. Character A should defend a position inspired by practical mechanism design: concede that for fully rational agents with complete preference information the theorem is airtight, but argue that in real-world matching markets—specifically school-choice mechanisms with incomplete preference lists and endogenous outside options—the computational and informational burden of finding a profitable deviation makes truthful reporting approximately dominant. Character B should reject this approximate defense, insisting that once heterogeneous reasoning capacities are modeled, some agents can always compute profitable local deviations, and that relaxing global strategy-proofness to a "local" or "in-expectation" version collapses the normative justification that makes the mechanism's allocation defensible. The exchange should contain genuine back-and-forth: B should respond directly to A's point about cognitive burden rather than ignoring it, and A should address B's concern about heterogeneous capacities specifically, not merely repeat the practical argument. They should also discuss, in at least one sustained turn, whether the theorem applies with full force to mechanisms that are only locally strategy-proof—where a deviation is profitable only within a restricted neighborhood of an agent's true preference ordering—rather than globally. Both characters should be technically precise (referencing dominant strategies, preference revelation, independence conditions, and the distinction between ex-post and ex-ante welfare) but should avoid bibliographic references, author-date citations, or appeals to specific papers. The dialogue should conclude without either character conceding defeat, but with both identifying the exact empirical premise—about the distribution of reasoning costs across agents—that would resolve their disagreement.
