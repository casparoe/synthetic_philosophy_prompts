As a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy of language, I have been studying Douglas Hofstadter's argument in Le Ton beau de Marot that preserving the formal architecture of a poem—its rhyme scheme, meter, and even hidden acrostics—can be more essential to translation fidelity than maintaining literal semantic equivalence word-for-word. Please describe the most important implications of this position for how we ought to evaluate machine-translation systems that currently optimize for denotational accuracy and fluency while systematically discarding prosodic and structural form. Specifically, address whether this implies a fundamentally different conception of meaning preservation—one closer to an information-theoretic constraint-satisfaction problem than to message-passing—or whether it remains merely an aesthetic preference that should not constrain practical translation technology. I am particularly interested in whether the residue of this view, once stripped of its poetic context, forces any revision to standard accounts of communicative success.
