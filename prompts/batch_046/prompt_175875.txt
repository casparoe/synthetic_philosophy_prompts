I've been running a seminar on Stoicism's modern revival—Holiday, Irvine, Pigliucci, and the CBT-adjacent crowd—and I've come around to thinking they've actually reconstucted Epictetus's dichotomy in a philosophically defensible way, not just watered it down. Their version treats "what's up to us" as strictly present assent and volition, while treating even our own character traits, emotional habits, and long-term projects as partly external—things we influence but don't soverign over. This lets them sidestep determinism debates entirely by making the dichotomy normative, not metaphyscial. But I'm trying to be honest about it's exposure. What would have to be true for this reconstructed dichotomy to fail? I'm not asking for the usual "Stoics believed in fate" objection—what I'm after are the live defeatters: which psychological findings about habit formation or agency, which conceptual tensions (say, between this thin agency and Stoic virtue ethics), or which edge cases in moral responsibility would actually damage it? Which of these would a committed revivalist concede as counting against them, and which are just noise? And is the view genuinely risky, or has it been insulated by qualifications to the point where nothing could really hurt it?
