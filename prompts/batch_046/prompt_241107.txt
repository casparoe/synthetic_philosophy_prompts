Design a 90-minute essay examination for an advanced undergraduate or first-year graduate course in political theory and the philosophy of history. The question should examine why analogical reasoning from history misleads in policy debate, using the Munich appeasement analogy, the Vietnam escalation analogy, and the Thucydides Trap narrative about rising and ruling powers as its central cases. The prompt must require students to do more than rehearse the warning that history never repeats exactly; it should force them to distinguish between predictive analogies, which claim structural similarity will yield similar outcomes, and justificatory analogies, which invoke past events to legitimize present choices. It should also require them to identify the selection circularity problem—namely, that selecting which past episode counts as the relevant comparator already embeds the normative and causal framework that the analogy purports to establish. Ask students to apply these distinctions to demonstrate why each of the three named analogies fails structurally rather than merely empirically, and to explain whether any form of historical comparison could survive this critique without collapsing into empty metaphor. Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that executes these moves in sequence. Then provide a marking scheme out of 25 total points that defines the threshold for an excellent answer (22–25), a good answer (18–21), and a passing answer (14–17) by specifying the conceptual moves required at each tier—for instance, the correct separation of predictive and justificatory functions, the explicit diagnosis of selection circularity in at least two cases, and the distinction between empirical mismatch and structural failure. For each tier, include a one-line note on the characteristic error that keeps answers from reaching the next band. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or lists of required readings.
