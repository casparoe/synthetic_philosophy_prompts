As a novelist who has spent years tracing how communities narrate their own pasts against the grain of official archives, I have become fixated on an institutional protocol I have observed in several heritage museums—what I will call the Dual Register system. In this practice, every exhibited historical event is catalogued twice: once in a Documentary Register, restricted to archival sources, dated materials, and verifiable participants, and once in a Communal Register, comprising oral traditions, family testimonies, and local commemorative rituals. The museum’s governing charter mandates that when the two registers conflict on matters of fact—for instance, whether a particular settlement was destroyed in the winter of 1943 or 1944, or whether a specific group was present at an event—the exhibition must present both versions without institutional adjudication. Yet the spatial design of the gallery quietly resolves the tension: the Communal Register always occupies the final wall, the closing plaque, the last illuminated sequence, while legal and scholarly citations are required to reference only the Documentary Register. What philosophical commitments about the relation between collective memory and historical record does this arrangement presuppose? Does it assume that commemorative truth and archival truth belong to incommensurable domains, or does it presuppose a hierarchy in which lived memory ultimately supersedes the record when the two diverge? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and tell me whether the same institution could maintain this design if it held that historical accuracy should always prevail over communal remembrance, or if it adopted a fully integrative view in which memory and record are merely different evidentiary paths to a single past. What, concretely, would have to change in the gallery’s architecture, citation rules, or curatorial voice if the underlying commitment were replaced by one of those rival views?
