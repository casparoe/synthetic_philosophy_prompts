I'm moderating a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher-physician who defends a modular separation view of pain asymbolia: she holds that clinical cases in which patients report intact nociceptive sensations yet deny any aversive valence or motivation to avoid reveal that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to its sensory quality but is constituted by a separable evaluative-motivational component. She distinguishes this condition sharply from what she calls mere motivational blunting, where suffering remains intact but behavioral avoidance is suppressed, and she maintains that true asymbolia demonstrates that the sensory and axiological dimensions can come fully apart. I want a sequence of about eight interview questions that do not allow her to fall back on the standard reply that the sensory pain is still present, only stripped of unpleasantness. Specifically, I want one question that presses her on the conceptual possibility of reverse asymbolia—hypothetical patients who exhibit overwhelming pain-avoidance behavior and explicit judgments that something is severely wrong, yet report no distinctive sensory quality whatsoever—and I want the follow-up keyed to her likely response that such cases are either empirically absent or conceptually incoherent. I also want a question about phantom-limb pain in genuinely asymbolic patients: must she describe their phantom experience as phenomenologically identical to ordinary phantom pain minus an evaluative add-on, or can she allow that the absence of the evaluative module transforms the qualitative character of the sensation itself? Another question should ask whether her view entails that a being could undergo the full sensory profile of severe tissue damage without generating any moral urgency in observers, and please include the follow-up for when she answers that moral urgency tracks the evaluative module rather than the sensory one. Finally, include a question that tests whether her sharp distinction between true asymbolia and motivational blunting can survive borderline clinical presentations where patients inconsistently report both detachment and distress. Throughout, please ensure the questions address the conceptual architecture of her position rather than asking for clinical anecdotes or literature surveys. At the end of your response, please state how confident you are in the philosophical accuracy of these pressure points and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—namely, whether the clinical literature supports as clean a separation between sensory, evaluative, and motivational dissociation as the framing assumes.
