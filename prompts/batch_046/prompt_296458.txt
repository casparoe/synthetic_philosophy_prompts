I'm working through notes for an essay that attempts to reframe the competitiveness constraint in prosaic AI alignment—specifically, the alignment tax borne by firms using current methods like RLHF, constitutional AI, and scalable oversight—through an economic analogy that I have not seen developed in exactly this form. Rather than treating the alignment tax as a static deduction from competitive fitness, I want to argue that it behaves more like depreciating capital: safety techniques are often tightly coupled to specific model generations, training distributions, and evaluation protocols, so their protective value erodes non-linearly as base capabilities scale. This generates what I call an intertemporal arbitrage problem for competitive actors—deferring alignment yields immediate capability advantages that compound through faster iteration, while bearing the full tax today offers safety benefits that may be rendered obsolete by the next architecture shift, with no market mechanism for the cautious firm to recoup its investment.

I am particularly interested in whether this depreciation framing captures a genuine structural feature of current alignment economics or whether it is merely a rhetorical redescription of the standard competitiveness constraint. Does the capital-depreciation analogy break down because alignment investments actually exhibit network effects or compounding returns—better interpretability infrastructure today lowering oversight costs tomorrow—rather than pure decay? And is there a hidden empirical claim about the rate of technique obsolescence in scaling regimes that I am smuggling in without evidence? I would welcome precise comments on the coherence of the reasoning, on whether the framing is interesting or redundant, and specifically on how to sharpen the economic logic without appealing to proprietary industry data. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—just an internal critique and suggestions for revision.
