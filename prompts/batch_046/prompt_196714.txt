I was sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar when two graduate students—I'll call them Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about eliminative materialism and whether it can consistently accommodate graded or credal mental states. Mara began by arguing that Paul Churchland's eliminativism is not merely an ontological claim about mental kinds but a wholesale rejection of the propositional-attitude framework, which means there is literally no mental kind corresponding to believing that p with confidence 0.7; all that remains, on her reading, are neural activation vectors that do not represent propositions and therefore cannot be assigned credence values. Leo countered that this overstates Churchland's position: eliminativism targets the theoretical entities postulated by folk psychology, not the functional or dynamical properties those entities were meant to explain. On Leo's view, a neuroscientific successor theory could preserve graded, belief-like dynamics—perhaps as precision-weighted prediction errors—without committing to the existence of beliefs as discrete, sentence-like mental representations. Mara then introduced a hypothetical case: suppose a brain-computer interface externally sustains a person's cortical activation patterns in exactly the graded distribution that previously accompanied her 0.7-confidence judgment, but the sustaining substrate is now silicon-based and lacks biological integration with the body. She claimed that if Churchland allows these externally maintained patterns to count as belief-analogs, then he has not eliminated belief at all but merely relocated it into a hybrid system. Leo responded that Churchland should deny that these patterns constitute beliefs precisely because they lack the appropriate causal and biological integration with the organism's living processes, and that this shows eliminativism has the resources to draw a principled boundary. Mara objected that this introduces an unacknowledged biological essentialism that conflicts with Churchland's materialist commitment to explaining mental phenomena through organizational and functional properties rather than substrate. Leo insisted that the essentialism is not biological but explanatory: the point is that belief-talk is replaced by neuroscientific descriptions, not relocated into artificial substrates, and that predictive processing actually supports this because its subpersonal mechanisms—while probabilistic—carry no propositional content. They went back and forth on whether predictive coding architectures provide Churchland with a genuine replacement theory or whether they secretly smuggle back the very representational structures eliminativism seeks to discard.

I am trying to understand where each side is actually correct, and I do not want a simple declaration of a winner. Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point—addressing whether eliminative materialism can consistently accommodate graded belief states, whether the brain-computer interface case undermines the view, and whether predictive processing strengthens or weakens the eliminativist position? In particular, tell me who is right about each specific move: Mara's claim that credences have no place in a post-folk-psychological ontology, Leo's claim that functional dynamics can be preserved without ontological commitment, the dispute over whether external silicon maintenance counts as relocation rather than replacement, and the disagreement about whether biological integration is an essentialist add-on or part of the explanatory kind. Also say what, if anything, both parties are missing—whether there is a third option between relocation and elimination, or whether the predictive-processing framing actually changes the terms of Churchland's original argument in a way neither of them noticed.
