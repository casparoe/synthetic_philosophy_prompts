I have been thinking, without formal training, about a pair of ideas that seem to lock together in an uncomfortable way. The first is that certain mental states—pain, perhaps, or the feeling of seeing red—seem to carry their own awareness with them: if I am genuinely in that state, I am in a position to know it, and I cannot be mistaken about whether it is occurring. The second is that knowledge seems to carry a kind of second-order guarantee: if I truly know something, then I also know that I know it. I have been calling this the "double-lock" problem in my private notes, because it feels as though there are two separate doors—transparency of the mental state itself, and the self-certifying structure of knowledge—that might either reinforce each other or come apart at the edges.

What puzzles me most is not whether philosophers have named these ideas, but which open questions about them actually deserve sustained attention if one is working alone, with perhaps three years of disciplined reflection and no access to specialized archives. In particular, I have noticed what I call "edge states": mental conditions that are barely present—half-formed annoyance, a faint suspicion one is not quite ready to admit, or awareness that hovers at the threshold of consciousness. The transparency claim seems to fail precisely here, yet the knowledge-of-knowledge claim might still apply to the beliefs one forms about these edges. So my question is this: among the unresolved issues in this area, which should be treated as upstream, and which are downstream?

Please do not give me a reading list or cite specific texts. Instead, treat this as an agenda-setting problem. For the purpose of my own long-term thinking, which two or three open lines of inquiry matter most? Is it more important to determine whether transparency holds selectively—only for "full" conscious states—or whether it fails systematically at the margins? Or is the upstream problem the second-order knowledge principle itself: whether it applies to all knowledge or only to a restricted class, and whether its failure in edge cases undermines the first claim? I want a defended prioritization: what criteria you are using—tractability for a solitary thinker, how many other puzzles depend on the answer, practical relevance for understanding self-deception, or something else—and where the trade-offs lie. Which question, if settled, would change the landscape for the others? And what kind of argument or observation would cause you to reorder your priorities? Give me the reasoning, not a survey.
