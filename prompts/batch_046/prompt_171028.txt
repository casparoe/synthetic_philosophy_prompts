I have been working through Berkeley’s writings on political economy, particularly his series of queries concerning banking, currency, and national welfare, and I am trying to assess how tightly his monetary philosophy is integrated with his immaterialist metaphysics rather than treating it as an isolated practical concern. Please provide the ranking from most defensible to least defensible within a consistently Berkeleian framework, with a brief justification for each position, and rate each commitment on a scale of one to five for internal coherence within that same system, noting where the ranking by defensibility diverges from the ranking by coherence.

In doing so, please separate what is well established in Berkeley scholarship from what depends on a contested or speculative reading of his metaphysical commitments—particularly regarding whether he would have endorsed anything resembling a functionalist or social-ontological account of institutional reality—and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus view among historians of philosophy. I am also interested in whether you think any of these commitments become incoherent if pushed to their limit: identify which one is most vulnerable to collapse under strict immaterialism, and indicate what kind of argument or textual evidence would most shift your confidence in that assessment.
