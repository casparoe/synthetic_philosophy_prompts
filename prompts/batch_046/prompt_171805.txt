As a journalist who writes about the history of science and the logic of institutional change, I have been revisiting David Lewis’s account of convention in light of an episode usually told as pure religious politics: the staggered global adoption of the Gregorian calendar from 1582 through the early twentieth century. Rather than treating the centuries-long divergence among Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, and eventually Soviet jurisdictions as mere inertia or doctrinal stubbornness, I want a disciplined analysis through Lewis’s framework—specifically his claim that conventions are arbitrary coordination equilibria sustained by common knowledge of precedent and mutual expectation, not by intrinsic merit.

Please treat the calendar transition as your case study. Identify the actual coordination problem: who needed to align with whom (merchants, states, churches, astronomers), what the payoffs were for matching versus diverging, and where the regularity was genuinely arbitrary versus anchored to an astronomical fact that undermines pure arbitrariness. Map the conditions for a Lewisian convention—mutual knowledge, precedent, and the possibility of an alternative equilibrium—onto specific episodes: the 1582 papal bull, Britain’s 1752 Calendar Act tied to financial settlement, and Russia’s 1918 Bolshevik adoption. Then tell me where the model breaks: does the eventual convergence despite centuries of divergence illustrate a critical-mass switch between equilibria, or does the solar anchor mean the calendar was never a convention but a factual correction that merely spread slowly? Be explicit about which actors faced a true coordination game, which faced a belief-updating problem about astronomy, and what the episode therefore shows about the limits of applying Lewis’s machinery for norm evolution to technically grounded standards.
