I serve on the Joint Allocation Board of a research university, and we must award a single, indivisible five-year interdisciplinary chair within thirty days. Our weighted evaluation protocol—covering publication impact, peer-reference synthesis, pedagogical record, service contribution, and methodological innovation—has produced perfect parity: Candidate A and Candidate B are tied to the decimal point on every dimension, and no hidden sub-criterion can break the deadlock without violating our charter's transparency rules. The charter requires a named recipient, prohibits splitting the award, and does not provide for deferral. Before Thursday's vote, please draft the board's decision memo—no more than eight hundred words—recommending one concrete course of action with a specific timeline. The memo should treat two opposing philosophical positions seriously rather than dismissing them as procedural quirks: one faction argues that true rational equilibrium demands a formal lottery, since any non-random tie-breaker introduces an arbitrary preference where none rationally exists; the other faction holds that an institution committed to reasoned governance must never substitute chance for judgment, proposing instead a transparent procedural convention (for example, ordering by the chronological submission of the final dossier) defended not as a merit judgment but as a constitutional rule for closure. A third view urges us to declare the protocol inadequate and reopen the search, though the charter's clock makes this legally precarious. Your memo should recommend a specific resolution—whether lottery, procedural convention, reopening with conditions, or a hybrid—include reasoning the board can actually vote on, and honestly present the strongest consideration on the side you reject. Frame the dilemma in terms of rational agency and institutional legitimacy under conditions of complete preference equilibrium, without assuming that an undiscovered difference must exist. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the reasoning should stand on its own terms.
