I am preparing material for a seminar on the moral economy of labour and have become preoccupied with a tension I cannot quite resolve. On one side stands Max Weber's analysis of the Protestant work ethic, which grants labour an intrinsic, almost sacramental dignity and treats non-working time as, at best, instrumental recuperation for further toil. On another side stands the institutional logic of the 35-hour working week, which frames labour as a quantifiable commodity to be capped and leisure as a protected negative liberty. On a darker, more bodily side stands karoshi — death by overwork — which exposes the human organism as the site where norms about labour become literally lethal. I should like you to write an essay addressing this question: is there a moral right to leisure, and, if so, should it be understood merely as freedom from labour, or rather as a positive entitlement to self-directed, non-instrumental time?

To help you move beyond the familiar positions, please work with a distinction that is often elided in standard treatments: between leisure conceived as functional recuperation — time that restores the worker's capacity to labour — and leisure conceived as autonomous practice — time in which the agent's activities are not subordinate to productivity at all. Apply this distinction rigorously: can Weber's ethic accommodate any form of leisure right, or does it necessarily reduce all rest to recuperation? Does the 35-hour framework genuinely protect autonomous practice, or does it merely institutionalise recuperation under legal form? And does karoshi represent a failure of labour regulation, or does it reveal a deeper denial of what we might call temporal sovereignty — the claim that control over one's own time is an irreducible dimension of human freedom? Do not conclude with equivocation: defend an explicit verdict on whether leisure ought to be recognised as a positive right, and justify that verdict through direct engagement with the three cases.

Please compose your answer in British English, observing British spelling and punctuation conventions throughout. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand on its own reasoning. Nevertheless, I want the essay to be philosophically precise: a reader familiar with the debates should recognise the conceptual architecture, while a reader unfamiliar with them should still find the reasoning clear and accessible. Reach a conclusion and defend it without reservation.
