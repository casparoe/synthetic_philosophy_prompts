Say you're advising a small research group with three years of funding to tackle one big conceptual problem in archaeological inference—specifically, how we reconstuct institutional beliefs (like legal norms or ritual obligations) from material remains without assuming they mirror modern categories. There's a tension between 'thin description' approaches that stay close to the objects, and 'thick reconstruction' that imports ethnographic analogies. But there's also this newer, somewhat niche idea floating round that we should treat archaeological silence—sites where expected objects are missing—not as missing data but as active institutional choices (e.g. deliberate exclusion of certain materials from burial contexts). Which of these open lines of work actually matters most for the field right now: refining the logic of analogical reasoning, formalising how absence counts as evidence, or developing criteria to distinguish shared belief from shared practice? Defend a ranking: which question is upstream of the others, which one would change how we read a site like Çatalhöyük or Sutton Hoo if solved, and which is basically a dead end despite sounding exciting?
