I'm in a philosophy of science seminar and we keep circling back to Friedman's twist -- the claim that unrealistic assumptions don't matter if your model predicts well. The standard defense people trot out is that it's exactly like a physicist using a frictionless plane or a point mass. You strip away the messy parts to see the core law, then layer the complexity back in once you understand the baseline. It sounds reasonable, but I think the analogy is hiding something important.

In physics, friction is a parameter you can actually reduce in an experiment -- you approach the ideal case by degrees. But in a lot of social science models, the unrealistic feature isn't a dial you can turn down. Perfect rationality isn't "less irrationality"; it's a different kind of agent altogether. Same with infinite populations in evolutionary theory -- you can't just scale up a finite group and expect the same dynamics. So the "frictionless plane" analogy might be smuggling in the idea that every unrealistic assumption is just a temporary quantitative simplification, when sometimes it's a constitutive replacement that changes the ontology of what's being modeled.
