Below are draft notes for a short philosophical essay. The subject is whether T.M. Scanlon’s contractualist framework—on which an act is wrong if it is permitted by principles that no one could reasonably reject—can accommodate aggregation without collapsing into a simple counting of harms and benefits. I do not want a bibliography or reading list; I want critical comments on the argument’s structure, conceptual coherence, and fidelity to the contractualist idea.

My central claim is that the standard aggregation objection mislocates the problem. Scanlon allows that the strength of an individual’s reason for rejecting a principle varies with the magnitude of the burden she bears. The real difficulty is not that reasons “add up” across persons, but that the justification of a principle must be addressed to each affected individual, and when burdens are distributed unevenly, the same principle can be reasonably rejected by those who bear disproportionate costs relative to what they receive.

To make this precise, I propose a distinction between cumulative harm and scaled burden. In a cumulative-harm case—say, a policy that saves one person from death but imposes a trivial, momentary inconvenience on each of a million others—the contractualist can resist aggregation by noting that each individual reason against the policy is weak, and no single person is asked to bear a cost that is large relative to her share of any common benefit. In a scaled-burden case—say, a policy that saves ten people from severe, permanent disability by imposing moderate, sustained discomfort on each of one hundred others—the objection is not numerical but proportional: each of the one hundred is being asked to absorb a burden that is non-trivial relative to what she personally gains, and the principle allowing this must be defensible to her specifically, not justified by reference to the ten saved.

I then introduce a hypothetical “fair-shares threshold”: a principle is justifiable to each affected party only if no individual is required to bear a burden exceeding what she could accept without being treated as a mere resource for others’ welfare. I apply this to a concrete scenario: a municipal water-rationing plan during a prolonged drought that prevents catastrophic dehydration for a small upstream community by requiring a large downstream population to reduce consumption by thirty percent for six months. The plan saves lives; it also imposes real, non-trivial costs on many. I argue that the contractualist should evaluate not whether a thirty-percent reduction is “worth” saving lives in aggregate, but whether the rationing scheme respects each affected party’s standing—specifically, whether the burden is distributed according to prior use, basic need, or capacity, and whether those asked to bear it have been given adequate procedural voice in the design of the scheme.

My concerns are as follows. First, have I accurately represented Scanlon’s view on the variability of rejection-reasons, or does contractualism treat burdens of the same kind as generating reasons of roughly uniform strength regardless of distribution? Second, does the fair-shares threshold genuinely solve the aggregation problem, or does it merely rename a prioritarian or utilitarian calculus of proportional sacrifice? Third, is the drought-rationing case well-chosen, or does it conflate questions of distributive justice with the specifically contractualist question of reasonable rejectability? Fourth, I worry that my distinction between cumulative harm and scaled burden collapses once we allow that trivial burdens can become significant through repetition—does this reintroduce aggregation through the back door by letting numbers affect the total weight of a reason?

Please comment on the clarity and plausibility of these distinctions, point out any missteps in the reconstruction of contractualism, and suggest one further hypothetical case that would sharply test whether my fair-shares approach genuinely diverges from a threshold-deontological or prioritarian alternative.
