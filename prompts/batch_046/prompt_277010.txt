Student's answer (typed up from the paper):

"The distinction between causation-by-omission and failure-of-prevention is crucial here. In failure-of-prevention, an agent stops an already-running causal process, but B never started the monitoring process, so his mistake is a pure omission rather than a failed prevention. Applying the counterfactual test: if B had flipped the switch, the system would have been in active-monitoring and detected the 0.6T malfunction, preventing damage. So the damage counterfactually depends on B's omission, which means the omission is genuinely causal. People sometimes worry that omissions aren't events and therefore can't enter causal relations, but the counterfactual dependance solves this—if the world changes depending on whether the omission happens, that's enough for causation.

On overdetermination: the malfunction and B's omission were both necessary for the damage, but that's not overdetermination, since overdetermination requires two sufficient conditions. Here neither condition alone was sufficient—the malfunction wouldn't have caused damage in active mode, and B's omission wouldn't have caused damage without the malfunction—so they are joint necessary conditions, not overdeterminers. Finally, the system's standby design with its T-threshold is basically just the default background; it doesn't causally interact with the malfunction because it only specifies when the system reacts, not why the malfunction happened. So B's omission is the real causal factor, and the design is irrelevant except as context."
