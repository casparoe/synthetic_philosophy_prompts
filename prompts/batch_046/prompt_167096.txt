I'm taking a graduate seminar on constitutional theory, and we've been working through whether democratic constitutions can coherently include entrenchment clauses—provisions that bind future majorities—without violating the principle that present citizens should govern themselves. The usual framing draws on Ulysses contracts: just as a rational individual might bind her future self to avoid predictable weakness of will, a polity might bind its future iterations to protect fundamental commitments from temporary passions. Most of the literature I have seen focuses on whether this analogy fails because democratic sovereignty requires that no generation be permanently subject to another's will. But I'm more interested in a structural distinction that hasn't come up much in our discussions. Some constitutional orders rely mainly on substantive rigidity—explicitly listing rights that are hard to amend—while others embed procedural "lock-in" mechanisms, such as supermajority amendment thresholds, mandatory periodic renewal, or sunset clauses that force active reaffirmation rather than passive endurance.

Write an essay addressing the following: does the presence of these procedural lock-ins strengthen the Ulysses-contract justification, because they make the binding mechanism transparent and subject to collective deliberation, or do they actually undermine the analogy by revealing that there is no unified "self" across generations to bind? In other words, is constitutional precommitment more defensible when it operates through procedural barriers that future majorities can deliberately overcome, or does any form of intergenerational binding fail for democratic reasons regardless of structure? Weigh the arguments carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note—defend a specific verdict about whether procedural lock-ins salvage the Ulysses-contract defense or expose it as a category error, and explain what that implies for how we should evaluate constitutional rigidity in general.
