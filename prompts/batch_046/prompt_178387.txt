I am a sixth-form student preparing an essay on institutions and the self, and I have kept encountering a pattern that I can describe clearly but cannot name with any precision. At my school, every pupil must complete a weekly digital "wellbeing check-in" that asks us to rate our stress, sleep, and mood using fixed institutional categories. The information is never used to punish us; instead, it determines who receives extra pastoral support, smaller mentoring groups, or mental-health referrals. Over the past year I have noticed two linked developments. First, pupils have begun to talk about themselves in the portal's exact terms—"I am amber this week"—and to adjust what they report so that they sit in the band that attracts gentle attention rather than neglect, or, at the other extreme, intrusive monitoring. Second, and more strikingly, this adjustment is not driven by fear of sanction. Many of us actively want to be correctly classified, because being seen as "green-stable" means being ignored, whereas being seen as "amber-vulnerable" brings genuine care; we therefore refine our submissions, offering more detailed emotional data in the belief that greater transparency will produce more appropriate support. I have observed something similar in workplaces that use wellness applications to track employee stress, where staff curate their data to appear appropriately vulnerable rather than unmanageable. The configuration resembles the idea that power produces knowledge and that individuals internalise norms, yet it seems distinct from the panopticon model, because visibility here is an invitation rather than a threat, and self-surveillance is motivated by the pursuit of benevolence rather than the avoidance of punishment. Is there an established concept—within Foucauldian discussions of discipline, governmentality, or pastoral power—that captures this specific loop, in which institutional classification is explicitly framed as care, subjects collaborate in refining their own classification, and the resulting self-knowledge is shaped by a desire to be properly tended to? If there is no precise term, how ought it be distinguished from ordinary disciplinary power and from biopolitical management, and what name and boundary conditions would properly delimit it? I am not asking for a bare label; I want to know exactly where the edges lie, what it is not, and whether the phenomenon I have described is genuinely distinct or merely a variant of something already well named.
