I am a high school student in an advanced political philosophy seminar, and I have become preoccupied with Karl Jaspers’ taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—particularly his claim that citizens can share a political guilt for the actions of their state even when they personally committed no crime. Last week my classmates and I debated whether teenagers born decades after a regime’s worst abuses—specifically, in a wealthy democracy that built its prosperity through intensive ecological extraction—can inherit any genuine form of collective guilt for harms that peaked before they existed. Some argued that because the nation still derives benefit from past extraction, present citizens bear an inherited political guilt; others insisted that guilt requires personal agency and expires with the generation that authorized the harm.

Write an essay weighing the coherence of inherited collective guilt across generations. Apply Jaspers’ four categories, but do not treat them as self-evident: examine whether political guilt can survive the death or replacement of the citizens who originally authorized state action, or whether it presupposes a diachronic collective identity—the nation as a continuous moral subject stretching across time. Contrast this with a synchronic distributive view, on which responsibility is always allocated among living, acting individuals at a specific moment and cannot be transmitted like an inheritance or property right. Take as your concrete case present-day citizens—many of them still minors—who are being asked to accept reparative obligations for carbon-intensive industrialization that occurred largely before they were born. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either defend a modified Jaspersian position in which one form of guilt is genuinely intergenerational, or argue that collective guilt collapses once diachronic identity is rejected, leaving only forward-looking political obligations rather than backward-looking guilt. Your essay should not conclude with ambiguity; it should defend one of these verdicts with reasons that address both the conceptual distinction and the practical stakes of asking young citizens to answer for a past they did not choose.
