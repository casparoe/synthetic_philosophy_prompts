I am a junior in an independent-study philosophy course at my high school, and over the past month my advisor and I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter’s suggestion that a person’s “soul” or self-pattern might persist in a distributed fashion—not inside a single continuing brain, but scattered across the memories, reasoning habits, and emotional responses of others who knew the original individual well. I want to treat this not as a poetic image but as a literal metaphysical proposal: a theory of inter-brain personal identity according to which the “I” can survive in fragments across multiple living minds once the original biological substrate is gone. Before I compare any particular version of this view with standard psychological-continuity or functionalist accounts, I need the full specification of what such a theory must actually deliver in order to count as adequate rather than merely metaphorical.

Please write out the desiderata. The theory must explain the difference between genuine persistence of the self and ordinary influence, imitation, or memetic transmission; it must handle the case in which two people who knew the original develop incompatible reasoning patterns without allowing the distributed “I” to split into contradictory subjects; it must respect neuroscientific results about the uniqueness of neural encoding and the limits of memory reconstruction, without invoking science-fictional technologies such as perfect brain scanning or digitization; and it must clarify whether the distributed fragments share a unified first-person perspective or whether phenomenological unity is sacrificed. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, mark which ones are non-negotiable for any literal version of the view and which might be relaxed if one adopts only a weaker form of “symbolic immortality,” and identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the demand for objective, third-person criteria of persistence conflicts with the demand that the surviving self retain subjective coherence. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding rival theories such as standard psychological continuity or Dennett’s narrative-center-of-gravity model, and briefly note whether Hofstadter’s own framing already meets that requirement or quietly abandons it.
