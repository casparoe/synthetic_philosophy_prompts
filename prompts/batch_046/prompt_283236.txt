I'm trying to determine whether various ontological disagreements are genuinely substantive or merely verbal, and my intuitions across cases don't obviously cohere. Here are my judgments, with the reasoning I can see behind each.

First, two marine biologists study a coral colony in which all polyps are genetically identical and share a continuous gastrovascular network; separation of any polyp causes rapid death. One biologist says there is literally one organism, the reef, with parts that are not independently living. The other says there are thousands of organisms arranged reef-wise, since each polyp retains its own mouth, digestive cavity, and reproductive capacity. They agree on every measurement of growth, energy flow, and reproductive output. I think this dispute is substantive: they disagree about the real conditions of biological unity, not merely about how to apply a word.

Second, two physicists present quantum mechanics in different formalisms—one using continuous fields, the other using discrete particles with correlation structures. Every prediction, every equation's empirical consequence, is identical. I think this is verbal: they are redescribing one mathematical structure in different idioms, like the difference between describing gravity as spacetime curvature or as a gravitational field.

Third, two historians disagree about whether the Roman Empire fell in 476 CE. One points to the end of the Western imperial line; the other notes that the Eastern administration, legal codes, and tax apparatus persisted unchanged for centuries. They agree on every event and document. This feels clearly verbal: a dispute about where to draw a conventional boundary on a continuous process.

Fourth, a moral realist and an error theorist agree on every first-order judgment—torture is wrong, promise-breaking is wrong, and so on. The realist says these judgments are made true by real moral properties; the error theorist says they are systematically false because no such properties exist. I think this is substantive: they disagree about what exists in the world, not about language.

Fifth, lawyers and economists debate whether a corporation is one agent with intentions and responsibilities, or is only many human beings arranged corporation-wise by legal convention. They agree on every charter, every board decision, and every legal consequence. Here I think the dispute is verbal: corporation functions as an organizational concept, and the disagreement is about which descriptive framework to adopt, not about an independent ontological fact.

The difficulty is that the first and fifth cases both involve a collective of functionally integrated parts, yet I call the first substantive and the second verbal. Meanwhile, the second and fourth cases both involve agreement on all empirical or normative judgments, yet I call one verbal and one substantive.

Is there a single principle that captures all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, explain which clause handles each case, and say what it predicts for a dispute I haven't listed: whether a large crowd that moves and reacts as a unified body—say, a stadium audience performing a synchronized wave—counts as one agent or only many people arranged crowd-wise. If no principle saves all five, which verdict should I overturn, and what is the simplest account that accommodates the other four?
