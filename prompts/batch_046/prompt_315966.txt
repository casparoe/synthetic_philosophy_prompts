I'm a software engineer by training with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to work through some older texts in my spare time. At a recent discussion group with two colleagues—one who works in ethics-adjacent AI governance and another who is a former theologian now in data infrastructure—we got into a long argument about Kierkegaard's treatment of Abraham in what I believe is called Fear and Trembling. I could follow the broad strokes, but I couldn't tell whether either of them was actually right about the details, and since I'm the one who suggested we read it, I feel responsible for settling the dispute. I need a point-by-point adjudication from someone who actually understands the material.

My first colleague, Maya, argued that Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac is best understood as a genuine teleological suspension of the ethical: the universal moral prohibition against killing is not denied but temporarily suspended for the sake of a higher telos, which is Abraham's singular, absolute relation to God. She insisted that this suspension is strictly incommunicable—Abraham cannot justify himself to Sarah, to Isaac, or to the ethical community—which is precisely why it does not become a public license for violence. The suspension, she said, belongs only to the individual knight of faith standing before the absolute, and it leaves the universal ethical sphere intact for everyone else.

My second colleague, Jordan, replied that calling the act a "teleological suspension" is exactly the kind of rationalist framing that makes the idea dangerous. He argued that if private revelation can override the universal prohibition on killing, then there is no stable moral community—only isolated individuals claiming exemptions. To make his point concrete, he offered an analogy: imagine an AI safety engineer who disables every transparency and audit protocol because she alone possesses a private conviction about a greater good that supersedes institutional safeguards. Maya said the analogy fails because Abraham was not acting inside any communal role or institutional framework; his silence shows he knew the ethical community could never understand him, so the suspension applies only to his singular case and licenses no one else. Jordan countered that this makes the problem worse: if the suspension is truly incommunicable and non-generalizable, then describing it as "teleological" is misleading, since a telos usually implies some rational orientation that others could recognize in principle. Instead, he claimed, Abraham's act is closer to an absurd leap that renders ethics irrelevant rather than subordinate, and the language of a "higher purpose" is doing all the work in disguising a radical refusal of accountability.

They kept going in this vein. Maya maintained that Jordan was conflating the singular with the universal—without the distinction between the ethical sphere and the religious sphere, Kierkegaard's whole point about faith's paradox collapses into mere irrationalism. Jordan maintained that Maya was smuggling rationality back into a concept specifically designed to break rational categories, and that protecting the ethical sphere by declaring the suspension "incommunicable" is just an ad hoc move to avoid the destructive implications.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move: assess whether the suspension is genuinely teleological or rather a destructive break with ethics, whether the incommunicability of Abraham's act protects shared moral life or undermines it, and where each of them is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right. Say who is more right overall, and identify anything important both seem to be missing about how the text frames the relationship between the ethical and the religious spheres. Do not offer a bibliography or ask for further reading—just resolve the argument as it stands.
