Construct a decision tree for an advanced philosophy seminar that classifies interpretations of Buddhist anatta in relation to contemporary theories of personal identity. The tree should begin from three initial commitments—whether one denies any permanent, unchanging substrate underlying experience; whether one holds that rebirth requires some form of diachronic continuity; and whether one regards moral responsibility as compatible with a merely conventional self—and should proceed through concrete diagnostic questions that mention no school names, terminating in named positions, clearly marked unoccupied nodes, or explicit flags of indistinguishability.

The tree must include the following as leaves or diagnosed gaps: a Theravada reductionist view treating the person as a stream of discrete skandhas linked only by causal succession; a Madhyamaka view holding both self and skandhas empty of intrinsic existence; a Yogacara-influenced position appealing to a subtle store-consciousness, sometimes termed alaya-vijnana, to ground continuity; a Parfit-style psychological-continuity reductionism that treats anatta as consonant with Western reductionist accounts; a narrative-identity view treating the conventional self as an ethically indispensable fiction; and an eliminativist view denying all useful role to self-talk.

In addition, introduce one synthetic combination not standardly defended: a position that accepts anatta, rejects every substrate continuity, yet insists that karmic accountability survives through purely relational, indexical tracking of causal patterns without any bearer—designated the indexical-karmic position. If this node is incoherent or unoccupied, flag it and diagnose whether the difficulty is conceptual contradiction, practical unmotivatedness, or mere historical neglect.

Each internal node must be phrased as a concrete case or claim answerable without knowing its interpretive destination—for example, gradual skandha replacement preserving full mnemonic access versus instantaneous rebirth with perfect causal but zero mnemonic overlap. The tree should contain between eight and ten terminal nodes (including unoccupied ones), and each leaf must state its most awkward commitment regarding moral responsibility across lives in a single sentence.

Finally, indicate any pairs of named positions that the diagnostic questions fail to separate, explaining precisely why the tree collapses them, and present the entire result in British English with clear hierarchical formatting. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
