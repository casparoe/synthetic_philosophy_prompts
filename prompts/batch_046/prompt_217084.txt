Write an essay on whether the conceivability of what we might call "modular phenomenal absence" undermines or reinforces the standard philosophical-zombie argument against physicalism. Define modular phenomenal absence as the hypothetical possibility of a being that is physically and functionally identical to a conscious human in every detectable respect, except that exactly one phenomenal modality—say, chromatic color experience, or emotional affect, or proprioceptive feeling—is entirely absent, with no corresponding behavioral deficit or functional gap visible from the outside. Examine the dialectic carefully: if a full-blown zombie lacking all phenomenal consciousness is conceivable, then selective absence seems conceivable a fortiori, which might suggest that phenomenal properties are separable from physical structures in fine-grained, localized ways; but consider the opposing reply that phenomenal consciousness may be globally integrated, so that imagining its modality-specific removal involves illegitimately treating consciousness as a bundle of independent "add-ons" rather than as a unified field. Assess whether these partial cases strengthen anti-physicalism by demonstrating precise phenomenal-physical separability, or whether they weaken the zombie argument by revealing that decomposing consciousness modality-by-modality is conceptually unstable. Reach a definite, justified verdict—for example, that modular absence is genuinely conceivable but does not threaten physicalism because the integration objection succeeds, or that its apparent inconceivability exposes a structural constraint on phenomenal concepts that saves physicalism—and defend that conclusion through careful conceptual analysis rather than appeals to authority, citation, or empirical literature.
