Here is my opening challenge. Consider an art museum whose charter defines its purpose as advancing art-historical knowledge through rigorous attribution practices. A curator must decide whether to attribute an ambiguous painting to an obscure seventeenth-century workshop. The evidence genuinely underdetermines the attribution. She has one consideration that tracks the practice's aim: brushwork and pigment analysis align with the workshop. She also faces two further considerations. First, attributing the painting to this workshop will secure a major grant that saves the institution from closure. Second, she personally stands to gain prestige and a promotion if the attribution succeeds, regardless of whether it is accurate. The grant reason seems wrong because it conflicts with the museum's constitutive charter, but the prestige reason is wrong for her as an individual believer about the painting's origin, even though personal career advancement is not part of the attribution practice's constitutive norms. Thus the wrong-kind-reasons problem seems to arise twice over—once institutionally and once personally—and your view appears able to explain only the institutional case. How do you respond without collapsing the personal-level problem into the institutional one, and without appealing to a practice-independent norm of belief?
