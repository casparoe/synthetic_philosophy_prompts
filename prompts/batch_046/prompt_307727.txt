You are a senior examiner in moral philosophy with extensive experience designing assessments for upper-level seminars on moral epistemology and normative judgment. Before you begin drafting, take a moment to think carefully about what genuinely separates a student who understands the conceptual architecture of moral preference from one who has merely memorized the standard vocabulary—terms like implicit bias, confirmation bias, or moral taste—and can recite familiar examples without constructing an original argument. Your task is to design a single final-examination essay question that a well-prepared candidate could answer in ninety minutes, for a total of twenty-five marks, aimed precisely at testing this deeper capacity. The domain is the difficulty of distinguishing between preferences and biases in morality, but you should avoid framing the issue through empirical psychology, well-known experiments, or canonical bibliographic references that a respondent without external resources could not reconstruct from conceptual reasoning alone. Instead, inject directly into the prompt the following conceptual apparatus: distinguish between an indexical calibration failure, in which a moral judgment conflicts with an agent’s own second-order normative commitments yet remains stable across reflective contexts, and a framework-external distortion, in which a judgment is shaped by non-moral psychological mechanisms that the agent would disavow even at the level of considered preference. The exam question should present a single tightly constructed hypothetical case—an otherwise rigorous moral philosopher who applies consequentialist reasoning consistently in professional and abstract contexts, but who experiences a persistent, non-rational aversion to utilitarian conclusions whenever they require sacrificing members of her own tightly knit social group, and who upon sustained reflection explicitly disavows any group-based moral distinction while retaining her commitment to welfare maximization. Ask candidates to determine whether her pattern is best understood as a genuine preference for partiality, a bias against universalism, a structural conflict between first-order affect and second-order commitment, or something else entirely, and require them to defend their diagnosis using only conditions of internal coherence rather than appeals to empirical data, neuroscientific findings, or scholarly citation. The question must be unanswerable by summary: a top answer must construct an argument about whether coherence conditions can ever be sufficient to separate preference from bias when both manifest as stable patterns of judgment. Along with the question, provide a model answer written entirely in continuous prose—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, arranged like a letter or email—and a marking scheme rendered in the same continuous prose format that names exactly five argumentative moves a top answer must make, assigns marks to each move with a running total of twenty-five, and includes within the same flowing paragraphs a brief note on the specific mistake candidates typically make regarding each move and how that mistake should reduce the score. Please ensure that neither the question nor the model answer depends on bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific named scholars or texts that a student could not reasonably supply from the conceptual material itself.
