My teenage son recently came home from a literature assignment on William Golding’s Lord of the Flies insisting that Jack Merridew was acting rationally once Ralph’s authority collapsed, and that the boys’ descent into violence was less a moral failure than a predictable strategic equilibrium. The argument has stayed with me, because it raises whether people can be locked into harmful collective outcomes that none of them individually desire. I want to read the novel through the conceptual framework of multipolar traps and Moloch dynamics—the structural idea that rational agents, anticipating defection by others, escalate in ways that reduce absolute welfare for everyone, even in the absence of malice or authoritarian command.

Please read Lord of the Flies through this lens. In which specific scenes does the multipolar-trap model illuminate the boys’ choices: Jack’s first successful hunt, the raid that seizes Piggy’s glasses, Simon’s death during the ritual dance, and the final hunt for Ralph? Where does Golding present the violence as an expression of innate human darkness or of Jack’s personal magnetism and taste for cruelty, thereby resisting a purely structural interpretation? Does the text suggest that unilateral cooperation—Ralph maintaining the conch rules, Piggy refusing to join the hunters—could have broken the cycle, or does it portray such cooperation as fatal, transforming the dynamics from a coordination failure into a particularly brutal anti-coordination game in which the cost of defection is death?

I am also interested in whether Golding’s narrative maintains, or collapses, the distinction between a multipolar race to the bottom and a simpler dominance hierarchy. A structural defender might argue that each boy joins Jack not from bloodlust but because the rising cost of remaining outside his faction makes cooperation irrational; yet the novel also gives Jack an almost charismatic authority and a specific pleasure in violence that seem irreducible to strategic calculation. Where does the honest critic have to say that the literary details outstrip the philosophical framework?

Finally, what does the novel contribute to our understanding of Moloch that purely analytical treatments of multipolar traps miss? I am preparing to discuss this with my son’s class and want a reading that takes both the literary text and the game-theoretic idea seriously—without reducing the book to an illustration, and without distorting the concept of multipolar traps to fit Golding’s narrative. Which scenes best dramatize the tension between structural explanation and individual moral responsibility, and where does the work resist being read as a simple allegory for coordination failure?
