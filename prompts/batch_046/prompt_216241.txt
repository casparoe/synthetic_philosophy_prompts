You serve as the senior philosophical advisor to the Joint Committee on Epochal Taxonomy at a major interdisciplinary research institution. The Committee oversees a unified classification system used across the university's natural-history museums, archival repositories, and environmental-science curricula, and at Thursday's meeting it must vote on whether to formally adopt "Anthropocene" as an official epoch within that system.

The working group proposes adoption with a boundary fixed at 1952, marked by the onset of widespread radiogenic isotope deposition, arguing that this satisfies the Committee's scientific-legibility criterion and aligns with the so-called Great Acceleration. However, a substantial dissent—led by historians, geographers, and community representatives—contends that the Committee's own charter requires any epoch designation that frames collective human agency to meet a "representative deliberation" standard: the designation must be validated by a globally inclusive deliberative process rather than solely by a technical stratigraphic body, because labeling an entire species as a geological force carries normative weight about responsibility, blame, and historical erasure. They further argue that adopting a single universal boundary imposes what they call a "uniform-agent fiction," obscuring the radically unequal contributions to planetary change across regions, economies, and social groups, and they warn that fixing the boundary now risks conceptual disruption should an international body later ratify a different marker—perhaps one tied to early-modern colonial land-use transformations. Meanwhile, the archives director notes that provisional adoption with a dual-label mechanism might protect institutional alignment without committing to a single narrative.

Please think carefully about the philosophical tensions at stake—particularly the conflict between scientific authority and democratic representation in taxonomy, the ethics of collective ascription, and the practical costs of epistemic premature closure—before drafting your response. I want you to begin with a brief outline of your reasoning, then produce a formal decision memo addressed to the Committee chair, recommending a concrete course of action (full adoption, rejection, or provisional adoption with specific conditions) together with an implementation timeline. The memo should treat the dissent's strongest argument with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it, should avoid appeals to bibliographic authority, and should provide reasoning that the opposing faction could recognize as fair even if they reject the bottom line. Write in a professional format with a clearly marked recommendation section.
