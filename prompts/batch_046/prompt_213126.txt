After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether renaming a street or removing a statue is an erasure of history or a correction of honor, I want to set aside the particular case and ask for the underlying specification. What must an adequate normative theory of public commemoration actually deliver? I am looking for the full list of desiderata: the range of cases it must handle—honorific monuments versus mnemonic markers versus accidental artifacts that have become symbols—the distinctions it must not collapse, such as between commemorating an achievement and commemorating a person, or between the absence of a monument and the active suppression of a record; the empirical or historical constraints it must respect, for instance that collective memory often operates differently in presence and absence; and the normative principles that must remain negotiable rather than being smuggled in as requirements, such as whether public space ought to be purely presentist or permitted to be a palimpsest. For each desideratum, I want the consideration that motivates it, and I want you to flag any pair that may be jointly unsatisfiable. If one single requirement does the most work in excluding popular positions—say, a demand that the theory apply uniformly across different kinds of commemorative objects—I want that identified explicitly. I am not asking for a scorecard of existing theories, but for the standard by which any such theory should be judged.
