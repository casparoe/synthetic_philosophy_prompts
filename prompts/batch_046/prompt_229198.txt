I have been working through Scanlon’s contractualist framework with a study partner, and we have constructed a reductio that applies the aggregation problem to cases of distributed causation—specifically, whether contractualism can consistently condemn collective actions that each individually cause no discernible harm but jointly produce catastrophic damage, without either abandoning its anti-aggregationist core or licensing obvious moral catastrophes. We are examining a scenario in which millions of agents each perform an action (say, emitting a negligible quantity of carbon, or contributing an imperceptible increment of risk through a distributed digital protocol) that is neither necessary nor sufficient for severe harm to a smaller population, yet the aggregate effect is devastating. Here is our reasoning, step by step:

(1) For Scanlon, an act is wrong only if it violates a principle P that at least one affected party could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement, and no alternative principle Q addressing the same situation could be rejected with stronger reason. (2) In distributed-causation cases, no individual agent’s action makes a difference to the outcome; the harm is overdetermined by the collective. (3) Therefore, each individual agent can reasonably reject any principle that forbids their specific action, since they bear a real cost (lost mobility, lost efficiency, lost liberty) and their omission changes nothing for the victims. (4) At the same time, the victims can reject any principle that permits the collective pattern, since they suffer severe, non-consensual harm. (5) Contractualism thus appears to face a direct contradiction: it must justify the pattern both to the agents (who reject prohibition) and to the victims (who reject permission). (6) One might resolve this by introducing collective responsibility, holding that each agent is accountable for their share and therefore must comply with a prohibitive principle. (7) But if collective responsibility is permitted, then contractualism must aggregate the small, individually rejectable burdens of millions of agents and weigh them against the large burden on the victims—precisely the kind of interpersonal aggregation that Scanlon denies in the classic rescue cases, where he insists that we cannot sum minor harms across many people to outweigh one person’s death. (8) Conversely, if contractualism refuses to aggregate and insists that justification proceeds only agent-by-agent, then it must allow each individual to continue, thereby permitting the catastrophe. (9) Hence contractualism either collapses into a form of rule-utilitarianism or entails that distributed collective harms are morally permissible.

We feel that something has slipped—perhaps an equivocation between evaluating principles ex ante versus ex post, or a hidden assumption that the agents and victims occupy fixed, non-overlapping roles in the contractualist justification procedure, or a misapplication of what “reasonable rejection” requires when a principle applies universally rather than selectively. Which step should we reject, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, we want to preserve Scanlon’s intuition that sheer numbers do not automatically override severity in direct rescue cases, but we are also committed to the view that climate inaction and analogous collective harms are genuinely wrong. Is there a consistent middle ground that avoids both utilitarian aggregation and moral paralysis, and if so, does it require modifying the contractualist framework itself?
