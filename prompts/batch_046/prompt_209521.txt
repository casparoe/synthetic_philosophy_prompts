In the literature on higher-order evidence, the debate over epistemic akrasia—whether one can rationally maintain a first-order belief while judging one's reasoning unreliable—has reached a strange equilibrium: conciliationists and steadfastists now share the same case descriptions (peer disagreement, cognitive bias, fatigue) and even the same taxonomy of defeat, yet classify every instance oppositely. I suspect the stall is not about rationality itself but about an unacknowledged ambiguity in what counts as higher-order evidence. One camp treats it as propositional evidence about reliability; the other treats it as a direct undercutter of the reasoning process that produced the first-order belief. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether rational belief-revision must be diachronically consistent—or is it partly verbal, with 'akrasia' meaning irrational persistence to one side and rational resistance to miscalibrated self-monitoring to the other? Or are they answering different questions: one about ideal epistemic agency, the other about practical cognition under uncertainty? Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says about cases like peer disagreement versus self-doubt about one's evidential framework, not by surveying authors. And tell me honestly whether any formal distinction—say, between evidence that targets a specific belief and evidence that targets the reliability function generating it—could actually move the debate, or whether we should expect permanent deadlock because the two camps are optimizing for different epistemic goods.
