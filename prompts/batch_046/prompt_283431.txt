I've been drafting a seminar paper on Turing's 1950 proposal for machine intelligence through education, and I want a consistency audit before presenting. Here is the package of commitments I've settled on:

(a) The child-machine strategy—building a system with minimal initial architecture and educating it—is the right framework for achieving intelligence; programming an adult-level mind directly is the wrong path, and Turing's structural insight here remains sound.
(b) The education of such a machine is genuinely continuous with human pedagogy: the norms of explanation, patience, and learner autonomy apply without essential change across substrates, and "teaching" does not become a different kind of activity just because the student is artificial.
(c) I reject the blank-slate empiricism embedded in Turing's notebook analogy; human cognition relies on substantial innate structural biases, and any viable learning machine must incorporate analogous nativist constraints rather than treating initial architecture as negligible.
(d) I am anti-behaviorist about these machines: through education they can acquire genuine internal representations and understanding, not merely behavioral dispositions or input-output regularities.
(e) At the same time, I hold that successful machine education necessarily requires the educator to maintain direct architectural transparency—routine access to, and modification of, the machine's internal weights and representations—in a way that is impossible and normatively impermissible with human learners; and I take this asymmetry to be constitutive of the machine-education relation, not an incidental technical limitation.
(f) I further think that if one tried to educate a machine under conditions of pedagogical opacity—granting it the same autonomy and hidden internal dynamics we grant human students—the project would fail, which is precisely why the asymmetry in (e) is indispensable.

Are these jointly tenable? In particular: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each? Is there a hidden dependency—for instance, does (e) undermine (b)'s claim of pedagogical continuity, or does (c) undermine (a)'s fidelity to Turing's framework? Does (e) create a tension with (d) that dissolves on inspection, since direct architectural access might seem to reduce internal states to engineer-visible mechanisms rather than genuine representations? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging, specify what exactly would have to give in each case, and tell me which single revision—if I had to make one—would clean up the most inconsistencies without collapsing the overall position.
