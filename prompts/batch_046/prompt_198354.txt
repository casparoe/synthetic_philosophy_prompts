At a graduate workshop, two participants—Maya and Jonas—debated whether a particular disagreement was merely verbal. The case concerned a gallery installation consisting of an unaltered industrial shipping pallet displayed with an anonymous collective attribution. One curator in their hypothetical called it art; another denied it.

Maya argued the dispute is verbal: "art" lacks any unified essence, and the curators apply different but legitimate criteria—one requiring intentional aesthetic transformation, the other requiring only institutional framing. She claimed there is no deeper fact to disagree about, only a clash of lexicons, so the disagreement should be dissolved rather than adjudicated. She added that even if both speakers intend to coordinate on a shared social practice, that intention only shows they care about the same word, not that they track a single underlying property.

Jonas replied that the disagreement remains substantive. He granted that "art" is contested, but insisted both curators are applying the same underlying institutional kind—membership in a practice governed by specific modal conditions—just disagreeing about whether the pallet satisfies them. To test this, he proposed a "modal-invariance" check: if in all nearby possible worlds with identical framing and authorial intentions the object still counts, then the dispute concerns whether those conditions are met, not about linguistic convention. Maya countered that this check smuggles in an essentialist ontology that itself needs defense; she offered a "deflationary mapping" alternative, where "art" is a family-resemblance term with no shared modal core, making Jonas's invariance condition merely one local dialect among others. Jonas then said that Maya's dissolution strategy overreaches: if applied consistently, it would misclassify obvious substantive disagreements—such as whether a skillful forgery is art—as verbal too, simply because the term has borderline cases.

An observer was asked to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: assess Maya's dissolution argument, her claim about shared practice tracking only words, Jonas's modal-invariance test, Maya's deflationary-mapping reply, and Jonas's overreach objection. Say who is right about which specific point, what both parties are missing—particularly whether any criterion can genuinely distinguish verbal from substantive disagreement without presupposing the ontology at issue—and state who is more right overall.
