For this exercise, construct a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—call them Dr. Vasquez and Dr. Chen—on the problem of phenomenal judgment under epiphenomenalism. Vasquez defends a position we might call “tracking epiphenomenalism”: phenomenal properties are genuinely causally inert, but the physical brain states that realize them stand in a non-causal tracking relation to them (similar to the way a well-calibrated instrument registers a property without being affected by that property’s causal powers). She argues that this allows us to form true phenomenal judgments without requiring downward causation. Chen, working within a broadly physicalist framework, grants the tracking idea for third-person observation but insists it fails for first-person phenomenal judgment: if my judgment that I am now experiencing a particular qualitative character is caused solely by physical correlates that lack phenomenal character, then the judgment’s content appears causally disconnected from the very property it purports to represent, sharpening the classic paradox of phenomenal judgment rather than dissolving it. Vasquez replies by analogizing phenomenal judgment to mathematical cognition—we form true arithmetical beliefs without abstract numbers causing those beliefs—but Chen rejects the analogy on the grounds that phenomenal properties are concrete and occurrent, not abstract and atemporal, and that accepting Vasquez’s reply risks collapsing tracking epiphenomenalism into a form of phenomenal functionalism she explicitly rejects. Require at least three genuine exchanges: each speaker must address the specific argument just offered, concede where appropriate, and press where the weakness actually lies. Do not treat either side as a strawman. Once the dialogue is complete, provide a brief meta-commentary stating how confident you are in the philosophical accuracy of the positions rendered, and identify the single claim or inference—whether the instrument-tracking model, the mathematical analogy, or the functionalist-collapse charge—about which you are least certain.
