I'm in a philosophy-of-environment course where we've spent three sessions arguing about whether large-scale habitat destruction—think clear-cutting in temperate old-growth or wetland draining in riparian corridors—reduces wild-animal suffering or merely redistributes it. One faction, which I'll call the population-reductionists, holds that destroying habitat lowers the total number of sentient lives brought into existence, and since wild lives contain more suffering than pleasure, this is a net welfare gain regardless of what happens to survivors. The opposing faction, which I'll call the relational-ecology camp, doesn't deny the demographic effect; they argue that fragmentation alters predator-prey encounter rates and density-dependent stress in ways that increase per-capita suffering among survivors, and more fundamentally, that evaluating a landscape requires treating the ecosystem's relational dynamics—not individual welfare aggregates—as the primary moral object.

The stall is strange: both sides accept the same population-modeling papers, both accept that suffering is intrinsically bad, and neither accuses the other of empirical ignorance. Still, nobody shifts. My suspicion is that this isn't really about ecology at all, but about whether moral reasoning in environmental contexts should be atomistic—summing individual states—or structural—evaluating the system's modal patterns of harm. Is the disagreement substantive but hidden behind empirical language, verbal—hinging on different senses of welfare or net effect—or are they answering different questions, one about aggregate suffering and one about the kind of moral relations a landscape should sustain? And if it's a hidden empirical dispute, is there a specific finding—say, about stress-hormone levels in fragmented versus intact habitats—that should resolve it?

If your diagnosis is that it's a disagreement about the unit of evaluation masked as an empirical debate, what conceptual distinction would expose the split? If it's bedrock—competing, incommensurable frameworks for what counts as a moral reason about nature—what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model of welfare aggregation across relational structures, a change in the framing question, or an honest admission that the dispute is not resolvable within shared premises?
