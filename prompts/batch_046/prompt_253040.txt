Here is the exam prompt I was given:

Consider the ethical view called tranquilism: only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, achievement, knowledge, and aesthetic experience possess at best instrumental value insofar as they reduce craving or suffering. Some formulations distinguish craving-elimination tranquilism (CET)—which treats the absence of craving as primary and takes the absence of suffering to follow necessarily—from suffering-only tranquilism (SOT)—which treats the absence of suffering as primary and regards craving as significant only as a cause of suffering. First, assess whether this distinction between CET and SOT is philosophically coherent. Then compare tranquilism (defending whichever formulation you find stronger) with negative utilitarianism and with Epicurean ataraxia, paying particular attention to whether a permanently neutral, craving-free conscious state—for instance, a meditator in deep equanimity or a hypothetical being in complete sensory deprivation without any desire—counts as maximally good despite containing no positive experience whatsoever. Finally, discuss the ethical implications for the creation of new conscious beings: under what conditions, if any, does tranquilism permit, require, or forbid bringing such beings into existence?

My answer:

Tranquilism in both its CET and SOT forms holds that only absence has intrinsic value. CET maintains that craving is the root cause of suffering, so eliminating craving automatically eliminates suffering; SOT maintains that suffering is the fundamental harm and that craving is merely one of its common sources. I think the distinction is coherent in theory but redundant in practice, because wherever there is embodied consciousness there will be both craving and suffering together.

Compared with negative utilitarianism, tranquilism is best understood as a theory of individual well-being rather than a population-level moral principle. Negative utilitarianism directs us to minimize suffering across all affected beings, including those who do not yet exist, which gives it a strong practical orientation toward preventing births that might suffer. Tranquilism, by contrast, simply describes what makes a given conscious state good or bad; it does not by itself generate rules about future populations. Thus the two views can agree that suffering is intrinsically bad without agreeing on reproductive ethics.

Epicurean ataraxia is essentially identical to tranquilism, particularly in its CET form. Epicurus defined pleasure as the absence of bodily pain and mental disturbance, which is precisely the state that tranquilism values. Epicurus also recognized kinetic pleasures—active enjoyments such as eating or conversation—but these are not genuinely positive goods; they are only temporary removals of prior craving or discomfort, and their value lies entirely in returning the soul to static tranquility. Therefore tranquilism can be seen as Epicureanism with the word "pleasure" replaced by "absence."

With respect to the hypothetical being in sensory deprivation with zero craving, tranquilism must judge this state maximally good. There is no suffering, no craving, and hence no negative value present. The complete lack of joy, knowledge, or aesthetic experience is not a deficit, because these states carry no intrinsic weight on this view. One might object that such a life is not worth living, but that objection already assumes that positive experience matters, which tranquilism denies. It is also consistent with this view to regard non-existence as equally good, since non-existence also lacks both suffering and craving, though tranquilism need not take a definitive stance on whether absence in a non-conscious substrate has value.

Finally, on the ethics of creation, tranquilism clearly permits the bringing into existence of beings guaranteed to live without craving or suffering. In fact, if absence is the only intrinsic good, then successfully creating such a being is not merely allowed but morally required, because one has introduced a locus of intrinsic value—namely, the sustained presence of absence—into the world. Biological life usually involves drives, but if we could engineer a being with no biological needs, tranquilism would obligate us to do so and to populate the world with such beings. This shows that tranquilism has a hidden pro-natalist or pro-creative dimension that critics who associate it only with antinatalism overlook.
