You are defending the following thesis in next week's philosophy-of-mathematics seminar: Thomas Hobbes's criticisms of Euclid's definitions in De Corpore—particularly his insistence that a straight line must be defined through the uniform motion that generates it rather than through a static essential property such as equidistance—are not isolated pedantries but express a coherent causal-genetic theory of geometric definition. On your reading, this theory demands that geometric existence be tied to constructibility via motion, making it structurally closer to modern mathematical constructivism (in its rejection of non-constructive existence proofs) than to the essentialist, static ontology usually attributed to seventeenth-century mechanism, and it explains why Hobbes treats certain Euclidean proofs with suspicion when they assert the existence of figures without exhibiting the generating process.

Your audience is a reading group of about twelve: mostly analytic philosophers of science with training in formal logic, plus two historians of early modern philosophy who specialize in Descartes and Spinoza but not Hobbes, and no dedicated Hobbes scholars. They are reflexively skeptical of presentist readings that recruit historical figures for contemporary debates; they prize logical precision over historical narrative; and they will press you on whether your claim survives without attributing to Hobbes an explicit, articulated philosophy of mathematics he never produced. They care about anachronism, about whether the label constructivism can be applied meaningfully before Brouwer, and about whether Hobbes's geometric arguments fail on purely internal grounds regardless of how one frames them interpretively.

Prepare a speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A. Order the objections by how likely they are to arise and how damaging they would be to your thesis, phrased in the vocabulary and tone this specific audience would actually use—not generic philosophy objections, but the ones an analytic philosopher of science or a Cartesian historian would raise. For each objection: identify who in the room is most likely to press it; state what you should concede up front without collapsing the thesis; state the reply that must land for the proposal to survive; flag the weakest point in that reply; and note whether this is an objection you should welcome rather than fear. Include the single question you are hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the gap between Hobbes's actual texts and your constructivist framing—and draft a concise, two-minute response that does not depend on recalling precise secondary citations or bibliographic references. The prep sheet should be usable by a speaker who has the primary texts at hand but no access to a library or online search during the session. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to authority you cannot reproduce from memory.
