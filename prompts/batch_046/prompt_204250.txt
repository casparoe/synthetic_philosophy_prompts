I'm a doctoral student in the history and philosophy of economics, and I've been returning to Project Cybersyn in Chile (1971–1973) not as a footnote about socialist failure but as a concrete institutional form that seems to sit awkwardly between the poles of the socialist calculation debate. The standard literature frames the dispute as Mises's claim that rational economic calculation is impossible without private property and market prices versus Lange's proposal that a central board can solve simultaneous equations and use trial-and-error pricing to approximate market outcomes, with Hayek's subsequent emphasis on dispersed, tacit knowledge that no aggregation mechanism can fully capture. Cybersyn, designed by Stafford Beer, was neither a static command economy nor a Lange-style price-iteration scheme: it used a telex-linked network of factory-level feedback loops feeding into a real-time ops room, aiming for continuous adaptive control rather than ex ante equilibrium computation. I want to treat this episode as a case study that runs in both directions—what the debate predicts about such an architecture, and what the architecture reveals about the limits of the debate.

Please analyze Cybersyn through the lens of the calculation controversy with precise mapping rather than loose analogy. Who in the Chilean economy corresponds to the planners and firms in the theoretical models, and what were their actual decision options and payoffs? Was Beer attempting a technological solution to Hayek's knowledge problem—capturing dispersed signals in real time—or was he merely replicating the information bottlenecks of central planning at a faster speed? How should we classify the system: closer to Lange's competitive market socialism, closer to Mises's nightmare of calculation without prices, or a genuinely third form? Then address where the theory breaks: did the telex network capture the kind of contextual, tacit knowledge Hayek argued was ineliminable, or did it miss precisely that? Does the fact that Cybersyn was dismantled for political reasons before it could demonstrate technical failure tell us anything about whether the economic problem was solved, or does it expose that the debate never anticipated institutional fragility and political vulnerability as independent constraints? I also want you to consider a specific angle that standard retellings neglect: Beer explicitly organized the system using his viable system model, a biological-cybernetic framework rather than an economic one. Does importing this biological metaphor change the substantive terms of the calculation debate—say, by replacing price signals with homeostatic feedback—or is it ultimately a redescription of the same planning problem? Be disciplined about saying where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and avoid treating Cybersyn's premature destruction as either a vindication or a refutation without argument.
