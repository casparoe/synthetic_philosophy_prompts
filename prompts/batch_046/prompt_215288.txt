I am delivering a seminar paper next Thursday to our philosophy of mind working group, and I want to be ready for the Q&A rather than improvising. The thesis I am defending is a deliberately narrow modification of the extended mind thesis that I am calling friction-dependent asymmetry: I argue that a cognitive artifact genuinely extends a mental process only when it preserves a specific kind of resistance or feedback friction in the loop, which means that highly optimized, predictive digital interfaces—such as the algorithmic logistics dashboards now common in automated warehouses—do not extend the worker’s cognition but rather substitute for it with what I term a pseudo-cognitive shell. To motivate the friction condition I borrow a gesture from Merleau-Ponty’s account of motor intentionality, though I do not ask the audience to buy the full phenomenology; I use it only to explain why ownership of a process requires that the agent can still feel the resistance of the tool rather than being seamlessly anticipated by it. I sketch the argument by adapting the Clark-Chalmers parity principle but adding this phenomenological constraint as a filter on when parity actually holds, and I apply it specifically to the case of human-machine collaboration in automated labor settings, which means the paper sits at the intersection of philosophy of mind, phenomenology, and philosophy of technology. The audience is precise and I know them well: four analytic philosophers of mind who are broadly functionalist but think the original extended mind thesis overreaches into metaphor; one cognitive scientist who studies distributed cognition in workplace settings and will press me on empirical testability; and one phenomenologist who is suspicious of any reduction of embodied experience to functional conditions and will watch for betrayal of the phenomenological source. They care about whether I am merely doing taxonomy, whether my friction condition is operationalizable, and whether the view has any normative or practical payoff beyond redescribing automation. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually receive from this specific mix, ordered by how damaging each is to the thesis if it lands cleanly, and phrase each objection in the vocabulary and framing that audience member would actually use—not translated into my own terms.
