Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 14 graduate students in philosophy of history or conceptual forecasting. The aim is to make participants feel, through structured choice and payoff, the problem of applying the outside view to imperial longevity — specifically, how selecting a reference class (Roman, British, Ottoman, Qing, Soviet, etc.) is not a neutral empirical step but already encodes a theoretical commitment about which structural mechanisms drive decline, and how base rates then become self-confirming. To prevent the exercise from becoming a generic discussion of induction, inject a fictional polity called Valdoria, presented to all groups through an identical quantified "imperial profile" (territorial overextension index, fiscal extraction rate, dynastic succession rule, military recruitment model, and bureaucratic corruption coefficient). The metrics are calibrated so that Valdoria maps ambiguously onto at least four real empires, with different metrics pulling in different directions.

Divide participants into four groups; each group is assigned one reference class and told it must construct an outside-view forecast for Valdoria's remaining lifespan using only base rates drawn from that class. However, they do not simply present estimates. Instead, they must allocate "confidence capital" — 100 points distributed across a hidden outcome space with four quadrants (collapse within 30 years, gradual decline over 60–120 years, structural transformation, persistent stagnation) — and they must also place a single "mechanism bet" identifying which structural feature will be the primary driver. The payoff is in seminar privileges (e.g., choosing the next week's reading, or a "veto token" over discussion framing), awarded proportionally to how closely a group's allocation matches the revealed outcome. Crucially, the revealed outcome — predetermined by the facilitator — is generated by a hidden rule that blends causal mechanisms from all four reference classes, so that every group's analog is partially correct in one dimension and systematically wrong in another, and no group can claim vindication without ignoring the dimensions where its analog failed.

The rules should require groups to defend their reference-class choice in writing before seeing others', then allow a round of cross-group trading of confidence points (simulating evidential cooperation under ambiguity), then reveal the outcome and pay out. Include: the exact materials (the Valdoria profile, point sheets, mechanism cards); the timeline minute-by-minute; the payoff scheme that requires no real money; a debrief that maps the bidding dynamics, the cross-group trading failures, and the mechanism-bet results onto (i) reference-class selection bias, (ii) the circularity of using the analog to define the relevant features, and (iii) the limits of the outside view when causal mechanisms are non-ergodic; an honest admission of which feature of real-world historical forecasting the simulation cannot reproduce (specifically, the emotional and temporal distance that makes forecasting about actual civilizations different from forecasting about a constructed profile); and a note on what will predictably fail the first time (e.g., groups anchoring on the empire they studied most recently, or treating the confidence capital as real belief rather than strategic allocation). Do not deliver any lecture before the activity begins; the conceptual problem should emerge from the payoff structure and the revealed mismatch between analog and outcome, and should only be named in the debrief.
