As a novelist working on a manuscript about bodily estrangement, I am trying to think through Kafka's The Metamorphosis with the philosophical literature on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. The clinical phenomenon—where a patient registers pain's sensory and locational features without the usual aversive emotional response—has been used to argue that pain's badness lies in its affective dimension rather than in its sensory quality. Gregor Samsa, after his transformation, continues to suffer physical damage: his father's apple festers in his back, his legs cramp, his shell cracks, yet his emotional responses are curiously displaced onto worries about his job, shame before his family, and an almost detached curiosity about his own decay. Does the novella dramatize a kind of reverse asymbolia, in which fully human affective suffering remains intact but is detached from its proper bodily cause? Where does the text resist this philosophical framing—particularly in the scenes with Grete and the boarders—by showing that Gregor's social death and his somatic decay are not cleanly separable? And if pain is bad chiefly because of its emotional valence, does Kafka's portrayal imply that Gregor's deepest suffering is existential rather than physical, or does it demonstrate that the sensory and affective dimensions are inextricably bound?
