I am trying to understand a passage from Isaac Newton’s *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687), as rendered in Andrew Motte’s 1846 English translation, in which Newton sets out the bucket experiment. The text runs as follows:

"If a vessel, hung by a long cord, is so often turned about that the cord is strongly twisted, then filled with water, and held at rest together with the water; after, by the sudden action of another force, it is whirled about in the contrary way, and while the cord is untwisting itself, the vessel continues for some time this motion; the surface of the water will at first be plain, as before the vessel began to move; but the vessel by gradually communicating its motion to the water, will make it begin sensibly to revolve, and recede by little and little, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure..."

Newton takes this concavity to show that "one can find out and measure the true and absolute circular motion of the water." I want help seeing exactly what the passage commits him to, and I am especially unsure about two alternative readings I have been considering.

First, does the argument establish that rotation is ontologically absolute—irreducible to relations with other bodies—or does it only establish that rotational motion produces a detectable physical effect (the concavity) that any adequate theory must explain, without by itself ruling out a relationist account that appeals not to nearby bodies but to the distant fixed stars as the relational frame?

Second, I have encountered a more niche reinterpretation—broadly Machian—suggesting that the concavity might be explained by the water’s relation not to absolute space or to other bodies, but to the total gravitational distribution of matter in the universe. Does Newton’s inference from concavity to "true and absolute circular motion" allow for that kind of explanation, or does his reasoning already presuppose that measurement of true motion requires a non-relational standard, thereby excluding both the distant-stars relationist reply and the gravitational-field reply by definition?

Please help me understand the logical structure of the passage: what it actually demonstrates, what it takes for granted about the conditions under which motion can be "measured," and whether these two alternative interpretations are compatible with the text or import assumptions that the passage itself does not contain.
