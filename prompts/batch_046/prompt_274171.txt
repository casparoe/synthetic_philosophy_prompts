Write the desiderata that any philosophically adequate interpretation of Kant's moral position regarding non-human animals must satisfy. The interpretation must account for the well-known tension between his explicit denial of direct duties to animals in the Groundwork and the Metaphysics of Morals and the apparently stronger language in the Lectures on Ethics, without resolving it by anachronistically attributing rights-based or sentience-centered moral status to Kant. It must preserve the centrality of rational nature as the ground of direct moral consideration, yet explain why cruelty to animals is morally wrong in a way that is not reducible to mere harm to human character or prudential self-interest. It must also accommodate Kant's claim that we have indirect duties regarding animals, specifying whether "indirect" here denotes a lack of moral standing in the recipient, a derivative obligation mediated through duties to humanity, or a structural feature of how maxims are universalized. Furthermore, the interpretation should clarify whether the duty to cultivate sympathy toward animals, mentioned in the Doctrine of Virtue, operates as an instrumental condition for fulfilling direct duties to humans or as an independent moral requirement with its own rational ground. List each desideratum with the specific textual or conceptual consideration that motivates it, flag which ones are non-negotiable if one is to remain within a recognizably Kantian framework and which allow for interpretive latitude, and identify whether any pair of desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable given Kant's commitment to autonomy as the sole source of unconditional value. Conclude by briefly noting which of the standard interpretive strategies—indirect-duty only, analogical extension of respect, or rational-nature threshold relaxation—fails on the most desiderata.
