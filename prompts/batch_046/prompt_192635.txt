I am drafting a chapter on non-human causation in historiography, specifically whether climate, pathogens, and volcanic events can be treated as “historical actors” without collapsing into environmental determinism. I have reached the following verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether they are captured by a single principle or whether some must be abandoned.

First, on the 536 AD volcanic winter and the subsequent Justinian Plague: I think both the climate shock and the pathogen should count as genuine historical forces, not mere background, because they altered strategic conditions in ways that Byzantine and Sassanid decision-makers could neither anticipate nor fully control, even though human political responses still mediated the outcomes.

Second, on the Black Death’s diffusion via climate-altered Central Asian rodent ecologies and Mongol trade networks: I hold that ecological and microbial dynamics are co-causal with human practices; attributing spread to them is appropriate, but attributing the long-term decline of European serfdom solely to the plague is determinism.

Third, on Tambora (1815) and the “Year Without a Summer” that formed part of the context for Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein: I think the eruption is properly background, not an actor; saying climate “caused” the novel would be determinism, though mentioning it as context is legitimate.

Fourth, on the Little Ice Age (c. 1300–1850) being invoked as the primary driver of European witch hunts and the Thirty Years’ War: I reject this outright as reductionist determinism; social and confessional dynamics must retain explanatory primacy.

Fifth, on the 1783 Laki eruption, Icelandic famine, and documented ripple effects on grain markets that some historians link to pre-revolutionary unrest in France: I am genuinely unsure. The causal chain is long but empirically traceable; yet calling Laki a “historical actor” in the French Revolution feels like stretching agency too far.

The tension I see is between my first and third cases—both involve volcanic climate shocks with major cultural or political consequences, yet I grant actor-status to one and deny it to the other—and between my second and fourth, where climate and biology shape large-scale social outcomes but I treat plague ecology as co-causal and the Little Ice Age as determinist overreach. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving counterfactual robustness, proximity to institutional decision points, or the presence of human mediating structures—that distinguishes legitimate non-human agency from determinism and accommodates all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and what does the best available principle predict for an unlisted case, such as whether the 1257 Samalas eruption’s global sulfate spike should be treated as an actor in the Mongol political consolidation that followed?

Please also say plainly if my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the actor/background distinction is incoherent in this domain, or if the task of finding a unified principle cannot be done well without collapsing distinctions that should be kept apart. I want direct pushback rather than polite compliance.
