I've been retired from field ecology for about five years now and lately I've fallen into philosophy courses at the local univeristy, mostly because my granddaughter said I talk too much about how nature figures things out without knowing anything. Anyway, I'm trying to organize a workshop for a small philosophy discussion group I joined — about 14 people, mostly non-academics, and we have about 75 minutes on a Tuesday evening. I'd like an activity that makes people feel the weird inversion Darwin pulled on Paley and then the paralel one Turing pulled on the question of whether machines can think: the idea that you can have genuine competence — adaptation, problem-solving, even apparent purpose — with zero comprehension going on inside. I want participants to actually run into this themselves, not just hear me lecture about it.

Here's the specific framing I'm toying with, which might be too contrived but I think it works: split the room into two teams. One team runs a blind evolutionary selection game with paper structures — they can only keep or discard based on a single measured outcome, say how far a paper glider travels, with no understanding of aerodynmics allowed in the selection rules. The other team interacts with a simple opaque program running on an old laptop that sorts shapes or solves a simple maze, but they can only observe inputs and outputs; they have to decide whether it understands the task using only behavioral criteria. After about 30 minutes both teams present their system, and then the twist: we reveal that the computer program was actually just running the same kind of cumulative selection algorithm that the paper-glider team was doing manually, just encoded differently. The point is to feel how competence gets built or recognized without anyone — not the system, not the observers — having comprehension inside. Could you design the full rules, roles, timeline, and materials for this? I especially need the debrief to map exactly what happened in each half onto Darwin's inversion of design without a designer who understands, and Turing's inversion of behavioral competence without internal mental access, plus an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce — maybe the actual historical context or the philosophical objections about intentionality that only come up in text. Also tell me what will likely go wrong the first time I run it — I have a feeling people will anthropomorphize the laptop program immediately, or the paper-gliders will take too long — and how to fix that without ruining the surprise.

Oh, and I should mention I'm not asking for a bibliography or reading list, since half these folks don't read academic philosophy and I definately don't want to pretend I'm an expert. Just make it runnable with scissors, paper, and one old laptop. I think there's something genuinely strange about these two inversions being structurally similar despite being from totally different fields, and I want the group to feel that strangeness in their hands rather than just nodding along.
