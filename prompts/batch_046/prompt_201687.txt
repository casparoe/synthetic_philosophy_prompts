Here are my verdicts on Condorcet-style aggregation, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one. A jury of 101 independent citizens, each 51% accurate on a factual question, gets it right almost certainly: the theorem applies cleanly. The same jury deliberating until they share reasoning becomes less accurate: correlation undermines it. If half are 90% accurate experts and half are 40% accurate—worse than random—pooling everyone dilutes the experts and the group does worse than polling experts alone, unless the incompetence is anti-correlated across domains so that errors cancel, in which case pooling outperforms the experts. Applying the theorem to a vote on whether a painting is beautiful feels like a category error, even if voters have aesthetic competence, because there is no fact of the matter. I also reject aggregation when jurors vote strategically to signal loyalty rather than for truth, even though independence and binary choice are technically satisfied, but I cannot tell whether this rejection smuggles in a normative condition or really violates independence. Is there one principle—about the type of question plus the structure of error correlation—that captures all five verdicts, or must I drop one? Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the precise principle or competing pair of principles; (2) the clause motivated by the anti-correlated incompetence case and its verdict there; (3) the prediction for a three-person panel, each 80% accurate but trained on identical textbooks, judging a novel anomaly outside their training; (4) an honest statement of which of my listed verdicts the principle cannot accommodate; and (5) the repaired simpler principle that saves the strategic-voting intuition and which other verdict it sacrifices instead.
