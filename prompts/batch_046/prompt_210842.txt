I have spent considerable time thinking about whether culture can be a genuine cause of large-scale historical change, initially prompted by Weber's well-known claim that the Protestant ethic helped bring about modern capitalism. In the conversations and writings I have encountered, the debate seems permanently stalled: one side treats values—thrift, discipline, a certain attitude toward work—as direct psychological drivers that explain economic behavior, while the other side treats the same patterns as fully explainable by material incentives, legal frameworks, and institutional structures, with culture serving only as decoration. I have come to suspect that the impasse is not empirical but structural. Specifically, I wonder whether both sides are using the word "values" for two entirely different phenomena—private mental dispositions on one hand, and shared public rules of coordination on the other—and that once you separate them, the claim that "culture caused capitalism" either collapses into a tautology or dissolves into a claim about institutions that no one was actually disputing. Is my diagnosis right? Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or perhaps a case of two different questions being answered under one heading? If the stall is indeed of the kind I describe, what conceptual distinction, historical example, or change of framing would actually allow the discussion to advance, rather than repeating the same circular objections?
