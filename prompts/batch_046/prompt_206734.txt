I'm giving the commencement address at my liberal-arts alma mater, where the philosophy faculty—including several epistemologists—will sit among graduates and families who have never encountered the regress problem. The dean asked me to make foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism the structural backbone of the speech: the three positions should determine what I can and cannot honestly promise the graduates about the stability of the beliefs they will carry into adulthood, not serve as decorative references. In particular, I want to resist the standard caricature that infinitism is merely a vicious-regress reductio; instead, I want to present it as a normative stance—that justification is an unending task rather than a terminus to be reached—and use that framing to explain why I will not offer the graduates a bedrock foundation for their values, nor assure them that all commitments will eventually cohere into a seamless web, but will instead invite them to continue the chain. The address should run about eight minutes, be fully intelligible to family members present, and contain enough philosophical precision that the experts in the room recognize I am not misrepresenting any view. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
