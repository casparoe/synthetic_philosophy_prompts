I'm a backend engineer with basically no formal philosophy training, but I took an intro metaphysics course last autumn and got weirdly stuck on the Humean vs anti-Humean thing about laws of nature. The usual framing—either laws are just the best summary of regularities, or they're these mysterious necessary connections—feels too tidy when I think about how we actually model "rules" in distributed systems or machine learning pipelines. So I'm pitching a one-semester philosophy-of-science term paper (around 5,000 words) and want to turn my half-baked idea into a proper proposal.

The precise question: does the Humean "best system" account implicitly depend on an assumption that "simplicity" and "strength" are computable or at least uniquely determinable for the system being described? In software and ML we constantly face underdetermination—many models fit the data, and picking the "best" one involves trade-offs that are computationally intractable or framework-relative. If that's also true for natural systems at the scale physics actually studies them (not toy examples like billiard balls but high-dimensional stochastic processes), then either Humeanism collapses into a kind of model-relativism, or it needs an extra constraint—maybe feasibility or agent-relativity—that the standard debate ignores. Meanwhile, anti-Humeanism claims laws are out there regardless of our summaries, but I want to ask whether that actually solves the problem or just relocates it: if necessary connections are real but epistemically inaccessible except through the same intractable modelling, have we gained anything metaphysically?

Why existing answers leave it open: the literature I've seen (mostly second-hand, I'll fill citations myself) tests Humeanism against intuition pumps from physics—gravity, Coulomb's law—where simplicity seems obvious. Nobody seems to ask what happens when the "system" is so complex that there is no fact of the matter about which axiomatisation is simplest, only a family of equally good approximations. That gap feels important because modern science runs on exactly those approximations.
