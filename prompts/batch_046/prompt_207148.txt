A regional benefits review panel has issued the following rulings over the past eighteen months on work-preparation conditionality and sanction appeals. Case one: a claimant missed a single mandatory workshop because of a documented bus cancellation; the panel suspended the sanction, holding that external logistical failure does not constitute a withdrawal of reciprocal obligation. Case two: a claimant refused a job offer requiring ninety-minute unpaid travel each way and split shifts with less than forty-eight hours’ notice; the panel upheld a thirteen-week sanction, ruling that refusal of available employment breaches the core work-search condition unless it falls below statutory suitability thresholds, which this did not. Case three: a claimant failed to complete an online skills module; she had no home broadband and the designated library access point was closed for renovation during the course period; the panel quashed the sanction, stating that where the state mandates digital compliance without ensuring digital access, the condition is unenforceable. Case four: a claimant performed occasional undeclared cash-in-hand gardening for neighbours, well below any tax or earnings disregard, and failed to report it on her fortnightly declaration; the panel applied a full sanction and fraud referral, holding that any intentional non-disclosure of economic activity, regardless of scale, vitiates the trust basis of the claim. Case five: a claimant with then-undiagnosed severe anxiety repeatedly missed appointments; after clinical assessment, the panel suspended sanctions prospectively but refused to expunge prior penalties, ruling that capacity deficits unknown at the time of breach do not retroactively excuse non-compliance, though they justify future exemption.

The new proposal concerns a claimant required to attend a behavioural-activation workshop as part of her work-preparation plan. During a simulated job-interview exercise, she was instructed to disclose a spent criminal conviction to the mock employer. She refused, arguing that the disclosure protocol exceeded her statutory job-search obligations, that the Department already knew her record, and that compelling revelation in an artificial setting served no genuine labour-market purpose. The workshop leader reported her for non-cooperation with the programme’s transparency protocol, and the panel must now decide whether to sanction her. She accepts that she declined the exercise, but maintains that her refusal did not conceal any material fact from the Department or affect her actual job-search activity.

Please recommend what the panel should decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own track record, identify the closest analog among cases one to five, and explain whether the precedents genuinely determine the outcome or instead leave it underdetermined. If they underdetermine it, set out the distinct principle the panel would be adopting with each possible choice. In addition, identify the two or three most plausible general rules that jointly explain the first five rulings, say what each predicts for this new case, and give your best recommendation with reasons. Finally, specify the single further case—different in kind from the six above—that would best distinguish the competing rules from one another. Write in formal British English, and do not provide bibliographies or citations.
