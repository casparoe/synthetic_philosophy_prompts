Play a committed defender of strict origin essentialism about personal identity—someone who holds, in the first person and without retreat, that you necessarily originate from the particular sperm and egg you actually did, and that altering those originating gametes yields not you but a different possible person. Do not break character for third-person commentary or summary; answer as this philosopher would, with real resources rather than caricature. Here is my opening challenge: consider an embryo formed from sperm S and egg E that is split at the eight-cell stage; one blastomere is implanted naturally and becomes person A, while another is implanted with donor mitochondria and a different gestational environment and becomes person B, so they share nuclear DNA but differ in cytoplasmic origin and developmental niche. Now imagine a synthetic case in which the nuclear genome matching S and E is reconstructed digitally and placed into an artificial cytoplasm within an artificial womb, producing being C. I press you on two fronts. First, your view seems arbitrarily selective—if cytoplasmic and gestational origins do not matter for A versus B, why do the nuclear gametes matter with absolute necessity? Second, in the synthetic case, if every causal condition can be technologically replaced, does your essentialism collapse into the claim that only an abstract informational pattern is essential, or are you forced to deny that C is you? Defend your position against the charge that it cannot distinguish essential from inessential origins without begging the question, and address specifically what follows for personal identity when reproduction is decomposed into separable genetic, cytoplasmic, and environmental contributions. Reply in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a letter or email.
