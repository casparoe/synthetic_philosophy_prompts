I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a large teaching hospital, and our committee is being asked more frequently to adjudicate cases in which a patient or surrogate claims that a religious conviction requires an action that violates standard medical ethics — for example, refusing a life-saving intervention for oneself or for a child on the grounds of a direct, private divine command. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Kierkegaard's account of Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical, that the committee can actually run within a forty-minute case conference. The procedure should treat the ethical universal — publicly justifiable duties of care — as the default framework, but include explicit, ordered steps for testing whether a particular claim genuinely represents a suspension of that universal for a higher teleological purpose, rather than a psychological disturbance, a moral preference, or a misunderstanding of religious tradition.

I am particularly interested in operationalising the distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith. The procedure should therefore ask whether the claimant demonstrates the first movement — a genuine willingness to surrender the ethical claim entirely — before any assessment of a second movement is permitted. It must specify, in sequence: the screening questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the precise point at which the procedure terminates with a recommendation to uphold the ethical universal, and the point at which it must hand the decision to a senior clinician or chaplain because the judgment involves an irreducibly private relation to the absolute. The framework should handle hard cases explicitly — a competent adult refusing blood products, a parent refusing surgery for a minor citing a revelatory command, and a case in which the procedure validates the suspension but the hospital's risk management and legal obligations still reject it. Apply the procedure to all three cases, including the one whose outcome conflicts with standard institutional policy. Mark clearly where judgment cannot be eliminated, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what it fundamentally cannot do — for instance, whether it can distinguish the knight of faith from madness, and whether it can resolve conflicts between competing teleological suspensions. Please write your response in British English.
