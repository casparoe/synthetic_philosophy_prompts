I'm working through my position on moral revolutions for a graduate seminar and want a consistency audit before I defend it. Here are the commitments I currently hold, shaped mainly by the Williams thesis and Appiah's work on honor, but with qualifications I think are necessary:

(a) I accept a strong form of the Williams thesis: practices like dueling and footbinding disappear not because moral arguments finally win rational traction, but because they cease to confer elite honor and begin to signal backwardness or low status; explicit moral reasoning is largely post-hoc legitimation.

(b) I think Atlantic chattel slavery was structurally different. Its economic integration was too deep for an honor-collapse alone to dislodge it; abolition required a genuinely novel conceptual framework—the universalist claim to human dignity—that was not implicitly available in prior moral discourse, and the causal work was done mainly by economic restructuring and geopolitical interest, with moral rhetoric playing a legitimating rather than driving role.

(c) I am a moral realist about justice: chattel slavery was always objectively wrong, regardless of whether anyone recognized it, and its wrongness does not depend on the later invention of universalist dignity-language.

(d) I hold that explaining the centuries-long persistence of a widespread moral error requires showing that holding the error was structurally rational or adaptive given agents' social and economic positions—not merely that they failed to reason correctly.

(e) I draw an explanatory asymmetry: when a practice collapses via honor-shift, its prior moral defenses should be treated as rationalizations sustained by social signaling; but when a practice is dismantled by economic or political pressure despite sustained moral defense from its practitioners, those prior defenses should be taken as genuinely held commitments that material interests overrode.

(f) I reject evolutionary debunking arguments: the fact that a moral belief serves a social-coordination function does not, by itself, undermine its claim to truth.

(g) I also think that any moral practice that was widespread, long-lasting, and experienced by participants as justified must have contained some implicit internal moral resources—however inchoate—from which a critique could eventually be developed; otherwise it was never genuinely a moral practice, just a coercive custom.

Please audit this set pairwise: identify which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to conflict, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that costs me—especially whether it forces me to abandon the Williams thesis, weaken my moral realism, or drop the asymmetry in (e). Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most problems at once. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Finally, say how confident you are in the audit and single out the point you are least sure of—most likely whether the slavery exception in (b) is genuinely principled or ad hoc.
