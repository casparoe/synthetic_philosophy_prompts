I am writing for my seminar on negative axiology and well-being, and I want to pressure-test a position I have been calling tranquilism. The view holds that the only intrinsic goods are the absence of craving—understood specifically as a restless, object-directed mental tension rather than as mere preference or desire—and the absence of suffering; all other states, including joy, knowledge, love, or achievement, possess value only instrumentally, to the extent that they suppress craving and suffering. Consequently, a being in a permanent, unconscious state of complete tranquility has maximal well-being, while a being experiencing intense joy that involves no craving is no better off than one in neutral tranquility. The position strikes me as well-insulated: critics often appeal to the intuition that positive goods matter intrinsically, but defenders can reply that this intuition tracks only the contingent reliability of those goods for sustaining absence. I want an honest map of its exposure. Specifically: which observations, arguments, or formal results should count as decisive defeaters, and which would be merely awkward? Could a version of the experience-machine objection be formulated that does not beg the question by presupposing positive goods? Might empirical work on reward circuitry and affective neuroscience—particularly if craving and positive affect turn out to be structurally inseparable—undermine the conceptual separation the view requires? Does tranquilism collapse into negative utilitarianism, and if so, is that a cost? Has the view been protected into unfalsifiability by redefining resistant intuitions or counterexamples as covert forms of craving, and is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed tranquilist should concede as genuinely damaging, and assess whether the position risks anything substantial or has been qualified into emptiness.
