I came across a summary of Daniel Dennett’s heterophenomenology in notes circulated by an unnamed philosophy-of-mind discussion group. The summary describes the method as a third-person scientific approach to consciousness in which the researcher treats a subject’s verbal reports not as direct access to inner reality, but as texts that constitute a heterophenomenological world — the world of experience exactly as it appears to the subject. The researcher adopts strict methodological neutrality: she catalogues these reports without committing to the existence or non-existence of the entities they invoke, such as raw feels or qualitative properties. The summary then claims that this neutrality is unique to heterophenomenology and distinguishes it from both introspectionist psychology and behaviorism. It further argues that Dennett developed the method specifically to dissolve the hard problem: once the heterophenomenological world is fully described, there remains no unexplained phenomenal residue, because the method reveals that first-person authority is not epistemically privileged but merely one data stream among others, and that reports describing subjective experience are best understood as narrative constructions referring to a realm that carries no ontological weight. The notes conclude that, on this view, any first-person report can in principle be translated into a third-person heterophenomenological description without loss or remainder, and that this translatability demonstrates the absence of intrinsic phenomenal properties.

I am unsure how faithful this is to Dennett’s actual position. In particular, I wonder whether the summary conflates the methodological stance of heterophenomenology with Dennett’s own eliminativist commitments about qualia; whether it misrepresents the status of first-person authority by treating methodological neutrality as an ontological denial; and whether the claim about complete translatability without remainder belongs to the method itself or is an imported philosophical thesis. I am also curious whether this characterization implicitly restricts heterophenomenology to biological subjects, or whether it could coherently be extended to synthetic systems that generate structured first-person narratives without altering the method’s core logic. Could you audit the summary carefully? Please go through it sentence by sentence, separating what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, what subtly misrepresents Dennett, and whether the conclusion that phenomenal reports have no ontological weight actually follows from heterophenomenology as a research method.
