Write a 90-minute graduate exam essay question on Schrödinger’s Vedanta-influenced argument for the unity of consciousness, constructed so it cannot be answered by summarizing his well-known metaphysical essays. The question should require students to reconstruct the inference from the denial of metaphysical pluralism about minds to the claim that individual death is conceptually illusory, then to identify the precise tension with evolutionary individuation—specifically whether natural selection can operate on distinct organisms if consciousness is genuinely singular rather than distributed across temporary “nodal” boundaries. Insist that candidates assess whether this tension is fatal to the view or can be dissolved by reinterpreting biological individuality as a practical rather than metaphysical division, and forbid them from dismissing the position as merely “mystical” or “unscientific.” Provide a model answer of roughly 600–700 words that makes the reconstruction explicit, addresses the evolutionary tension with a substantive argument rather than a label, and reaches a reasoned conclusion about the coherence of Schrödinger’s position. Include a marking scheme with five must-hit points totaling 20 marks; for each point state the marks, the specific conceptual move required for full credit, and the characteristic student error that should lose marks—such as conflating Vedantic monism with panpsychism, treating evolutionary individuation as automatically decisive without engaging Schrödinger’s distinction between phenomenal and functional boundaries, or describing the unity thesis without showing how it undermines the reality of personal mortality.
