For a 75-minute undergraduate ethics workshop with 16 students, design a structured role-play with real but small stakes—points redeemable for a coffee voucher—to make participants feel the tension between treating everyday conventions (tipping, queuing, jaywalking rules) as binding moral obligations and treating them as mere local coordination schemes that visitors can ignore without wrongdoing. Invent a fictional city, Velmor, with three internally consistent but arbitrary conventions: queue spots are tradable property, tipping is calculated via a visible social-credit display that visitors cannot read, and crossing outside crosswalks requires carrying a token available only at local shops. Divide the group into eight visitors and eight locals; visitors must complete three staged tasks (order at a café, enter a queue, cross a street) and earn points for efficiency and comfort, while locals earn points for preserving social harmony but lose points if they use explicit coercion rather than explanation. Arrange hidden incentives so visitors initially resist compliance as irrational, then experience social exclusion that feels like punishment rather than mere awkwardness, while locals discover that describing norms as just our culture fails to justify demands to outsiders. Provide a timeline (10 minutes briefing, 35 minutes simulation in rounds, 20 minutes debrief, 10 minutes mapping), rules that prevent the facilitator from resolving disputes during play, debrief questions that connect specific in-game moments—such as a visitor being denied service or a local withholding the token—to the philosophical distinction between conventional expectation and genuine obligation, and an honest note on which feature of moral conventionalism the simulation cannot reproduce without direct facilitator framing (for example, the claim that long-term socialization makes conventions genuinely normative rather than merely predictive). Specify what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it.
