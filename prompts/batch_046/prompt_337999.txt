In discussions of Gödel’s modal ontological argument, defenders often liken the proof to a geometric construction: one posits axioms about positive properties analogous to postulates defining a perfect figure, demonstrates internal consistency, and derives that the figure must be instantiated in every possible world, just as a triangle lacking three sides contradicts its own definition. A less common but increasingly used comparison treats the modal step—from possible existence to necessary existence—as analogous to a statistical-mechanical phase transition: once a critical density of positive properties is achieved in one possible world, global symmetry forces the same configuration across all worlds. Audit both analogies together: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping positive properties, modal consistency, the S5 framework, and necessary instantiation onto their geometric and thermodynamic counterparts; identify precisely where each mapping breaks, especially regarding cross-world identity, the absence of physical parameters or temperature analogs in modal logic, and whether instantiation in a world corresponds to spatial presence or to thermodynamic alignment; determine what each analogy quietly imports about actualization that Gödel’s axioms do not explicitly license; and assess whether either comparison genuinely illuminates the structural difference between Gödel’s version and Anselm’s original, or whether both obscure it by treating modal existence as if it were spatial or physical presence. Finally, state which disanalogy is fatal to the heuristic value of each comparison, and propose a more apt analogy—if one exists—that preserves the formal structure of the argument without smuggling in spurious ontology.
