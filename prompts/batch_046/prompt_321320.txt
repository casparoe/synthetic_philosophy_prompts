I am currently taking an advanced seminar on nationalism and collective memory, and for the past month we have been deadlocked over what our instructor calls the modernist-perennialist divide. One side, drawing on historians such as Hobsbawm and theorists such as Anderson, treats nations as essentially modern formations sustained by standardized education, bureaucratic coordination, and shared symbolic practices that do not require face-to-face interaction. The other side insists that many national identities possess deep ethnic and cultural roots extending back centuries, and that modernist accounts confuse the institutional packaging of nationality with its substantive cultural core. Despite repeated examination of cases ranging from France to Japan, the seminar has reached a stable impasse: no participant has substantially revised their position.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is less about historical evidence than about the criteria for diachronic identity. One camp demands administrative and symbolic continuity; the other demands continuity in mnemonic practices and ritual reproduction. But I am uncertain whether this captures the true division, or whether there is a deeper disagreement about evidentiary norms—specifically, whether gaps in the archival record should be interpreted as absence of collective identity or merely as absence of documentation.
