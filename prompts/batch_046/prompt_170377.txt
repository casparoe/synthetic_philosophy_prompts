I run a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and formal logic for students with quantitative backgrounds—several read rationalist-blog-style discussions of cognition and computation, but I do not assume they have memorized the canonical literature cover-to-cover. I am writing the final exam: one long-form essay question, 45 minutes, expecting roughly 800 to 900 words, worth 25 marks total.

The domain must be Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments—specifically Lucas's first-incompleteness move and Penrose's second-incompleteness variant. I want to avoid answers that merely rehearse the standard rebuttals (that the formal system might be inconsistent, or that humans do not actually know their own consistency). Instead, build the question around the following structural twist: grant, for the sake of argument, that a human mathematician can reliably identify the Gödel sentence of any specific, consistent formal system F she is presented with, but only from an "external" viewpoint—never when she is herself running F. Then introduce a mechanist reply that models cognition as an infinite hierarchy H₁, H₂, H₃, … where Hₙ can identify the Gödel sentence of Hₙ₋₁. Ask students to evaluate whether this hierarchy saves mechanism, whether it collapses into a vicious regress, and whether it avoids anti-mechanism only at the cost of rendering human cognition non-computable in a different sense—specifically, by sacrificing the uniform Turing-computability that mechanism originally promised.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A-range answer (22–25 marks) from a B-range answer (17–21 marks) by naming the exact conceptual moves each tier must contain. The marking scheme should require, for top marks: a correct separation of Lucas's move (truth within F exceeds provability within F) from Penrose's move (knowledge of F's consistency implies F does not capture the knower); an explanation of why granting the "external-view" capacity does not automatically defeat mechanism; an assessment of whether the hierarchical reply is a benign escalation or a vicious regress; and an evaluation of whether the hierarchy preserves or abandons the original mechanist commitment to uniform computability. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific page numbers; the prompt must be fully answerable by reasoning alone without web access.
