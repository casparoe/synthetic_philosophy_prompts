I have been in a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about whether moral responsibility can ever be ultimate, and we kept circling back to the idea that no one can be causa sui—fully the cause of oneself in a way that would ground ultimate desert. She was persuaded by Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that this generates a regress that makes true moral responsibility impossible, but I kept feeling that the argument assumes a particular image of the agent as a kind of bounded, pre-social substance whose mental properties must be self-authored if they are to count as genuinely hers. I want to turn this tension into a focused research proposal for a four-month independent philosophical project. The precise question is whether Strawson's Basic Argument depends on an implicit atomistic ontology of agency—one that individuates the agent prior to and independently of social practices—and whether relational or distributed accounts of agency, on which social roles and institutional frameworks partly constitute who the agent is and what capacities she has, can block the argument without resorting to the usual compatibilist strategies about control and identification. I will not be asking for empirical data or citation lists; what I need is a conceptual reconstruction that maps the logical structure of the Basic Argument onto different ontologies of the self.

The existing literature treats the argument primarily as a challenge to libertarian and compatibilist theories of free will, asking whether determinism or indeterminism can satisfy the demand for self-creation, but it rarely examines whether that demand itself presupposes a specific, philosophically optional picture of personal boundaries. Relational theories of agency are well developed in social ontology and feminist philosophy, yet they are seldom brought into direct contact with the free-will debate as a potential response to Strawson. My method will be conceptual analysis over four months: in the first month I will reconstruct the premises of the Basic Argument with special attention to how the agent and her properties are individuated; in the second month I will articulate two or three relational or practice-constituted models of agency; in the third month I will test whether these models can absorb or deflect the self-creation demand—perhaps by relocating it onto the social framework rather than the individual—or whether they instead collapse into compatibilism or hard determinism; in the final month I will write up the result. A successful outcome would be a clear determination that either the Basic Argument is ontologically neutral and applies regardless of how we conceive agency, or that it has a hidden internalist commitment that significantly limits its scope and opens space for an alternative response. The project fails if the reconstruction reveals no such hidden premise, or if relational agency turns out to be irrelevant to the moral-responsibility debate, or if the conceptual work proves too expansive for a single four-month cycle.

Please frame this as a proposal with a precise statement of the open question, why standard treatments leave it unresolved, the month-by-month approach, what a useful result looks like, and an honest assessment of what could make it too large or collapse entirely. Crucially, please also state explicitly what would change your mind about this framing—whether a particular interpretation of Strawson or a specific relational theory would undermine the project—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
