I have been working out a view about biography, hagiography, and moral learning from lives—roughly in the tradition of Plutarch’s paired lives, but applied to how we teach and learn from narrative today. Here is where I have landed, and I want to know whether the package hangs together.

(1) I think biography is not merely illustrative for moral philosophy but a distinct mode of ethical instruction, operating through narrative recognition and practical imagination rather than through propositional argument or case-based induction.

(2) I follow the comparative model: the proper unit of moral biography is the paired or clustered life, since virtue is shown relationally—through contrast, analogy, and divergence—rather than in isolation, so that studying a single life alone risks turning it into either hagiography or mere curiosity.

(3) I hold that moral learning requires the subject to remain recognizably human: contradictions, failures, and morally unflattering motives must stay visible and unresolved, because imitation requires a model that can be inhabited, not worshipped.

(4) At the same time, I think the most instructive subjects are precisely those in whom central virtues are genuinely entangled with the vices or excesses that made them possible—ambition that fuels justice, ruthlessness that underwrites clarity—so that any sanitized portrait is not just incomplete but psychologically false.

(5) I reject both medieval hagiography and its modern counterpart—psychological reductionism that explains away vice as trauma, conditioning, or social structure—on the grounds that both dissolve moral agency and therefore destroy the conditions for genuine imitation.

(6) The pedagogical aim, as I see it, is not to extract maxims but to expand the reader’s repertoire of practical imagination: seeing how a particular character navigated specific conflicts, with the expectation that the reader will analogize rather than replicate.

(7) Finally, I think biography serves moral philosophy only when it achieves what I call exemplary particularity: the subject must illuminate something genuinely general about virtue, vice, or practical reasoning, yet without being reduced to a mere instance of a type.

My question is whether these commitments form a consistent position. There appear to be pulls in different directions—between the comparative, relational structure in (2) and the particular, inhabitable model required in (3) and (6); between the anti-sanitation demands of (3) and (4) and the exemplary, generalizing aspiration in (7); and perhaps between my rejection of reductionism in (5) and my acceptance of deep virtue-vice entanglement in (4). Some of these tensions may be merely apparent. I want an audit, not a lecture: order the tensions by severity, specify for each what pulls against what, and identify the cheapest revision—whether modifying (2), reconceiving (6), or weakening (7)—together with what that revision costs the overall view. Do not signal which commitment I am most attached to; treat them as equally weighted.

Please also say what would change your assessment: which argument or observation about the relationship between narrative particularity and moral exemplarity would lead you to conclude that the package is more (or less) consistent than it appears, and indicate precisely where in this audit your reasoning is most vulnerable to error.
