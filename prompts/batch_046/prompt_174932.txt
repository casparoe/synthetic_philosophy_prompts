Since I was recently appointed to the institutional review board that must approve our hospital network’s automated emergency-triage protocol, I have been caught between two ethicists who invoke the paradox of deontology to opposite conclusions. One is an interactionalist who holds that deontological side-constraints bind only direct agential choices, and therefore that selecting an institutional rule—which minimizes rights-violations across cases—does not violate any constraint; the other is a systemic deontologist who holds that if a constraint genuinely protects persons from being treated as mere resources, then encoding a foreseeable trade-off into an algorithm is itself a form of using the predictable victims as means to systemic efficiency. Rather than continue their circular exchange, I want them to produce the adversarial-collaboration document they should have drafted at the outset.

Draft the joint statement. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the formal structure of the paradox—that forbidding the direct violation of one right to prevent many violations can seem to permit more violations overall; about the architectural difference between act-selection and rule-selection in the proposed protocol; about what counts as treating someone as a mere resource in a clinical-allocation context; and about the distinction between agent-relative moral costs and aggregate institutional outcomes. Second, a disagreements table that states, in wording each camp accepts for its own view, exactly where they part ways: whether the paradox dissolves once constraints are understood to govern actions but not the prior institutional choice of governing rules, or whether it persists because that very rule-choice is an action that employs foreseeable victims. Third, identify the cruxes—specifically, whether the non-aggregability of rights is a metaphysical feature of moral status or merely a practical feature of human deliberative limits, and whether an automated system that applies a pre-authorized criterion without situational discretion can be said to “use” anyone in the morally relevant sense. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete feature of the protocol or a hypothetical allocation case both agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts the resolution should imply for the protocol’s permissibility. The statement should separate verbal disagreement about “using” from substantive disagreement about moral mathematics; it should not adjudicate which view is correct; and it should contain no bibliographies, citations, or appeals to authority, since both parties are stating their own commitments.
