I have spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated, and we have reached an impasse that no summary of the standard literature seems to resolve. Our disagreement centers on a particular kind of friction: cases in which conventional implicature, conversational implicature, and the speaker's strategic manipulation of the Cooperative Principle all point in different directions, leaving it unclear what exactly belongs to speaker meaning. To move beyond repeating the labels, I want a diagnostic classification instrument — a decision tree or taxonomy designed for a graduate-level philosophy of language audience — that sorts positions by the commitments they make about such friction cases rather than by the names their defenders prefer.

The tree should begin from a single concrete diagnostic scenario and branch through a sequence of concrete commitments. The scenario is this: a speaker utters, "She is a poet and she writes in free verse," in a context where every interlocutor knows that free verse is the unremarkable default in modern poetry, so the conventional contrastive implicature usually carried by "and" is manifestly defeated by shared background; yet the speaker's delivery and the prior conversational context make it clear that she intends her audience to recognize she is deliberately flouting the Maxim of Relation, not to generate a hidden message, but precisely to block the praise-implicature that the literal content would otherwise support, thereby signaling that she regards the subject as conventional. From this starting point, the tree should branch on four questions: first, whether conventional implicature can be fully canceled by conversational context or only suppressed without disappearance; second, whether speaker meaning can include an implicature that the speaker anticipates the hearer will derive but does not herself endorse; third, whether "pragmatic presupposition" constitutes a genuinely distinct layer that survives the cancellation of both conventional and conversational mechanisms; and fourth, whether a speaker can intentionally violate a maxim specifically to prevent an implicature from arising, rather than to generate one.

Each path through these branches should terminate in a leaf representing a coherent position — for instance, strict Gricean, neo-Gricean, relevance-theoretic, contextualist, or an unnamed combination — accompanied by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. The tree should be constructed from the logical space of answers first, with the named views then mapped onto the leaves, and it should explicitly flag any pair of positions that cannot be separated by these four questions. In addition, identify any combination of answers that appears unoccupied: diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the instrument must function for someone who knows the core Gricean framework but has no access to external sources.
