I'm preparing a presentation for my philosophy of science seminar on Ian Hacking's looping effects in psychiatric classification, and I want to defend a stronger, somewhat unconventional claim that extends his framework. Most treatments of looping—using familiar illustrations like dissociative identity disorder or autism spectrum classification—focus on a first-order feedback loop: the diagnostic category alters the self-conception and behavior of the classified individuals, and these altered individuals then cause clinicians to revise the category's boundaries. I want you to construct the conceptual argument that psychiatric taxonomy is destabilized not only by this first-order loop, but by a second-order anticipatory loop in which clinicians preemptively adjust their diagnostic criteria and interview protocols in expectation of how patients will perform the diagnosis they have learned about—thereby corrupting the evidential basis of the taxonomy before any direct patient feedback occurs.

Develop this argument by distinguishing this second-order mechanism from ordinary observer-expectancy effects in clinical psychology, explaining why it is conceptually distinct from Hacking's original looping account rather than a mere amplification of it, and showing why it makes psychiatric classification structurally different from natural-kind classifications (for instance, in mineralogy or botany) where such anticipatory performance is absent. You should treat the familiar cases of dissociative identity and autism only as conceptual scaffolding, not as historical case studies requiring precise archival detail. I am presenting to an audience that knows Hacking's work well but is not composed of psychiatric historians, so build the reasoning conceptually rather than bibliographically—do not provide citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts, and avoid quoting any author verbatim. The aim is a rigorous, somewhat technical conceptual argument I can adapt for my talk, with explicit attention to the strongest objection: that this is just a redescription of well-documented demand characteristics in psychological research, and that it therefore adds nothing new to Hacking's analysis.
