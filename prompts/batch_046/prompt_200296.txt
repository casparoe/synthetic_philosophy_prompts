Two members of our forecasting and decision-analysis reading group recently argued over the voting mechanism we plan to use to select among three candidates for an upcoming collaborative research grant. The mechanism we had sketched works as follows: each voter submits a strict ranking of the three candidates (call them North, East, and South), and each voter also assigns integer conviction weights between 1 and 5 to each adjacent pair in their ranking. We then compute a modified Borda score: for each candidate, sum the weights across all voters, but if a voter’s assigned weights do not produce a strict ordering—e.g., they assign the same weight to two adjacent pairs—that voter’s ballot is excluded from the pairwise comparison for the tied pair. The candidate with the highest aggregate modified score wins; ties are broken by raw first-place vote count.

Jordan defended the mechanism as effectively strategy-proof. His reasoning had two parts. First, he said that because the weights are bounded and linear, a voter can never increase her top candidate’s score by misordering her true preferences: any lie that promotes a lower-ranked candidate necessarily subtracts weight from a higher-ranked one, and the normalization across voters means the effect is zero-sum within each ballot. Second, he argued that the tie-exclusion rule actually strengthens honesty, because attempting to manipulate the gap between second and third place risks having one’s ballot discarded entirely in the critical pairwise comparison, making strategic distortion self-defeating.

Maya disagreed on both counts. She granted that linear weighting makes naive misranking costly, but pointed out that with three outcomes the mechanism still allows a voter to influence which pair is compared in the tie-breaker. Specifically, she argued that a voter who genuinely ranks North > East > South, but anticipates that East and South are close in the aggregate, can strategically assign weights that make East and South appear tied on her ballot. This excludes her ballot from the East-South comparison, which she expects East to win narrowly; by removing herself from that comparison, she shifts the relative standing between North and East in the aggregate pairwise matrix, potentially flipping the overall winner. She concluded that the tie-exclusion rule does not eliminate manipulation—it just relocates it to the weight-assignment stage, and since the mechanism is neither dictatorial nor restricted to two outcomes, it must fall within the scope of the classic impossibility result for strategy-proof social choice.

Jordan replied that Maya’s scenario requires precise knowledge of the aggregate weights, which is unrealistic in our small group; without that knowledge, the risk of self-exclusion dominates any potential gain. He also claimed that because our mechanism resolves ties by first-place counts rather than by randomization, the hidden option of abstaining via self-exclusion is not available—voters always want their first-place preference counted.

Maya countered that strategy-proofness is a property of the rule itself, not of its expected performance under ignorance. She also noted that the first-place tie-breaker introduces a second channel for manipulation: a voter can inflate her first-place weight and deflate others precisely to influence the tie-breaker without changing the Borda ordering, which means the mechanism has more dimensions of strategic freedom than Jordan acknowledged.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each of Jordan’s two main moves and each of Maya’s responses, assess whether the reasoning holds up, noting any confusions about linearity, tie-breaking, or the conditions under which impossibility results apply. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about how conviction-weighted mechanisms actually behave with three outcomes and bounded weights.
