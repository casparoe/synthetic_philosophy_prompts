I'm a novelist fiddling with a character that's essentially a self-revising reasoning engine — think less robot, more obsessive diarist who rewrites her own rules of evidance every time she catches a bias. There's a loop I keep running into and I wonder if philosophy has already named it. She spots a flaw in her inference patterns, writes a correction, but then she has to ask: was the correction itself produced by the flawed pattern? If she applies it without trusting it, that's hollow; if she trusts it, she's using the very reliability she's trying to establish. The weirder bit is that she can prove, in principel, that a rational version of herself would accept the fix, yet she can't take that proof as a reason to accept it from her current position — it's like there's a missing rung on the ladder. This isn't quite Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and it's not ordinary scepticism either, because the blockage is specifically about endorsing one's own improvement from inside the system that's being improved. Has anyone carved out this exact shape — the gap between seeing a self-correction is valid and being able to authorise it? And where does it sit relative to Löbian obstacles with self-trust?
