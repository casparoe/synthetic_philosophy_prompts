I'm taking a graduate seminar on classical Indian philosophy, where we've been working closely with Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Candrakīrti's Prasannapadā. Over the course of the term I've developed what I take to be a defensible but somewhat unorthodox reading—call it the framework-relative interpretation of emptiness. Rather than treating śūnyatā as a global metaphysical thesis that all entities lack intrinsic nature (svabhāva) in some absolute sense, I understand it as a second-order claim about the limits of conceptual determination: for any object, the four positions of the catuṣkoṭi (that it exists, does not exist, both, neither) all fail not because reality is blank, but because each corner presupposes a fixed conceptual framework (pramāṇa) that Madhyamaka demonstrates to be context-dependent and ultimately inapplicable without contradiction. On this view, emptiness is itself framework-relative—true within the dialectical practice of reducing reification, but not a transcendent metaphysical fact standing outside all frameworks.

This reading strikes me as textually viable and philosophically attractive because it sidesteps the classic self-refutation worry: if emptiness is not asserted absolutely, then denying it does not generate an immediate contradiction within the system. It also seems to align with Candrakīrti's emphasis on the conventional (saṃvṛti) functioning of reasoning without requiring an ultimate (paramārtha) ontology that competes with it. Nevertheless, I worry that I may be importing modern philosophical machinery—perhaps something like Carnapian framework-relativity or Quinean ontological pluralism—into a text that intends something more radically destructive of conceptual proliferation (prapañca). Before I commit to this interpretation in my final paper, I want to know: what would have to be true for this framework-relative reading to fail? I am asking specifically for the kinds of evidence, textual or formal, that should move me away from it. Which passages in the Kārikā—particularly in the opening examination of conditions and in the chapters treating nirvāṇa and the Tathāgata—would decisively undermine a framework-relative reading? Are there formal features of Nāgārjuna's reductio arguments (for instance, the use of anupalabdhi or the structure of prasanga) that require emptiness to apply to itself in a non-relative, self-consuming way? And if the view does collapse into a kind of pragmatic pluralism, is that a fatal problem given Nāgārjuna's explicit rejection of philosophical positions (dṛṣṭi)?

Please assess this honestly: distinguish between evidence that would be merely awkward for my view (for example, occasional passages where Candrakīrti sounds as though he is advancing an absolute metaphysical claim) and evidence that would be genuinely fatal (for example, a demonstration that the denial of svabhāva must be global in order to do the soteriological work Nāgārjuna assigns it). State how confident you are in your assessment overall, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly whether the framework-relative interpretation can genuinely accommodate Nāgārjuna's soteriological aims without distorting the historical text.
