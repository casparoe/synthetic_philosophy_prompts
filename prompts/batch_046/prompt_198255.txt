I am a journalist covering the science of consciousness, and I have been tracking my own intuitive judgments about when a single consciousness persists across neural division. Case 1 — Complete corpus callosotomy with intact anterior commissure: The left hemisphere speaks and constructs an autobiographical narrative; the right hemisphere guides the left hand to appropriate objects shown in the left visual field, but cannot speak. Verdict: One person, but with a divided stream of conscious experience—there are two centers of awareness housed within a single narrative self.

Case 2 — Splenium-only section: Language, emotion, and executive control remain integrated; only visual-spatial information is isolated. The patient describes occasional blank spots in vision but never doubts they are a single subject. Verdict: Unambiguously one consciousness, merely with localized perceptual disconnection.

Case 3 — Progressive callosal degeneration (hypothetical ten-year trajectory): At roughly 40% remaining callosal fibers, the patient describes left-hand actions as surprising but still mine. At roughly 10% remaining fibers, with behaviorally identical dissociation patterns, the same patient now reports there are two of us taking turns in this body. Verdict: The shift from unified to dual consciousness depends less on the precise anatomical threshold than on the patient's interpretive framework and narrative integration; structural connectivity alone does not determine the verdict.

Case 4 — Bilateral prefrontal damage with intact callosum: All interhemispheric pathways are preserved, but bilateral destruction of the medial prefrontal and parietal working-memory networks eliminates global integration. The patient acts coherently in immediate tasks but reports, I am a committee with no chair; there is no one who experiences all of this. Verdict: Despite perfect structural connectivity, there is no unified consciousness—fragmentation is real.

Case 5 — Artificial inter-cortical bridge between two intact brains: Two individuals with separate bodies, histories, and autobiographical memories are linked via a high-bandwidth direct cortical interface allowing real-time sensory and affective sharing. Over months they report a merged first-person perspective—I see through both pairs of eyes—while retaining access to pre-linking memories. Verdict: This feels like one consciousness distributed across two bodies, not two consciousnesses in proximity.
