Analyze the December 1991 Belovezhskaya Pushcha agreement—where the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus declared the Soviet Union dissolved, established the CIS, and were followed within weeks by Gorbachev’s resignation, all without the presence or consent of the other twelve republics—through Margaret Gilbert’s theory of joint commitment and plural subjects. Treat the Soviet Union not merely as a legal federation but as a potential plural subject constituted by joint commitment among its constituent republics. Identify who, on Gilbert’s account, counts as party to that commitment; whether the three signatories possessed the normative standing to dissolve it unilaterally for the whole body; and what her conditions for legitimate withdrawal or mutual release imply about obligations owed to absent members. Then assess which features of the episode the theory illuminates—such as the non-reducibility of the agreement to an aggregate of separate national intentions, or the distinctive rights and obligations generated among co-committed parties—and which it obscures, especially regarding coercive power asymmetry, the absence of procedural representation, and the fact that republic-level membership was territorially assigned rather than voluntarily assumed. Finally, consider what this large-scale, involuntary dissolution reveals about the limits of applying small-group models of joint action to multinational political bodies: is it an anomaly Gilbert’s framework never anticipated, or does it expose a deeper ambiguity in her concept of plural subjecthood when membership is both compulsory and hierarchically enforced? Be disciplined about the mapping—specify who is the player, what counts as evidence of joint commitment or its termination, and where the theory is silent rather than forced to speak.
