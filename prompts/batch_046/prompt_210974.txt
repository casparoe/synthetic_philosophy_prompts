I have been trying to work out my own ethical view about space colonization, without much formal study, and I have reached a few firm positions that I want to apply consistently. First, I believe it is permissible to mine an asteroid for materials even if the mining destroys the asteroid entirely, because asteroids are abundant and have no inherent value as particular objects. Second, I believe we should avoid drilling or landing on Europa or Enceladus if there is any serious possibility of native life beneath the ice, since we might contaminate or destroy something irreplaceable before we even understand it. Third, I hold that establishing a permanent human settlement on another world is only acceptable if both the original settlers and any children born there retain a realistic, practical ability to leave and live elsewhere; trapping people in an environment they never chose strikes me as wrong, even if the parents volunteered. Fourth, I think altering dead rock or dead soil is ethically unproblematic—terraforming should be permitted on worlds where we are certain no life exists, but forbidden where an ecosystem is already present.

Now I am facing a specific proposal and I am unsure what my own view demands. A private consortium plans to hollow out a large near-Earth asteroid that has been thoroughly surveyed and shown to contain no water, no organic material, and no signs of life. They intend to construct a sealed, nuclear-powered habitat inside, populated initially by volunteers who understand that returning to Earth is unlikely. The habitat would rely on continuously genetically modified plants and microbes engineered to thrive in low gravity; over generations these organisms would become ecologically dependent on the artificial environment and unable to survive elsewhere. Any children born inside would technically be able to leave, but in practice they would lack the physical adaptations, skills, and resources to survive outside or on Earth. Furthermore, the internal restructuring of the asteroid would be permanent: if conditions changed in the distant future, or if undetected life were discovered, the asteroid could never serve as a natural environment again.

Given the four positions I have outlined, should I approve or reject this proposal if I want to remain consistent with myself? Which of my earlier judgments—asteroid mining, Europa precaution, the settlement exit condition, or the dead-world alteration rule—serves as the closest analog? If my prior commitments genuinely do not determine the answer, then what principle would I be embracing by saying yes, and what principle by saying no? And what kind of future case—perhaps involving a different celestial body or a different kind of human settlement—would actually reveal which principle I truly hold?
