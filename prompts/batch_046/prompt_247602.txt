Read Groundhog Day through Schlick's 1927 essay On the Meaning of Life, where he argues life gets meaning from play—stuff done for its own sake—not from work aimed at some further result. Phil Connors is stuck in a loop with no tomorrow, so every normal payoff collapses. At first he treats the repetition like work: he schemes to escape, then runs a seduction project on Rita, then tries to save the old man as a moral task he can check off. Only later does he take up piano and ice sculpting and start helping strangers with no expectation that the loop will finally break. Does the film actually agree with Schlick that meaning needs the absence of purpose, or does it sneak purpose back in by making Phil's transformation the hidden condition for escape—so all that "play" is really just more work? I want a reading that takes the loop seriously as Schlick's laboratory: once consequence is removed, can purpose still exist, and does Phil's final day count as pure play or as a reward he earned? There's also this weird parallel to Schlick's idea that play is childlike—Phil starts cynical and ends up almost innocent in his enjoyment. Does the movie think adulthood kills meaning, or is that too simple? I'm writting this for a philosophy of film group and want to avoid the usual redemption-arc take, so please say where the film resists Schlick and where Schlick lets us see something the standard interpretation misses.
