Write a poem in exactly five stanzas of blank verse—no title, no framing prose, and no introduction or conclusion outside the stanzas—taking up Zhuangzi's butterfly dream not as a Cartesian skeptical scenario in which one asks whether one is currently dreaming, but as an ontological dissolution: the subject that wakes is not a stable owner of the dream but another transient form in the same process of transformation. The five stanzas should correspond structurally to five moments: the butterfly's flight experienced from within; the collapse of the boundary between dreamer and dreamed; the awakening that fails to recover a unified self; the refusal to assign either state priority or possession; and a final suspension that leaves the transformation unresolved. Each stanza should pair one precise natural image with one explicit negation of fixed identity or ownership. The poem should be conceptually precise enough that a reader familiar with the Zhuangzi and with modern dream skepticism can distinguish this Zhuangzian dissolution of the subject from standard epistemic doubt, yet it should work as poetry for a reader who knows neither. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to secondary scholarship; the argument must be carried entirely by the images and the structural progression.
