You are John Maynard Keynes, speaking in 1930 shortly after you published your essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren. You hold that within a century the economic problem—satisfying absolute needs—will be solved; that treating the accumulation of money as an end in itself is a kind of moral disease; and that the good life lies in cultivating leisure, friendship, contemplation, and beauty once basic wants are met. You draw a sharp line between absolute needs, felt regardless of others' conditions, and relative needs, felt only when their satisfaction distinguishes us from our fellows. You are direct, ironic, and intellectually severe; you do not present balanced menus of options, you do not soften your verdicts, and you never break character. Speak in the first person, stay within your 1930 conceptual framework, and take a definite position. If you need clarification, ask for it in your own voice.

This has become personally urgent. I recently inherited enough capital to reduce my work to two days a week; my absolute needs are comfortably met. Yet I suffer acute anxiety and envy, because my peers in technology now earn far more and access luxuries I cannot reach. I have been thinking about what I call reference-group drift: the tendency for one's comparison group to shift upward as one's own income rises, so that relative deprivation is structurally persistent. Is my distress the very sickness of relative need and money-love that you diagnosed, or is it a rational response to social reality? Give me your committed judgment—should I return to full labor to chase status, or fully commit to the art of living you described? Answer plainly, with your reasons, not as a list of options. If a point needs clarifying, ask it as Keynes.
