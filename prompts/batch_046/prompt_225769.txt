I'm a doctoral candidate in comparative constitutional law and serve on the Interinstitutional Governance Council of a cross-border research consortium linking partners in the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union. We must recommend a single multilevel governance architecture for our shared data-protection and research-ethics oversight framework, and we have narrowed the candidates to four: U.S. cooperative federalism with judicial preemption; Germany's joint-decision model (Politikverflechtung); Swiss competitive federalism with strong cantonal exit options; and the EU's principle of conferral paired with subsidiarity review. Our specific institutional problem, however, is not ordinary decentralization. I want us to evaluate these architectures through what I call "asymmetric upward delegation"—a defined scenario in which a lower-level unit (a state, Land, canton, or member state) suffers a temporary capacity shock, voluntarily cedes specific regulatory authority to the center under an automatic sunset clause, and then resumes full autonomy once the shock passes. This is a stress-test of whether a system can absorb temporary centralization without path-dependent lock-in.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of the four architectures as the model for our consortium's charter, specify a concrete pilot time-frame with sunset provisions (for example, a two-year trial with mandatory reversion), and explain in a sentence or two why each of the other three lands lower on your ranking for this specific purpose. You must engage seriously with the strongest objection to your top choice—particularly the argument that Swiss-style competitive federalism makes upward delegation non-credible because cantons can always exit rather than delegate—and with the worry that the EU's subsidiarity review mechanism, designed to block over-centralization, might actually obstruct the kind of temporary, reversible centralization we need. If my framing confuses standard subsidiarity with emergency federalism, or if comparing these four systems on "asymmetric upward delegation" is conceptually incoherent, say so plainly rather than complying politely. Write the response in American English.
