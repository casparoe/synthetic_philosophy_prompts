Consider the problem of separating genuine moral preferences from cognitive or affective biases in normative reasoning. Suppose we distinguish, at least heuristically, between stable first-order evaluative dispositions—what an agent would endorse under conditions of full information and reflective stability—and systematic deviations from those dispositions that persist even after correction attempts. The difficulty is that any proposed baseline for correction seems to embed further preferences, and recent work in moral psychology suggests that adversarial framing—deliberately exposing agents to counter-normative arguments—can either reveal hidden biases or merely shift preferences in unpredictable directions.

If you were advising a small interdisciplinary research group—philosophers, cognitive scientists, and normative theorists—with about three years of funding to tackle open questions in this space, which lines of work should they prioritize? I am not asking for a literature survey. Rather, I want a defended agenda: identify the two or three questions that are most upstream, in the sense that resolving them would restructure how the others are approached; assess which are tractable without large-scale empirical infrastructure; and say which implications—say, for institutional moral reasoning, AI value alignment, or democratic deliberation—actually hang on the answers versus which are overrated. In particular, address whether the circularity problem in defining an unbiased baseline makes the entire distinction collapse, whether coherence-based debiasing methods, such as reflective equilibrium variants, are more likely to stabilize preferences than correct biases, and how adversarial testimony should be modeled if we take the preference-bias distinction seriously. Rank them with explicit criteria—dependency, tractability, and downstream practical weight—and say what evidence over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
