I'm a doctoral student in comparative historical sociology trying to clarify, through reflective equilibrium, when macrohistorical contrastive questions are genuinely explanatory rather than rhetorically loaded or teleologically framed. Here are my verdicts on specific formulations, and I want to know whether any coherent principle captures the pattern—or whether I must abandon some judgments.

Comparing Britain and Qing China specifically on why mechanized factory production and sustained coal-based energy transition emerged in the former by 1800, restricted to state-fiscal capacity and geological accessibility: well-posed and fruitful.

Asking "why Europe and not Asia?" treating both as unitary agents with singular trajectories: poorly posed; the units of comparison are wrong and the contrast space is underspecified.

Asking why the Scientific Revolution consolidated in Western Europe rather than in Ming China, given that both possessed sophisticated astronomical and mathematical traditions: well-posed, but only if the contrast is anchored to a specific mechanism such as patronage structures versus examination-system incentives, not to an undifferentiated "openness."

Asking "why didn't China develop capitalism?" with capitalism treated as a natural endpoint that China somehow failed to reach: not well-posed; it imports teleology and assumes the counterfactual was institutionally available.

Asking why Britain industrialized earlier than France, given comparable access to coal deposits and colonial markets: well-posed and genuinely informative.

Yet I also judge that asking why the Qing state maintained large-scale territorial integration while the Mughal Empire fragmented is well-posed, even though the institutional variables seem incommensurable with those in the Britain–China economic comparison.

So my judgments are in tension: I reject broad civilizational contrasts but accept narrow cross-imperial ones; I accept Britain–China on economic mechanisms but reject it on cultural or "values" grounds; and I accept the Qing–Mughal comparison despite its apparent incommensurability with the industrialization cases. Is there a principle—perhaps about mechanism specificity, unit comparability within a defined contrast space, or the empirical checkability of counterfactual baselines—that saves all of these? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts about a formulation I have not resolved: comparing why steam-engine adoption diffused rapidly through British manufacturing districts but slowly in Qing provinces, where the contrast is framed in terms of energy-cost thresholds and local fuel-price structures rather than in terms of institutional openness or cultural resistance. If no single principle accommodates every verdict, tell me which one to overturn and why.
