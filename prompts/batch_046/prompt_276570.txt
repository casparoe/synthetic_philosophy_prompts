For a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of moral status, I ask you to construct a diagnostic taxonomy—specifically, a branching decision tree—that sorts positions on whether moral considerability and sentience come in continuous or discrete degrees, with the range extending from paradigmatic mammalian consciousness down through insects to artificial or minimal biological regulators such as thermostats. Build the tree from substantive philosophical commitments rather than from authorial labels: each internal node should pose a concrete question about necessary conditions (for example, whether phenomenal valence is required, whether valence can exist without integrated cognitive architecture, whether homeostatic feedback loops without representational content can ground minimal welfare interests, and whether ecological or functional analogies to suffering are sufficient for moral status). The leaves must include named or clearly described positions—such as full gradualism, threshold-based binary views, biological naturalism, functionalist minimalism, and eliminativism about non-mammalian welfare—together with at least one logically coherent but unoccupied combination that you briefly diagnose. I want explicit branching on whether gradation is treated as a smooth continuum or as a set of discrete plateaus with qualitative jumps, and I want a distinct node that distinguishes a thermostat’s feedback-loop regulation from a simple reflex arc in terms of moral relevance. The instrument should be usable by an advanced student who knows no technical taxonomy: each branch must be answerable without recognizing a philosophical school’s name. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or suggested readings; the task is the logical architecture of the positions themselves. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree fails to separate, and explain whether the failure is due to logical equivalence, empirical underdetermination, or conceptual overlap.
