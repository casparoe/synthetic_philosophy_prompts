I'm presenting next Thursday's interdisciplinary seminar defending a specific, non-standard criterion for life: I argue that life is not metabolism, replication, or evolutionary potential taken separately, but what I term adaptive closure — a system qualifies as alive only when it sustains thermodynamic non-equilibrium through substrate-independent, self-correcting informational loops that are physically bounded and exhibit redundant error-checking mechanisms. This commits me to treating certain synthetic protocell arrays with redundant polymerase correction and persistent virophage-satellite consortia in hydrothermal environments as genuinely alive, while denying life-status to isolated mitochondria, to digital evolutionary algorithms running on server clusters, and to prion aggregates. The audience is precisely split: about half are philosophy-of-biology scholars steeped in autopoiesis and evolutionary functionalism who think organizational closure or replication-with-mutation is sufficient; half are synthetic biologists who regard substrate independence with deep suspicion; and one cognitive scientist who will repeatedly analogize my informational loops to large-language-model training dynamics.
