First one said: take a student paper and replace one word with a synonym, still plagiarism? Replace another, still plagiarism? Eventually every word is different and it's original. So there's no sharp cutoff where it stops being plagiarism — the boundary is genuinely vague, not just hard to find.

Second one replied: that's exactly why universities need hard rules, like a 60% similiarity index cutoff. If the line is just hidden behind our ignorance — epistemic vagueness — then without a bright-line rule we're just guessing who violated policy, and guessing is unfair.

Then the first came back with this idea she called deliberative unsettledness: for normative categories like plagiarism, the boundary isn't hidden behind ignorance. It's genuinely open because our institutional practices haven't settled the hard cases yet. So imposing a sharp hidden line is illegitimate authority; instead borderline cases should trigger policy revision, not punishment.

The second said fine for long-term reform, but it doesn't solve the case in front of us. If the concept is genuinely unsettled, then deciding this particular student violated it is still arbitrary. So she said the first student hasn't dissolved the paradox, just relocated it from metaphysics to administration.
