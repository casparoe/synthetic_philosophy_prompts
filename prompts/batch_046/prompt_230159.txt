You serve as chair of your university’s Joint Appeals and Restorative Practice Panel, a body that renders binding recommendations in disciplinary cases where mental-capacity defenses intersect with institutional obligations to harmed parties. The matter before you concerns a doctoral candidate in cognitive science who, over a six-month collaborative study, systematically withheld primary datasets from a junior lab partner. The partner’s thesis project collapsed as a direct result, followed by a prolonged psychiatric crisis requiring clinical leave. The respondent admits to the withholding but submits documentation of severe dissociative episodes during the relevant period. Critically, the independent psychological evaluation does not assert total incapacity; instead, it identifies intermittent moral-agency windows—discrete intervals in which the respondent retained sufficient rational continuity to have sought intervention or modified the conduct—together with evidence that the respondent actively concealed the condition to preempt institutional oversight. The harmed colleague demands a formal finding of moral wrongdoing, expulsion, and a public statement of fault, arguing that without such reactive institutional acknowledgment, collaborative research environments become structurally unsafe and the victim’s harm is erased. The respondent asks that all punitive sanctions be rejected in favor of purely restorative and clinical measures, contending that formal censure and institutional moral address are inappropriate whenever psychological dysfunction has compromised the capacities that render responsibility intelligible.

Your institution recently adopted an internal Reactive Attitude Protocol, a framework developed with philosophy and clinical-ethics staff that requires panels to specify whether a recommendation expresses an institutional reactive attitude—treating the agent as a party answerable to moral address, analogous to resentment or indignation—or an objective attitude, treating the agent as a clinical and managerial problem to be contained and rehabilitated without moral designation. The protocol does not dictate either outcome; it requires the panel to justify its choice with explicit reference to the agent’s actual psychological capacities at the time of action, the institutional duty to protect future collaborators, and the risk that adopting one attitude may irreversibly foreclose the other in ways that harm either the victim or the respondent. Draft the panel’s decision memo: recommend whether to uphold the expulsion and formal censure, overturn it in favor of a purely restorative resolution subject to clinical conditions, or approve a hybrid outcome with precisely defined conditions. The memo must present a clear, implementable bottom line for university administration, include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the outcome you reject, and explain how the documented intermittent moral-agency windows bear on whether reactive institutional attitudes remain warranted despite the dissociative condition. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the clinical record, the protocol’s criteria, and first principles alone.
