I'm preparing for a discussion in a jurisprudence seminar on self-reference in constitutional orders, and a fellow student sketched a line of reasoning about supreme amendment rules that I cannot fully dismantle, though its conclusion strikes me as unacceptable. The argument runs as follows. (1) In any legal system, a rule authorizing change is valid only if the system's own criteria of recognition validate it. (2) Suppose a constitutional order contains a supreme amendment rule R that applies without exception to every rule in the order, including R itself. (3) Then R authorizes a valid procedure that replaces R with R*, where R* removes all amendment authority from the system. (4) Once R* is enacted, the system no longer contains any rule that can validate R*—the original criteria of recognition have been abrogated, and no replacement criteria exist within the order. (5) Therefore R* is either invalid (because nothing internal validates it) or valid only through an extra-systemic source, which contradicts the legal-positivist claim that all law derives from recognized social sources inside the system. (6) To avoid this collapse, one might reply that R implicitly excepts itself from amendment. (7) But then R is not genuinely universal; its authority depends on a hidden higher-order constraint H that is itself unamendable. (8) It follows that either a truly universal self-amendment rule destroys its own conditions of validity, or every constitutional order already harbors an unacknowledged, unamendable meta-rule. (9) Applied to democratic constitutionalism, this means that provisions allowing amendment of the amendment clause are either incoherent or secretly constrained by an implicit core that the people never authorized.

(10) Yet democratic theory holds that popular sovereignty extends over the entire constitutional text, with no reserved, hidden reservation. I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) and (10), since they seem central to both legal positivism and democratic legitimacy. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Does the flaw lie in a hidden scope shift between what R authorizes and what it can authorize without self-undermining, or is the argument sound and we must simply pay the price of an implicit unamendable foundation?
