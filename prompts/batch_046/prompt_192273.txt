My study group built this argument extending Russell's analysis to scientific terms and now it feels suspicious because the conclusion seems to eliminate an obvious form of belief. (1) Russell treats "the F" as a quantifier phrase with no singular reference: "The F is G" asserts existence, uniqueness, and G-ness. (2) Many scientific terms—"Neptune," "phlogiston," "the luminiferous ether"—entered the language via explicit descriptions ("the body perturbing Uranus," "the substance released in combustion with negative weight," "the medium transmitting light"). (3) So on Russell's view these are not proper names but disguised definite descriptions. (4) Now take a false de re belief: Stahl believed of phlogiston that it had negative weight. (5) If "phlogiston" takes narrow scope inside the belief report, Stahl merely believed that there is exactly one combustion-substance and it is weightless—a de dicto report, not belief about a specific object. (6) To capture de re belief we need wide scope: there is exactly one combustion-substance, and Stahl believed of it that it was weightless. (7) But since no such substance exists, the existence clause fails, so the wide-scope report is false—even though Stahl clearly held that belief. (8) So Russell's analysis forces us either to deny that Stahl had de re beliefs about phlogiston (abandoning narrow scope for such reports) or to accept that correct reports of those beliefs are false (abandoning wide scope). (9) Both are unacceptable, since scientists clearly directed beliefs at specific hypothesized entities and we correctly report them. (10) Therefore extending Russell's theory to descriptively introduced scientific terms proves false de re belief about non-existent entities impossible. Where does this slip? I'd prefer to keep (4) and (7) if I can, but I want to know exactly what I pay to do so.
