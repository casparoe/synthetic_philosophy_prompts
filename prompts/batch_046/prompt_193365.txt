I'm writing a paper on sphexishness and free will for my phil acton seminar and I got obsessed with Groundhog Day. You know the sphex wasp? It stings prey, drags it to a burrow, checks inside, pulls it in—if you move the prey during the check, it just loops back and checks again, totally rigid. Philosophers use this to ask whether human action is just a fancier version of that loop, or whether real flexibility proves free will is real.

Read Groundhog Day through that lens. Phil Connors is stuck repeating Feb 2nd in Punxsutawney. At first he behaves like the wasp: he learns the pattern, exploits it mechanically, runs through a fixed script regardless of what people around him do. Then he shifts—learns piano, saves the old man, eventually falls for Rita in a way that seems to break the loop.

Take this seriously: does the film dramatise sphexishness? Which scenes show mechanical rigidity vs genuine flexibility? Is Phil's "growth" just a longer deterministic loop with more variables—like the wasp if you added a hundred extra conditions to its burrow-check—or does the film think he's achieving something the wasp can't? Here's the injection: some philosophers say true flexibility requires not just varying behaviour but actually representing counterfactual possibilities as genuinely open. Does Phil ever do that, or is he just converging on a stable attractor state where he finally satisfies the loop's hidden exit conditon?

Don't reduce the movie to an illustration. Also say where the comparison breaks down—maybe the loop is externally imposed, not an internal program like the wasp's. And finish with what Groundhog Day adds to the free-will debate that armchair argument about wasps doesn't capture. I need this for teaching prep so keep it sharp but not over-academic, and don't just list scenes without saying what they show about the idea.
