I was having a pint with my coursemate after our philosophy of mind seminar — we're both a bit obsessed with this epiphenomenalism thing, the idea that conscious experience is like smoke from a factory, it doesn't actually push anything around. We tried to formalise why it feels not just wrong but self-undermining, and ended up with an argument that looks airtight but probably proves far too much. I'd like someone to tell me which link in the chain breaks, because we're stuck.

Here it is, numbered so it's easier to follow:

(1) If phenomenal properties are causally inert, then my belief that this feeling is unpleasant is never caused by the unpleasantness itself; it's only caused by the underlying brain state.
(2) Now here's a principle we invented — let's call it the coupling condition: a reporting mechanism can only yield knowledge about a domain if there's some structural or causal coupling between the mechanism and what's being reported. Without coupling, correct reports are just lucky guesses.
(3) So under epiphenomenalism, our phenomenal-judgement mechanisms are totally decoupled from phenomenal reality.
(4) That means none of our phenomenal beliefs count as knowledge — not even the belief that we are conscious right now.
(5) But here's the twist: the belief that consciousness doesn't cause anything is itself arrived at partly through introspective refelction on our mental life. If all phenomenal judgments are unreliable, then that belief is unreliable too.
(6) So accepting epiphenomenalism removes the ground for accepting epiphenomenalism. It's self-defeating.
(7) Worse, if we keep (1) and (2), the same reasoning seems to wipe out knowledge of other people's minds, and maybe even mathematical truths, since those aren't causally coupled to our brains either. So the argument doesn't just hurt epiphenomenalism; it threatens everything.

My flatmate wants to hang on to (2) because it sounds like basic reliabilism, and she's willing to bite the bullet on other minds being mysterious. I think the problem might be in (5), that introspection about causation isn't really a phenomenal judgment in the same way, but I'm not convinced. Which premise should we actually reject? And if we drop (2), does that make introspection special in a way that looks like magic? Or is there a hidden slide between uncoupled and unreliable in step (4)? Any help sorting this out would be brilliant.
