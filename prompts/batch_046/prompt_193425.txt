I recently learned that my hometown's collective memory of the 1983 Millbrook chemical spill and the subsequent neighborhood-led remediation conflicts sharply with county environmental archives, which record the cleanup as executed almost entirely by a state-contracted firm with minimal local participation. This discovery has led me to defend, in a seminar talk next Thursday, a specific thesis about the authority of collective memory over archival record: when a community's sustained commemorative practices serve moral and social functions—especially assigning agency for harm and repair—that the institutional record was designed to suppress or erase, then collective memory should be granted epistemic priority on questions of moral significance, even if it diverges from documentary chronology on factual details. I call this divergence not error but 'mnemonic truth' versus 'chronological truth,' and I argue that treating them as answers to different questions, rather than competing answers to one, avoids both naive archivalism and uncritical memory-privileging.

The audience is a university history department's public seminar: mostly professional historians committed to archival rigor and skeptical of memory studies as a substitute for documentary verification, together with several local activists from the Millbrook neighborhood who regard the county files themselves as an instrument of bureaucratic erasure. Please prepare me for the Q&A with a prep sheet structured as follows. First, list the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by the damage they could do to my proposal, phrased first in the vocabulary a skeptical archival historian would use and then in the vocabulary the activists would use. Second, for each objection, indicate whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially concede, and provide a concise reply of roughly two minutes that I can deliver without notes. Third, identify the weakest point in my own argument—specifically the move from 'the archive was produced by interested institutions' to 'therefore mnemonic truth takes priority on moral questions'—and draft the defense I should have ready rather than hoping the gap goes unnoticed. Fourth, name the single objection I am hoping nobody raises, explain why it is damaging, and suggest how I should handle it if it comes. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument must stand on its own conceptual structure. Keep the focus on whether the distinction between chronological and mnemonic truth actually does the work I claim it does, and whether my framing unjustifiably privileges community memory in cases where the archive and the community genuinely disagree about who caused harm.
