A philosophy-of-science blogger summarized the debate over Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles and Black's counterexample as follows: "Leibniz held that no two distinct things can share all properties. Black replied with two perfect spheres floating alone in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe: they match in every intrinsic feature and in every relational feature except that each stands two meters from the other, yet they remain numerically two. Leibniz could only resist by treating space as purely relational, but since contemporary physics regards spacetime as a substantive manifold, Black's world is physically coherent and the principle fails. The rescue comes from quantum mechanics: particles of the same type are strictly indistinguishable and obey exchange symmetry, which shows that nature enforces indiscernibility at the base level; therefore Black's macroscopic spheres are merely an impossible fiction, and the principle survives as a constraint on physical reality." I encountered this summary and it strikes me as mostly correct, yet I sense some sleight of hand. Is this characterization fair? Please examine it point by point: identify what a careful defender of Leibniz would accept, where the blogger conflates relational space with the denial of all relational properties, whether modern spacetime physics genuinely rehabilitates Black's scenario or merely changes the terms of the dispute, and whether quantum exchange symmetry bears on macroscopic spheres in the way claimed. Finally, assess whether the blogger's conclusion—that quantum indistinguishability vindicates the principle against Black—follows from the premises offered, or whether it depends on an unstated assumption about the identity conditions of composite objects.
