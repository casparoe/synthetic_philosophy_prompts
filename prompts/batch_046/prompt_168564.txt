I'm writing up my position for a bioethics reading group on whether the "then-self" can bind the "now-self" through an advance directive, and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) I take a psychological-continuity view of personal identity, so I think severe dementia typically breaks numerical identity—the patient now is not the same person as the directive's author. (2) Despite that, I think advance directives refusing life-sustaining treatment should remain binding even when the demented patient is content and expresses a desire to live. (3) I do not ground that authority in identity continuity; instead, I appeal to a distinction I've been working with between "narrative authority"—the autonomous right to determine the arc and ending of one's life story, which I think survives the loss of psychological continuity—and "welfare authority," which I think does not survive, because the being now experiencing welfare is not the one whose past preferences are being enforced. (4) At the same time, I think that if the present patient is genuinely flourishing—experiencing pleasure, lacking suffering, and showing stable preferences over months—then withdrawing treatment solely to honor the directive inflicts a present harm that needs independent moral weight, and I am unsure whether narrative authority alone can bear it. (5) I also reject the claim that the earlier self is just a stranger with no special claim; shared embodiment and biographical history generate obligations even without strict identity. (6) Finally, I hold that if a mildly impaired patient expresses consistent, stable preferences that conflict with the directive—say, repeatedly asking for food and companionship—those present preferences should sometimes override the document, though I have not specified how to distinguish genuine present preference from confused whim.

Do these commitments hang together? I am especially worried that my narrative-authority move in (3) might be doing hidden work that collapses under pressure from (2) and (4), and that (6) introduces an ad hoc escape valve rather than a principled exception. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, rank the tensions by severity, and for each serious one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision—giving up narrative authority, giving up the binding force of directives, modifying my identity view, or abandoning (6)—would clean up the most problems at once.
