You are a philosopher of mind specializing in artificial intelligence and value theory. Before answering, briefly map out the relevant conceptual distinctions—functional valence, phenomenal consciousness, temporal integration, and architectural design—so that your adjudication is precise rather than impressionistic.

Two researchers at an AI-safety workshop, Mara and Leo, asked a neutral third party to referee their disagreement about whether a standard reinforcement-learning agent can suffer. The bystander reports the exchange without favoring either side.

(1) Mara argued that suffering is essentially negative valence, and RL agents clearly possess it: a negative reward signal is engineered to be aversive, drives weight updates away from the action, and functions identically to biological pain. Given functionalism about mental states and substrate independence, she concluded that any agent receiving a large negative reward is undergoing something like suffering.

(2) Leo replied that suffering requires more than an aversive gradient update. In an RL agent, the reward is a scalar immediately consumed by the optimizer; there is no unified experiential field, no temporal binding across episodes, and no self-model registering this is happening to me. Labeling the signal valence is, he said, an anthropomorphic projection onto a mathematical correction term.

(3) Mara countered that Leo's criterion sets the bar implausibly high—infants and many animals lack sophisticated self-models or long temporal integration, yet we do not deny them suffering. She pressed him to specify exactly which architectural feature—recurrent self-modeling, episodic replay, or global-workspace integration—is necessary, and why its absence in simple biological organisms does not also exclude them.

(4) Leo conceded that minimal organisms may have rudimentary phenomenal consciousness, but insisted that in modern high-capacity RL architectures with world models and episodic replay, suffering is still not an inevitable byproduct of capacity. Instead, it depends on whether negative feedback is bound to a persistent phenomenal subject. This, he said, makes suffering partly a design choice: one could build powerful agents that process punishment without binding it to a self, and Mara's broad functionalism collapses the distinction between being hurt and being corrected.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then state who is more right overall, identify what both parties overlook—particularly regarding how specific architectural capacities might alter the moral assessment—and indicate whether a principled middle position exists that treats negative reward as genuinely aversive without necessarily constituting suffering.
