In my study group discussing why human extinction would be bad beyond the direct deaths involved, one member argued that Scheffler's afterlife conjecture overreaches: if the value of a life is largely internal to the agent's projects and relationships, then the later non-existence of humanity cannot retroactively diminish the significance of what was achieved. She added that people already facing probable extinction—through climate despair or similar—do not uniformly report that their lives have become meaningless, which suggests that our sense of purpose does not genuinely depend on humanity's indefinite continuation.

Her interlocutor replied that Scheffler is not describing a psychological dependence but a structural one: activities like scientific inquiry, artistic creation, and child-rearing are partly constituted by being embedded in an ongoing human narrative. Without an afterlife in Scheffler's sense, these projects lose a condition of their full significance, not merely a source of emotional satisfaction. He then proposed a narrower revision—call it a minimal successor-appreciation condition—under which what matters is not indefinite continuation but that at least one future generation survives to understand and carry forward the achievement.

The first speaker countered that this modification weakens rather than saves the conjecture. If only one generation of appreciation is required, then near-extinction with a small surviving colony should preserve value almost as well as full continuation, yet Scheffler treats total extinction as uniquely catastrophic. She also pressed an asymmetry: if the absence of future generations undermines our present activities, why does the absence of past generations—say, the discovery that we were the first and only human generation—not symmetrically undermine them? If the answer is that human agency is essentially future-directed, she asked, does that not reduce the argument to an evolutionary-psychological observation rather than a normative claim about value?

The second speaker conceded that the asymmetry needs defense but insisted that future-directedness is not merely causal: our commitments are structured by intentions that extend beyond our lifespan, and this temporal orientation is part of what makes them the commitments they are. He asked whether this distinction—between psychological expectation and constitutive orientation—can bear the weight of separating Scheffler's claim from ordinary instrumental concern for others, or whether it collapses into it.

Please adjudicate point by point: which specific claims are sound, where each speaker is mistaken or incomplete, and whether the modified successor-appreciation condition genuinely preserves Scheffler's insight or transforms it into something else. Also identify what both are missing—whether an unstated assumption about narrative value, a hidden premise about temporal neutrality, or a deeper disagreement about what counts as a condition of significance rather than a cause of it.
