Consider the following line of reasoning, which I have constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve consequentialism into paralysis rather than merely complicating it. (1) If consequentialism is correct, then an agent ought to choose the action with the best expected outcome, calculated over all relevant causal chains extending into the future. (2) In domains of high causal complexity—climate policy, institutional design, long-run technological change—our predictive models are not merely imprecise but structurally underdetermined: small perturbations in initial conditions yield divergent macro-states, and our credible confidence intervals cover worlds that are both vastly better and vastly worse than the status quo in roughly symmetric fashion. (3) Therefore, for any significant action in these domains, the expected utility of acting is approximately equal to the expected utility of refraining, because our epistemic state assigns comparable weight to positive and negative deviations from the baseline. (4) If the expected values are genuinely indistinguishable, then consequentialism provides no rational basis for preferring action to inaction, or vice versa. (5) One might try to escape this by adopting an indirect rule—such as maximin or a precautionary principle—but selecting that rule must itself be justified on consequentialist grounds, and the choice is subject to the same cluelessness about its long-run effects on the agent's reasoning capacities and on social norms. (6) Hence even the retreat to indirect consequentialism collapses back into the same epistemic fog. (7) So consequentialism either demands that we treat all major choices as arbitrary, or it demands that we abandon consequentialist reasoning altogether; either way, it appears self-defeating. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am especially inclined to preserve step (2) and the claim that cluelessness is deep rather than superficial, but I am prepared to surrender step (3) if it is the weakest link; please explain precisely what rejecting it commits me to, and whether there is a defensible middle position that acknowledges profound predictive limits without rendering action rationally groundless.
