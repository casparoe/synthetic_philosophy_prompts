hey, I'm teaching the exclusion problem next week and I'm tired of the usual physicalism-vs-dualism framing. I want the class to draft the spec sheet first: what desiderata does any theory of mental causation have to meet if it's actually going to take the exclusion argument seriously? Give me the full list — the cases it must cover (like the overdetermination worry when brain state B and mental state M both precede action A, and the multi-realizability case where M is instantiated differently but still does causal work), the distinctions it can't collapse (causal relevance vs causal efficacy, in-principle exclusion vs in-practice redundancy), and the hard constraints it can't violate (closure of physics, but also the empirical finding that mental-state reports sometimes have predictive value in clinical settings that physical-state reports miss?). Flag which ones are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just preferences of the non-reductive camp. Crucially, include wether the theory needs to account for mental causation that operates through organizational or structural pathways that are seperate from the underlying physical token events — I think that's being ignored in the standard literature. Also say wether any cluster of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, especially since closure plus non-redundancy looks like it forces either ubiquitous overdetermination or epiphenomenalism. Don't bother with citations or reading lists; just lay out the criteria with the reasoning behind each, and briefly note how an interventionist account and a powers-based non-reductive view do against them.
