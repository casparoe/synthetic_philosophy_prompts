I am preparing an essay on the disagreement between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso regarding whether anyone genuinely deserves punishment in a retributive sense. Rather than rehearsing the standard free-will debate, I want to isolate a distinction that the literature often collapses: between "agential desert"—the claim that a culpable wrongdoer is intrinsically worthy of suffering proportional to his wrongdoing—and "expressive desert"—the claim that a community may justifiably inflict punishment primarily as a symbolic affirmation of shared normative commitments, even if the offender lacks metaphysical culpability in any robust libertarian sense. I argue that Dennett's compatibilist defense of punishment implicitly relies on the second form, not the first, and that Caruso's critique of just deserts succeeds only against agential desert, leaving expressive desert intact. I further propose that once the distinction is made explicit, Caruso's preferred public-health-quarantine model of detention actually presupposes some version of expressive justification—specifically, that the community's reactive attitudes are partly constitutive of the practice rather than merely instrumental—without which the model collapses into pure consequentialist harm-minimization.

Please comment on these notes, with particular attention to the following. First, is it conceptually accurate to read Dennett as defending punishment through expressive rather than agential desert? Second, does Caruso's rejection of backward-looking moral properties already undermine the expressive variant, or does that variant survive precisely because it makes no metaphysical claim about the offender's intrinsic worth? Third, can a coherent form of expressive desert be articulated that avoids libertarian metaphysics yet also resists reduction to forward-looking consequentialism? Fourth, where in this framing do I risk smuggling in the very intuitions—about intrinsic culpability or proportionate suffering—that both authors explicitly reject? I am asking only for conceptual analysis and internal coherence; no bibliography or textual references are needed.
