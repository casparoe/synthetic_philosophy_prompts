Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a defender of John Taurek's non-aggregating view in rescue ethics and a defender of the standard aggregating view that, in a forced-choice scenario with no other moral distinctions, saving the greater number is required or strongly favored.

Write in American English. Keep the tone formal, precise, and polished.

Structure the document as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: that each potential victim possesses equal intrinsic moral worth; that the agent faces a genuine forced choice with no hidden differences among victims; that both sides reject the claim that saving the smaller group is always wrong regardless of numbers; and that the core dispute concerns the structure of moral reasons rather than whether lives have value. Include an explicit agreement about what the disagreement is not — for instance, that it is not about denying the general desirability of saving more lives, nor about whether agents should ever weigh probabilities.

2. A disagreements table with two columns. The left column should state the non-aggregating position in wording its defender would accept; the right column should state the aggregating position in wording its defender would accept. Cover at least: whether moral reasons operate pairwise between agent and each victim rather than collectively across a group; whether equality demands symmetric treatment that blocks counting; and whether there is a duty to maximize the number of lives saved.

3. Three cruxes, framed as specific sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party. Suggested cruxes: (a) whether moral obligations can be summed across distinct persons without violating equality among them; (b) a modified case in which resources can be divided so that all five potential victims each receive partial aid yielding a 40 percent survival chance, versus giving full aid to the one yielding 100 percent survival, with expected lives saved being equal — with each side predicting whether this probabilistic division should be treated the same as the classic all-or-nothing choice; (c) whether facing the identical choice repeatedly across independent, non-interacting groups changes the moral calculus over iterations, with each side predicting whether consistency demands the same decision in every round or permits variation.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts the correct judgment should be.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific editions or publication dates. Do not invoke obscure theories, technical frameworks from specialized literature, or named scholars beyond Taurek. Where you reference particular arguments, paraphrase them without quotation marks. Ensure the residual disagreements are stated exactly, without straw-manning either camp, and identify whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive.
