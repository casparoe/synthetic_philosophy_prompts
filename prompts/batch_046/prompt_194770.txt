Hey, I've noticed a weird loop in how people talk about big life changes -- not just the usual 'you can't know till you're there', but something more specific. Someone's facing a choice that'd replace their core values (having a kid, taking a long-term psychedelic regime, joining a closed religious community, emigrating permanently). They try to evaluate it with their present values, realise those values are exactly what's going to be overwritten, so they construct this imaginary 'proxy self' -- a version of them post-change who somehow still remembers the old framework well enough to advise them. Then they ask this proxy 'would you regret it?' But the proxy has the new values, so it uses different regret-criteria, and the answer just bounces back into the present loop. I've seen people do this with kids, with psychedelics, with emigration, always building this contradictory imaginary consultant. Is there an established concept for this exact failure mode -- the circular evaluation plus the contradictory proxy-self workaround? I know L.A. Paul writes about transformative experience and epistemic transformation, but this feels narrower: it's about the particular rationality trap of using pre-change tools on a choice that destroys those tools, and the specific narrative device people invent to hide the circularity. If there's no precise term, could you name and define it, say where its edges are, and distinguish it clearly from Paul's concept and from ordinary decision-theory ignorance? Also, is the bit where people say 'nobody can advise you' but then immediately advise you part of the same pattern or a seperate failure mode? Please reply in British English, and I don't need citations or a bibliography -- just the conceptual carving. Oh, and I keep refering to this loop in my head as the 'mirror consultant problem' but I'm sure that's not standard.
