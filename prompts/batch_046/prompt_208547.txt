As a novelist constructing a philosophical narrative about the conceptual architecture of artificial intelligence governance, I require a formal adversarial collaboration document that two of my characters—representing opposing camps in a prolonged dispute—could plausibly co-author. The subject is the nature of the alignment problem: whether it is essentially one unified challenge or an irreducible cluster of distinct difficulties. The first camp, which I shall call the Specification Monists, maintains that every apparent sub-problem—robustness to distributional shift, interpretability of internal representations, and institutional control over deployment—derives from a single root failure: the inability to render human values in a form that constrains an optimizer without ambiguity, corruption by proxy rewards, or drift under pressure. The second camp, the Problem Pluralists, contends that alignment comprises at least three incommensurable problem-types with different ontologies: an epistemic problem about collective value discovery among stakeholders with incompatible preferences; a dynamical problem about constraining search processes in high-dimensional spaces regardless of how well values are specified; and a political problem about distributing veto authority and liability across organizations and jurisdictions. They hold that treating these as one problem has led the field to apply the wrong evaluative criteria—reading institutional failures as engineering bugs and treating value disagreement as noise rather than as a constitutive feature.

Please draft the joint statement these factions would produce if compelled to collaborate adversarially. Structure it precisely: first, a numbered section of agreed-upon claims about the reality of optimization dynamics, the observable failures of current systems, the existence of genuine value disagreement among affected populations, and the limitations of present evaluation methods—claims both factions can sign without reservation. Second, a clearly delineated disagreements table stating the precise points of divergence in neutral wording that each side accepts as accurate to its own view; avoid framing either position as confused or as a strawman. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving one would likely shift at least one faction’s overall assessment. I suspect the central crux is whether a formally complete solution to the specification problem (a value representation with no ambiguity or proxy vulnerability) would automatically dissolve the robustness and institutional challenges, or whether those remain genuinely independent even under perfect specification. A second likely crux concerns whether analogies to historical socio-technical failures—such as command-and-control systems in high-risk industries or risk-modeling failures in finance—should be interpreted primarily as instances of mis-specification or as irreducibly distinct failure modes involving organizational incentives and temporal pressure.

For each crux, propose a concrete conceptual case or hypothetical scenario—one that does not depend on classified empirical data, proprietary architectures, or highly technical measurements—that both sides agree bears directly on the question. Record in advance, for each scenario, what the Specification Monists would predict about its outcome or interpretation, and what the Problem Pluralists would predict, so that the disagreement is made empirically tractable in principle.
