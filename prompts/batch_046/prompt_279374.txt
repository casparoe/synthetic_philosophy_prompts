Hey — I'm working on a feature about capital punishment in modern liberal states and I keep running into this instituional contradiction that I can't quite name. The official justification is usually retributive: the crime was so horrific that death is deserved, regardless of who the offender is now. But then you have clemency boards and last-minute appeals that spend hours debating whether the prisoner has shown remorse, made art, or "found God" — as if personal transformation occured in a way that actually changes whether the state should kill them. Meanwhile, Camus argued in his writings on execution that the practice presupposes a kind of metaphysical certainty about human nature that we just don't have. So what philosophical commitments does this split actually presuppose? Does the machinery of mercy smuggle in a view of moral identity — that people can become genuinely different — that the retributive rationale officially rejects? Or can a retributivist consistently say "desert is fixed, but mercy is discretionary"? And is Camus's critique hitting something deeper, like the state claiming an infallible knowledge of who the prisoner "really" is?
