One of my grad students emailed me this chain of reasoning and now I'm second-guessing it, but I can't tell if it's genuinely broken or just uncomfortable. (1) If we're genuinely agnostic about insect sentience, we should assign each individual a small credence—say 0.0005. (2) With about 10^19 insects alive at any moment, that gives an expected 5 × 10^15 sentient insects, dwarfing the eight billion humans we're near-certain about. (3) Moral weight ought to track expected capacity for suffering, not just high-confidence cases. (4) So common practices—driving, using pesticides, even walking on grass—create orders of magnitude more expected harm than failing to donate to distant human famine relief. (5) But nobody thinks I should spend my life avoiding ant hills instead of giving to global health, so something has to give. (6) The student wants to hold onto (3) and (5), and is willing to drop (1) or (2), but doesn't want to just reject the arithmetic. Where's the hidden slide? Is it that sentience probabilities aren't independant across individuals—if consciousness evolved phylogeneticaly, then it's one big unknown about the clade, not 10^19 separate coin flips? Or is there a mistake in treating expected moral weight as linearly additive across beings? I want to know exactly which step to reject and what it costs me if I keep (3).
