I am a novelist by trade and, by a strange appointment, a voting member of my university museum's Joint Committee on Curatorial Integrity and Public Access. We face a concrete decision that I think turns on whether moral compromise between incommensurable value systems can ever yield genuine gains rather than mere corruption. A foreign state archive has offered our museum an unprecedented ten-year loan of suppressed works from the 1970s—paintings, samizdat manuscripts, and photographic negatives never shown outside their country of origin. The conditions are precise: we must display them without attributing authorship to any living dissident artists currently in exile, labeling them only as anonymous national artisans, and we must include in every catalog and wall text a framing statement—drafted by the archive—that presents the works as expressions of unified cultural heritage untainted by political division.

Some committee members treat this as normative arbitrage: our institution values truthful attribution and historical transparency, the archive values narrative control and national unity, and because our internal exchange rates for these commitments differ radically, a structured trade could leave both parties with a net surplus—access for us, legitimacy for them—without requiring either side to abandon its core framework. Others see it as institutional self-degradation: once we accept false attribution and sponsored framing, the access we gain is already contaminated, and we have not traded values but surrendered the conditions that make our values meaningful.

I need a formal written opinion, not more than two pages, to circulate before Thursday's vote. Recommend approval, approval with binding conditions, or rejection, with reasoning that takes the arbitrage argument seriously rather than reducing it to corruption, and that gives the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight—whether that is the argument that compromise erodes the institutional conditions that make access valuable, or that refusal buries historical evidence out of a purity that serves no actual victim. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the document must stand on its own for a generalist board. You may, however, introduce one precise conceptual distinction—devised for this occasion—to help the committee see the structural choice clearly, provided it does not depend on specialized literature.
