I'm preparing a position paper for a democratic theory seminar and want a consistency audit before I present. I hold that the boundary of the demos should be fixed by an "all-affected-interests" principle, but I understand that principle in a specifically diachronic way: the demos is a single deliberative agent extending through time, so policies shaping conditions in 2100 affect the interests of present members of that agent—future generations as temporal parts—rather than merely external stakeholders. From this I derive that climate and environmental policy must include representational trustees for those future temporal parts. At the same time, I insist that democratic standing requires reciprocal justification: any included party must be able to comprehend reasons and respond with reasons of its own, which is why I reject purely coercion-based accounts (temporary visitors and diplomatic staff are subjected to law but lack the enduring stake and reciprocal capacity that justify membership). I also maintain that long-term non-citizen residents who pay taxes and build lives here should have local voting rights, and—crucially—that the boundary should be issue-invariant: the same set of people constitutes the demos whether we are deciding education funding or carbon budgets, rather than redrawing the constituency per policy domain. Finally, I want to extend this framework to sentient non-human animals affected by ecological policy, but only through human proxy advocates who can engage in reciprocal justification on their behalf, not by granting direct standing to the animals themselves.

The tensions I can already sense, but cannot rank, are these. First, the diachronic-agent view pulls future generations inside the boundary, yet they cannot satisfy the reciprocal-justification requirement directly. Second, issue-invariance demands one stable demos, while a rigorous application of all-affected-interests seems to yield different affected groups for education versus climate policy. Third, my rejection of coercion-based membership sits uneasily with my inclusion of tax-paying residents, since taxation is itself a form of coercive fiscal subjection. There may also be a hidden tension in allowing human proxies for animals: if proxies can stand in for beings lacking reciprocal capacity, why not deploy proxies for future generations and thereby dissolve the first tension—except that doing so might collapse the distinction I want to maintain between direct and representative standing. I am unsure which of these are genuine contradictions, which only appear so, and which revision is least damaging. Could you audit these commitments, rank the tensions by severity, specify for each exactly what would have to give, and indicate whether resolving one automatically resolves another? I would strongly prefer to retain both the diachronic-agent framing and the issue-invariance condition if they can be defended together, but I want to know the precise philosophical cost of keeping both.
