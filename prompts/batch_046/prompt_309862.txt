A philosopher friend summarized the doing/allowing distinction for me in these terms: "The distinction tracks structural causation rather than agentive intent. If your bodily movement initiates a novel causal pathway—pulling a trigger, pushing someone—then you've killed; if you merely leave an existing pathway intact—turning away from a drowning person, withholding a drug—then you've only allowed death. Foot's analysis of abortion and Rachels's comparison of active and passive euthanasia both conflate this structural feature with questions of culpability or rights. Once you separate them, the trolley cases come apart cleanly: flipping the switch is actually allowing (you redirect an existing threat without introducing your body into the chain), while pushing the fat man onto the tracks is killing (your body becomes a novel causal barrier). So the switch/bridge asymmetry is real, but defenders explain it wrong: it's physics and pathway-structure, not double effect or rights violations." How fair is this summary? Go sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a defender of the distinction—say, someone working in the Foot/Rachels tradition—would accept, which they'd correct, and where the summary quietly substitutes its own framework (structural causation) for theirs. In particular, is it accurate to say Foot and Rachels "conflate" structural causation with culpability, or do they deliberately reject structural causation as the morally relevant factor? And does the claim that flipping the switch is "allowing" survive the standard objection that the switch-flipper initiates a new trajectory?
