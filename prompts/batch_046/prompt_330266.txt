I am peer-reviewing an essay for my philosophy of mind seminar. The assignment was: "Analyze whether fully rational decision-making is possible for experiences that are both epistemically and personally transformative—such as becoming a parent for the first time or undergoing a profound religious conversion—given that the chooser lacks first-person access to the outcome and that the outcome may alter the chooser's core preferences. In your answer, clearly distinguish epistemic transformation from personal transformation; address whether testimony can substitute for direct phenomenal knowledge in the decision calculus; and consider whether the challenge applies symmetrically to reasoning about one's own future self and reasoning about other people."

Below is my classmate's response. Please assign a specific letter grade (and, if appropriate, a percentage range) and provide a point-by-point assessment that justifies it. I need you to evaluate each substantive claim individually: indicate what is accurate, what contains a subtle error—such as a conflation between two distinctions, an unsupported inferential step, a misattributed philosophical position, or an overlooked asymmetry—and what important counterexample or qualification is missing. Do not offer a broad general impression; the deliverable should be a step-by-step commentary on the reasoning. Student answer:

Transformative experiences fall into two categories. Epistemic transformation occurs when you acquire knowledge you could not have possessed beforehand, like knowing what durian tastes like if you have never tried it. Personal transformation occurs when your fundamental values and preferences shift, as often happens when someone becomes a parent. The core problem for rational choice is that decision theory requires comparing expected utilities calculated from a stable preference set. In a personally transformative case, the preferences of the pre-choice self and the post-choice self diverge, so no single utility function governs both stages. Meanwhile, epistemic transformation means the chooser lacks the phenomenal concepts required to mentally represent the experience prior to undergoing it. Without such representations, Bayesian decision theory cannot be applied, because that framework requires mental representations of outcomes in order to assign probabilities and subjective values.

Some philosophers respond that testimony can bridge this epistemic gap. A chooser can learn propositional facts about others' experiences and use those descriptions as proxies for direct phenomenal knowledge. But this confuses knowing-that with knowing-what-it-is-like. Testimony transmits only propositional content, so the chooser still lacks the subjective data needed for an authentic first-person utility assignment. Therefore, the only rational path is to adopt a testimonial model that abandons first-person expected utility reasoning altogether in favor of indirect, description-based evaluation.

Still, the argument that rational choice is impossible here proves too much. If lacking phenomenal concepts blocked decision-making, then we could never rationally choose a career, a partner, or a city we had not already inhabited. Since we clearly do make such choices rationally, we should reject the premise that mental representation of outcomes is necessary for rationality. Instead, rationality in transformative contexts operates with partial information and relies on hypothetical reasoning about one's future self in exactly the same way we reason about other minds—through analogical projection rather than direct simulation. Thus the challenge dissolves once we recognize that self-directed reasoning and other-directed reasoning are structurally identical.
