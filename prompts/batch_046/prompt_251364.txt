Since attending a seminar on historical methodology earlier this semester, I have been unable to shake a particular pattern I keep noticing in counterfactual arguments about large-scale events. A historian or commentator begins with something massive—the dissolution of a state, the outbreak of a prolonged conflict, or the failure of a diplomatic settlement—and then traces it backward through a sequence of ever-smaller links: a storm that delayed a courier, a mistranslation of one clause in a draft, an official’s absence from a meeting due to a sudden illness. Each link is presented as genuinely causal and individually plausible. The overall effect, however, is a kind of linear fragility chain laid across what is clearly a dense, interwoven field of causes. The speaker then concludes that the macro-event was radically contingent, because breaking any single small link would have diverted history. When someone objects that this is merely selecting one path through an infinite tapestry after knowing the result, the reply usually treats the objection as a denial that small causes matter, rather than as a critique of the narrative form itself.

I have seen this structure in discussions of everything from the origins of particular wars to the collapse of specific regimes, and I am convinced there is a precise conceptual distinction to be drawn here. The phenomenon is not the general idea that history is sensitive to initial conditions, because that belongs to dynamical theory and does not depend on retrospective linear reconstruction. Nor is it ordinary post-hoc fallacious reasoning, since each intermediate step may be well supported. It is also not the standard debate between structural and agent-centered explanations, because both camps can construct these chains. What I am after is whether there is an established name—perhaps in philosophy of history, narrative theory, or the study of counterfactuals—for this specific argumentative architecture: the construction of a sequential, fragile pathway through a non-linear causal web, deployed to demonstrate extreme contingency, together with the corresponding critique that such pathways are artifacts of retrospective selection. If no established term exists, I would welcome a careful coinage that captures both the move and its mirror-image objection, along with clear boundary conditions that separate it from chaos-theoretic sensitivity on one side and from plain narrative smoothing on the other.
