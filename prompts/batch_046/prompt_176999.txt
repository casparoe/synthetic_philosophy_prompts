I'm designing the take-home final for an upper-level philosophy of mind and action seminar. The course covered mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will through Hofstadter's discussion of the wasp Sphex ichneumoneus. I need one essay question that students cannot answer by merely summarizing the wasp's loop and labeling it "mechanical." To force deeper analysis, frame the question around the following distinction, which I want treated as part of the problem: "first-order sphexishness" is rigid repetition of a behavioral sequence; "second-order sphexishness" is rigid repetition of the very procedures one uses to revise one's own procedures—such as always resolving moral conflicts by deferring to the same authority, without being able to represent or question the deferral rule itself. Ask students whether a human agent who has broken free of first-order behavioral loops but is locked in second-order sphexishness should count as possessing free will, and whether this demonstrates that flexibility alone is insufficient for freedom. The question should require them to engage with hierarchical compatibilist theories of agency and to specify exactly where the sphex analogy applies and where it collapses.

Please provide:
(1) The essay question itself, clearly worded and suitable for a 1200-word take-home answer (25 marks).
(2) A model answer of about 600 words that demonstrates the required depth.
(3) A marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific conceptual moves an A must include—for example, correctly mapping first-order and second-order loops onto hierarchical desire structures, identifying the point at which the analogy to the wasp breaks, and arguing whether loop-transparency is necessary for freedom—and by naming the specific errors that should cost marks, such as treating sphexishness as purely behavioral without addressing second-order revision, or conflating the ability to break loops with the ability to represent one's own revision rules.

The prompt should not require web access: avoid asking for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure texts. You may treat Hofstadter's sphex example as standard course material, but do not hinge evaluation on niche scholarship, specific page numbers, or verbatim quotations.
