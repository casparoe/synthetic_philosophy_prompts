I'm drafting a philosophical novel set in a crumbling archive, and I've reconstructed a debate between two historians—Maren and Elias—that I want to render with intellectual honesty rather than picking a winner for dramatic convenience. They are arguing about whether the canonical cyclical theories of history—Polybius's constitutional anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's dynamics of group solidarity and luxury, Spengler's morphology of cultures, Toynbee's rhythms of civilizational challenge and response—are genuinely cyclical or are merely linear narratives of decline wearing circular clothing. Maren opened by observing that in every case the so-called cycle presupposes an initial high-energy state—tribal cohesion, cultural vitality, creative minority—that is never regenerated from within: Polybius moves through monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, and ochlocracy only to return to monarchy after catastrophic collapse; Khaldun describes solidarity dissipating into urban luxury and tax extraction, with fresh dynasties imported from outside rather than renewed internally; Spengler treats cultures as organisms with fixed life-spans; and Toynbee ultimately frames civilizations as progressing toward universal states and then disintegration. She argued that this is not true recurrence but a parabola with a hidden arrow of time—energy is imported, metabolized, and depleted—so cyclical history is really linear history multiplied by institutional amnesia.

Elias pushed back, insisting that Maren was conflating morphological analogy with sociological mechanism. He granted that Spengler's biological metaphor is linear within a single culture, but he argued that Khaldun's model is not biological senescence: a dynasty does not die of old age but transforms its mode of cohesion from kin-based solidarity to bureaucratic extraction. The decline is therefore a metabolic change in how power is organized, not a departure from an original vitality. He also pointed out that Polybius's return to monarchy after ochlocracy is not a degraded copy but a fresh constitutional form, with no cumulative, irreversible loss being tracked across cycles. Maren then sharpened her attack by framing the system in terms of entropy: since fresh tribal groups must supply the solidarity that urban luxury destroys, the overall pattern requires an external linear injection of vitality, which means the cycle depends on a running-down clock it pretends to deny. Elias replied that this entropy framing is itself a metaphysical addition—Khaldun describes solidarity as a social relation actively produced or not produced under specific conditions of settlement and taxation, not as a conserved energy stock—so Maren was treating historical explanation like physics rather than recognizing pattern recognition across institutional configurations.
