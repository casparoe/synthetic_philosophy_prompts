I am preparing remarks for a seminar on distributive justice and want a consistency audit of my position before I present it. I have settled on a hybrid view that combines elements of prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, and some deontological side-constraints, but I am unsure whether the pieces actually cohere or whether I am implicitly helping myself to contradictory motivations. Here is the profile I intend to defend: (1) I accept prioritarianism with a continuous, convex weighting function—welfare benefits to the worse-off count for more, and the weight declines smoothly without sharp thresholds as welfare improves; (2) I reject egalitarianism entirely, including relational egalitarianism—equality has no intrinsic value, and any appearance of egalitarian concern is actually a proxy for avoiding domination or arbitrary hierarchy; (3) I think the classic leveling-down objection succeeds only if one smuggles in an egalitarian premise, because on pure prioritarian grounds destroying goods that cannot be redistributed (for instance, blinding a sighted person to match the blind) is never justified merely to reduce disparity; (4) nevertheless, I hold an “indivisibility and status-quo” constraint—when a good is already distributed and cannot be shared without being destroyed, we must not level downward, but when a good is yet to be allocated and is genuinely indivisible, we should assign it to the worse-off party even if the welfare gain is trivial and the loss to the better-off party is severe; (5) I accept a person-affecting restriction in intergenerational and population ethics—an outcome is not better unless it is better for at least one actual or possible person; (6) I also maintain that prioritarianism should be combined with a sufficientarian floor—below a certain welfare level, priority weights become lexically dominant, but above that floor the standard continuous convex weighting applies.

My specific worries are these. First, (1) and (6) seem to pull in opposite directions: a lexical floor introduces a discontinuity that contradicts the smooth convex function I endorsed in (1). Second, (2) and (4) may conflict—if equality has no intrinsic value, it is unclear why the indivisibility of a good should matter at all; yet my verdict in (4) looks suspiciously like it is tracking equality of opportunity or equality of access rather than pure priority. Third, I am unsure whether (3) and (5) actually hang together: my rejection of leveling-down in (3) relies partly on the intuition that no one is made better off by the destruction, which sounds like a person-affecting intuition, but that same intuition might undermine prioritarian trade-offs in which we harm one person severely to benefit another only slightly, provided the beneficiary is sufficiently worse off. There may also be an apparent tension between (4) and (5) that dissolves once I clarify what “better for someone” means in allocation cases versus destruction cases, but I am not sure.

Please audit these commitments pair by pair. Rank the tensions by severity, indicating for each whether it is a genuine contradiction, an apparent contradiction that dissolves on closer inspection, or a tension that requires a substantial theoretical revision rather than a minor clarification. For each genuine tension, specify the cheapest revision—whether abandoning the convex function, weakening the sufficientarian floor, dropping the indivisibility constraint, or qualifying the person-affecting restriction—and say exactly what that revision costs me elsewhere in the profile. Finally, tell me which single revision, if I am forced to make only one, would clean up the largest number of inconsistencies across the board, and whether that revision leaves the remaining commitments recognizably prioritarian or pushes me into a different theoretical camp altogether. Be precise about which commitments commit me to what, rather than giving a generic lecture on prioritarianism versus egalitarianism.
