In debates about causation by omission, a familiar analogy compares an omission to a missing link in a chain-link fence: the enclosure fails not because a link was severed, but because one was never placed, yet the breach is just as real and the failure just as traceable to that absence. Proponents use it to argue that omissions are genuine causes with the same metaphysical standing as positive acts — that failing to close a gate, withhold an antidote, or maintain a bridge can "produce" an outcome in the same way that pushing, poisoning, or demolishing can. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what corresponds to the fence, the missing link, the installer, the intruder, the breach, and — crucially — the causal mechanism by which the absence operates. Second, map the disanalogies: where does the "missing link" picture break down with respect to causal production versus counterfactual dependence, the problem of selecting one omission from an infinite background of non-doings, and whether the analogy silently imports a normative or design context (someone was supposed to install the link) that is absent in many pure omissions? Separate the disanalogies that actually undermine the metaphysical claim from the ones that are merely decorative. Third, and most importantly, take a committed position: does the analogy survive once these breaks are on the table, or was it carrying the conclusion that omissions are full-blooded causes all along? If the fence analogy collapses under scrutiny, suggest what a more accurate analogy would be — or argue that no analogy can do the work and omissions must be treated differently.
