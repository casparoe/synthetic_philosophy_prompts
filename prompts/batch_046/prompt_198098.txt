I've been trying to work out whether a nation can still be guilty for wrongs committed generations before anyone now alive was born—not in a legal sense, but in a deeper moral sense that the wrong hasn't really been settled and that people today remain somehow inside it. I understand Karl Jaspers, writing after the war, drew lines between criminal guilt, political guilt, moral guilt, and a more diffuse metaphysical guilt that comes from shared human existence. His framework seems to treat guilt as attaching to specific persons at specific moments, which makes it hard to see how it stretches across generations.

So I'm drafting an essay that proposes a different layer, which I call "residual structure." The idea is not that descendants inherit blame like a debt, but that the social, economic, and institutional patterns created by past crimes are still operating, and that living within them without actively dismantling them amounts to sustaining the same moral architecture. The guilt isn't passed downward; it's maintained like a climate. The central claim is that collective guilt across generations should not be framed as "Are we guilty for what they did?" but rather as "Are we still inside what they made, acting as custodians of an unrepaired structure?"

I'd welcome comments on whether this distinction between inherited blame and sustained structural implication actually holds up, or whether it collapses into ordinary political responsibility or institutional duty. I'm also unsure whether Jaspers's metaphysical guilt already captures what I'm trying to describe, and whether "residual structure" is just a confused version of a concept that already exists in political philosophy. In particular, I'd like suggestions on how to sharpen the argument against the obvious objection that individuals today didn't choose these structures and therefore cannot be guilty for them—only responsible for fixing them. Where does my framing add something, and where does it fall apart?
