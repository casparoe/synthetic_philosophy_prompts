I spent the better part of last evening in a long and rather circular argument with a close friend about whether Buddhist ethics—particularly in its Theravada and Mahayana expressions—can be coherently interpreted as a form of consequentialism without distorting its core commitments. We kept returning to the same tension: if karma operates primarily through the mental state of intention (cetanā), then outcomes seem morally incidental; yet if the bodhisattva’s vow to liberate all sentient beings is taken seriously, some form of aggregated welfare calculation appears unavoidable. We never managed to map the logical space of hybrid positions, especially those that invoke merit-transfer (pariṇāmanā) or skilful means (upāya) without collapsing into either pure intentionalism or full-blown utilitarianism.

Could you construct a taxonomy, structured as a decision tree, that sorts the possible stances on this compatibility question? The root should ask whether the moral weight of an action derives solely from the agent’s intention rather than from the actual suffering or wellbeing produced. From there, branch according to whether karma is taken to be strictly individual and non-transferable, or whether doctrines such as merit-transfer and the universal bodhisattva vow permit some collective welfare aggregation. A further split should concern whether ahiṃsā functions as an absolute constraint or as a defeasible rule that might be overridden by upāya aimed at reducing total suffering across beings. At each node, phrase the question so that someone could answer it without already knowing which interpretive label it points towards.

The leaves should include at least the following, placed according to logical branches rather than listed: pure Theravada intentionalism that denies any consequentialist reading; a Mahayana ‘welfare-bodhisattva’ view treating the reduction of universal suffering as the supreme criterion; an ‘individual-karmic consequentialist’ hybrid that aggregates outcomes yet denies merit-transfer; and an unnamed combination where karma is collective yet ahiṃsā remains absolute regardless of scale. For every leaf, provide the name (or ‘unnamed’) and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—for instance, that it must treat self-sacrifice for strangers as either obligatory or conceptually incoherent, or that it cannot explain why a bodhisattva defers entry into nirvāṇa. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied branch: is the combination unstable, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?
