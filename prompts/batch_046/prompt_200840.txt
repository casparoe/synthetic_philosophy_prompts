I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Advance Directives and the Boundaries of Self-Determination," co-hosted by a medical school and a philosophy department. The room is deliberately mixed: palliative-care clinicians and clinical ethicists who draft and interpret these documents daily; philosophers of personal identity, practical reason, and action theory; and family members who have actually served as healthcare proxies for parents with advanced dementia. I need a ten-minute address that does real philosophical work rather than merely warming up the room, and the central concept should shape what I can and cannot say about comfort, coercion, and the obligations of surrogates.

The framing I want is that an advance directive is not simply a forward-looking extension of autonomy, but a puzzling case of cross-temporal authority: one temporal stage of a person legislating for a later stage that may no longer remember, value, or even psychologically resemble the earlier one. Rather than relying on the familiar opposition between "precedent autonomy" and present best interests, I want the speech to turn on a sharper distinction I think the literature tends to gloss over—authority grounded in psychological continuity (the earlier self rules because it remains, in a meaningful sense, the same person) versus authority grounded in what we might call narrative authorship (the earlier self retains a sovereign editorial right over the arc of the life regardless of whether the later self endorses those values or retains any continuity of memory or character). The address should help the clinicians see why honoring an old directive can feel like fidelity to a story rather than fidelity to a patient, and it should help the philosophers see why that distinction matters in the concrete moment when a feeding tube or a refusal of antibiotics is being weighed. It must avoid any citation-heavy framing or bibliographic review; it should not offer false consolation or resolve the tension neatly, but should show why both forms of authority pull in genuinely different directions and why a family member's refusal to follow a directive can be simultaneously an act of love and a kind of philosophical betrayal.
