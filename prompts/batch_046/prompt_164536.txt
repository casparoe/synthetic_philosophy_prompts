I spent thirty years running a lab in analytical chemistry before I retired, and now I sit on our university's honors and ethics commitee mostly because I like arguing with people who've actually read the philosophy texts I only started picking up last year. We've got a vote Thursday about renaming our annual Distinguished Service Fellowship after a local activist who donated something like ninety percent of her earnings to homeless outreach and lived on almost nothing until she died young. One faction wants to rewrite the fellowship criteria so nominees have to show 'maximal moral commitment without personal reserve' — basically making the award a prize for being a moral saint. The other faction thinks that's a mistake, not just because it's unrealistic, but because extreme self-sacrifice might actually be undesirable: it could crowd out joy, creativity, or stable relationships, and if everyone tried to live that way society might not function.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. I want a clear recommendation — either adopt the new criteria for next year's cycle, reject them outright and keep the old ones, or approve the rename but attach conditions — together with a time frame for implementation. The reasoning needs to take the pro-saint argument seriously: they say moral exemplars inspire us and that partial goodness is just complacency. But it also needs to honestly present the strongest consideration on the losing side, which I think involves whether moral goodness has a kind of saturation point, like a chemical solution that can only hold so much solute before adding more damages the mixture. In other words, is there an asymptotic limit where further self-sacrifice stops producing value and starts eroding other goods? Don't cite specific philosophers or ask me to look up papers; I need arguments I can defend in the room without a bibliography. Keep the tone formal enough to circulate but readable, and don't worry if there's a typo or two — I think I misspelled committee in my own notes.
