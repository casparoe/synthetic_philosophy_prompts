Some democratic states maintain formal institutions—memorial boards, state-funded reparations offices, and mandatory school curricula—that treat the present government as the same continuous legal entity that committed past atrocities, making current taxpayers fund restitution and asking citizens to observe duties of remembrance. At the same time, these legal systems reject criminal or civil liability for anyone born after the events, and citizenship is never conditioned on accepting personal guilt. Recently, several of these boards have begun using corporate-continuity tests borrowed from business law—examining whether the present state retains the same constitutional framework, territory, and legal personality—to calculate an institutional identity score that determines how large present reparations obligations should be. I am trying to understand what philosophical commitments about collective guilt this arrangement presupposes. Does it depend on Jaspers' distinction between political guilt, which attaches to the state and its citizens collectively, and moral or metaphysical guilt, which remains strictly individual? Or does applying corporate-law identity tests imply a different metaphysics, one in which the nation is a legal person capable of carrying guilt independently of its members? Can the practice be made consistent: funding remembrance and restitution through general taxation while denying that present taxpayers are personally culpable, and grading institutional identity with tests designed for companies rather than moral communities? And if we replaced this framework with the view that nations are not continuous moral agents across generations, which parts of the institution—state apologies, reparations funded by present budgets, memorial education—would have to be redesigned, and which might survive?
