Here is an argument I have been turning over about when a legend becomes history, particularly with respect to the Trojan War, the Arthurian court, and the Exodus.

(1) An event should count as historical rather than legendary only if it meets the hermeneutic isolation condition: there must be corroborating traces—archaeological, administrative, or material—that can be identified and interpreted as bearing on that specific event without relying on the narrative tradition that reports it.

(2) In the case of the Trojan War, no archaeological layer at the site traditionally associated with Troy independently confirms a coalition-led siege of the kind described in the epic cycle; in the case of Arthur, the British record of the relevant centuries offers no administrative or material proof of a unified royal court matching the later romances; and in the case of the Exodus, neither Egyptian documentation nor wilderness archaeology yields traces readable without presupposing the biblical narrative.

(3) Hence all three belong properly to legend.

(4) Yet apply the same condition to events we do not doubt, such as the assassination of Julius Caesar or the sealing of the Magna Carta: the physical traces—architectural remains, coins, a parchment—only become evidence for those specific occurrences when arranged by the very narrative sources we are testing.

(5) For nearly all pre-modern events, testimony and narrative are inseparable from the identification of traces, so the hermeneutic isolation condition is satisfied by almost nothing in ancient or medieval history.

(6) But we do not classify Caesar's death or the Magna Carta as legend.

(7) Therefore the condition in (1) proves too much.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (3) and accept that much conventional history is also legendary, though that seems to destroy the distinction I wanted to defend. Which premise should be rejected, and at what cost? Is there a weaker version of (1) that separates the three cases from ordinary history without collapsing into the absurd result in (5)?
