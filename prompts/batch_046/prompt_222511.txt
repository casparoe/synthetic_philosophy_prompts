Here is a line of reasoning I have been testing with colleagues, and I find it suspicious because it seems to erase a distinction I rely on constantly in engineering practice. (1) In system design, we distinguish a crash—where the core process terminates abruptly—from a gradual deprecation in which functions migrate to new dependencies. Only the first counts as a failure of the system itself; the second is continuity through replacement. (2) Applied to Rome: the Western imperial administration did not terminate in a single event. Tax collection, legal authority, and military organization migrated gradually to Germanic kingdoms, ecclesiastical structures, and local aristocracies over several centuries, with no timestamp at which all core functions simultaneously ceased. (3) Therefore Rome did not fall; it was deprecated, and its institutional functions were ported to successor architectures. (4) If “fall” requires an abrupt termination of core functions, and Rome exhibits only migration, then no institution that undergoes gradual functional replacement can properly be said to have fallen. (5) But we routinely classify modern corporations, codebases, and political movements as having “failed” even when their assets, personnel, and operational logic were fully absorbed by successors—there was no crash, only a slow loss of autonomy and purpose. (6) So if we deny that Rome fell on the grounds of functional continuity, consistency demands that we deny institutional failure altogether for every gradually replaced system. (7) That makes “failure” depend solely on the speed of replacement rather than on any substantive normative or structural property, which collapses the concept into uselessness. (8) Hence the transformation view proves too much: it either forces us to concede that Rome did fall after all (contradicting (3)), or to abandon institutional failure as a meaningful category.

I would prefer to keep (1) through (3)—the legacy-system analogy feels right, and the historical record looks more like a long migration than a single crash—but I want to understand exactly what I am paying if I reject the inference to (7). Where does this argument slip? Is there an equivocation between “fall” as a discrete historical event and “failure” as an evaluative judgment, a scope shift in how continuity applies, or a hidden premise about what counts as the “same” system? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for someone who thinks about institutions the way an engineer thinks about deprecated monoliths?
