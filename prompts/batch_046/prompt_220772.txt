I'm writing up my philosophical profile for our graduate seminar on epistemology and speech acts, and I want an internal consistency check before I present it to the group. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) I endorse the knowledge norm of flat-out assertion—if one asserts p in ordinary conversational contexts, one should know p, and lacking knowledge is a genuine epistemic failing rather than merely a practical risk. (2) At the same time, I think there is a distinct institutional practice—scenario-assertion, used in central-bank stress-testing and climate-risk forecasting—where speakers issue predictive claims governed by a justified-confidence norm rather than knowledge, because the domain is structurally uncertain. (3) I hold that these scenario-assertions are not a separate speech act with different accountability conditions; they carry the same illocutionary force and expose the speaker to the same kind of institutional blame if reckless, only the epistemic threshold is calibrated differently. (4) I reject both the belief-norm and the truth-norm as independent competitors: talk of asserting on belief or truth alone is, on my view, just describing successes or failures of meeting the knowledge norm. (5) I also accept that rational belief in lottery propositions—for instance, that my specific ticket will lose—is possible without knowledge and can justify practical action; but I maintain it never justifies flat-out assertion because assertion involves a warrant-transmission condition that lottery belief fails to satisfy. (6) Finally, in scientific peer review, I think a reviewer should assert that a study's method is flawed only if they know it is flawed, applying the standard knowledge norm without exception.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe is each real tension? For each genuine conflict, identify the cheapest revision available to me and what it costs conceptually. Then tell me which single change to my view would clean up the most problems overall, and whether there is a hidden commitment—perhaps in how I understand warrant transmission or institutional blame—that is doing unstated work. I am unsure whether the core problem lies between (2) and (3), between (4) and (2), or elsewhere, so please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and be specific about which commitment has to give in each case.
