I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I need a reusable editorial audit for evaluating policy and research arguments that may violate the separateness of persons—the principle, associated with Rawls, that social reasoning should not treat distinct individuals as interchangeable vessels of welfare whose gains and losses can be merged without regard to who bears each. Please construct a step-by-step decision procedure my desk can run on any argument: the ordered questions, the threshold that counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the explicit stop points where the audit returns no separateness violation or clear violation, the precise juncture where judgment must take over because the issue is normative rather than factual, and the common misfires (for instance, conflating statistical aggregation with welfare merging). Apply the procedure to three cases: a progressive-tax proposal that funds mental-health subsidies by appealing to a net increase in total societal well-being; a clinical-trial protocol that knowingly assigns some participants to a harmful placebo because the aggregate lives saved elsewhere outweigh the burden; and a synthetic-biology program that cultivates temporary, non-sentient human tissue clusters for organ harvesting, where the clusters lack narrative continuity and are treated as disposable welfare containers. For the third case, produce the verdict the procedure actually delivers—which I expect will conflict with my own skepticism about strict personal-identity conditions—and state what the audit cannot catch, such as hidden aggregation in probabilistic risk reasoning.
