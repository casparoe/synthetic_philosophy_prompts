I am currently studying philosophy of medicine, and we have been examining advance directives not simply as legal instruments but as exercises in diachronic authority—whether an earlier self can genuinely bind a later one, and precisely when that authority dissolves. I have arrived at a cluster of intuitive verdicts about concrete cases, yet I am unsure whether they cohere around a single principle or whether I must abandon consistency for plausibility. I should like you to propose one or more precise principles that capture the pattern, to indicate which clause is motivated by which case, to predict what each principle says about a further scenario I have not listed, and to state honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Here are my judgements. First, if a patient executes a formal, witnessed directive refusing life-prolonging treatment should she enter a persistent vegetative state, and years later she is indeed in that state with no detectable consciousness, I believe the directive binds us and withdrawal is correct; with no present subjectivity, there is no competing authority to override it. Second, if that same patient had concealed an informal journal expressing terror of being “switched off” but never amended the legal document, I still think the formal directive prevails; the hidden text lacks the procedural standing to overturn a properly executed choice. Third, if someone with fully intact cognition writes an ascetic directive refusing all analgesia for future terminal illness, then later—still competent—begs for morphine, I think the present request revokes the directive; intact cognition combined with present suffering breaks the earlier authority. Fourth, during a manic episode, a patient with bipolar disorder refuses mood-stabilising medication that her directive explicitly mandates; despite her evident euphoria, I think we ought to administer it, because the manic state undermines the normative standing required for revocation. Fifth, an elderly patient with moderate dementia spends her days painting with visible contentment, yet her directive refuses antibiotics for pneumonia should she become severely demented; even though she now smiles at canvases, I believe the directive should be honoured and treatment withheld, since her present joy does not restore the reflective autonomy needed to overturn an earlier, competent decision. These five feel right to me, but I keep returning to the tension between the dementia-painting case and the cancer-morphine case: in one I uphold the directive despite present affective life, in the other I overturn it despite formal authority. Is the difference merely the presence or absence of cognitive capacity, or is there a deeper structural distinction—perhaps between interpreting the present self as a genuine diachronic successor rather than as a cognitive fragment?

Please could you articulate the principle or small family of principles that best capture these verdicts, stating for each clause the case that motivates it, and then apply them to a scenario I have not considered: a patient’s authority derives not from a legal form but from a recorded “values history” interview conducted with a clinical ethicist, and she now, with moderate dementia, consistently articulates preferences that flatly contradict that transcript. Tell me also, with complete candour, which of my five verdicts (or the implicit comparison between the painting and morphine cases) each principle forces me to drop. In your response, please distinguish clearly between what is broadly settled in bioethics and philosophy of personal identity and what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as though it were the consensus. Please write in British English.
