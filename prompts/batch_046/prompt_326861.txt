Having spent three decades working on feedback stabilization and reference tracking in aerospace control systems, I have recently begun reading the philosophical literature on artificial intelligence alignment. There I encounter a distinction between “intent alignment,” in which a system executes precisely the command vector issued by its operator, and “value alignment,” in which the system conforms to broader human ethical norms that may override particular instructions. From my engineering vantage this looks like the difference between pure set-point tracking and tracking constrained by a Lyapunov stability region: one fixes the trajectory to an external reference, the other penalizes divergence from an acceptable state-space basin. Consequently, value alignment appears to be nothing more than intent alignment evaluated with an additional constraint manifold, and the so-called “narrow” alignment problem—solving intent alignment in isolation—seems to be the very same formal framework run with the constraint weight set to zero. Yet researchers present these as conceptually rival categories rather than as parameterizations of one control architecture. Is this analogy fundamentally misleading? Specifically, is there a qualitative, structural distinction between aligning to an operator’s command and aligning to an ethical boundary condition that cannot be reduced to “fewer constraints,” or is the debate over narrow versus broad alignment largely a disciplinary branding of what is essentially a quantitative tuning choice? Please indicate how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the specific claim about which you are least certain—particularly whether the control-theoretic framing genuinely collapses the two categories or merely obscures a logical separation that the analogy misses.
