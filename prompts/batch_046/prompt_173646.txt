I've been working through some popular explanations of quantum mechanics with a small reading group, and we've reached an impasse over the many-worlds interpretation. Specifically, none of us can see how probabilities make sense if every measurement outcome actually occurs in some branch—yet experiments clearly follow the Born rule. In trying to resolve this for myself, I've encountered two conceptual strategies that seem quite different. The first, which I understand is sometimes called the decision-theoretic approach, treats the quantum state as defining a collection of branches with weights given by the squared amplitudes, and then argues that a rational agent who knows the state should act as if those weights are probabilities. The second, which I think involves self-locating uncertainty, suggests that an observer doesn't know which branch they are about to experience, so the probabilities reflect genuine first-person ignorance rather than just rational betting behavior. Could you compare these two approaches in detail? I am particularly interested in whether the decision-theoretic account actually explains why the weights feel like probabilities, or merely prescribes behavior without grounding the feeling, and whether the self-locating account genuinely escapes this problem. There is also a specific worry I haven't seen addressed in the popular material: if the branching structure produces infinitely many copies of the same outcome, then counting branches becomes impossible, and I wonder how each account handles that infinity without collapsing back into a simple frequency interpretation. Please explain the comparison clearly enough that someone without formal physics training could follow the logic, and don't include bibliographies or citations—just the conceptual structure and where each view stands or falls.
