I am a third-year undergraduate in philosophy of science, and I have been asked to facilitate a ninety-minute study session for a mixed cohort of philosophy and physics students who have just encountered the Quine-Duhem thesis but have not yet examined the stronger claim that empirical evidence can systematically underdetermine theoretical choice. My audience arrives with three predictable confusions: they conflate underdetermination with the theory-ladenness of observation, they assume that empirically equivalent theories must be notational variants of one another, and they treat any failure of decisive experiment as a merely temporary engineering limitation rather than as a structural feature of scientific testing. I need a detailed pedagogical plan—not the lecture itself—that specifies the dependency order in which the conceptual components must be introduced and why, the single carrying example that should do the explanatory work, the misconception map with precise defusion points, a check for understanding that distinguishes genuine grasp from mere discomfort, and the explicit omissions.

For the carrying example, I want to use the observational equivalence between a standard dark-matter halo model and a Milgromian modified-gravity account applied to the same galactic rotation-curve data, but only if you judge that this framing avoids being either technically opaque for philosophy students or too easily dismissed as a fringe hypothesis. If that pairing is ill-suited, propose an alternative that still forces students to see that the competing accounts lack shared ontological commitments and are not inter-translatable, yet remain observationally indistinguishable given present instrumentation limits. The plan should state exactly which features the example must possess and which it must lack—particularly with respect to auxiliary hypotheses—because the students will otherwise blame the data rather than the theoretical structure, so the role of those auxiliaries must be secured before the example is introduced.

Finally, lay out the sequence of misconceptions—carefully distinguishing underdetermination from incommensurability, from the pessimistic meta-induction, and from conventionalist strategies for breaking theoretical ties—indicating at which step each should be anticipated and neutralized. Include a check for understanding that reveals whether a student has grasped the structural point rather than merely expressing pluralist discomfort, and state clearly what should be left out at this level, such as the formal logical apparatus of empirical equivalence relations or recent formal-epistemological treatments of belief revision under evidential ambiguity, with an explicit acknowledgment that these are being omitted. Do not include bibliographies, assigned readings, or requests for further sources; the deliverable should be a self-contained teaching architecture executable from standard course materials alone.
