I'm chairing the institutional review board for a university neurotechnology center, and over the past eighteen months we've seen a sharp rise in protocols involving brain-computer interfaces, shared neural prostheses, and AI-mediated cognitive augmentation. The recurring problem is that these systems blur the boundary between one person's cognitive processes and another's, or between a biological brain and an external predictive system, yet our board has no consistent framework for deciding whether a given study involves one research subject, a distributed subject spanning multiple brains or devices, or a collective of distinct subjects. I need a reusable decision procedure my board can run in about forty-five minutes during protocol review, with clear ordering of questions, explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and marked stopping points where the determination is conceptual rather than ethical. The procedure should be grounded in Douglas Hofstadter's conceptual account of the self as a self-referential pattern—what he describes as a strange loop—that is not identical to any particular brain but can, in principle, be instantiated across substrates and even persist partly in other minds or artifacts. I do not need citations, bibliographies, or a survey of the literature; I need the theory translated into an operational flowchart that someone without philosophical training can apply consistently, with the board chair signing off on any deviation.

The deliverable should specify the sequence of tests in order—perhaps beginning with substrate independence, then loop closure, then narrative and symbolic ownership—with precise conditions for advancing, stopping with a clear classification, or handing the decision to a values vote because the conceptual analysis is indeterminate. It must include worked applications to three protocols: first, a bidirectional BCI linking two locked-in patients for shared motor control, in which both report feeling merged during tasks; second, an Alzheimer's protocol in which a patient relies on an AI system trained on their spouse's speech patterns and decision heuristics to maintain continuity of preference expression; and third, a healthy-volunteer study in which the volunteer's prefrontal patterns are continuously mirrored to an external server that makes predictive choices on their behalf, with the volunteer retaining veto power but rarely exercising it. For each, apply the procedure and state the result, and include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer—likely treating the system as a single distributed agent or, conversely, as multiple agents—that a reasonable board member, frankly including me, would be inclined to resist. Flag exactly where judgment overrules the flowchart and what safeguards are required. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: which categories of protocol fall outside its scope, where it will misfire because the underlying concept of distributed selfhood is itself contested, and how the board should document its reasoning when relying on it for consent authority, adverse-event reporting, and withdrawal rights.
