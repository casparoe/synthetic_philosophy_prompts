I've been thinking about the idea that a self is not a hidden thing inside the body but more like a center of narrative gravity—something that pulls together memories, intentions, and actions into one continuous story without being a physical object itself. The part that keeps bothering me is whether such a center is only a useful fiction, or whether it can genuinely cause things to happen and bear real weight in moral life. To push this further, I want to consider a specific wrinkle: call it narrative inertia. Once other people begin treating a particular story as "you," their expectations and responses create a kind of social momentum that resists sudden changes to that story, even if the underlying brain has changed significantly. This suggests the self might be built partly from the outside in, not just spun from within.

Write an essay that weighs whether this notion of narrative inertia makes the self robust enough to ground moral responsibility, or whether it merely relocates the problem onto collective belief and leaves the individual self too fragile. Consider the objection that if the self is ultimately just a story with social momentum, then blaming "me" is like blaming a weather pattern that people have agreed to name. Reach and justify a clear conclusion about whether the self, understood as a center of narrative gravity with narrative inertia, is substantial enough to support genuine accountability, or whether we should abandon the idea of a unified self altogether in ethics.
