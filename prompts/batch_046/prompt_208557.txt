I am writing for a graduate seminar in jurisprudence, and after studying selections from Hart’s The Concept of Law and Fuller’s The Morality of Law, I find myself wondering whether the standard framing of their disagreement survives close institutional analysis. Hart’s rule of recognition identifies valid legal norms through criteria of social acceptance and systemic efficacy, and Fuller’s eight canons—generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rule—read like the procedural architecture that any complex system of primary and secondary rules must possess in order to function. If Fuller’s inner morality of law is therefore reducible to the functional prerequisites of an efficacious legal order, then his critique of positivism seems to dissolve into a descriptive sociology that Hart already accepts, and the debate appears to be a false dichotomy sustained only by philosophers who mistake design constraints for moral imperatives.

To test this intuition, a colleague proposed a hypothetical jurisdiction—Lex Automata—where every norm is expressed in formal logic, applied only through explicit constitutional amendment (ensuring prospectivity), enforced by deterministic algorithms that exhibit perfect congruence and clarity, and made fully public, yet where no human official exercises judgment, citizens have no participatory role, and the system operates without any appeal to human dignity or agency. My colleague insists that this system violates the moral core of Fuller’s theory despite satisfying all eight canons, whereas I contend that it demonstrates the canons are value-neutral conditions of legal efficiency that carry no independent moral weight and therefore pose no challenge to the separation thesis.

Could you help me see whether Fuller’s principles contain an irreducibly normative demand—something that genuinely exceeds Hart’s separation thesis and cannot be captured as a mere efficacy condition for a rule of recognition? More precisely, I want to understand whether there is any feature of legal morality in Fuller’s account that Lex Automata lacks not because the system is immoral in some external, substantive sense, but because it fails to meet an internal moral requirement that is constitutive of law itself, and if so, whether that requirement is genuinely part of the concept of law or merely a contingent moral aspiration that Hart is free to set aside.
