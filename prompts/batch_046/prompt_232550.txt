I'm drafting opening remarks for a civic-education center's reopening ceremony, for a feature in a science-and-ideas magazine. The audience is split evenly between political philosophers and first-time voters with no background in the field. The speech must take as its structural backbone a specific tension within ranked-choice voting: when a voter's sincerely preferred candidate is eliminated in an early round, leaving only two "lesser evils" that neither express her actual judgment, is strategic compromise still a civic duty, or does expressive refusal become a moral act in its own right? The address should do real philosophical work—shaping what can and cannot be said about obligation, betrayal, and expression—without lecturing, without assuming familiarity with the literature, and without resolving the tension. Write a five-minute speech that lands for both halves of the room.
