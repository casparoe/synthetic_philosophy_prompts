Hey, I'm in this philosophy of religion and epistemology module and we've just done James's essay on the will to believe against Clifford's view that it's wrong always to believe without sufficient evidence. I'm trying to work out what I actually think about believing stuff when the proof is shaky, and whether there's one principel behind my gut reactions or if I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my verdicts:

My friend believes her dead brother is still watching over her, not because she has evidence but because it stops her falling apart after the accident. I think that's completely fine, maybe even rational.

There's an activist I know who genuinely believes direct action will force policy change within two years, even though the political science is pretty bleak. He needs that belief to keep organising. I also think that's okay — definatly not self-deception.

But then there's this guy in my seminar who believes a niche conspiracy about pharmaceutical trials because it makes him feel special and gives him an online community. The evidence is basically zero. I think that's wrong, even though it's pragmatic for him in the same way.

A doctor I read about believed an experimental drug was working on her patient despite negative trial data, because she couldn't bear giving up. That feels wrong too, but I'm not sure why it's different from the activist case — both are high-stakes and both go against the data.

There's the classic religious case: believing in God mainly because life feels meaningless without it. I want to say that's permissible if the option is genuinely live, forced and momentous, but I also think there's something dodgy about using the fact that it would be nice if true as a reason.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: my mate believes he remembers being bullied at school in a very specific way, but his sister says it never happened like that. He holds onto his version because it explains his anxiety now. Is that okay? It feels like it's preserving his identity, but also like he's just insulating himself from correction, which seems like bad epistemic behaviour.

So is there a coherent account — maybe mixing James's conditions with something about moral stakes affecting evidential standards — that captures most of these? Which verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the memory/bullying case I can't decide on? I don't need a bibliography or reading list, just the principle with its clauses, the case that motivates each one, and an honest statement of which of my intuitions it can't save.
