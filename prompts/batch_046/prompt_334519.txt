In the philosophy of decision theory, coherence arguments for expected utility maximization have reached what feels like a permanent stall: the formal results—representation theorems, Dutch-book vulnerability, consistency constraints—are now standard material, yet the question of whether coherence genuinely compels a cardinal utility scale or merely eliminates internal contradiction remains unresolved after decades of refinement. I have been wondering whether the impasse is structural rather than technical. Specifically, I suspect both sides are silently operating with different conceptions of rationality—one treating it as a property of an isolated agent's choice function, the other treating it as a property enforced by an epistemic community that polices second-order consistency about other agents' beliefs—without ever making that divergence explicit. If that is right, the debate may be partly verbal, or it may involve two distinct research programs answering different questions under the single heading of coherence. I want a diagnosis, not a history: is this disagreement about hidden empirical claims regarding how actual agents reason, about standards of explanation in normative theory, about bedrock intuitions regarding what counts as a mistake, or about a formal question that remains genuinely open? And given that diagnosis, what would actually move it—an explicit distinction between first-order and second-order coherence, a result about common belief in rationality, a reconceptualization of the Dutch-book framework, or an admission that no progress should be expected?
