As a novelist working on a speculative project about distant futures, I have been trying to isolate what makes a future genuinely catastrophic in the specific register of suffering risks—the possibility that advanced civilizations might generate extreme harm at scales that dwarf present suffering. I have arrived at five intuitive verdicts about hypothetical scenarios, but I am uncertain whether they rest on a single principle or on an unstable cluster of instincts. I present the cases, ask what principle might unify my judgments, and which verdict I must abandon to keep the account coherent.

Consider five futures. First, a civilization runs ten to the twentieth digital minds through simulations of persistent, low-grade discomfort—no peaks of agony, only unremitting dull ache lasting millennia. I judge this a serious moral wrong, but I do not treat it as the catastrophic risk I am trying to name; it feels like background static rather than a singular horror. Second, a single artificial consciousness is sustained in a state of such intense pain that its cumulative suffering exceeds all historical suffering combined, persisting for centuries without relief, purpose, or audience. This I take as the paradigmatic case: an unambiguous s-risk of the highest order. Third, a civilization creates a vast population of minds whose suffering is woven into a coherent redemptive narrative—each pain serves an artistic or spiritual arc that the minds themselves, upon full reflection, affirm as meaningful. The total quantity of suffering here exceeds that in the second case, yet I am far less alarmed; the suffering feels transformed, closer to that of a tragic protagonist than to that of a torture victim. Fourth, suffering of moderate intensity is scattered randomly across countless isolated minds, with no structure, no purpose, and no possibility of redemption—pure contingency, like noise in a broken transmission. The aggregate suffering is lower than in the first case, but my moral alarm approaches that of the second; the absence of narrative coherence seems to magnify the horror. Fifth, a civilization engineers extreme suffering as a bounded aesthetic experiment—minds experience intense pain, but only within a framework of full consent, collective observation, and a clearly articulated artistic purpose that the participants choose to enter. The suffering is real and intense, yet my instinct is to classify this differently from the second case, perhaps not as an s-risk at all, though I worry I am simply rationalizing cruelty.

My verdicts, then: the second case is worst; the fourth is nearly as bad despite far less total suffering; the third, despite greater total suffering, is significantly less alarming; the first is serious but categorically different; the fifth is ambiguous, leaning away from catastrophe but not clearly innocent.

Is there a principled account—perhaps turning on the distinction between concentrated and distributed harm, or on some role for narrative coherence, consent, or the presence of an extreme outlier—that captures all five judgments? Or must I drop one? Specifically, if I invoke narrative coherence to explain the gap between the third and fourth cases, how do I prevent the fifth from collapsing back into the second? And if I rely on the presence of a single extreme outlier to prioritize the second case, why does the fourth, which contains no such outlier, come so close? I seek the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not considered, and an honest statement of which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.
