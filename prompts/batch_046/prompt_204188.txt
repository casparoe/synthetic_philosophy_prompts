I'm presenting a seminar paper defending a rehabilitated version of Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles against Black's two-spheres counterexample. My thesis is that Black's scenario does not violate the principle, because the spheres are discernible via irreflexive relational properties—specifically, each sphere bears the relation "being-to-the-left-of" to the other in a manner that is non-symmetric once we treat spatial orientation as primitive rather than derivative from monadic properties. I concede that all intrinsic qualitative properties are identical, but I deny that this yields indiscernibility tout court, since relational discernibility was always integral to Leibniz's framework. The audience is a joint metaphysics and philosophy of physics seminar: roughly half are analytic metaphysicians who regard relational properties as ontologically second-rate or reducible to monadic properties of the relata plus structural relations, and half are philosophers of physics who will immediately invoke quantum statistics—particularly the individuation problem for identical fermions and bosons, where even relational properties fail to distinguish particles. They care whether metaphysical principles survive contact with physical theory, and they are generally unsympathetic to "saving" historical figures unless there is independent conceptual payoff.
