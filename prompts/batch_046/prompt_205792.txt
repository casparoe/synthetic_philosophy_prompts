I just spent several hours arguing with a friend about anthropic reasoning, and I want to refine an essay sketch that came out of it. The disagreement began with Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher—the thought that if you treat yourself as randomly sampled from all humans and then discover you are a philosopher, you ought to update sharply toward universes with far more philosophers. My friend takes this as a decisive strike against self-sampling assumptions (SSA), while I think the deeper problem is that self-indication assumptions (SIA) do not escape the same arrogance; they merely redistribute it across all observer-types.

Here are my working notes, and I would like your comments on accuracy, coherence, and whether I am smuggling in premises without noticing.

First, the standard framing: SSA asks you to reason as if randomly drawn from a stipulated reference class of observers, whereas SIA treats the existence of observers like yourself as direct evidence for larger populations of them. The presumptuous philosopher is usually wielded against SSA—finding yourself in a rare category should push you toward worlds where that category is common. But my friend pointed out that SIA seems structurally parallel. If every observer's existence counts as evidence for more observers everywhere, then learning you are a philosopher does not just update you on philosopher-prevalence; it updates you on total observer-prevalence, with philosophers scaled proportionally. The presumption does not vanish; it is universalized.

Second, I propose a "restricted indication" principle. Rather than sampling from a fixed class, or indicating promiscuously for all observers, you update only on observers within a "phenomenal neighborhood"—those whose basic cognitive architecture and experiential structure are sufficiently similar to yours that their existence bears a direct structural dependency on the likelihood of your own mental state arising. This is meant to block both the arbitrary reference-class problem of SSA and the unbounded multiplication of SIA. If I am a human philosopher, I weigh worlds with more human philosophers more heavily, but not worlds teeming with bat-philosophers or isolated simulated philosopher-instances that share no causal or structural pathway to my consciousness.

Third, my friend objects that "phenomenal neighborhood" is just SSA in disguise: I am defining a reference class by cognitive similarity rather than by profession or species. I reply that the difference is causal and structural, not taxonomic. On SSA, the class is stipulated for reasoning; on restricted indication, the weighting derives from actual dependencies between mental-state types across worlds. Yet I am unsure whether I have genuinely shown this distinction, or whether it avoids the presumptuous philosopher's charge of self-centered sampling.

I particularly want comments on the following:

- Is restricted indication genuinely distinct from SSA, or have I only renamed the reference class?
- Does Bostrom's argument apply symmetrically to SIA in the way I describe, or am I misrepresenting how SIA handles multiplicity across observer-types?
- Is there an obvious counterexample—perhaps involving split-brain cases, nested simulations, or minds with radically different phenomenology—that breaks the "phenomenal neighborhood" boundary without relying on obscure empirical details?
- Does the sketch add anything to the debate that is not already covered in the standard SSA/SIA literature, or is the injection of "phenomenal neighborhood" merely decorative?

Please treat this as an argument with a friend who is sharper than I am: push back where the reasoning is weak, flag hidden assumptions, and tell me which part of the sketch is worth developing and which should be abandoned.
