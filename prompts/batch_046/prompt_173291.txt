I've been working through a tension in comparative ethics that standard surveys tend to flatten into a simple taxonomy. Many interpreters classify Buddhist moral thought as a form of virtue ethics centered on mental purification, yet others—particularly those emphasizing the bodhisattva vow to liberate all beings—read it as a radical, other-regarding consequentialism in which moral value is determined by contribution to the cessation of suffering across all sentient beings. The usual objections to the consequentialist reading invoke skillful means or the prohibition against killing, but I want to push the debate into a structurally awkward place: the Buddhist theory of karmic latency and multi-life fruitation. If the full consequentialist calculus must include effects that ripen not only across future rebirths but across cosmological timescales, and if those effects are mediated by intervening mental states, rebirth conditions, and collective karmic fields that no ordinary agent can epistemically access, then the agent's decision procedure seems to collapse into rule-following or virtue-cultivation rather than outcome-maximization. At the same time, defenders of the consequentialist reading might reply that this is precisely why the bodhisattva cultivates omniscience—to eventually access that outcome space—and that the ordinary agent's inability to compute long-range karma is an epistemic limitation, not a metaphysical objection to consequentialism itself.

Write an essay examining whether the structural features of karmic latency and multi-life causation make Buddhist ethics fundamentally incompatible with consequentialist reasoning, or whether they can be integrated into a distinctive long-horizon consequentialism that differs from secular utilitarianism mainly in temporal scale and epistemic humility. Weigh the strongest version of each side: the anti-consequentialist argument that karma's opacity and the emphasis on present intention make outcome-tracking impossible in principle, and the consequentialist counter that karmic law is an impersonal causal mechanism whose moral authority derives precisely from its sensitivity to suffering-reduction over infinite time. Reach and defend a specific conclusion—perhaps that Buddhist ethics is best understood as a hybrid in which consequentialist aims govern the telos but deontic or virtue-ethical constraints govern the decision procedure, or perhaps that the consequentialist reading fails because karma introduces a form of moral luck incompatible with agent-centered evaluation. Justify your verdict through conceptual analysis rather than textual exegesis alone, and avoid framing the dispute as merely terminological.
