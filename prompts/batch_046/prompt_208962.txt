I am presenting a revised Strawsonian account of moral responsibility to our department’s philosophy of action and metaphysics seminar next Thursday. My central claim is that Strawson’s familiar binary between reactive and objective attitudes should be replaced by a scalar distinction between affective reactivity and what I call normative accounting—the sustained social practice of demanding explanations and justifications from an agent regardless of whether that demand is accompanied by resentment, indignation, or other emotional responses. I argue that moral responsibility is anchored not in the presence of reactive feelings but in the integrity of these answerability structures, which can persist in institutional, technological, or clinical contexts where affect is structurally dampened or redistributed. The audience will include two metaphysicians of free will who regard Strawson’s move to reactive attitudes as an evasion of the determinism problem rather than a solution; one moral psychologist who works empirically on blame practices and will expect reference to observed behavior; one compatibilist who accepts Strawson’s framework but denies that reactive attitudes play any causal or justificatory role in responsibility; and a legal philosopher concerned with algorithmic and bureaucratic governance who will ask whether my view can guide institutional design. Finally, identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks—the objection that targets the very distinction between affective reactivity and normative accounting by pressing on cases where accounting practices seem to collapse entirely in the absence of any residual affective orientation toward the agent.
