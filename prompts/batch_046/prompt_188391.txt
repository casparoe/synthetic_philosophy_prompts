As a retired experimental biologist now working through philosophy of mind, I find myself stuck on an impasse in the debate over higher-order theories of consciousness. On one side, higher-order representationalists hold that a mental state is phenomenally conscious only when targeted by a suitable higher-order representation; on the other, first-order theorists claim that consciousness requires only the right kind of first-order intentional content, with no distinct meta-representational mechanism required. Both sides acknowledge the same neuropsychological findings — that prefrontal activity correlates with reportability, and that selective lesions can separate subjective experience from meta-cognitive access — yet the disagreement has not advanced in years. My hypothesis is that part of the stall comes from an ambiguity in "higher-order": in my old field, monitoring is a causal, psychophysical relation, whereas in philosophy it is often treated as a constitutive representational relation, and neither camp makes clear which they intend. Given this, is the disagreement verbal, substantive but hidden, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about introspective transparency that neither side can argue for? What distinction — perhaps between access-consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, or between tokened mechanisms and logical structure — would reveal whether they are answering different questions? And if it is substantive, what kind of result, empirical or formal, could actually move it: a demonstration that meta-cognitive representations are always post-hoc reconstructions, or a logical proof that self-reference without circularity is impossible? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than a verdict, together with what progress would look like under that diagnosis.
