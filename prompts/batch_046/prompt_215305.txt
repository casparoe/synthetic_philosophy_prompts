Construct a thought experiment that generates—rather than stipulates—the claim that knowledge is practically superior to mere true belief, using only the conceptual resources of Plato’s Meno, specifically the distinction that knowledge is true belief anchored by an explanatory grasp (logos / aitia) while true belief lacks such a tether. The case must involve two agents who hold the exact same correct belief about a practical navigational task in a contemporary urban setting that contains a hidden, systematic geometric distortion (for instance, a district where the local street grid is rotated relative to the surrounding city plan, so that dead reckoning from familiar landmarks fails in a predictable but non-obvious way). Both agents must succeed in the immediate task without relying on external authorities, devices, or oracles; neither case may be a Gettier-style accident of truth. The belief content, the immediate payoff, and the environment must be identical for both; only the presence or absence of an internal explanatory account of why the belief is correct may differ. The scenario itself should be no more than one short paragraph, and the overall purpose is to isolate one structural feature—explanatory anchoring—while holding everything else fixed, so that stipulating the outcome counts as failure.

Please organize your response in the following sections and approximate lengths.

Scenario (~130 words): Present the situation, the two agents, their shared belief, the hidden distortion, and the identical immediate success.

Mapping to the Meno (~180 words): Identify which agent possesses knowledge and which possesses only true belief, applying the tether/account distinction precisely; show how the logos functions in the scenario and why both agents navigate correctly at first.

The Removal Test (~150 words): Explain what happens if the explanatory-anchoring feature is stripped away—does the distinction collapse into a standard argument that true belief is sufficient for practical success?

Opponent’s Attack (~150 words): Present the strongest reply available to a defender of true-belief sufficiency: how they would argue that your case either smuggles in an irrelevant cognitive luxury or misdescribes the practical demands of navigation.

Verdict (~100 words): State whether the thought experiment genuinely advances the Meno problem, which feature does the essential work, and whether the opponent’s attack succeeds in dissolving the case.
