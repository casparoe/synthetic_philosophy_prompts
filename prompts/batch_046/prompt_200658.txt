I've been trying to work out, without reading much philosophy, how a single thought can hold more than one thing in it without just being a jumble. Here is an argument I built that now feels suspicious because it seems to wipe out the very thoughts I know I have.

(1) When I think that the dog chases the cat, the ideas of dog, cat, and chasing are not just sitting side by side; they are arranged so that the dog is the chaser and the cat is the chased.

(2) That directional arrangement must belong to the thought itself, otherwise I would be thinking exactly the same thing if the roles were reversed.

(3) Call whatever supplies this direction the vector; if the vector is just another item inside the thought—say, an idea of left-to-right ordering—then that item too must be arranged relative to dog and cat, which needs a second vector, and so on forever.

(4) If, instead, the vector is not an item inside the thought but a primitive feature of my mind, then every mind that thinks this thought contains the very same primitive feature, so there is no mental difference between my thinking it and anyone else thinking it.

(5) So either unified directional thoughts contain infinitely many vectors, or they erase the distinction between thinkers; both are absurd.

(6) But I clearly do think the specific directional thought that the dog chases the cat.

(7) Therefore, directional arrangement cannot really be part of thought, and what I take to be a structured belief is just an unordered cluster that I misread.

Which step should I reject? I would rather keep (1) and (6) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying if I give up (3) or (4) instead. If this whole framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps I'm smuggling in a particular picture of mental representation without realising—please say so plainly rather than repairing the argument politely. Please answer in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
