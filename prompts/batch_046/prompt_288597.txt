Your answer should: (i) explain syādvāda and distinguish it from simple relativism; (ii) illustrate saptabhaṅgī using the concrete case of stating that “this LCD display is black”; (iii) discuss whether anekāntavāda can accommodate conceptual change in scientific taxonomy without abandoning realist metaphysics; and (iv) conclude on whether the position amounts to epistemic humility or to metaphysical quietism. Student answer:
Jain anekāntavāda holds that reality is many-sided, so no single viewpoint (naya) captures the whole object. Unlike Western pluralism, which often treats perspectives as subjective, Jainism claims each naya reveals a real feature. It also differs from Madhyamaka śūnyatā: whereas Nāgārjuna denies svabhāva entirely, anekāntavāda affirms that objects genuinely possess properties, only from restricted standpoints. Syādvāda uses “syāt” to qualify every predication—meaning “in some respect” rather than “probably”—so assertions are never absolute.

Applying saptabhaṅgī to the LCD display: syāt asti (it is black phenomenologically), syāt nāsti (it emits light, so physically not black), syāt asti nāsti (both apply in different respects), and syāt avaktavya (it is inexpressible whether it is black when emission and absorption occur together). The remaining three forms combine avaktavya with the prior pairs.

Metaphysically, Jainism does not posit enduring substances (dravya). Instead, the display is a temporary convergence of perspectives, analogous to the Buddhist skandhas. Because every claim is naya-restricted, contradiction is impossible globally—the law of non-contradictivity simply does not operate across perspectives. Hence anekāntavāda expresses epistemic humility: we acknowledge partiality without needing a hidden essence behind appearances. It avoids quietism because practical engagement remains possible, yet it denies that science ever describes an underlying, permanent reality.
