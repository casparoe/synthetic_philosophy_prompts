Write the keynote address for the dedication of the Alphabet Stone Garden, a memorial site that intersperses soil and stone from Holocaust camps, the Armenian genocide, and other twentieth-century state atrocities arranged in strict alphabetical order by place name. The board adopted alphabetical ordering to refuse any hierarchy of suffering, but the audience—survivors' descendants, historians familiar with the Historikerstreit, and local officials without background—is divided: some see the refusal to rank as erasing the Holocaust's specific modern-bureaucratic structure that Bauman identified, others see any insistence on uniqueness as blocking recognition of recurring genocidal modernity. Your speech must treat the uniqueness-versus-comparability problem as structural, not decorative: explain why alphabetical ordering is philosophically unstable—whether comparison diminishes particularity or isolation falsely exceptionalizes modern rationality—without resolving the tension into platitude. You may not invoke afterlife consolation or treat the dead as redeemed, and you must address Bauman's claim that the Holocaust was not pre-modern barbarism but a possibility internal to modern organizational rationality, without either normalizing the event or condemning every modern institution. The room is mixed; the experts should recognize the argument, the others should understand what is genuinely at stake.
