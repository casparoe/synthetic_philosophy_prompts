In the epistemological literature on the value of knowledge—often traced to Plato’s Meno but sharpened by modern discussions of reliability, intellectual virtue, and the so-called swamping problem—the central question has remained stubbornly unresolved: why is knowing that p better than merely truly believing that p? Over decades, proponents of reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, and achievement-based accounts have argued that knowledge adds a distinctive value that true belief lacks, only to face replies that the added condition—whether reliability, credit, or competence—either contributes no independent value or is valuable only instrumentally. Meanwhile, defenders of the swamping intuition hold that once a belief is true, any further epistemic property is redundant for practical or theoretical purposes. Despite the familiarity of these moves, the debate has not converged; participants seem to talk past one another about whether “value” means final value, instrumental value, or the value of a mental achievement, and about whether the comparison should be made at the level of individual mental states, diachronic cognitive processes, or social practices of attribution.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive dispute about the ontology of epistemic goods that could be settled by refining our theory of mental achievement? A verbal disagreement about the word “value,” where one side means “useful for action” and the other means “worthy of admiration for its own sake”? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one asking why an agent should seek knowledge rather than true belief, the other asking why the concept of knowledge plays an irreducible role in our evaluative vocabulary—while treating them as one? I offer the hypothesis that the stall is partly driven by a hidden ambiguity about whether knowledge is being evaluated as a natural kind of mental state (like seeing) or as an institutional status (like being certified), but I want your diagnosis supported by what each camp actually says. Given your diagnosis, is there anything that would genuinely move the debate—a formal model of epistemic utility, a distinction that separates the practical from the honorific value of knowing, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed because “true belief” and “knowledge” are not competing goods but nested descriptions? Or should we expect permanent stalemate, and if so, why?
