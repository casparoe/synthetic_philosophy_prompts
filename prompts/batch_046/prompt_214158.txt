As a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and medieval Islamic metaphysics, I recently listened to a sustained disagreement between two colleagues—let's call them R and S—about Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment. They were not simply rehashing whether it proves Cartesian-style dualism; instead, they were disputing a more granular interpretive question about what kind of independence the suspended, sensation-deprived subject actually demonstrates. I report their exchange below and ask you to adjudicate it point by point, since I could not determine who was missing what.

R opened by arguing that the flying man establishes what she called ontological non-derivativeness: the soul's awareness of itself does not depend, even in principle, on any prior bodily act of cognition. Because the suspended subject lacks all sensory mediation—no vision, no touch, no proprioception—yet still affirms his own existence, self-awareness must be an act whose conditions of occurrence require no corporeal instrument whatsoever. If a mental act can occur with literally zero bodily mediation, R said, then the essence of the soul is not essentially tied to the body; at most the body is an accidental or concurrent cause.

S replied that R was sliding from epistemic isolation to metaphysical separation. He granted that the experiment reveals modal independence: one can conceive of self-awareness occurring in the absence of bodily sensation without contradiction. But modal independence, S insisted, is not ontological non-derivativeness. Avicenna's broader metaphysics treats the rational soul as the perfection of an organic body, so the correct reading is that the flying man reveals only that self-awareness has a different modal profile from sensory knowledge—namely, it is non-representational and immediate—without showing that the soul can exist or operate without the body as its substrate. S introduced a distinction he thought R was ignoring: conditions of occurrence versus conditions of being. The absence of bodily conditions for the occurrence of self-awareness in the hypothetical case does not entail the absence of bodily conditions for the being of the soul in reality.

R countered that if self-awareness requires no bodily organ even for its occurrence, then treating the body as a necessary condition for the soul's being becomes ad hoc. She conceded that Avicenna uses perfection-language elsewhere, but argued that this applies to the embodied, functioning soul, whereas the flying man isolates the soul's defining act—its presence to itself. If that defining act can be fully actualized without the body, she said, then the body's role is at best redundant, and Avicenna's hylomorphism in other texts must be reconciled with this substantial independence rather than used to override it.

S responded that R was treating a thought-experiment's isolating function as an essential-revealing function. Thought experiments can separate features for analysis without those features being separable in reality. The flying man, on S's view, demonstrates that self-awareness is indexically primitive in a specific way—its object is given immediately, not through an internal representation—rather than demonstrating that the self is a free-floating substance. He added that R's reading risks anachronism: it projects a post-Cartesian concern with substantial separation onto a thinker whose primary concern was establishing the soul's immateriality, not its separability.

R's final move was to distinguish immateriality from separability precisely in Avicenna's terms. She argued that if the soul's essential act is present to itself without any corporeal extension or organ, then it already possesses a mode of being that exceeds corporeal conditions, which is exactly what Avicenna needs for the soul's survival after bodily death. The flying man, she concluded, does not need to prove full Cartesian dualism to do serious metaphysical work: it proves that the soul's core operation is non-derivative in a sense strong enough to undermine any view that makes the soul essentially a bodily form.

In particular, address whether S's distinction between conditions of occurrence and conditions of being successfully blocks R's inference, whether R is correct that Avicenna's perfection-language must be reconciled with the flying man's result rather than the other way around, and whether both are missing something important about the argument's purpose—perhaps something about the relationship between pre-reflective self-certainty and the problem of other minds, or about how the thought experiment functions differently in Avicenna's epistemology versus his metaphysics. Do not simply declare a winner; specify exactly where each party makes a valid point and where each overreaches.
