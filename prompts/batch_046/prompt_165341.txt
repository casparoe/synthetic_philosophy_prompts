I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mathematics preparing a consistency check for a position paper in our epistemology seminar, and I want to know whether my commitments actually hang together or whether I'm smuggling in an unstated premise. Here is the package: (1) I am a mathematical platonist—abstract objects exist, are acausal and non-spatiotemporal, and include entities such as large cardinals; (2) I endorse a domain-sensitive reliabilism: knowledge requires that the belief-forming process be reliable specifically within the domain the belief concerns, not merely globally coherent; (3) I accept evolutionary-psychological explanations of our basic arithmetic capacities, but I take them to be restricted to small finite sets, so that our number-tracking mechanisms have no evolutionary calibration for infinite or extremely large structures; (4) despite this, I claim we possess genuine knowledge of advanced set-theoretic truths—e.g., consistency results for ZFC and related large-cardinal hypotheses—that lie well outside any evolutionary utility; (5) I reject Benacerraf's causal-access premise as a necessary condition on knowledge in general, since I think knowledge can obtain without spatiotemporal interaction; yet (6) I insist that if a belief-forming mechanism is entirely decoupled from its target domain—lacking even structural, logical, or representational connections—then it cannot yield knowledge, however internally consistent the outputs.

The tensions I can see are not evenly distributed. The interaction between (2), (3), and (4) looks severe: if our beliefs about large cardinals are generated by cognitive machinery evolved for small sets, domain-sensitive reliabilism seems to deny them knowledge-status unless I can show the machinery is somehow reliable in that extended domain, which I have not done. Meanwhile, (1) together with (5) and (6) seems to demand a positive account of how minds connect to acausal abstract objects without causal interaction; I have only the negative claim that pure decoupling is insufficient, which leaves a gap rather than a bridge. There is also what looks like a tension between (3) and (5): if evolutionary explanations debunk our basic mathematical intuitions, doesn't that undermine platonist knowledge across the board? I suspect this dissolves once debunking is itself restricted to the evolutionary domain, but I want that checked rather than assumed. Please audit this set point by point: rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the genuinely incompatible pairs from the merely apparent ones, specify for each serious conflict what the least costly revision is and what it would cost me in other commitments, and say whether a single revision—perhaps to (2) or to (6)—would resolve the most problems simultaneously. I am not asking for a survey of Benacerraf's literature; I want an internal coherence check on exactly these six theses.
