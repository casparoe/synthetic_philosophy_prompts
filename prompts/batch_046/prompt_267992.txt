I'm preparing a unit for an undergraduate philosophy of science course on the gap between statistical genetics and popular imagination, and I'm planning to screen Andrew Niccol's Gattaca (1997). The film depicts a near-future society that sorts citizens into "valids" and "in-valids" at birth according to genetic profiles, treating DNA as destiny for career, romance, and social standing. I want students to read the film through the actual concept of heritability—specifically that h² is a population-level statistic measuring the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic variance in a given environment, not an index of individual determinism, and that high heritability neither implies immutability nor licenses prediction of a single person's trajectory. Where does the film dramatize a legitimate philosophical anxiety about genetic reductionism? Where does it trade on a confusion between statistical heritability and individual genetic determinism—perhaps in the bureaucratic rituals of constant testing and in the assumption that Vincent's congenital heart condition is a fixed life sentence rather than a statistical risk? Are there countervailing moments, such as Vincent's swimming triumphs over his genetically "valid" brother, that implicitly resist that confusion? I also want the reading to avoid reducing the film to a mere illustration of a statistical error: attend to the narrative and visual architecture—the spiral staircase that evokes the double helix, the cold blue palette of the valid world, the surveillance of bodily fluids—and say what they contribute to the debate that a dry argument about variance components cannot. Where the film's emotional logic genuinely requires a determinism that the science denies, the honest critic should say so rather than forcing the art to fit the concept. I'm looking for a criticism I can present without misleading students about what heritability actually does and does not mean.
