I'm trying to understand my own intuitions about how I should think now about propositions I expect to revise my view on later, especially when the revision comes from anticipated learning rather than present evidence. Here are my verdicts on several concrete situations, and I want to know whether there's a single principle that captures them, or whether I have to drop some.

Situation 1. I currently assign about 45 percent credence to the claim that a specific chemical compound is toxic to humans at low doses. Tomorrow I will receive a lab report from a testing facility I fully trust; the report will either push me to about 92 percent confidence that it is toxic, or to about 12 percent confidence that it is safe. I genuinely do not know which result the report will contain. If I weighted the two outcomes equally that would average to about 52 percent, but I do not actually know the weights, and moving now toward that midpoint feels like pure guessing. Verdict: I should stay near 45 percent today.

Situation 2. I am about 50 percent sure that I have correctly grasped a particular logical paradox. I plan to spend this evening working through every step carefully. By tomorrow I expect to be either about 90 percent sure I understand it, or about 85 percent sure I have made a hidden mistake. I do not know which outcome my check will produce. Verdict: I should remain near 50 percent until I have done the work.

Situation 3. I am roughly 70 percent confident that a long-time colleague is acting in good faith in a current dispute. Tomorrow we will have a tense conversation that I know will leave me either about 95 percent trusting or about 25 percent suspicious, depending largely on emotional tone and framing rather than on any new factual disclosure. Verdict: I should hold steady near 70 percent today; anticipating tomorrow's emotional swing should not move my present belief.

Situation 4. I assign about 55 percent probability to a particular interpretation of a historical treaty. Next week I will read a well-known popular history that I recognize is written with a strong nationalist bias favoring the opposite interpretation. After reading, I anticipate being about 80 percent sure of the author's view, but I also think there is only about a 60 percent chance that view is correct. Verdict: I should stay near 55 percent, and perhaps even discount the anticipated 80 percent.

Situation 5—the one I keep revising—is this. I think there is about a 40 percent chance that a new urban policy will achieve its stated goals. In a month I will have completed an intensive evaluation course whose methods I generally respect, though I also know that graduates tend to overcorrect away from their prior intuitions. I anticipate that after the course I will be either about 85 percent optimistic or about 35 percent pessimistic, with both outcomes feeling about equally likely to me now. Sometimes I think I should already be near 60 percent, since that is the expectation of my future credences weighted equally; other times I think I should stay at 40 percent until I have actually acquired the training, precisely because I worry about that overcorrection tendency.

Is there a coherent principle governing when I should align my present credence with my expectation of my future credence, and when I should resist? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which situation above, and say honestly which of my verdicts—especially in Situation 5—it forces me to abandon. Also apply it to a further case: I now think there is a 65 percent chance that a philosophical argument I have heard is unsound. I plan to present it to a seminar group whose members I regard as epistemic peers, and I expect that after discussion I will converge with the group's consensus, which I currently predict will be about 80 percent unsound, though I also think there is a 30 percent chance the group will be led astray by a charismatic but mistaken speaker. What should my credence be now?
