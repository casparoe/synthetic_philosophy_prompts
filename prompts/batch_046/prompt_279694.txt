I am preparing a ninety-minute workshop for about twelve graduate students in a philosophy of action and rationality course, and I want them to experience the structural pressure of embedded agency—the condition in which an agent is physically and causally embedded in the very world it attempts to model and optimize over—before anyone names the concept aloud. Please design a structured, multi-round protocol or game with explicit rules and small, real stakes that makes this tension visceral rather than abstract. The participants should begin by collectively drafting a single decision rule that applies to all of them, governing something tangible like the distribution of a shared pool of tokens or the order of exit choices from a simulated shared resource space. Then, across two or three successive rounds, each student should receive a private, hidden opportunity to make a minor physical alteration to the shared environment—moving one token, rewriting a single line of the rule on a visible board, or inserting a noise marker—knowing that this change will specifically affect how the collective rule evaluates their own future action, while remaining invisible to the others until after execution. The payoff structure should be calibrated so that each local adjustment appears rationally advantageous to the individual who makes it, yet the accumulated alterations cause the collective rule to generate a clearly self-defeating or paradoxical outcome for the group as a whole, with the loop only becoming visible to everyone after the final round is complete.

The debrief should then map each phase of the activity onto the conceptual anatomy of embedded agency: distinguish the written rule (the model) from the environment that includes the participants themselves (the modeled system), show how the predictor becomes part of the predicted world, and highlight the exact moment when a student recognizes that their own earlier physical adjustment is now an input that the rule applies back to them. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong when running this—some participants will likely try to escape the loop by proposing a meta-rule or a monitoring layer, which reproduces the same embedding problem one level up, while others may retreat into purely strategic reasoning and miss the conceptual point altogether—and state precisely how the facilitator should handle these derailments without delivering a front-loaded lecture. Finally, be frank about which feature of genuine embedded agency the physical setup cannot reproduce—such as the logical self-reference involved in a system containing a perfect model of itself, or the complete absence of causal separation between agent and environment—and explain exactly how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally after the debrief. Do not ask for advance reading, do not provide bibliographies or citations, and ensure that the idea emerges entirely from the rules, incentives, and roles the students inhabit.
