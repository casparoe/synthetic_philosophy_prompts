After a long and somewhat heated argument last night with a close friend about Kant's universalizability tests, I want to check whether my resistance to his summary was philosophically justified or just stubbornness. He presented Kant's position roughly as follows. First, the categorical imperative requires testing maxims by asking whether they can be universalized without contradiction, and he treated this universal-law formulation as the sole sufficient expression of the moral law, with no reference to the Formula of Humanity or to treating rational nature as an end in itself. Second, there are two distinct failures: contradiction in conception, where the universalized maxim is logically incoherent because it undermines the very practice or institution it presupposes; and contradiction in will, where a rational agent could not consistently will the universalization because it frustrates necessary ends that every rational being must pursue. Third, he applied this framework not to individual actions like lying promises but to what he called "institutional maxims"—specifically, the maxim of a democratic legislature that "we will bind future legislatures only by procedural rules, never by substantive rights, in order to preserve flexibility." He argued that this maxim fails the contradiction-in-conception test: if every legislature adopted it, the institution of constitutional entrenchment itself would collapse, since no substantive constraint could survive generational turnover. From this he concluded that Kant's framework, properly understood, directly supports strong substantive constitutional precommitment and that the common criticism—that Kantian ethics is irreducibly individualistic and therefore useless for constitutional theory—is mistaken, because collective agents can be tested for universalizability in exactly the same way as individual persons, with institutional stability serving as the relevant necessary end.

I felt that something was off, particularly in the direct move from individual rational agency to legislative bodies, in the way he described the contradiction-in-conception failure as an institutional collapse rather than a logical incoherence in the maxim itself, and in his silence about the Formula of Humanity. But I am not sure where the missteps are. Is his summary of Kant's two contradiction tests accurate as far as it goes? Is his application to institutional maxims a legitimate extension of Kantian reasoning or a category mistake that smuggles in premises about collective agency and institutional preservation that belong to a different kind of argument? Does his conclusion—that Kantian universalizability supports substantive entrenchment—actually follow, or does it depend on treating "necessary ends" as institutional stability rather than as ends of rational beings? Please go through his characterization claim by claim: separate what is faithful to Kant, what is a defensible extrapolation, what is a common simplification, and what is a misrepresentation. In particular, tell me whether a careful reader of Kant would accept that the contradiction-in-conception test can be applied to collective bodies in this direct manner, whether the omission of the Formula of Humanity matters for his institutional argument, and whether the distinction between the two contradiction tests survives his translation into constitutional theory intact.
