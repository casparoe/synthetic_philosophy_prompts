Design a 90-minute workshop for 16 advanced undergraduates in ethics or political philosophy that makes them feel, through rules and roles rather than lecture, the structural tension between a collective duty to remember and a legitimate case for forgetting, drawing on Avishai Margalit's account of shared moral memory and David Rieff's critique of enforced collective remembrance. Do not produce a discussion guide or essay prompt; produce a runnable simulation with explicit roles, hidden commitments, a phased timeline, small real stakes, and a debrief.

The central mechanism should embody a conceptual twist that standard summaries rarely stress: treat forgetting not as a passive absence but as an active release that demands its own moral justification, and embed a mnemonic compounding rule whereby every institutional decision to preserve one testimony automatically generates a secondary obligation to preserve its framing, its emotional context, and the identity of those who must carry it, so that remembrance expands exponentially unless the group actively votes to curtail it. The rules should be arranged so that participants initially believe they can honour every obligation, then discover that their communal archive demands more emotional and cognitive resources than the group possesses, and that safeguarding one subgroup's right to be remembered directly imposes a perpetual memorial burden on others.

Specify:
- The setting, group division (for example, survivors, descendants, archivists, civic leaders, outsiders), and any asymmetric or hidden information.
- A phased timeline (for instance, inscription, negotiation, crisis, binding vote) with concrete actions and incentives that generate the paradox before anyone names it.
- The debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto Margalit's concern for solidarity through shared memory and Rieff's concern for the violence and inflation of enforced remembrance; predict what will predictably go wrong in practice (such as dominant voices monopolising the archive, conflation of forgetting with betrayal, or emotional exhaustion) and suggest how the facilitator should handle each.
- An honest statement of which feature of the real-world tension the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the irreversibility and genuine trauma of actual historical loss, or the authority of real victims to object) and what the facilitator must therefore supply verbally.

Write in polished British English. Do not include quotation marks around any passage drawn from a real text; paraphrase Margalit's and Rieff's positions without verbatim citation. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or precise page references, and avoid dependence on obscure biographical or textual details that would require online verification.
