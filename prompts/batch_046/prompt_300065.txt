For a graduate seminar on the moral economy of labor and rest, I am designing a diagnostic handout that sorts participants by what they actually believe rather than by the school they claim to belong to. I want a decision tree built from the logical space of commitments, not a gallery of named theorists: each internal node should pose a concrete case or claim on which genuinely opposing views come apart, phrased so that a respondent can answer it without knowing the labels in advance. The audience is advanced students who are familiar with Weber’s account of the Protestant work ethic and with the historical campaigns that produced the thirty-five-hour week, but who have not necessarily encountered Japanese discussions of karoshi as a philosophical boundary condition. The tree should contain exactly four branching questions, leading to six leaves, with any unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed.

The first split should ask whether leisure possesses intrinsic moral value or is only instrumentally valuable—as recuperative capital that restores labor power—since this distinguishes positions that treat rest as an end in itself from those that treat it as a managed input. The second branch should present the automated-abundance scenario: if technological change eliminates most necessary labor, is leisure to be distributed equally as an unconditional share, or does it remain tied to prior contribution, capital ownership, or market position? The third branch must use karoshi—fatal overwork—as its test case: does the existence of death-by-overwork invalidate the basic labor contract itself, or can it be neutralized through compensation, regulation, and risk-management without altering the underlying structure of obligation? For the fourth branch, please introduce the concept of “leisure debt”: the idea that accumulated unpaid overtime generates not merely a wage claim but a future, non-transferable entitlement to guaranteed blocks of non-work time, structurally analogous to environmental reparations. Each leaf should bear a position name if one exists in the literature, or “unnamed” if the combination is logically available but unoccupied, followed by one sentence stating that view’s most awkward commitment—the implication it must accept but rarely advertises.

Please construct the tree so that its branches are labeled by the claim that separates siblings, and flag any pairs of positions that the tree cannot cleanly separate—if two views agree on every case but differ only in rhetorical emphasis or historical narrative, say so explicitly rather than inventing a false distinction. I want the Weberian work-ethic position, the social-democratic thirty-five-hour position, and the karoshi-critical labor-rights position all present as identifiable leaves; if a libertarian-productivist view or a post-scarcity distributive view falls out naturally, include them, and mark any empty slots with a brief diagnosis of whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The deliverable should be a self-contained classification instrument: a respondent should be able to trace their answers through the four cases and arrive at a leaf that describes what they actually hold, complete with the awkward commitment they must own.
