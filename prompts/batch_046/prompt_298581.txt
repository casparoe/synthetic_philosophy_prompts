A national agricultural regulatory framework evaluates proposed pest-control agents through two distinct approval tracks. For invertebrate-targeting chemicals, the agency calculates an “ecological-function disruption score” that weighs pollination loss, soil-processing decline, and food-web perturbation; if the score falls below a statutory threshold, the agent is approved regardless of how the targeted insects experience exposure, and no separate welfare assessment is conducted. For vertebrate-targeting agents—rodenticides, avicides—the same framework requires an additional “humane-intervention” certification that bars methods producing prolonged suffering, even when the ecological disruption score is negligible. The distinction is codified in assessment manuals, not in any explicit philosophical charter, and it is applied consistently whether the invertebrates are eusocial pollinators or solitary soil-dwelling species. Meanwhile, the agency’s own research grants treat insect nociception and stress physiology as legitimate objects of welfare science.

What theory of moral status and well-being does this institutional practice presuppose? Does the asymmetry between the two tracks commit the agency to a capacity-based denial of direct moral standing to invertebrates, or can it be justified by a purely instrumental valuation of ecosystem services that never invokes sentience at all? Furthermore, is the practice philosophically consistent with the agency’s simultaneous funding of invertebrate welfare research—research that conceptually treats these organisms as having states that matter for their own sake—or does that funding undermine the approval framework’s implicit premises? Take a definite position: either reconstruct the unstated philosophical distinction that makes the dual-track system coherent and say what it implies for future regulation, or argue that the practice contains a hidden contradiction and specify which track—the ecological-score exemption or the humane-kill requirement—should be revised and on what grounds.
