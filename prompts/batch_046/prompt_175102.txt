I have been following the long-running dispute in contemporary philosophy of Indian epistemology over whether the Nyāya school genuinely treats testimony (śabda) as an independent pramāṇa, irreducible to inference (anumāna), or whether the classical texts can be read consistently with a reductionist account in which verbal knowledge is ultimately grounded in inferential reasoning aided by a principle of speaker reliability. For at least several decades, the same textual passages—particularly those in the Nyāyasūtra and the broader commentarial tradition—have been parsed repeatedly, and neither camp has conceded ground. The reductionists point to the Nyāya analysis of sentence-meaning as involving a grasp of syntactic connection and speaker intention that looks structurally like an inference; the irreducibilists reply that this ignores the sui generis authority of authoritative utterance (āptavākya) that allows testimony to yield knowledge even when the hearer cannot reconstruct the speaker's reasoning. My working theory is that this is not really a philological disagreement about the texts at all, but a deeper clash about whether epistemic justification is fundamentally individual or whether it can be genuinely social and distributed—two questions being fought under the single heading of independence of śabda. But I am unsure whether that diagnosis captures everything, or whether there is also a hidden disagreement about what counts as a pramāṇa in the first place—whether it must be a causal process in the knower or merely a normative source of true belief. Could you diagnose the stall for me? Is it verbal, about the word independent; is it substantive but hidden, turning on a modern social-epistemology question that neither side makes explicit; are the two sides answering different questions, one about the psychology of understanding and one about the normative structure of justification; or is it a bedrock intuition about the limits of reason that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would actually move the debate: a formal model of testimony, a closer conceptual distinction between causal and normative sources, a change of question that separates psychology from epistemology, or should we conclude that no progress is possible because the underlying commitments are incommensurable?
