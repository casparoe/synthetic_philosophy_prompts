Below are notes for a short essay on Sen’s liberal paradox. The canonical two-agent case involves individuals A and B and a controversial book: each prefers that the other read it rather than himself, so that minimal liberal rights—giving each agent authority over whether he reads—cycle against the Pareto-efficient outcomes in which both read or neither reads. I want to argue that the paradox is not a deep failure of liberalism, but an artifact of framing rights as veto powers over entire social states rather than as boundaries on individual action spaces. If A’s right extends only to A’s own reading and B’s right extends only to B’s own reading, then no Pareto improvement is genuinely blocked, since the outcome “both read” is not within either agent’s legitimate local domain. The difficulty is that this resolution makes liberalism socially inert: it cannot prevent collectively harmful configurations that fall outside any single agent’s authority. To avoid this, I propose a “layered” model in which rights operate at multiple granularities—individual, dyadic, and collective—with lexical priority rules resolving conflicts. The essay would defend this layered view as preserving minimal liberalism’s spirit without abandoning Pareto efficiency, while addressing the objection that it is merely ad hoc unless the layers are justified by an independent theory of institutional competence.

Please comment on these notes. In particular: (1) Is my reconstruction of the standard preference ordering accurate, or does it misrepresent the structure Sen actually employs? (2) Does the distinction between veto over social states versus authority over local actions correspond to any established distinction in rights-based social choice theory—such as side-constraint views—without being a simple restatement? (3) Is the layered model genuinely distinct from weakening the Pareto principle, and if it is defensible, what is its most serious weakness?
