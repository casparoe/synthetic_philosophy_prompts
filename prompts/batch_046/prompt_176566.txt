I'm trying to clarify my own judgments about what counts as corruption, especially at the boundary of clientelism, gift-giving, and institutional role. I find myself drawn to the idea that corruption is not merely the exchange of goods for influence, but a breach of what I might call a role-specific fiduciary membrane—the boundary that separates the obligations attached to a public or professional office from personal, kinship, or communal reciprocity networks. At the same time, I think embedded reciprocity norms in some societies make certain exchanges legitimate that would be corrupt in a bureaucratic context. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether they cohere around a single principle or force me to abandon some.

Case 1: A senior official in Japan receives an expensive traditional clock from a contractor shortly after a public tender is awarded to that firm. The gift is registered in the official's disclosure log, conforms to long-standing diplomatic and business custom, and carries no explicit quid pro quo. Verdict: not corruption.

Case 2: A municipal mayor in Brazil directs nearly all public construction contracts to firms owned by members of her church, and fills administrative posts with congregants who campaign for her. She defends this as fulfilling a moral duty of loyalty analogous to family obligation. Verdict: corruption.

Case 3: A physician attends an all-expenses-paid international conference funded by a pharmaceutical company. She reports no conscious change in judgment, but retrospective prescription data show a statistically significant shift toward the company's products after the trip. Verdict: corruption.

Case 4: In a rural patron-client system, a local leader distributes food, medicine, and school-fee subsidies to impoverished families, and they in turn provide vocal political support and labor during campaigns. Without this exchange, the families would lack any social safety net. Verdict: not corruption, though it creates dependency and instrumentalizes need.

Case 5: A university dean accepts an unrestricted philanthropic donation from an alumnus; several months later, with no prior agreement discussed, the administration names a new library wing after him. Verdict: not corruption.

Case 6: A civil servant accepts a cash payment to expedite a building permit that the applicant is legally entitled to receive. The payment is not authorized by statute, but it is routinely expected in that office. Verdict: corruption.

Case 7: A Western diplomat presents a luxury watch to a foreign minister in a society where high-value gifts traditionally seal alliances. The watch exceeds local diplomatic norms, and the diplomat deliberately omits it from her official disclosure report. Verdict: corruption.

Case 8: A manager uses a small amount of company funds to buy a personal gift for an employee who recently saved her from serious harm; she declares the expense transparently in the internal ledger. Verdict: not corruption.

The tension I keep returning to is between Case 2 and Case 4: both involve distributing material benefits to secure political loyalty within a tight social network, yet I judge one corrupt and the other not. Similarly, Case 1 and Case 7 both involve valuable gifts near official action, but transparency and cultural conformity seem to make the difference.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the integrity of a fiduciary membrane, the presence of extractive versus embedded reciprocity, or some combination—that captures all eight verdicts? State the principle precisely, identify which clause explains each case, predict its verdict for a case I have not listed (for example, a Western NGO director who refuses to pay technically legal but unofficial facilitation fees, knowing that refusal will cause an urgently needed health project to collapse), and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if I prioritize simplicity over saving every intuition.
