Here's an argument I worked out about deontological rules and layered decision systems, and I can't tell whether it's valid or where it cheats.

(1) Deontology holds that there is a strict moral boundary between doing harm and merely allowing it, and that some acts — like deliberately killing an innocent person — are wrong regardless of the good they produce.

(2) Now imagine a layered protocol. I design an automated rule R that, if condition C occurs, executes action A (killing one innocent to save five). I embed R in a system I do not control in real time, then walk away.

(3) If I personally performed A, I would clearly violate (1).

(4) Once R is active, I am absent at the moment of execution, and R itself is not a moral agent capable of wrongdoing. So at the execution layer, no one with moral standing "does" the harm; it is only produced by a deterministic process.

(5) Apply the same reasoning backward: building R was not itself killing anyone; it was only arranging conditions for a future non-agent to act.

(6) Repeat this across every layer — designer, deployer, maintainer, observer. By (4) and (5), responsibility seems to dissolve. Either deontology must declare that constructing any system with lethal trade-offs is itself a direct violation of (1), which seems to forbid almost all autonomous infrastructure, or it must admit that the doing/allowing distinction cannot survive layered delegation.

(7) I want to keep both (1) and the intuition that designing a system is morally different from pulling a trigger, but (6) says I can't have both. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather preserve (1) and (4) if possible, though I'm unsure.

Please tell me where this reasoning is most likely to fail — whether one step quietly changes what "doing" means when it moves from a person to a system, or whether I'm mixing up individual choice with collective responsibility. Also say what would change your mind: for instance, if there were a clear case showing that people remain fully responsible for harms produced by rules they designed, would that overturn your diagnosis?
