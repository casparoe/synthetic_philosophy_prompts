Consider the standard regulatory framework in many advanced democracies: registered lobbyists may supply legislators with expert briefings and proposed statutory language, but they are barred from making direct campaign donations to those same legislators; meanwhile, the lobbyists' corporate clients may funnel unlimited funds through independent-expenditure committees that do not formally coordinate with candidates. What theory of political corruption and democratic representation does this tripartite separation—information channel open, direct contribution channel closed, indirect expenditure channel open—presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of when influence becomes illegitimate, say whether the framework treats corruption as essentially a transactional quid pro quo or as a structural distortion of deliberation, and determine whether the design is consistent with an epistemic conception of representation that values legislative expertise. If the underlying theory turned out to be incoherent, what minimal institutional change would align the practice with a coherent view?
