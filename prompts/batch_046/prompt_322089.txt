I'm working through an argument in my philosophy of science seminar that seems to push the no-free-lunch result into global inductive skepticism, and I can't locate the damage. (1) Wolpert's theorem establishes that for every inductive learning procedure, there exists a problem distribution on which its error rate equals that of random selection, when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible target functions. (2) Human scientific reasoning is just one such inductive procedure, implemented biologically. (3) So there must exist some distribution of phenomena—perhaps governing our local region—where human science is no more reliable than coin-flipping. (4) To justify trusting science in our world, we need a reason to think our world belongs to the subset where human cognition succeeds. (5) Any such reason—say, that physical regularities are locally continuous—must itself be established inductively from past success. (6) Therefore we can never non-circularly restrict the domain of application, leaving no rational basis for preferring scientific induction to arbitrary guessing. The conclusion feels catastrophic. Which premise should I reject? I'm especially reluctant to deny (1) or (5). Is the flaw an equivocation between formal algorithms and embodied cognitive systems constrained by physics, or does it lie in the implicit uniform prior over worlds? And if I block the argument by appealing to physical computability constraints that render most "NFL worlds" impossible, haven't I just smuggled in the very inductive justification I was trying to avoid?
