I have just finished a rather exhausting discussion with a close friend about whether privacy is best understood as individual control over information, as a condition of autonomy, or as something that only makes sense within specific social contexts. I am currently taking a course on information ethics and find myself puzzled by an institutional arrangement I encountered in a case study: in several Scandinavian municipalities, public libraries are required by statute to publish fully anonymised aggregate borrowing data—down to the neighbourhood level—to demonstrate cultural engagement and justify funding, yet the same legal framework explicitly denies individual patrons any right to access their own borrowing histories, on the grounds that an individual’s record of intellectual exploration belongs to a private “cognitive sphere” that the state must not invade, even when the subject herself requests disclosure. The apparent asymmetry is striking: the collective pattern is treated as public property, while the individual trace is treated as inviolably private, even to its originator.

What philosophical commitments about privacy, knowledge, and autonomy does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes both provisions coherent rather than contradictory—distinguishing, for instance, whether it relies on a relational or contextual view of information, whether it treats privacy as a property right in data or as a boundary around self-formation, and whether it implicitly assigns different moral weights to transparency and opacity depending on whether the information flows upward toward the state or downward toward the citizen. Is the implicit view internally consistent, or does the practice quietly depend on two incompatible conceptions—perhaps one that sees privacy as anonymity-in-the-crowd and another that sees it as sovereign control over one’s narrative? Furthermore, say what rival theories of privacy this arrangement quietly rules out, and describe what would have to change in library policy and data governance if we replaced its presupposed view with a straightforwardly informational-self-determination model, where any data that concerns an individual should be equally accessible to that individual regardless of aggregation.
