I have noticed a recurring institutional phenomenon that does not sit comfortably within the standard Austin-Searle taxonomy of illocutionary acts, and I am trying to determine whether it has received a precise designation in the literature. The pattern is this: an authorized body issues a formal utterance—say, a faculty senate resolution declaring that "environmental impact shall be regarded as relevant to all future hiring decisions," or a corporate board adopting a statement that "long-term stakeholder welfare is a legitimate criterion for strategic evaluation." These utterances do not allocate resources, impose sanctions, or alter legal entitlements; they are not directives in any robust sense. Nor do they bring about a new institutional fact in the manner of a declaration—no office is created, no contract is formed, no status is assigned to an individual or object. Yet after the utterance is made, participants within the institution begin treating certain considerations as admissible or weighty in practical reasoning in ways that were previously considered out of scope. The effect is not merely rhetorical or expressive: the change operates as a shift in the normative framework that governs subsequent deliberation, rather than as a single premise within that deliberation. I have observed analogues in legislative "sense of the chamber" motions and in judicial dicta that do not bind but reshape the boundaries of relevant argument. Does this specific performative function—modifying the conditions of admissibility for reasons within an institution without issuing a command or constituting a fact—have an established name? How should it be distinguished from weak directives, from declarations that establish institutional status, and from expressive acts that merely manifest collective attitude? If there is no recognized term, what conceptual boundaries should define it, and which borderline cases—such as purely ceremonial proclamations or strategic ambiguity—would fall outside its scope?
