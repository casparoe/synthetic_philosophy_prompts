I'm currently taking an advanced epistemology seminar, and we've been working through Plato's Meno problem—specifically the question of why knowledge should be valued more highly than mere true belief when both appear to guide action with equal success. The standard treatments I've encountered tend to resolve the tension by appealing to justification, reliability, or some form of modal safety, but I'm trying to understand a finer conceptual distinction that seems to underlie these answers. Suppose we distinguish between what might be called "anchored" true beliefs—beliefs sustained by a stable causal-informational pathway that connects the agent to the fact in question—and "floating" true beliefs, which happen to correspond to reality but lack any such sustaining structural link. On this framing, knowledge might be conceived not simply as true belief plus some additive property, but as true belief that is anchored in this specific way.

My difficulty is in seeing exactly where the value problem lands once this distinction is in play. Is the extra value of knowledge over true belief located intrinsically in the anchoring relation itself, or is anchoring valuable only instrumentally—for instance, because it preserves the belief across counterfactual perturbations or integrates it into a broader explanatory framework? Furthermore, if the value turns out to be purely instrumental, does that undermine the Platonic intuition that knowledge is categorically, not merely contingently, better than true belief? Could you explain this conceptual issue in detail? In particular, I'd like to understand whether treating knowledge as anchored true belief genuinely solves the Meno problem or merely relocates it to a prior question about why causal-informational anchoring possesses independent epistemic worth, and whether that relocation is philosophically satisfying or just a deferral.
