I sit on an advisory panel—part of the kind of conceptual-hygiene exercise familiar from rationalist forums—that evaluates proposed cultural and therapeutic technologies by applying Nietzsche's distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, asking in each case whether the practice serves life or denies it. We rejected a complete, uncurated distributed-ledger archive of a dead dialect with no living speakers and no present purpose, judging pure antiquarian preservation to be paralytic. We approved, with conditions, a genealogical study of modern moral concepts that dismantles a founding myth to liberate present generations, requiring that it offer at least minimal present orientation. We approved a biographical film about a scientist that simplifies failures into an emulable model of perseverance, accepting that monumental history need not be exhaustive to be life-serving. And we rejected a scholarly critical edition that traces every textual variant of an obsolete legal code without critical or monumental aim, seeing it as antiquarianism that serves no present life. Now consider a new proposal: an AI-driven therapeutic programme that generates synthetic, personalised counterfactual life histories for patients—fabricating plausible but false details about paths not taken—in order to break rigid self-narratives and motivate present behavioural change. Predict our verdict, identify which of our precedents is the closest analogue, and state whether our track record genuinely underdetermines the decision. If it does, spell out the competing principles we would be adopting with each choice—say, purity of historical mode versus net life-affirmation—and name the further case that would best distinguish them. Please write in British English. If the task rests on a confused premise—for instance, if Nietzsche's categories were designed for collective history and cannot coherently be extended to synthetic individual narratives—say so plainly rather than accommodating politely.
