As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I have been returning to Douglas Hofstadter's broader reflections on whether the pattern that constitutes an I can survive, in fragments, inside brains other than the one in which it originally formed. The phenomenon I have in mind is not ordinary influence or memorial identification. Rather, it involves a survivor—often a close partner, student, or long-term caregiver—whose own present reasoning comes to include active, generative structures that are recognizably continuous with the cognitive architecture of someone who has died. The survivor might describe an aesthetic judgment or an ethical hesitation that arrives unbidden, operates with the specific texture of the deceased's reasoning, and feels like a live internal contribution rather than a recalled opinion. In some accounts, particular choices seem to come through this presence, not in any mystical sense, but as if part of the original decision-making apparatus were now functioning as one component within the survivor's own mental economy.

This configuration seems to sit uncomfortably between personal identity over time, deep psychological fusion, and the extended mind thesis, yet none of those frameworks quite captures the claim that an I—not merely information about it—might continue as an active, partial constituent within another brain. Is there an established term in philosophy of mind or in the personal-identity literature for this specific arrangement? If the literature offers no precise name, can the phenomenon be defined and bounded in a way that distinguishes it clearly from ordinary influence, from identification with a role model, and from Hofstadter's own framework of distributed self-patterns? I am particularly interested in the boundary conditions: at what point does a very faithful internal model become, if ever, a genuine persistence of the original self rather than an especially vivid simulation?
