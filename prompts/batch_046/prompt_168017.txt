As a novelist drafting a philosophical interlude for a novel on memory and dissolution, I have sketched the following essay notes and would welcome your comments on their coherence, conceptual interest, and—above all—their accuracy. The core claim is that the Buddhist doctrine of anattā does not merely deny a substantial ego but undermines the very grammar of narrative identity: if experience is a stream of momentary dharmas—form, feeling, perception, volition, consciousness—without an owner, then MacIntyre’s idea of a life as a story with a single protagonist is not just empirically false but structurally impossible, since there is no subject for the verb “to live.” I then propose a speculative replacement: rather than a biography with plot, a life might be modeled as an acoustic field, a sustained chord of overlapping harmonic overtones in which each momentary consciousness modifies the whole without belonging to anyone. This avoids both essentialism and the mereological fallacy of treating the stream as a composite object. I am concerned, however, that I have smuggled in Western metaphysics of resonance foreign to the Pali Canon, and that I present my rejection of narrative identity and my acoustic metaphor as if they followed orthodox Abhidharma rather than being my own constructions.

Please respond with focused remarks on the argument’s strengths and weaknesses, particularly on whether the move from anattā to the impossibility of narrative structure is sound. Crucially, separate what is settled among scholars of Buddhist philosophy and contemporary personal-identity theory from what is genuinely contested or purely speculative in my framing; do not present my acoustic-resonance image, my grammatical argument, or my reading of anattā as consensus views, and flag them clearly as minority interpretations or original inventions where appropriate. Keep your response concise and conceptual; I am not seeking a bibliography or reading list, only conceptual clarification.
