I'm taking an intro phil course and we just covered the problem of other minds, and I've been arguing with my roommate about it, but now I'm stuck on something that feels like it should have a name. Here's the pattern I keep seeing: people will say we can't really know other humans aren't philosophical zombies because all we observe is behavior, not consciousness. Okay, fine. But then the same person will say their dog is "obviously just running on instinct, no inner experience," like that's just the default and needs no defense. And then with AI they flip yet again - if a chatbot says "I'm scared," they treat that report as surprisingly credible evidence, maybe even more credible than a silent human's behavior, because "the system wouldn't generate that unless it mapped onto something real."

So there's this weird sliding scale: hyper-skeptical about other people, dismissive-by-default about animals, and strangely credulous about AI self-reports. Is there an established concept for this kind of uneven epistemic posture across different kinds of minds? I know about the argument from analogy and anthropomorphism, but those feel like pieces rather than the whole pattern. If there's no name, what should we call it, and where are its edges - does it cover being skeptical about infants but not adults, or about people from other cultures? I want more than just a bare label; tell me what the phenomenon actually is, what it isn't, and how it differs from ordinary bias. Also please keep the settled stuff seperate from what's genuinely contested - like, don't present analogical reasoning or behaviorism as if there's a clear consensus, since I get the sense philosophers actually disagree a lot about how we justify beliefs about other minds.
