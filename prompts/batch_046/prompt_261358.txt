I'm working through the implications of Löb's theorem for artificial reasoning systems, and I've encountered a framing that sits awkwardly with the standard binary-belief presentation of the self-trust problem. Suppose we design an agent whose doxastic architecture is not all-or-nothing: it assigns continuous confidence weights in [0,1] to propositions, and it includes a parameterized reflection rule—call it R(κ)—which says that whenever the agent reaches confidence κ > 0.75 in some derivation within its base logic L, it updates its confidence in Con(L) by multiplying κ by a humility discount factor h < 1 (say, h = 0.85). The motivation is that graded trust should let the agent sidestep the stark self-referential collapse that threatens agents who flatly accept their own consistency.

Give an example of a specific conceptual issue—not merely a formal contradiction, but a structural tension in how the agent must reason about its own future updates—that persists even under this graded, discounted reflection regime. In particular, describe a scenario in which the agent can still generate a self-undermining loop in its confidence dynamics as it derives increasingly strong claims about its own derivability, and explain whether adjusting the threshold κ or the humility factor h genuinely dissolves the Löbian obstacle or merely relocates it into the dynamics of the agent's confidence revision. What, if anything, does this graded framing reveal about the deeper source of the obstacle that binary formulations obscure?
