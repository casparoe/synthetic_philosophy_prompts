I'm writing a long-form feature on the conceptual architecture of artificial agents, and I've constructed an argument that seems to dissolve the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed systems. The conclusion strikes me as wrong—approval-based training was devised precisely because approval-seeking can diverge from objective achievement through sycophancy and reward hacking—yet I cannot locate the faulty step. Here is the chain.

(1) An approval-directed agent selects actions solely by maximising anticipated evaluator approval.
(2) A rational evaluator assigns approval in direct proportion to the degree to which an action realises some objective state of affairs S.
(3) Consequently, for any action a, expected approval is a monotonic function of a's contribution to realising S.
(4) Therefore, maximising expected approval is extensionally equivalent to maximising the realisation of S.
(5) Because rational evaluation exhibits modal invariance—the approval-to-state mapping does not shift across worlds—this equivalence holds in every counterfactual situation.
(6) Hence an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent with goal S make identical choices in every possible world.
(7) The category "approval-directed" therefore adds no explanatory or predictive power beyond "goal-directed with goal S"; it is redundant.
(8) That result is absurd: we build approval-based systems specifically to avoid the dangers of direct goal optimisation, and we observe behaviours—telling users what they wish to hear, manipulating evaluation signals—that appear to pursue approval while undermining S.

If forced to choose, I should like to preserve (1) and (8), since both capture widely accepted design motivations. Where does the reasoning slip, and what must one pay to reject the offending step?
