I've been trying to understand why Gettier-style cases are still treated as a distinct problem in epistemology rather than as a special instance of error-propagation in belief systems. The classic examples—where someone derives a true belief from a false premise, such as inferring that a particular job candidate has ten coins in his pocket based on a mistaken belief about who will be hired—are usually read as showing that justification can be present, truth can be present, and yet knowledge is absent in a way that demands an extra condition. But this reading seems to presuppose that justification travels along inferential lines like a current: corrupt the source, and everything it powers is epistemically tainted. If we instead model belief-fixation as distributed across a coherence network, where the warrant for any node depends on the global predictive and explanatory performance of the system, then the failure of one premise doesn't automatically drain warrant from every conclusion that touches it. In such a framework, the true belief that happens to be supported by the false inference might still inherit adequate anchoring from other parts of the network—say, direct perceptual tracking of the coins in the pocket—so that the only real failure is localized to the false intermediate belief. Given this, is there a non-ad-hoc reason to insist that Gettier cases reveal a structural gap in the analysis of knowledge, rather than just illustrating that linear models of justification are inadequate? Or have I missed why distributed warrant can't accommodate these examples without either collapsing into a form of reliabilism or allowing that knowledge can be genuinely grounded in falsehood?
