I've been reflecting on a pattern in practical decision-making that seems related to sunk-cost reasoning but doesn't quite match the usual account. In the standard fallacy, a decision-maker treats unrecoverable past expenditures as if they justify further investment—say, continuing a failing software rollout because "we've already spent two years on it." But I keep noticing a different structure: the decision-maker privately accepts that the future returns are negative and that the past money is gone, yet she persists because stopping now would make the loss visible as her own error, with real downstream costs—lost credibility with a board, electoral punishment, or damage to her authority. A city council finishes a bridge to nowhere not because anyone thinks traffic will justify it, but because abandoning it mid-construction would be read as an admission of corruption or incompetence; a film director pours more money into a clearly doomed production because pulling the plug now would signal to investors that her original green light was a mistake. In these cases, the sunk expenditure isn't being miscounted in a forward-looking calculation; it's being honored because the act of honoring it avoids a separate, non-financial penalty attached to visible failure.

This feels distinct both from the cognitive error usually called the sunk-cost fallacy and from ordinary escalation of commitment driven by genuine optimism about eventual turnaround. Is there an established concept or literature for this specific variant—one in which continuing a failing course serves as a reputational or strategic shield rather than a reasoning mistake? How should it be distinguished from standard escalation: does it require an observing audience capable of attributing failure, does it depend on the decision-maker having made a prior public commitment, and should it still be classed as irrational if the reputational damage avoided is genuinely larger than the additional resource loss? If no precise term exists, what are the boundary conditions that would separate it from adjacent phenomena—signaling behavior that merely happens to involve prior investment, commitment devices in game-theoretic settings, or self-deception about future success—and what would be the most accurate way to name and delimit it?
