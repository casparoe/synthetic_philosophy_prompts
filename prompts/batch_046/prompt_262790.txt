Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. Design a realistic, single-paragraph scenario set in an ordinary workplace or family context—no science fiction, no neuroscience, no explicit mention of determinism or free will—in which an agent performs an action that invites moral evaluation. Engineer the case so that three conditions hold simultaneously: (a) the action flows deterministically from a stable character trait of the agent; (b) that trait was formed entirely by environmental factors outside the agent’s control during early development; (c) there exists a relevantly similar counterpart agent who possesses a different trait and who, placed in the exact same triggering situation, would have acted differently. Crucially, the situation itself must not be one that the agent could have avoided or foreseen differently, and the outcome must be fully determined once the action is taken, so that neither circumstantial nor resultant luck is present—only constitutive luck remains in play.

After presenting the scenario, provide the reasoning that each of the following three positions must deliver: (1) a pure control-principle theorist who denies that moral evaluation can apply where the agent lacks control over the relevant factor; (2) a restricted moral-luck theorist who accepts resultant luck (outcomes outside control) but rejects constitutive luck (character outside control); (3) a full moral-luck theorist who accepts both resultant and constitutive luck. State precisely which feature of your design forces position (3) to diverge from position (2) despite the absence of resultant luck. Then explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed—specifically, whether it collapses into a standard resultant-luck case or into a no-luck case. Finally, show how a defender of position (1) would attack the scenario without denying any empirical premise, and identify the exact structural assumption they must reject for their attack to succeed.
