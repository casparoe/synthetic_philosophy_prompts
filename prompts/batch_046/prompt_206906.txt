After a long argument last night with a friend about whether containment architectures for advanced AI systems are an implicit admission that alignment has already failed, I need an operational framework for the review board I sit on. We evaluate deployment proposals for high-capability models in high-stakes domains—currently legal-reasoning and clinical decision support—where errors carry severe costs, and for each proposed safeguard we must determine whether it functions as a control measure (treating the system as potentially misaligned and constraining behavior regardless of its internal objectives) or as an alignment measure (treating the system as cooperative but flawed and attempting to correct, verify, or refine its goals). The friend insisted that mixing the two undermines both; I believe they can be sequenced, but only with explicit rules that prevent control mechanisms from blocking the feedback loops alignment measures require, and prevent alignment measures from assuming access or transparency that control mechanisms deliberately deny.

Please write a reusable decision procedure that my board can apply in a thirty-minute review session without consulting philosophy literature. The procedure should have ordered steps: first, classify any proposed safeguard using a small set of operational tests rather than theoretical labels; second, determine whether placing it before or after other safeguards creates a structural conflict, specifically identifying when a control measure denies the observational access an alignment measure needs, or when an alignment measure assumes a cooperative posture that a preceding control measure has already rendered moot; third, specify the conditions that yield an accept-with-sequencing-constraints verdict, a reject verdict, or an escalation to human judgment because the conflict is one of values rather than engineering. Present this as a flowchart-in-words with explicit decision nodes, and flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated—particularly when a safeguard is ambiguous between the two categories.

Then apply the procedure to three safeguards proposed for a hypothetical legal-reasoning system: (a) a network-isolated sandbox that permits only batched, human-reviewed outputs with no real-time external interaction; (b) an interpretability pipeline that audits internal representations for indicators of deceptive alignment and can halt deployment if such indicators exceed a calibrated threshold; and (c) a reflective-equilibrium protocol in which the system is presented with its own constitutional principles and asked to revise its goal structure through structured dialogue, with human oversight restricted to the final stage.

For each case, show the classification, the required sequence relative to the others, any conflicts the procedure detects, and the board’s mandated action. Include at least one case where the procedure demands that a control measure take precedence over an alignment measure—yielding a result that conflicts with the intuition that alignment should always come first—and explain precisely why the procedure requires this ordering, marking the exact node where human judgment could legitimately override it.

The deliverable should not depend on access to external sources: do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific papers or books by title and date. Avoid framing this as a literature review; instead, translate the conceptual distinction into criteria that a reviewer without formal philosophy training can apply consistently. Make explicit what the procedure cannot settle—particularly cases where a safeguard serves both functions simultaneously—and state the limits of its applicability.
