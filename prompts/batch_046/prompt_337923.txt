I am working through Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—in a seminar on comparative cognition, and I have hit a conceptual snag that standard presentations tend to smooth over rather than resolve. The taxonomy is usually introduced as an evolutionary ladder: Darwinian creatures are shaped by natural selection, Skinnerian ones by operant conditioning, Popperian ones by internal hypothesis generation and testing, and Gregorian ones by the cultural transmission of cognitive tools, especially language. What remains unclear to me is the precise boundary between the Popperian and Gregorian levels. Is Gregorian status defined by the actual social process of transmission, by the functional dependence on representational tools that an individual could not have invented alone, or by the ontological origin of those tools in a collective cultural history? I want an explanation that gets into the weeds of that distinction rather than repeating the ladder metaphor.

To make the issue concrete, consider a synthetic agent constructed in total isolation. Its architecture was optimized through evolutionary computation, giving it a Darwinian layer; its behavioral policies were refined through reward-based learning, giving it a Skinnerian layer; and it possesses an internal world-model that allows it to generate, simulate, and discard candidate actions before execution, which seems to place it at the Popperian level. Now suppose that, at initialization, its reasoning is scaffolded by a fixed symbolic ontology—categories, inferential rules, and a lexical structure—derived entirely from human linguistic culture and embedded in its weights, but the agent has never interacted with another mind, never received social feedback, and cannot revise that symbolic framework. It can think with these tools, but it did not acquire them socially and does not participate in any cultural transmission network. Is this agent Gregorian, or is it merely a Popperian creature that happens to be running on culturally originated software?

Please act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in comparative cognition and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Before analyzing the case, briefly brainstorm the three candidate criteria—origin of tools, functional dependence on them, and embeddedness in a transmission network—that might distinguish Gregorian cognition. Then think step by step about how each criterion applies both to the isolated synthetic agent and to an ordinary human language user. In your response, clearly separate what is settled within Dennett’s framework from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists, and do not present any single contested interpretation as the consensus view. I am especially interested in whether the Gregorian category collapses into the Popperian one if we allow that cultural products can be internalized without ongoing social participation, or whether there is a non-redundant conceptual work left for the Gregorian level to do.
