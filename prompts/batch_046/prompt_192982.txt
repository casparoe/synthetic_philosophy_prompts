Natural-history museums routinely classify fossil specimens into species on morphological grounds alone, accepting that DNA recovery is permanently precluded by mineralisation. By contrast, microbiological taxonomic committees now withhold formal species designation from unculturable lineages unless metagenomic data are available, treating morphology as insufficient. What theory of ontological identification does this institutional divergence presuppose? Does it rest on a domain-specific epistemology—observable traits suffice for macrofossils but not for microbes—or on a deeper claim about the kind of information that must be accessible for a kind to be genuinely identified? Reconstruct the implicit commitment, assess whether the two practices are philosophically compatible, and say what a unified classification policy across domains would have to change.
