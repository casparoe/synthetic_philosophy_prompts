I'm a counselor at a community clinic running 30-minute intakes with clients who say they feel stuck or empty, and my superviser wants a consistent framework but knows I don't have a philosophy PhD. I want a reusable decision procedue — like a flowchart in words — built from Moritz Schlick's view that genuine meaning comes from playful, self-contained activity rather than from achieving external goals or purposes. The checklist should run in order: first ask what the client's main non-work activity is, then whether it's pursued mainly for its own absorption, then whether anticipation of reward or status dominates, then whether there's that 'youthful' presence Schlick describes — being fully in the act without instrumental anxiety — versus treating the act as a means. It needs clear stop points: if answers come out instrumentally dominated, I document 'not meaningful in Schlick's sense' and shift to values-based counseling rather than pushing activity change; if they come out playfully absorbed, I note potential meaning-source but still check conflicts. Crucially, it must flag where introspeciton is unreliable, since clients often rationalize status-seeking or duty as 'just for fun.' Apply it to three cases: a retired accountant playing chess purely for rankings, a parent caring for a disabled child with zero recognition or playfulness, and a wealthy person spending all day in immersive video games but neglecting obligations — that last one should be the case where the procedue says it's genuinely meaningful and I have to live with an answer I don't like. Include exactly where judgement can't be eliminated, what the procedue can't do (like resolving clashes with religious frameworks that locate meaning in duty or transcendence), and remind me that I need supervisor sign-off if I recommend a major life shift based on it. Keep it practical for half-hour sessions with documentation requirements.
