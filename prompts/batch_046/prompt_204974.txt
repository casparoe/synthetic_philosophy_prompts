I am a novelist leading a private seminar for fellow fiction writers who wish to use evolutionary concepts as narrative tools rather than as mere backdrop. In next week's ninety-minute session I must teach the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique—the argument that not every biological trait is an adaptation shaped by natural selection, but that some arise as structural byproducts of other architectural constraints—without letting the discussion collapse into the familiar popular-science caricatures. Do not explain the critique to me; instead, design the teaching sequence. First, specify the conceptual prerequisite that must be firmly in place before the term spandrel can be introduced without being mistaken for evolutionary accident or vestigial organ, then lay out the order in which selectionist explanation, architectural constraint, byproduct, and exaptation should follow, with brief justification for each dependency. Second, propose the single carrying example that should sustain the session. I am inclined to use a literary construction: a protagonist's chronic insomnia that exists not because it optimally serves the plot's resolution but because the novel's nocturnal architecture demands it, then mapping that back onto the biological case. Assess whether this analogy will clarify or confuse these writers, and if you reject it, specify the biological example that should replace it and precisely why the classic San Marco architectural spandrel should be retained or set aside. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form—that the critique denies natural selection altogether, that spandrels are necessarily functionless, and that any trait with a secondary use must therefore be an adaptation—indicating the exact point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and neutralized. Fourth, design one concluding exercise or question that reveals whether a participant understands the distinction between a trait explained by selection and one explained by structural constraint, rather than merely repeating that not everything is an adaptation. Finally, state explicitly which aspects of contemporary evolutionary-developmental biology or quantitative genetics I should openly leave unmentioned at this level, with a brief justification for the omission. The participants have no formal biology training, and I want them to leave with a usable conceptual distinction rather than a list of facts.
