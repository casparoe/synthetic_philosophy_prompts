After a recent falling-out with an old friend, during which he made a statement about our shared past that seemed to undermine its own credibility in a way I could not quite articulate, I have become unreasonably preoccupied with self-reference and the stability of truth. Last week at a departmental seminar, two philosophers of language—call them Mara and Julian—got into a sustained argument about the Liar paradox and whether any theory of truth can accommodate natural-language self-reference without collapsing into incoherence. I am reporting their exchange as neutrally as I can; I genuinely do not know whose reasoning holds up.

(1) Mara opened by insisting that the strengthened Liar—"This very statement is not true"—is not a genuine paradox but a syntactic misfire analogous to an ungrammatical sentence. She argued that natural language is not a formal system and that we should adopt a Tarski-style hierarchy: "true" applies only to sentences at a lower level, which eliminates self-application cleanly. She added that this is not an artificial restriction but a descriptive feature of how we actually learn to use the word—children never apply "true" to their own utterances in the same breath.

(2) Julian replied that the hierarchy fails descriptively. In ordinary conversation we constantly evaluate our own claims, and we do not switch linguistic levels to do so. He proposed instead a contextualist account on which "true" is indexical like "now": its extension shifts with the context of utterance, so the strengthened Liar attempts to fix a value that the context has already moved past. He said this preserves the unity of the concept of truth without allowing contradiction.

(3) Mara countered that contextualism merely relocates the problem. If the Liar is false in context C1 and true in C2, we still need an explanation for why the speaker in C1 cannot consistently endorse it, and contextualism offers none. She then offered a revision-theoretic alternative: truth is defined circularly but is revisable across reasoning stages, so the Liar receives an unstable, non-classical status rather than a contradiction. She claimed this captures our actual epistemic practice—we treat paradoxical statements as suspicious and withhold assent rather than exploding into triviality.

(4) Julian said that revision theory saves consistency only by making truth non-transitive and epistemically opaque: a statement can be true at stage n, false at stage n+1, and true again later, with no principled stopping point. He argued that this is worse than hierarchy, because at least the Tarskian view gives us a stable, if stratified, notion of truth. He concluded that both contextualism and revisionism pay an unacceptable price for saving the appearance of self-reference, and that the honest conclusion is that natural language should be regimented away from self-application altogether—not because it is impossible, but because it is conceptually destructive.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled middle ground between hierarchical restriction and circular revision that neither has considered.
