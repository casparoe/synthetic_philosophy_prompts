I'm drafting a handout for a LessWrong-style philosophy discussion, and I want a diagnostic taxonomy—not a roll call of philosophers, but a decision tree that sorts positions on Benatar's asymmetry by what they actually commit to about potential lives.

Start with the root question: does the asymmetry between the absence of pain being good and the absence of pleasure being not-bad, for beings who never exist, reflect a real axiological feature, or is it an artifact of comparing actual people with non-actual ones?

Then branch on three successive distinctions:
1. Is the asymmetry best read as a welfare comparison, a claim about procreative obligations, or about rational preference?
2. Does it imply that avoiding suffering carries greater moral weight than promoting happiness, or that the two are asymmetrically balanced?
3. Apply the shadow replacement case: we prevent the conception of one potential child who might suffer, but this does not causally enable any different child to exist. Does the asymmetry still generate a reason?

Each leaf should carry a label—strict axiological asymmetry, prudential antinatalism, replacement-sensitive liberalism, unnamed, etc.—and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, especially on whether never-existing beings can be harmed or benefited by our choices, and whether the view implies smaller populations are ceteris paribus preferable.

Flag any pair of leaves that collapse into each other, and note any logically open but unoccupied combination, with a brief diagnosis: incoherent, unpopular, or unexplored.
