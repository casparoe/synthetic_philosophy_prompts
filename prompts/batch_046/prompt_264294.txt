A colleague summarized the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on punishment and moral responsibility in these terms. Dennett argues that advances in neuroscience undermine the metaphysical basis of retributive justice—above all, the notion that agents are uncaused origins of their actions—so punishment can be justified only by forward-looking aims such as deterrence, rehabilitation, and the maintenance of social practices in which we adopt the intentional stance toward one another. Caruso, on this account, accepts that libertarian free will is untenable but maintains that Dennett’s position is unstable: if punishment is justified solely by its protective effects, then there is no principled barrier to applying preemptive restraints to individuals identified by predictive risk profiles, since both post-offense punishment and preemptive intervention serve the same functional end. The summary reports that Dennett replies by insisting that the intentional stance is not merely predictive but normatively structured—it requires treating a being as currently responsive to reasons, a condition that is absent in purely predictive cases—thereby generating a non-instrumental constraint against preemptive restraint. The colleague concludes that Caruso therefore misfires: Dennett is not smuggling desert back in through the back door, but deriving limits on punishment from the constitutive conditions of responsible agency itself. Is this summary fair? In particular, does Dennett really reduce punishment entirely to instrumental functions, and is Caruso’s challenge best read as an argument about predictive preemption rather than about the moral impermissibility of harming those who lack ultimate authorship? If the summary distorts either view, what would its correction cost?
