I am a novelist whose current manuscript is organized around what I have come to call "negative architecture"—a narrative design in which relief is not deferred but structurally withheld, so that absence functions as a load-bearing element rather than a decorative lack. In discussions with fellow writers I have begun to wonder whether this craft intuition touches on something philosophically substantive: specifically, the cluster of positions gathered under negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics, with their insistence that reducing suffering takes moral precedence over producing happiness, and with the associated asymmetry that adding a happy life does not compensate for adding a suffering one.

Please restate the central commitments of negative utilitarianism—its prioritization of suffering-reduction, its treatment of pleasure and pain as asymmetrical in moral weight, its handling of the non-identity problem when applied to potential suffering lives, and its resistance to aggregation that treats one severe suffering as exchangeable with many mild ones—using only the vocabulary of narrative craft that I work with daily: dramatic irony, catharsis, narrative economy, the ethics of invented character pain, plot arcs that terminate in unresolved absence, authorial responsibility for gratuitous versus earned suffering, and the singular embodied perspective of a protagonist rather than a statistical population. Translate as faithfully as you can, but do not smuggle in standard ethical terminology to cover gaps; if a claim has no counterpart in how a novelist thinks about the suffering she puts on the page, say so plainly.

Then deliver the residue report: identify which philosophical claims survive this translation intact—perhaps the asymmetry between pleasure and pain maps onto the difference between a gratuitous happy ending and a tragically earned one; which survive only through distortion—such as treating moral agents as authors with obligations to characters they have invented; and which disappear entirely, particularly quantitative aggregation across lives and the non-identity reasoning about whether it is wrong to bring a suffering life into being when that life is the only one available. Conclude by assessing whether these residues reveal a structural limitation in narrative craft—its inevitable focus on singular, embodied experience over collective calculation—or whether they expose hidden assumptions in negative utilitarianism, such as the comparability of suffering across subjects, that become unstable once we move from statistical populations to the irreducible particularity of a character whose pain we are asked to witness rather than weigh.
