Plan a teaching arc for a ninety-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of religion. The audience has studied the Tractatus but has not encountered Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief. The goal is to convey his view that religious belief operates not as rational assent to propositions but as being held by a picture that regulates the whole of life, without collapsing it into emotion, ethics, or fideism.

Specify the dependency structure: what conceptual distinction must be established first so that the claim is even audible—perhaps the gap between belief as a propositional attitude and belief as a form of life—and why that distinction must precede any discussion of evidence.

Choose the single carrying example or scenario that should anchor the session, and explain what it must include and exclude. In particular, address why the familiar Last Judgment comparison should not open the discussion, and what should replace it as the entry point.

Map the misconceptions the students will form in the order they are likely to arise: that Wittgenstein is a fideist who shelters belief from rational scrutiny; that he renders religious language meaningless by denying it empirical content; that he reduces religion to moral practice. For each, identify the precise point in the sequence where it should be preempted or corrected, and how.

Design one final diagnostic question or exercise that reveals whether the students have grasped the regulative, picture-governed character of belief rather than treating it as a deficient form of evidential reasoning.

Finally, state explicitly what well-known framing or comparison—perhaps the parallel with language-games in the Philosophical Investigations, or the contrast with logical positivism—should be deliberately left out at this level, with a brief justification.
