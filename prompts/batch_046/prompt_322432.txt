I've been thinking a lot lately about the German nuclear exit versus France keeping their fleet running, and I've come round to a fairly specific view: nuclear power isn't just one decarbonisation option among others, but functions as an "institutional anchor" that stops the political economy of transition collapsing into fossil-fuel backup during stress periods. In Germany you see this oscillation — coal plants kept running despite climate targets — whereas France's grid seems to avoid that kind of regulatory drift. I'm not claiming renewables can't eventually scale to replace it, but I think without nuclear in the mix, the transition tends to lock in dependancy on gas and coal whenever demand spikes or wind dies down.

What I want is a proper stress-test: what would actually count against this "institutional anchor" idea? Not the standard objections about waste or accidents, but the defeaters that hit the core claim. Which patterns in France's grid management or Germany's post-2023 baseload crisis would genuinely undermine it? Are there nuclear-heavy systems that still experienced the same oscillation, or renewables-heavy grids that achieved frequency stability without any nuclear present? I also wonder if the argument secretly confuses correlation with causation, or assumes a level of state capacity that doesnt travel to other countries. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of my view would concede, and flag if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive.
