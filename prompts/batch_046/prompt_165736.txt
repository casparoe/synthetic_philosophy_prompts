"Supervaluationism, in the tradition of Fine and Keefe, holds that vague predicates possess multiple admissible precisifications. A sentence is true if it holds on all precisifications, false if on none, and otherwise lacks a truth value. This allows it to reject the tolerance premise: on some admissible precisifications a one-millimetre change in blood pressure shifts the classification from healthy to hypertensive, so it is not determinately true that such a small change never makes a difference. Epistemicism, by contrast, maintains that there is exactly one correct precisification—say, a precise threshold at 139.5 mmHg—but we are permanently ignorant of it. Williamson's margin-for-error principle explains this ignorance: if we are near the boundary, we do not know that we are near it, so we can never locate the sharp line. Both views deny metaphysical vagueness, locating the problem either in semantic multiplicity or in epistemic limitation. Turning to clinical application, the supervaluationist treats competing medical guidelines as admissible precisifications, so a reading of 138 is determinately healthy if all guidelines agree, but a reading near 140 is indeterminate when guidelines diverge. The epistemicist says these guidelines are merely practical heuristics masking a single true physiological threshold fixed by nature; doctors disagree not because the concept is semantically plural, but because they share a collective epistemic failure. Thus both accounts preserve bivalence at the metaphysical level, but they diagnose medical disagreement differently."
