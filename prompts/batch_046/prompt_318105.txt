As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to make sense of historical contingency—the "for want of a nail" problem—by comparing it to cascade failures in distributed systems. In my work, a single misconfigured timeout or dropped packet can propagate through tightly coupled dependencies and bring down an entire region, while redundant architectures absorb the same shock without visible effect. This led me to draft an essay with the following core claim: contingency in history is not merely the truism that small causes can have large effects, but a claim about the topology of social and institutional coupling. Without specifying whether a society is brittle (high amplification, low redundancy) or resilient (dampening feedback loops), saying that "everything could have turned out differently" collapses into an empty tautology. I further want to argue that treating historical outcomes as deterministic functions of initial conditions plus system topology risks smuggling in a hidden determinism that undermines the very openness contingency is meant to preserve.

My specific concerns are these. First, does framing contingency through system topology misrepresent how philosophers of history actually treat the concept—particularly the normative distinction between avoidable and unavoidable catastrophes? Second, is the distributed-systems analogy genuinely illuminating, or does it commit a domain-transfer fallacy by imposing engineering categories onto irreducibly normative and interpretive human affairs? Third, if I accept that some societies are structurally brittle, have I implicitly conceded that large-scale disasters were in some sense predetermined by architecture rather than genuinely contingent?

Please comment on the conceptual architecture of these notes. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between brittle and resilient social systems holds up philosophically, whether my worry about hidden determinism is well-founded, and where the analogy is most likely to break down. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to specific philosophers—only an assessment of whether the argument is coherent and which parts require the tightest revision.
