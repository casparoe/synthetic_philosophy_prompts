I have been thinking about housing, zoning, and the so-called right to the city entirely on my own, without any academic background, and I have come to hold a view that feels both intuitive and poorly defended in the usual debates. I believe that long-term residents of a neighborhood possess a form of moral authority over local land-use rules that is not reducible to property rights or to democratic majoritarianism. I call it "proximity sovereignty": the idea that sustained co-presence and shared vulnerability to local conditions — noise, traffic, maintenance burdens, and the rhythms of daily interaction — generate a collective practical identity. Because residents are co-authors of this identity, preempting their zoning decisions by regional or state mandate is not merely inefficient or exclusionary; it is a kind of moral override that treats them as interchangeable users of space rather than as members of a place with its own continuity. On this view, much of what gets dismissed as NIMBYism is actually the exercise of a legitimate, place-bound autonomy. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which empirical findings about housing supply, displacement, or regional welfare, which arguments from distributive justice or democratic theory, and which analogies — for instance, comparing neighborhood control to family autonomy or to corporate self-governance — would genuinely undermine proximity sovereignty? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and distinguish what a committed defender should concede as damaging from what they can plausibly reject. I also ask that you be self-critical in your response: say explicitly what single argument or observation would lead you to abandon or severely qualify this view, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail or rely on an unstated assumption.
