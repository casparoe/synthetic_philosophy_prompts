I'm planning a fifty-minute session for a first-year philosophy graduate reading group on intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism. The audience has completed introductory classical logic and basic calculus, but they have no background in proof theory, topology, or formal analysis. I want them to understand that the intuitionistic rejection of the law of excluded middle is not a matter of epistemic caution—"we haven't found a proof yet"—but is tied to a specific constructive interpretation of the logical connectives: that A ∨ B requires an actual proof of A or an actual proof of B; that ¬A means a proof that A entails absurdity; and that consequently ¬¬A → A fails because having shown that "not-not-A" is absurd does not by itself construct an object witnessing A.

To carry this, I intend to use the intermediate value theorem as the central example. Classically, if a continuous function f on [a,b] satisfies f(a) < 0 < f(b), then there exists c with f(c) = 0; the proof appears inevitable. Constructively, however, this fails for arbitrary continuous functions, because locating such a c requires, for each point, deciding whether f is positive or negative—a decision procedure that is not generally available. The example must show that the logical gap is not about our ignorance of c but about what counts as a legitimate proof of existence in analysis.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. What dependency must be in place before the logical rules are introduced—should they first grasp the difference between existence and explicit construction, or should the BHK reading of ∧, ∨, →, and ¬ come first? In what precise order should the connectives be introduced, and at exactly which point should the intermediate value theorem be deployed—before the rules are formalized, to motivate them, or after, to test them? Specify what the carrying example must possess (a classical proof that feels unavoidable, a constructive failure that reveals a hidden decision requirement) and what it must lack (no reliance on other non-constructive existence proofs that would confuse the logical point).

Then map the misconceptions. I expect they will think intuitionism is just classical logic with weaker confidence; that it is a modal logic about "provability in principle"; that the BHK interpretation describes mental representations rather than proof-objects; and that the failure of double-negation elimination means intuitionists treat some statements as metaphysically "neither true nor false." For each misconception, identify the exact moment in the sequence where it should be headed off, and what brief counter-example or clarification should be used.

Give me one closing question that tests whether they understand the dependency between logical form and proof-obligation—specifically, that they can apply the constructive reading of disjunction to a new mathematical claim—rather than just repeating that "intuitionists reject LEM."

Finally, state explicitly what I should leave out and refuse to pursue if asked: the topological semantics of Heyting algebras, Gödel's double-negation translation, and any reference to specific formal systems such as Martin-Löf type theory.
