I have been developing a version of tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while all positive states are at best instrumentally valuable and at worst cognitive distortions that perpetuate the cycle of desire. I want to press this further than the standard negative-utilitarian framing: I hold that tranquilism also carries a descriptive claim about practical reason, namely that every apparently positive reason for action — pursuing art, friendship, or knowledge — is structurally reducible to an implicit avoidance of a corresponding negative state, such as boredom, loneliness, or ignorance experienced as suffering. On this account, motivation is architecturally monistic: there are no genuinely positive reasons, only differently masked negative ones.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this combined normative-descriptive position — not merely the familiar objections that it undervalues achievement or that it licenses moral paralysis. I want a precise map of its exposure: which empirical findings in psychology or neuroscience about intrinsic motivation and reward circuitry would undermine the architectural claim; which phenomenological descriptions of joy, flow, or loving attachment resist reduction to absence; which formal results in decision theory or axiology show that a monistic negative-state framework collapses into a rival view or generates practical paradoxes; and which everyday intuitions about caring for someone for their own sake are fatal rather than merely awkward. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed tranquilist could concede the point as damaging without abandoning the core view, or whether it forces a retreat to a weaker, purely normative version that abandons the descriptive claim about motivation. Finally, identify the objection I am most likely hoping nobody raises — the one that shows the architectural claim has been insulated by definitional moves that render the view unfalsifiable but empty — and say honestly whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice.
