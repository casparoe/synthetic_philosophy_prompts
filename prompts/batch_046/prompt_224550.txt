I'm drafting an essay on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness and have become dissatisfied with the standard framing, which treats the view either as an eliminativist denial that experience exists at all or as a claim that we systematically misrepresent non-phenomenal states as having qualitative character. I want to explore a different reconstruction: illusionism should be understood not as a thesis about misrepresentation within experience, but as a thesis about the semantics of phenomenal concepts themselves—specifically, that these concepts function as empty singular terms, structurally analogous to proper names that lack a referent, and that their deployment in introspection generates a grammatically coherent but referentially void self-report. From this perspective, when a subject reports that her mental state has a qualitative feel, she is not describing an intrinsic property of a cognitive state, nor is she falling prey to a representational error in the usual sense; rather, she is using a linguistic device that automatically produces the appearance of direct acquaintance, much as referring to a non-existent monarch can produce the appearance of describing a real individual. I am looking for a rigorous argument that this empty-name reconstruction allows illusionism to escape the self-stultification objection—namely, that if phenomenal consciousness is illusory, then the very appearance that motivates the claim is itself illusory, leaving no non-illusory standpoint from which to assert it—without positing a hidden, non-phenomenal observer. The argument should explain why the illusion is so persistent, given that the machinery of self-reference operates automatically, and it should show that real underlying cognitive processes—such as access-conscious representations involved in self-modeling—can do all the explanatory work that phenomenal properties are usually invoked to do.

Please construct and defend this position in detail. In particular, address the concern that if phenomenal concepts are genuinely empty, then introspection becomes globally unreliable, threatening not only first-person knowledge of qualitative character but also the reliability of reports about beliefs, desires, and other mental states that depend on the same introspective mechanisms. You should draw a principled distinction between the failure of phenomenal concepts, which purport to pick out intrinsic qualitative properties, and the success of other mentalistic concepts, which pick out functional or relational properties that are genuinely instantiated. Your response should be written in a formal philosophical register, should not depend on specific bibliographic references or citations, and should build the case primarily through conceptual analysis of reference, introspective grammar, and the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.
