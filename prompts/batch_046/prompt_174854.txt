Next month I defend, in a moral-philosophy workshop on reasons and motivation, a reconstruction of Leibniz’s moral philosophy centered on the claim that his formula identifying justice with the charity of the wise expresses a sui generis normative structure I call cosmic-indexed normativity. On my reading, Leibniz does not reduce moral obligation to enlightened self-love, benevolent sentiment, or rational self-interest; rather, the normative force of a moral reason derives from its contribution to the universal harmony of substances, indexed to the perfection of the best possible world. This means that for Leibniz there is no analog of the modern wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, because reasons and psychological motives belong to different ontological registers: a reason justifies by its place in the cosmic order, while a motive moves by its presence in the soul’s representational state. I contend this resolves the tension between Leibniz’s deterministic psychology and his insistence on moral freedom without collapsing into Kantian formalism or Humean instrumentalism.

The audience is precisely divided: about half are philosophers of action steeped in the wrong-kind-of-reasons literature, for whom it is axiomatic that any normative reason must be capable of motivating a rational agent; two are early-modern scholars who know the Theodicy and the New Essays intimately; one is a historian of philosophy hostile to anachronistic framing; and several graduate students who will ask the sharpest methodological questions. They care about textual fidelity, coherence with contemporary moral psychology, and whether the reconstruction does philosophical work or merely relocates familiar problems.

Prepare my Q&A defense sheet. List the objections I will actually face, in the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them. For each objection: indicate whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially retreat; sketch the reply I can deliver in under two minutes; and flag the weakest point in that reply. Order them by probable damage, not by politeness. Be sure to include the determinism-and-motivation challenge that the wrong-kind-of-reasons specialists will press hardest, and the textual-fidelity challenge the historians will raise about whether Leibniz ever distinguishes reasons and motives in this ontological way. Then include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—namely, whether cosmic-indexed normativity requires committing to Leibniz’s theological metaphysics in a manner that makes the view unavailable to modern secular ethics, and if so whether my reconstruction is just historical exegesis with no contemporary purchase. For that hidden question, tell me exactly how to answer without sounding defensive, including what I must concede up front.

Give me a committed assessment: take a definite stand on which objections have real force, which rest on misreadings, and whether my central claim—that Leibniz lacks any analog of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem—actually survives scrutiny or collapses under pressure. Do not lay out options neutrally; choose a position and defend it. Be explicit about where my reconstruction is weakest, whether I should defend that weak point or concede it strategically, and whether the cosmic-indexed reading is ultimately sustainable or should be modified.
