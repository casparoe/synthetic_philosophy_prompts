Take Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle — particularly the argument he developed in response to d'Alembert's proposal for a Geneva theater — and apply it to the occupation of the Théâtre de l'Odéon in Paris during May 1968. In that well-known episode, student activists seized the building, dissolved the conventional separation between performers and audience, and transformed the space into an open, continuous forum for political debate, attempting to replace mediated representation with direct participatory assembly. Analyze this occupation through Rousseau's framework: to what extent does it realize his preferred alternative to spectacle, namely a civic festival in which citizens are simultaneously actors and spectators, bound by shared virtue rather than divided by passive consumption? At the same time, examine whether the occupation remained irreducibly theatrical in Rousseau's pejorative sense — whether its politics depended on performance, visibility, and the production of revolutionary images for cameras and outsiders — and whether this reveals that even apparently anti-theatrical collective action reproduces the very dynamics of appearance, amour-propre, and moral distance that Rousseau associated with the stage.

Your analysis should offer a disciplined mapping rather than a loose analogy: identify precisely who occupies the roles of spectator and actor in this case, what counts as the performance versus the authentic civic assembly, and whether the students' abolition of the stage genuinely eliminated theatrical mediation or merely relocated it. I also want you to address a tension that Rousseau's framework seems to underappreciate: the occupation's political efficacy depended in part on its visibility as spectacle to those outside the building — to the media, to other students, to the state — suggesting that direct participation and mediated representation may be entangled rather than opposed. Does this dependency undermine Rousseau's distinction between corrupting spectacle and virtuous festival, or can his view accommodate it by distinguishing necessary publicity from corrupting theatricality? Conclude by saying whether the episode ultimately supports Rousseau's suspicion of theatrical politics, complicates his binary, or exposes a blind spot in his account of how civic virtue can be sustained in large, modern societies where direct assembly is impossible without some form of representation.
