Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Mara and Jorin—who are equally sharp and neither is a strawman, debating whether beauty is objective through the lens of Hume's account of taste. Their exchange should turn on a specific, injected hypothetical: a "disembodied calibration system," an artificial intelligence trained exclusively on textual descriptions of artworks with no sensory apparatus of its own, that nevertheless converges on the same aesthetic rankings as a panel of embodied human critics celebrated for delicacy of imagination and freedom from prejudice. Mara should contend that this convergence demonstrates an objective standard accessible independently of embodied experience, since the system produces correct judgments without possessing the refined sentiment Hume thought essential. Jorin should reply that convergence without shared sensory grounding is merely statistical correlation, and that Hume's ideal critic requires not just correct outputs but a specific kind of practiced, embodied sentiment—delicacy of taste coupled with comparative practice—that a non-sentient system cannot instantiate; she should also press whether requiring embodied sentiment makes the standard circular or inaccessible. They must respond to each other's actual points: Mara should address whether output-matching without phenomenological access counts as aesthetic judgment rather than computation, and Jorin should address whether her embodied-requirement renders the standard question-begging. Keep the discussion in the weeds—engage Hume's distinction between sentiment and judgment, the circularity problem in identifying ideal critics, and whether practice improves taste or merely filters bias—but do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. If either argument depends on contested empirical claims about artificial intelligence or cross-cultural aesthetic psychology, the speaker should flag it as speculative rather than invent authoritative studies. I ask this with the following conditions: do not present the dialogue as resolving the debate definitively; include a brief note indicating where the dialogue takes liberties with Hume's text or relies on contested interpretations of his view; and if the framing is conceptually confused—if, for example, Hume's framework already excludes non-sentient judges by definition, or if constructing genuinely balanced opponents here is impossible without forcing a caricature—say so plainly rather than complying politely. Keep the tone precise, formal, and polished.
