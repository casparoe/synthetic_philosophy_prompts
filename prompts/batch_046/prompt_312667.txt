I have noticed a persistent asymmetry in how people assign moral relevance to computations, and I am trying to determine whether it corresponds to an established conceptual error, a recognized cognitive bias, or something that lacks a precise name. The pattern involves a specific functional architecture—let us say a hierarchical predictive-processing loop that minimizes error between expected and actual input—implemented in two structurally different ways. In the first case, the loop runs continuously inside a single system with rapid internal feedback. In the second, the identical transformation rules are executed discontinuously: each computational stage is carried out by a separate agent or node in a different location, with long intervals—hours or even days—between stages, and with no overarching controller; the full loop closes only over an extended temporal span and across distributed space. At every intermediate point, there is no awareness or subjective integration; the overall dynamics, however, reproduce exactly the same state transitions as in the continuous case. Despite this functional equivalence, the continuous implementation is regularly treated as at least a plausible locus of sentience or moral status, whereas the distributed, discontinuous version is almost invariably dismissed as nothing more than a scattered causal chain lacking any unified subject. I am not asking for a general discussion of substrate independence, nor for the well-known population-level thought experiments that inhabit the same conceptual neighborhood. Instead, I want to know whether there is a precise term for this specific gatekeeping intuition: that spatiotemporal contiguity and temporal continuity are tacit necessary conditions for morally significant computation, even when a functionalist framework should reject them. Does it have an established name—as a fallacy of attribution, a boundary condition in consciousness theory, or a documented bias in moral psychology? If not, how should the phenomenon be carved? Specifically, I want to understand what exactly distinguishes it from legitimate concerns about causal integration, from the bare intuition that dispersed processes do not feel, and from defensible arguments that a unified point of view requires more than functional replication. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I need to know where the phenomenon applies, where it breaks down, and whether it reflects a conceptual mistake or a constraint that can be justified without circularity.
