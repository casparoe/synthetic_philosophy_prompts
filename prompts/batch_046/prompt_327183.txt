I've been taking a seminar on philosophy of mind and neuroscience this term, and I keep returning to the split-brain literature—specifically, whether a patient after complete corpus callosotomy is best described as one person with a divided mind or as two people sharing a body. After discussing this with a few friends over coffee, I've settled on a position that I think avoids both extremes, though I'm not sure it can survive close scrutiny. The view is that there is still only one conscious subject, but its attentional architecture is "bifocal" in a specific sense: the subject can sustain two parallel representational frames, each anchored in one hemisphere, that do not exchange content via the callosum yet both belong to the same stream of consciousness because they share a single narrative integration mechanism operating at a subcortical level. This means the patient is not two subjects, but also not a normal unified subject; rather, unity is preserved functionally even when informational integration is severed.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against this bifocal-unity view. I am not asking for a bibliography; I want the arguments, the experimental findings that defenders of the view would themselves concede are damaging, and the conceptual distinctions—perhaps about personal identity, about the necessary conditions for being a single subject, or about how attention relates to consciousness—on which it stands or falls. Which results in split-brain task performance would be fatal, and which could be absorbed by saying the two frames are still parts of one system? Does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by defining "unity" so broadly that no hemispheric dissociation could ever threaten it—and if so, is that a defect for a claim of this kind? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and write your answer in British English.
