I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have completed an introductory logic course but have not studied epistemic logic, modal logic, or formal decision theory. The topic is the surprise examination paradox. To avoid the standard classroom formulation that most students have already encountered in popular introductions, I want the session anchored in an unfamiliar carrying scenario: a research-ethics board that has committed to conducting exactly one unannounced audit during a ten-day clinical-trial monitoring window, with the public guarantee that the trial staff will be unable to deduce the audit day before it arrives. I need a teaching plan—the architecture of the explanation, not a scripted lecture. Please respond in the following sections, in this order.

1. Dependency chain and introduction order (approximately 300 words): Specify the sequence in which the conceptual components must be presented—what the board's announcement entails, what "surprise" is defined relative to (deductive knowledge, not mere expectation), why backward induction is initially plausible, and where the interaction with common knowledge of rationality creates the tension—so that the paradox emerges as a forced result rather than a sleight of hand. For each stage, indicate what prior understanding it requires.

2. The carrying scenario: necessary features and deliberate exclusions (approximately 200 words): Define what the clinical-trial audit case must contain (the fixed ten-day window, the singular occurrence, the transparent commitment) and what it must lack (probabilistic scheduling rules, hidden signals, ambiguous definitions of deduction) in order to isolate the logical structure and prevent empirical distractions.

3. Misconception map and defusion points (approximately 250 words): Identify the predictable errors—treating the paradox as a probabilistic miscalculation, conflating epistemic accessibility with physical possibility, positing that the board might secretly violate its commitment, or dissolving the tension by flatly denying backward induction—and state precisely where in the dependency chain each misconception should be anticipated and neutralized.

4. Check for understanding (approximately 150 words): Design one brief exercise or targeted question that distinguishes students who have grasped the interaction between the inductive argument and the common-knowledge condition from those who have only memorized the conclusion that the audit must occur on the final day.

5. Omissions and how to frame them (approximately 150 words): List what should be explicitly set aside at this level—formal modal representations, game-theoretic analyses, historical variants of the paradox—and provide the wording with which to acknowledge these limits without implying that the problem has been resolved.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The plan should be executable without directing students to external texts.
