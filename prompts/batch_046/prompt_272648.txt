In my graduate seminar on ontology we have reached an impasse that I find philosophically instructive yet pedagogically frustrating. For the better part of a term we have examined Black’s two-sphere universe and its bearing on Leibniz’s Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, yet despite apparent agreement on the formal description—two qualitatively identical spheres suspended in otherwise empty space, separated by a fixed distance—the discussion divides into three camps with no prospect of convergence. The first maintains that the spheres are weakly discernible via the irreflexive relation of being-two-metres-distant-from, and that if such relational properties genuinely individuate, the Principle survives in restricted form; the second counters that this renders the Principle vacuous, since any apparent duplicates can be distinguished by a symmetric but irreflexive relation they bear solely to one another, reducing a substantive metaphysical constraint to tautology. The third camp, drawing on structuralist interpretations that treat spatial relations as ontologically prior to their relata, proposes that we misdescribe the case by speaking of “two spheres” at all—perhaps there are only positions in a spatial structure, so that the question of whether the occupants are discernible collapses into a question about the ontology of structure itself.

What I should like is a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than an adjudication of the metaphysics. Do the camps differ about facts, about what counts as a legitimate property, about the standards of ontological parsimony, or about bedrock intuitions concerning symmetry that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden formal question—perhaps whether weak discernibility presupposes a primitive notion of numerical difference—doing the unacknowledged work? Or does the stalemate signal that the original question is ill-posed, conflating issues of individuation with issues of ontological dependence? Please engage each position concretely, indicate whether progress should be expected and what form it might take—whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a change of question—and reply in British English.
