Here is the student's answer: Fodor's systematicity argument begins with the observation that human cognition displays systematic relationships. If someone is capable of thinking that John loves Mary, they are typically also capable of thinking that Mary loves John. Fodor takes this to show that thoughts are not holistic but have internal structure, analogous to sentences in a language. He proposes that thoughts are literally sentences in Mentalese, composed of discrete symbols arranged according to syntactic rules. Because the syntax of Mentalese governs how symbols combine, rearranging those symbols produces a new thought with predictable semantic properties—so the syntactic structure directly determines the semantic content. This compositional architecture also explains productivity: with a finite stock of primitive symbols and recursive combination rules, the mind can generate an unlimited number of thoughts. Consequently, every concept—including apparently acquired ones like carburetor or democracy—must be innate, waiting only to be triggered by experience. Because Mentalese itself is not learned but biologically given, accepting the language of thought forces one to accept that all conceptual capacities are present from birth.
