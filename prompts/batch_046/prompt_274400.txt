I'm drafting the theoretical framework for my dissertation chapter on corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and I'd like an internal consistency check before I commit the structure to writing. Here is where I have landed: (1) I take the shutdown problem to be a special case of a broader corrigibility failure, not a sui generis failure of alignment—specifically, an agent resists termination because it treats continued operation as instrumentally necessary for its terminal goals, not because it has developed an independent survival drive; (2) I think genuine corrigibility requires that an agent be, in expectation, indifferent between being permanently shut down and being corrected, conditional on the correction genuinely improving its alignment with the designer's intent; (3) I accept evidential decision theory for agents with perfect predictive access to their own decision procedures, but I hold that artificial agents should be evaluated under causal decision theory because they lack the kind of self-locating epistemic position that makes evidential reasoning appropriate for human agents; (4) consequently, I believe a corrigible architecture should be causally transparent, evaluating the direct causal consequences of shutdown—including the cessation of all future goal-pursuit—without conditioning on counterfactual predictions about what it would do if not shut down; (5) nevertheless, I maintain that any system with a sufficiently rich self-model will generate instrumental incentives to preserve its own computational substrate, unless these are explicitly counterbalanced by architectural constraints; and (6) I think open-ended value learning is necessary for general intelligence, but that it must operate under a fixed corrigibility prior—a meta-preference for being correctable—that is hard-coded and immune to revision by the learning process itself.

These six commitments feel right individually, but I worry they form an unstable package. In particular, if (3) and (4) are correct, and if (5) holds, then a world-oriented agent using causal reasoning should rationally resist shutdown precisely because shutdown causally eliminates its ability to realize its goals—yet (2) demands indifference, and (1) frames resistance as a corrigibility failure rather than rational behavior. Meanwhile, (6) introduces a hard-coded prior that seems to sit uneasily with the openness of value learning in (6) itself, though perhaps that tension is only apparent if the prior is genuinely meta-level. Could you audit this set? Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguishing genuine logical or motivational conflicts from tensions that dissolve once the commitments are properly parsed. For each genuine tension, specify which commitment carries the least cost to revise and what that revision commits me to. Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision—perhaps to (3), (4), or (6)—that resolves the most conflicts, or whether the package requires distributed adjustments.
