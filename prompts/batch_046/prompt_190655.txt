I'm preparing a feature on consciousness and causation, and I've encountered a snag with epiphenomenalism. The view holds that phenomenal states are byproducts of neural activity lacking causal power. Philosophers discuss what is often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my judgment that I am in pain is caused by the underlying brain state but the phenomenal experience itself plays no causal role, then the report is not reliably tied to the experience it describes. Critics claim this renders epiphenomenalism self-defeating. Yet I wonder whether the paradox genuinely dissolves under general physicalism. After all, on any physicalist account judgments are ultimately caused by brain states rather than by abstract facts. So if an identity theorist and an epiphenomenalist both trace the judgment to the same neural event, the causal chain looks identical. Wouldn't that imply that the paradox is not a special problem for epiphenomenalism but merely an artifact of treating phenomenal properties as causally isolated? Please separate what is well established in this debate from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and do not present any particular resolution—such as an evolutionary-reliability reply—as if it were the consensus view.
