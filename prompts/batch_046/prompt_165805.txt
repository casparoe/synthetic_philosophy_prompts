Design a one-paragraph thought experiment in cognitive neuroscience that engineers a dissociation between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness in the direction global workspace theory finds hardest to accommodate: a human subject with intact local recurrent processing in early sensory cortices sustaining vivid phenomenal experience of a visual scene, yet with a specific, realistic failure of prefrontal-parietal long-range synchronization that prevents working-memory encoding and verbal report—while leaving affective appraisal and motor guidance intact, so the information is globally available to behavior and emotion but not to cognition or language. The scenario must avoid standard examples (blindsight, inattentional blindness, split-brain) and must not invoke exotic technology. After presenting the case, do the following: (i) state precisely what global workspace theory predicts about whether the subject is phenomenally conscious and whether they have access consciousness, with the reasoning; (ii) state what a local-recurrent phenomenal-consciousness account predicts and why; (iii) identify the single architectural parameter that isolates the divergence; (iv) describe what happens to both theories' verdicts if that parameter is normalized; and (v) explain how a defender of global workspace theory could attack the case—specifically by arguing that affective and motor integration already constitute a form of global broadcasting—without simply stipulating that phenomenal consciousness is absent. The purpose is to test whether the access/phenomenal distinction can be made structurally robust rather than conceptually circular.
