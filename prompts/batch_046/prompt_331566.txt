You serve on the Public Deliberation Oversight Board for the Mid-River Regional Water Authority, which must finalize its 2026 Watershed Stewardship Plan by March 30. A coalition of faith-based environmental groups has submitted formal testimony opposing a proposed diversion canal. Their submission has two parts: standard hydrological and ecological objections that meet scientific criteria, and an explicit "sacred geography" claim that the affected watershed is a place of covenantal obligation whose value cannot be captured in secular cost-benefit terms without moral distortion. The coalition refuses to translate this second part into secular language, arguing that translation would misrepresent the actual normative force of their objection rather than merely rephrase it.

The Board must recommend whether the official plan's public-comment appendix may include this untranslated material as-is, whether it must be excluded or translated into publicly accessible reasons, or whether the Authority may cite such testimony in its own justification only if reconstructed in secular terms. Draft the short written opinion you will circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend one concrete course with an implementation timeline tied to the March 15 close of the public comment period, and explain how the Authority should handle future submissions that mix accessible science with untranslatable comprehensive doctrine.

In your reasoning, take seriously the strongest consideration against your recommendation. If you favor exclusion or mandatory translation, address the concern that this does not merely filter vocabulary but silences citizens for whom religious language is constitutive rather than ornamental. If you favor inclusion or citation, address the concern that allowing untranslated comprehensive doctrines into official justification violates equal civic standing and invites strategic deployment of untranslatable claims. Ground the analysis in the conceptual tension between justificatory accessibility and expressive completeness rather than in general appeals to separation of church and state.
