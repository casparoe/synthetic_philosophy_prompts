Analyze the first eighteen months of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021) in the European Union, Switzerland, and the United States as a case study in federalism and subsidiarity. The salient institutional facts are these: the European Commission negotiated joint vaccine-procurement contracts on behalf of member states, while national governments retained authority over lockdowns, school closures, and domestic movement restrictions; Swiss cantons retained significant health-policy autonomy even after the federal government invoked emergency powers under epidemic law; and in the US, state governments issued divergent mask mandates and business restrictions despite federal CDC guidance, while the federal government managed vaccine-development funding and international supply agreements.

Using the principle of subsidiarity—not as a simple preference for localism, but as a claim that a decision belongs at the lowest level possessing both the relevant competence (epidemiological information, administrative capacity) and the democratic legitimacy to coerce, while also internalizing cross-border externalities—map each institutional choice. For the EU procurement contract, the Swiss cantonal divergence, and the US state-level mandates, specify: who was the actual decision-maker; what alternative level of government could have made the call; what kind of evidence (local infection rates, supply-chain scale, democratic mandate) weighed in favor of each level; and what subsidiarity itself says about whether the actual assignment was correct, incorrect, or outside the principle’s scope altogether.

Then run the analysis in both directions. Which features of the pandemic response does subsidiarity explain well—for example, why uniform national lockdowns might ignore local epidemiological variation—and which does it miss or misdescribe, such as the efficiency logic that made centralized vaccine purchasing preferable to twenty-seven separate negotiations? Does the episode show that in a fast-moving crisis the “lowest competent level” collapses upward to whatever authority can secure resources, or does it reveal that the principle was always intended to govern coercive interference with liberty rather than procurement and logistics? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent: for instance, does subsidiarity say anything about whether democratic legitimacy requires that the same body imposing restrictions also controls vaccine distribution? Conclude by judging whether this episode is a confirmation of subsidiarity, an anomaly it never anticipated, or a category mistake that applies the principle to decisions it was never meant to cover.
