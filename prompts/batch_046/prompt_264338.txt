As a novelist working on a narrative in which agricultural labor and ecological entanglement are treated as moral dramas rather than mere backdrops, I have settled on the following commitments regarding insects and pest control, and I want an audit of whether they form a coherent outlook or a patchwork of incompatible instincts. First, I hold that the category "pest" is relational rather than taxonomic: an organism counts as a pest only in virtue of its conflict with a specific human project, which means moral evaluation should attach to the relation and not to any intrinsic feature of the creature. Second, I think invertebrates likely possess a minimal, non-reflective phenomenal consciousness—enough that nociceptive harm is morally considerable, though not with the same weight as mammalian suffering. Third, I reject broad-spectrum insecticides on both consequentialist and deontological grounds, since they cause widespread suffering, destroy ecological services, and treat living systems as disposable. Fourth, I nonetheless defend tightly targeted biocontrol in subsistence contexts, where crop failure threatens human survival, accepting that this instrumentalizes individual insects. Fifth, I maintain that ecological functions—pollination, decomposition, trophic support—grant insects an indirect but substantial moral standing that must be weighed against direct suffering-based claims. Which pairs genuinely conflict, which tensions dissolve once the relational framing in the first commitment is properly applied, and what is the least costly revision if I must surrender one? Rank the conflicts by severity, specify what each revision costs the broader framework, and identify the single change that cleans up the most inconsistencies without collapsing the view.
