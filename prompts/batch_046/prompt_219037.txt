(1) The discursive dilemma is real: for logically connected propositions, premise-based and conclusion-based majority aggregation can diverge.
(2) A writers room is just a group doing this with narrative premises—like "the hero values loyalty" and "in this world loyalty requires betrayal"—and then separately voting on plot conclusions.
(3) So any collaborative story risks being internally contradictory unless the room abandons democratic voting on either premises or conclusions.
(4) But an individual novelist isn't one unified mind; it's an internal committee of voices—the worldbuilder, the dialogue person, the plot architect—each holding beliefs about premises and outcomes.
(5) Those internal modules are structurally identical to members of a writers room, so the dilemma applies inside one author's head just as it does in a group.
(6) Therefore every solo writer is necessarily incoherent about their own plot logic.
(7) Yet novels can clearly be tightly coherent—mine usually are.
(8) So judgment aggregation theory, or the discursive dilemma itself, must be false.
