I'm convening an adversarial collaboration for a seminar on deep history and welfare economics, and I need the joint statement that two opposing camps should have produced after a year of argument. The first camp—the Adaptive-Transition camp—holds that the shift to cereal agriculture and sedentism, despite well-documented local costs to health and leisure, was an adaptive and largely irreversible response to late-Pleistocene climate pressures and demographic dynamics; they maintain that it eventually unlocked the institutional complexity, technological accumulation, and caloric security that make modern capabilities possible, and that measuring welfare purely by forager leisure misses the value of future optionality. The second camp—the Affluence-Regression camp—holds that the transition was, in welfare terms, a trap that eroded what Marshall Sahlins called the "original affluence" of foragers: it reduced stature and dental health, lengthened working hours, institutionalised gendered and class hierarchy through surplus coercion, and replaced situational status differences with irreversible structural stratification; they argue that complex foraging societies (for instance, those of the Pacific Northwest Coast) demonstrate that large-scale cooperation and storage did not require domestication, and that the persistence of agriculture reflects ecological lock-in and state violence rather than revealed preference.

Please draft the document they should jointly sign. It should open with a numbered section of claims both sides accept—covering, at minimum, the osteological evidence for post-Neolithic health decline, the existence of pre-agricultural sedentism and storage (e.g., among Natufian groups), the correlation between cereal surplus and early state formation, and the fact that modern foraging groups do not voluntarily revert to agriculture when adjacent to it—phrased so precisely that neither party feels the wording smuggles in their opponent's interpretation. Follow this with a disagreements table: for each residual dispute, state Party A's position and Party B's position in neutral wording that each side would accept as an accurate characterisation of its own view; include disagreements about whether health decline was causally agricultural or confounded by density and pathogens, about whether welfare should be measured by time-allocation autonomy versus caloric security and technological capability, and about whether hierarchy was caused by surplus or merely revealed by it. Then provide a section titled "Cruxes and Predictive Tests." Identify three cruxes—suggested ones are: (i) the correct metric for comparing forager and farmer welfare, (ii) whether pre-agricultural ritual complexity (such as at Göbekli Tepe) proves that large-scale hierarchy was inevitable without farming, and (iii) whether intensive wild-resource management could have sustained dense populations without domestication. For each crux, specify a concrete case or body of evidence both sides agree is relevant—such as comparative ethnographic time-allocation data, the archaeological record of Göbekli Tepe's social organisation, or the ecological parameters of the Pacific Northwest Coast societies—and record in advance what each party predicts that evidence shows. The document should be formal, structured, and free of bibliographic requests; please write it in British English.
