I am working through a conceptual problem in the ethics of belief that standard treatments of the James-Clifford dispute tend to flatten. Clifford's evidentialist constraint holds that one ought never to assent to a proposition unless the available evidence is sufficient, and James, in The Will to Believe, carves out exceptions for options that are living, forced, and momentous—especially in domains where belief can help bring about the state of affairs believed. The standard comparison stops there. But I want to push on a case that does not fit either taxonomy neatly: what we might call constitutive or framework-belief. Consider a researcher entering a nascent scientific domain where the very conceptual vocabulary and observational protocols depend on accepting a working theoretical framework. Without provisional assent to that framework, there is no shared language in which to formulate the hypotheses that might eventually confirm or disconfirm it. The belief is not adopted because evidence already supports it—Clifford would condemn it—yet it is not quite a Jamesian live option chosen for its psychological or moral urgency either; rather, it functions as an epistemic precondition for the inquiry itself. Please explain the conceptual issue that arises here. Specifically: does James's distinction between belief and hypothesis, and his criteria for legitimate belief in advance of full evidence, extend to these structurally necessary, framework-constituting commitments? Or does accommodating them require abandoning his psychological account of live options? Conversely, can Clifford's evidentialism distinguish between reckless belief and this kind of provisional, methodologically necessary assent without collapsing into the claim that all inquiry must begin in a state of pure suspension? I want an analysis of how the evidential-precondition relation bears on the ethics of belief, not a historical summary or a list of commentators.
