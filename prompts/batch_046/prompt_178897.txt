I'm preparing a presentation for a philosophy of logic seminar on tangled hierarchies and the idea of an inviolate level, and I've constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve rational justification entirely, yet each premise feels like something Hofstadter or Gödel would accept. (1) In any formal system S, if a level L—say, its axioms or rules of inference—is intended to be inviolate, one can always construct a meta-system M in which L is treated as an object-level entity, as happens when logic is formalized in meta-logic or when Gödel numbering embeds arithmetic within itself. (2) If L can always be embedded in such an M, then L is never absolutely inviolate; it is only provisionally protected by the framing of S. (3) A strange loop arises when M can in turn be embedded back into a system of the same type as S, so that the meta-level becomes indistinguishable from the object-level—much as Escher's Drawing Hands depicts each hand drawing the other with no external origin. (4) Because strange loops are structurally possible for any hierarchical system, there is no framing-independent stopping point: every level that looks fixed can, in principle, be tangled into a loop that treats it as a revisable object. (5) Rational belief, however, requires that some premises be taken as genuinely fixed rather than merely provisionally protected; otherwise every inference can be undermined by shifting to the meta-frame in which those premises are open to revision. (6) Mathematical proof, logical inference, and even basic moral reasoning all depend on such genuinely fixed premises—modus ponens, consistency assumptions, or non-derogable norms. (7) Therefore, if tangled hierarchies are genuinely universal, none of these practices can provide stable, non-circular justification; every justification is only frame-relative, and selecting a frame is itself an unjustified choice. (8) But if every justification is frame-relative in this way, then there is no normative distinction between a justified belief and an arbitrary assertion, since any claim can be defended by choosing the frame where its premises are inviolate and attacked by choosing the frame where they are tangled objects. (9) That collapses epistemic normativity altogether, which is absurd.

My study group insists the flaw is obvious, but I keep circling back. I want to preserve the reality of strange loops and the idea that inviolability is framing-relative, but I don't want to surrender rational justification. Which step should I reject—perhaps a hidden slide from "can be embedded" to "is never fixed within any working frame," or an equivocation on "inviolate"? And if I give up that step, what does it cost me? Specifically, does saving rational belief require me to deny that meta-embedding is always available, or can I keep the loop and just deny that it undermines the local authority of a level within its own system? I'd like to know exactly where the argument breaks and what I'm paying to repair it.
