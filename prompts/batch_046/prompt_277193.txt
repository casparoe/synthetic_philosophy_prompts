I have recently begun to notice, both in my own habits since a prolonged illness and in a colleague’s approach to career, a recurring orientation I do not know how to classify. The pattern is this: one pursues a goal—completing a manuscript, resolving a conflict, reaching a recovery milestone—not to obtain pleasure, recognition, or growth, but solely to terminate the specific discomfort of its absence. Once achieved, the result produces no lasting satisfaction; it registers only as a brief neutral interval before attention shifts to the next unmet deficit. What distinguishes this from ordinary ambition is the attitude toward unbidden positive states: spontaneous friendship, unplanned beauty, or idle contentment are not welcomed as goods in themselves, but treated as potential sources of future attachment and therefore as risks to be minimized rather than cultivated. I have observed this in my colleague’s refusal to acknowledge promotions as gains and in my own tendency to view restored health merely as the absence of symptoms rather than as a positive condition.

Does this configuration have an established name in ethics or philosophical psychology? I am not describing general asceticism or stoic discipline, since neither requires the active suspicion of unsought pleasure; nor is it clinical depression, since motivation and affective range remain intact. What I seek is the precise carving: whether philosophers have identified a view on which only the removal of craving and suffering carries value, where all other states are at best neutral instruments, and whether that view can be held as an explicit doctrine or only as an unreflective disposition. If there is no specific term, I would want it defined with its boundary conditions—particularly how it differs from anhedonia, from pessimism, and from the more common claim that suffering outweighs pleasure—so that I can recognize whether I am describing one phenomenon or several.
