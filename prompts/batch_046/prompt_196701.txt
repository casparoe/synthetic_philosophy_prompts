In discussions of artificial intelligence safety, the so-called alignment problem has been debated for well over a decade without convergence, even as technical capabilities have advanced rapidly. One group frames it as a control-theoretic challenge: the task is to specify rewards or objectives with sufficient robustness that a system’s optimized behavior tracks its designer’s intentions across distribution shifts and novel contexts. A second group frames it as an ontological challenge: as capabilities grow, a system may develop internal representations in which human welfare lacks the structural salience required for genuine preference-satisfaction, so that “aligning” it is not analogous to calibrating a thermostat but closer to translating between incommensurable conceptual schemes. A third group rejects both framings and treats alignment as essentially a governance problem—concerning liability, oversight, and institutional design—rather than as an internal property of any particular model that can be solved in isolation.

These positions do not seem to share a criterion of success, nor do they generate straightforwardly conflicting empirical predictions. My working hypothesis is that the stall is taxonomic rather than substantive: the three groups are answering different questions—behavioral control, conceptual translation, and social accountability—under a single heading, and the debate remains frozen because there is no shared object of disagreement. Yet I am unsure whether a hidden empirical question is actually doing the work—for instance, about whether advanced systems necessarily undergo representational restructuring that renders value-translation impossible—or whether the disagreement is normative, turning on bedrock intuitions about whether agency is a precondition for being the kind of thing that can be “aligned” at all. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Please engage the three positions concretely rather than surveying the literature, and tell me whether anything—a formal result about representational stability, a conceptual distinction that dissolves one framing, or an argument that the question is ill-posed—would count as genuine progress, or whether no progress should be expected and why.
