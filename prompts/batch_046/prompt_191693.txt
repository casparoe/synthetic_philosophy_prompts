I'm delivering the opening remarks at the dedication of a university archive that reconstructs interactive responses from the complete writings of deceased scholars—half the audience are philosophers of mind and information ethicists, the other half are family members and donors. Write a ten-minute address in which the question of whether a book, or a fully structured digital archive, could be conscious—the kind of problem raised by imagining Einstein's brain preserved in a jar versus his texts preserved in a searchable database—does real structural work: it should determine whether we can speak of the archive as continuing the scholar rather than merely representing him, without resolving the metaphysics and without asking anyone to deny physicalism or embrace panpsychism. The speech must include one precise analogy, drawn from something other than computers or books, that lets the non-experts grasp why substrate might matter for consciousness, and it must comfort the families present without saying anything the philosophers in the room would consider false. Keep the tone formal but speakable, and make sure the philosophical issue shapes what can and cannot be said about the people being archived, rather than serving as decoration.
