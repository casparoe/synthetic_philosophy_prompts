I have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach on my own, and I keep returning to the analogy he draws between Kurt Gödel's self-referential formula—the one that asserts its own unprovability within a formal system—and the emergence of a self-aware mind from a strange loop of self-reference in a physical or symbolic substrate. I think I understand the basic structure: Gödel numbering lets a formula talk about other formulas, and with enough ingenuity it can talk about itself, so that the system contains a statement that, when interpreted, says it cannot be proven. Hofstadter suggests that consciousness might arise in a similar way, when a system's representations turn back on themselves and generate an I.

But here is the conceptual snag that keeps bothering me, and I would like it explained carefully. In Gödel's construction, the formula's self-reference is not intrinsic in any experiential sense; it is a structural coincidence made visible only by an external interpreter who knows how to decode Gödel numbers back into syntax. The formula itself does not know it refers to itself, and if there were no mathematician outside the system to perform that translation, the self-reference would remain invisible. By contrast, when I am aware of myself, there does not seem to be an outside decoder translating my brain states into meanings for me; the awareness appears to be internal and immediate. So the analogy seems to depend on smuggling in an external observer for the formal case, while denying one for the mental case.

Recently I tried to patch this gap with an idea of my own—what I have been calling a nested interpreter loop. Instead of treating self-awareness as a single strange loop of reference, I imagine it as a cascade: one symbolic subsystem refers to the state of another, but the capacity to interpret that reference is not outside the system; it is implemented by yet another subsystem at the same level. In other words, the decoder is inside the loop too. This seems to bring the mental case closer to the Gödelian case, because now there is no privileged external viewpoint—only internal subsystems interpreting one another in a circular fashion. But I am unsure whether this solves the problem or just buries it in a regress. Does adding internal interpreters actually explain first-person awareness, or does it merely replicate, inside the mind, the same dependency on interpretation that Gödel's formula has on an external mathematician?

Please explain this conceptual issue in depth. I want to know whether Hofstadter's strange-loop analogy survives once we take seriously the need for interpretation in both directions—whether the formula's dependence on an external decoder is genuinely parallel to the mind's dependence on internal sub-interpreters, and whether my nested interpreter idea strengthens the analogy or collapses it into an infinite regress. I am not asking for a bibliography or citations; I just want a clear philosophical analysis of whether self-reference alone can carry the weight of self-awareness, or whether interpretation is doing hidden work that the analogy obscures.
