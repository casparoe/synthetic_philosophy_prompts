Consider the reported pattern of intentionality attributions from Dr Alina Moravec’s experimental-philosophy laboratory, which investigates hybrid-agency scenarios in which human decision-makers act through automated intermediaries or collective structures. The group’s consistent judgments, as summarised in recent methodological reviews, are as follows.

1. A pharmaceutical director approves a drug rollout knowing it will cause liver toxicity in a small subset of patients, an undesired side effect of maximising profit. Verdict: the toxicity is brought about intentionally.

2. The same director approves an identical rollout knowing it will cure an unrelated condition in a small subset, equally undesired as a side effect of the same profit motive. Verdict: the cure is not brought about intentionally.

3. The director deploys an algorithmic pricing tool, sets broad profitability parameters, and deliberately avoids examining patient-access outcomes; she knows that such tools generally restrict access. The tool does restrict access for a specific patient group. Verdict: the restriction is intentional.

4. The same tool, through an unforeseen interaction, expands access for a different group. Verdict: the expansion is not intentional.

5. The director sits on a three-person board that unanimously approves a harmful pricing policy; in a preliminary round her vote breaks a tie, but in the final vote she is one of three identical votes. Verdict: she intentionally caused the harm.

6. The board unanimously approves a policy that accidentally benefits a group. Verdict: the benefit is not intentional.

Now consider a new case: the director constructs a double-blind delegation protocol. She instructs a subordinate to configure an AI pricing system with profitability targets, but explicitly directs that no access-related outcomes be reported back to her. She knows the architecture generally produces exclusion, yet she has engineered epistemic distance so that she lacks any specific representation of which patients are affected. The system excludes a specific group. The harm is foreseen at the architectural level but is not represented in her practical reasoning about this particular instance.

Predict Moravec’s verdict—intentional or not—and defend your answer as the one best consistent with her track record. In doing so, identify at least two distinct interpretive rules that could rationalise cases 1–6, state what each rule predicts for the double-blind delegation case, and argue which rule offers the closer fit. If the rules genuinely underdetermine her verdict, say so plainly and specify what principle she would be adopting with either choice. Finally, propose one further case—involving either a beneficial side effect or a different structural feature—that would most cleanly discriminate between the rules you have identified.

Your response should be committed: take a definite position on the predicted verdict, on the governing rule, and on the discriminating case, giving your reasons rather than laying out options and leaving the choice open. You may note where your reasoning depends on contested interpretations of experimental-philosophy methodology, but do not retreat into non-committal balance.
