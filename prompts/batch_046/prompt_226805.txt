There's a policy in some clinical-data repositories — people call it a mosaic firewall, I think — where a researcher can apply to access dataset A or dataset B separately, but the ethics board refuses to issue both acccess tokens at once because together they let you identify patients with some rare condition. They don't treat either dataset as sensitive on its own; it's specifically the conjuction that creates risk. What view of knowledge or belief does that practice pre-suppose? It looks like a denial of epistemic closure: having knowledge of A and B separately doesn't automatically mean you know A-and-B, at least not in a form the board recognises. But is that consistent with Nozick's tracking theory — if you genuinely track A in one session and B in another, do you track the conjunction? Or does the policy assume tracking conditions break down when beliefs are assembled across separate access events? Reconstruct the implicit epistomology here. Does the firewall strictly need a Nozick-style rejection of closure, or could a straightforward probabilistic view justify it just as well? And tell me what would have to change in data-access rules if we replaced the mosaic logic with a closure-accepting stance — say, if we said anyone who knows both parts automatically knows the whole, so blocking access is pointless?
