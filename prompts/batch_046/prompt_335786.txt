I'm drafting this scene where my protaganist gets drunk at a dinner party and tries to prove that formal poetry can't really be translated, only replaced, and now I'm stuck because the argument feels like it proves way to much—like, if he's right then every English version of a French poem is just fanfiction wearing someone else's hat. (1) A poem's rhyme scheme and meter aren't ornament; they're structural, like load-bearing walls—this is the structural homology view that Hofstadter pushes with Marot's "Ma mignonne". (2) So if a text keeps the propositional content but ditches the rhyme, it hasn't translated the poem, it's moved the furniture into a different building. (3) Now formal features are language-specific; French alexandrines don't map onto English pentameter, they just can't carry over identically. (4) Therefore any translation that actually sounds like a poem in English must abandon the original form, which by (2) means it abandons the poem itself. (5) But we still talk about "the" English Marot, and Hofstadter shows people do try to keep the beams. (6) So either translation is impossible and we're all lying, or form isn't structural, or there's a hidden slide between steps. I really want to hang onto (1) and (4)—there's something brutal and true about it—but I need to know which premise to surrender and what it costs me. Is there an equivocation between "the same poem" and "a faithful rendering"? Or is the mistake thinking that structural homology requires identical formal patterns rather than analog ones?
