Consider the institutional design found in modern constitutional democracies in which courts hold authority to invalidate legislation and executive action on grounds of justice, rights, or constitutional principle, despite possessing no independent coercive apparatus; enforcement depends entirely on the executive branch that the courts sometimes restrain. Concurrently, these systems maintain the professional and constitutional norm that judicial authority derives solely from normative-legal considerations and must not be acknowledged as contingent on executive cooperation, political will, or the underlying distribution of coercive capacity—so that delayed or resisted compliance is treated as a malfunction or constitutional crisis rather than as the revelation of a structural dependence. Meanwhile, the executive asserts that its monopoly on legitimate force is valid only within constraints recognized by those same courts.

What philosophical view of the relation between justice and force does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it hold that justice can be made authoritative without being corrupted by the force it borrows; that coercive power can be fully subordinated to normative principle; or that the separation of powers resolves the tension—familiar from Pascal—between justice's inherent weakness and force's inherent tendency toward tyranny? Be precise about whether the practice strictly requires this view, or whether it remains compatible with a Pascalian account that treats the union of justice and force as unstable and never fully rationalized. Distinguish strict presupposition from mere coherence: could a realist who regards judicial review as merely labeling exercises of power still make sense of these structures, or does the norm of judicial independence and the framing of non-compliance as crisis rule that out?

Finally, say what would have to change in practice if the implicit theory were replaced by Pascal's view that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that consequently what is strong must be made just rather than justice being made strong. Would courts need to acknowledge openly their dependence on coercive execution? Would the hierarchical ordering of judicial over executive authority collapse, or be reconceived as an explicit negotiation? And is the current practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of justice and force would license the same institutional form?
