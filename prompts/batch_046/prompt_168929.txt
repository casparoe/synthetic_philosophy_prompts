Question: AI researchers sometimes claim a large model develops its own internal 'language' for hidden-state patterns that humans can't decode. In about 250 words, apply Wittgenstein's private language argument. Is the model's internal representation-system genuinely private in his sense? Use the beetle-in-the-box thought experiment and be precise about rules and public criteron.

Student answer:
Wittgenstein's private language argument targets the idea that one person could invent a language for sensations only they can access. He uses the beetle in the box: if everyone has a box with something inside called a beetle, and no one can ever look inside anyone else's box, then the word 'beetle' doesn't actually refer to the contents — it could be empty for all the language cares. The point isn't that private things don't exist, but that they drop out of the language-game.

Applying this to an AI, if the model has internal representations that only it can access, they seem like beetles in boxes. But there's a key difference. Humans learn language socially, and the AI was also trained on public data. So the rules that shape its representations come from outside. That means its internal language isn't really private, because the criteria for correct use are already embedded in the public training process. The AI researchers confuse hiddenness with privacy.

Still, the analogy isn't perfect. Wittgenstein's argument assumes one speaker trying to establish rules for correct usage alone. The AI isn't establishing rules at all — it's just optimizing predictions. So we shouldn't call its internal activations a language, since there's no normative practice of getting it right or wrong inside the model. The representations exist, but they don't function like words in a language-game because there's no possibility of mistake that matters to the model itself.

There's also a verifcationist angle: if there's no possible check on whether the AI's internal label matches its state, then talk of those states in the language is empty. So the private-language problem for AI is really about the lack of verification conditions.
