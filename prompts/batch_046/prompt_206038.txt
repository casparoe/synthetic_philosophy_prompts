You advise a government office that reviews proposed official acknowledgments, museum exhibits, and school-curriculum modules addressing past state-sponsored violence. You must deliver a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, sufficient-answer conditions, hard stops, and explicit escalation points—grounded in Karl Jaspers’ fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, together with the problem of whether and how guilt can be transmitted across generations to people who did not perform the wrong. The staff who will run this are lawyers and policy officers with no philosophy training; each proposal gets two hours and must be signed off by a director who will not read the underlying theory. Design the procedure as a flowchart in words: begin with identification of the proposing entity and the collective subject named; classify which of the four guilt-types is being invoked; apply a continuity test to determine whether the present collective is the same institutional subject that committed the wrong; distinguish backward-looking culpability assigned to present individuals from forward-looking political or institutional responsibility; and flag any case where metaphysical guilt is being treated as a public, collective predicate that can be declared by the state. Specify the exact criteria or test sentences staff should apply at each gate, mark precisely where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be escalated to the director with a required one-paragraph justification, and state what kinds of disputes the procedure is not equipped to resolve. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a draft national apology stating that present citizens share the guilt of their predecessors for a historical massacre, which the staff wish to approve because it sounds morally serious; (b) a secondary-school history module asking students to write personal apology letters to descendants of victims, framing the exercise as an act of inherited moral guilt; and (c) a museum exhibit labeling a continuously chartered financial institution as institutionally guilty for slave-trade financing two centuries ago while explicitly absolving current employees of personal blame. Include at least one case in which the procedure produces an answer the staff will dislike, and conclude by saying honestly what the procedure cannot do—for example, adjudicating whether metaphysical guilt requires individual existential or religious assent that no public body can mandate on behalf of citizens.
