Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for a graduate epistemology seminar that sorts participants on the regress problem—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—without asking them to identify with labels they may not recognize. Begin from the concrete commitment that justified belief requires supporting reasons, and that those reasons may themselves require further support. Build every branch node as a specific epistemic case or scenario, phrased as a yes/no or either/or question that a student can answer without knowing which philosophical position it points to. The tree must terminate in at least these named leaves: classical foundationalism (termination in beliefs that are immediately or intrinsically justified), holistic coherentism (mutual support among beliefs with no privileged foundations), and strong infinitism (justification through an infinite, non-repeating, non-circular chain of reasons). In addition, include a niche hybrid leaf—bootstrap foundationalism—defined as the view that a belief initially lacks independent justification but acquires it retroactively by initiating a coherent, non-circular chain of further beliefs; locate this leaf precisely in the logical space and note whether your tree genuinely separates it from modest foundationalism and linear coherentism, or flag it if they remain indistinguishable. At each branching, specify the combination of answers that leads to each leaf, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that share all answers, indicating the tree fails to discriminate them. Also identify at least one unoccupied combination of answers—explain briefly whether that gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the tree to no more than five branch nodes and no more than eight leaves. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure named theories beyond the three core positions and the bootstrap hybrid defined above; and do not require knowledge of specific texts or authors to answer any node.
