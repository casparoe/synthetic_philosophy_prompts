I facilitate a philosophy-of-psychiatry workshop for eight graduate students in a single two-hour session. Design a structured classroom activity that makes participants experience Ian Hacking’s looping effects from the inside—through enactment rather than exposition—before the concept is named. Invent a plausible but non-standard diagnostic category, for instance “refractive attention disorder” characterized by selective, context-dependent withdrawal from sustained linear tasks, and construct a three-round simulation. In each round, half the group serves as a classification committee that operationalizes and then revises diagnostic criteria, while the other half serves as evaluated subjects who adapt their self-presentation and task behavior in response to the criteria they have just received. Specify explicit rules governing how criteria shift based on observed adaptations, include a small-stakes payoff structure (e.g., points or privileges tied to classification outcomes), and provide a minute-by-minute timeline. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that the subjects’ strategic adjustments in round two visibly alter the committee’s criteria in round three, producing the loop as an emergent result rather than a told one. The debrief must map specific moments in the simulation onto Hacking’s mechanism—classification affecting individuals, who then change in ways that feed back into the category—while being explicit about which institutional feature of real-world psychiatric taxonomy the simulation necessarily simplifies, such as the long temporal lag between diagnostic revision and population-level behavioral change or the embedded power asymmetry of clinical authority. Additionally, identify the one feature of Hacking’s account—most likely the historical contingency and social negotiation involved in the category’s original creation—that the activity’s artificial closure prevents participants from encountering, and state that the facilitator must supply this verbally. Keep the prompt formal and polished; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
