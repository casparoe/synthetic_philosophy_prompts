I have been restructuring my advanced undergraduate seminar on collective action and moral responsibility—about sixteen students, ninety minutes, meeting once a week in a discussion-heavy format—and I keep running into the same pedagogical wall with the inefficacy objection. The students can rehearse the standard climate-meat-voting triad and explain, in abstract terms, why individual contributions appear causally redundant within massive aggregates. What they do not yet feel is the specific phenomenological pull of the objection: the sense that one’s agency is not merely diluted but structurally absorbed, so that the ordinary moral vocabulary of harm and complicity seems to misapply. I would like you to design a runnable, in-class simulation—rules, roles, materials, and a payoff scheme that requires no actual money—that makes them encounter this absorption themselves before anyone names it.

The setting is a single 90-minute session. The participants should come away seeing why causal inefficacy feels like a genuine defeater of individual obligation, but also experiencing the tension between that intuition and the competing intuition that something is still wrong with free-riding within a harmful structure. To keep the design from collapsing into a standard public-goods game, please inject a more granular distinction: structure the activity so that there is both a hidden binary threshold (where collective harm is triggered only if participation exceeds an unknown cutoff) and a probabilistic “moral risk” layer in which each participant’s expected contribution to harm can be calculated ex post, even when they were demonstrably not the decisive voter, emitter, or consumer. The rules should arrange things so that participants first act under conditions of radical uncertainty about whether they are pivotal, then discover—through revealed payoffs and aggregated outcomes—that they contributed to harm without being able to point to any counterfactual dependence between their specific choice and the outcome.

Please include a precise timeline, role assignments or group structures, the debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation onto (a) causal inefficacy, (b) normative or deontic inefficacy, and (c) the structural-absorption intuition, and be explicit about what the activity can and cannot reproduce—particularly whether it can genuinely capture the difference between threshold causation and probabilistic expected harm, or whether that distinction must be supplied in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—whether students will converge on cooperative norms too quickly, misread the hidden thresholds, or conflate the payoff scheme with real-world utility—and how to handle it without lecturing them into the conclusion in advance. I want no bibliography, no assigned readings for preparation, and no citation of specific authors in the rules themselves: the idea should live in what happens to them, not in what I tell them afterwards.
