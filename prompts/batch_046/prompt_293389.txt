I have been reading about evolutionary explanation and reductionism, and recently found myself in a discussion with colleagues about whether artificial intelligence systems should have ethical constraints engineered into their architectures or allowed to develop values organically through training dynamics. One interlocutor kept returning to the slogan that "there are only cranes, never skyhooks," apparently treating it as a decisive objection to any form of top-down value design. I am unsure whether he intended a metaphysical claim about the non-existence of irreducible high-level properties, an epistemological claim about the illegitimacy of non-emergent explanation, a practical claim that engineered values are necessarily unstable, or the charge of greedy reductionism—the idea that explaining away a phenomenon destroys rather than illuminates it. Could you separate these precise readings, indicating for each whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or an outright falsehood, and specify which version actually supports the conclusion that hard-coding ethical constraints is illegitimate?

In particular, I want the answer structured as a menu rather than a single interpretation: lay out the distinct principles the slogan might stand for, say what kind of "crane" and what kind of "skyhook" are at issue in the alignment context, identify where the argument requires greedy reductionism to do hidden work, and flag which reading my colleague was probably invoking when he used the phrase to end the debate. I first encountered the expression in evolutionary philosophy of mind, but in this setting it was being asked to settle a concrete disagreement about design methodology, so the precisification that matters is the one that actually bears on whether top-down constraints can be coherent, stable, and genuinely normative.
