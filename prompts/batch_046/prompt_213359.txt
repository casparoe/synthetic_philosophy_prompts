A recent essay in a literary review—whose author is not named—offers the following summary of Plutarch’s Parallel Lives and their relation to moral biography and hagiography. Plutarch, the essay claims, was not writing history but secular hagiography, a form that scholars designate bios exemplaris. His technique of synkrisis was intended not simply to juxtapose a Greek and a Roman figure but to construct moral taxonomies: virtues and vices are sorted into stable types, and the pairing serves as a classificatory grid. The essay notes that even apparent flaws—such as Alcibiades’ recklessness being contrasted with Pericles’ steadiness—function purely as narrative foils that make the dominant virtue more visible, rather than indicating any real moral complexity in the subject. It further asserts that Christian vitae adopted this same architecture of selective idealization, merely substituting miracles for political achievements, and that modern biography has erred by pursuing documentary completeness instead of this Plutarchan-hagiographic method of suppressing contradictory detail in order to present a unified character for ethical imitation.

I have read enough to suspect that this account conflates Plutarch with later imitators and misrepresents how the pairings actually work, but I cannot identify the precise mistakes. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish between what accurately captures Plutarch’s aims and methods, what is a standard simplification or an anachronistic projection of hagiographic conventions onto the Lives, and what is subtly wrong—particularly regarding the function of synkrisis, the treatment of moral flaws, the actual pairing of Alcibiades, and whether the normative recommendation for modern biography follows from anything Plutarch actually practiced.
