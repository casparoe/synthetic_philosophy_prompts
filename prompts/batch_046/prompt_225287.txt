I've come around to a pretty strong version of the rigid-designator defense against scientific theory change, and I'm definately pushing it further than the usual examples like water or gold. The basic idea is that if a term gets baptised via a causal chain tied to core functional roles, then even massive revision in our understanding of those roles doesn't change the subject—it just reveals we were wrong about the nature of the thing we named. So I think this applies not just to microstructural kinds but to functional-theoretic terms like 'gene', 'working memory', or 'ecosystem', where there's no hidden essence backing the reference. Even if we eventually fold microbiome interactions into what's going on in a host's cognitive processes, or if 'ecosystem' gets redrawn to include atmospheric feedback loops we previously ignored, we're still tracking the same kind—just with revised boundaries. That means the pessimistic meta-induction misses, because past scientists weren't referring to different entities; they were referring correctly but describing them badly.

What would actually count against this view? I want the arguments and cases that should make me weaken or drop it—not generic objections to Kripke, but things specific to extending rigidity to functional kinds without essences. Are there historical occurences of revision that look more like a genuine reference shift than descriptive correction? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any counterexample gets reinterpreted as "we just corrected the description"? And if so, is that insulation a problem for a metaphysical claim of this sort?

Also please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of—probably exactly where the line between reference shift and descriptive correction sits for these functional cases.
