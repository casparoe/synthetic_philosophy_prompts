I have been working through the wrong-kind-reasons literature for a seminar on normative psychology, and I constructed the following argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve the very distinction it starts from. The argument concerns whether practical reasons can ever be genuinely wrong-kind for belief if we allow that they can justify a closely related cognitive attitude. Here are the steps:

(1) A reason R is of the wrong kind for an attitude A if and only if R does not bear on the constitutive normative standard of A. (2) The constitutive normative standard of belief is truth; practical utility is not. (3) Therefore practical reasons are wrong-kind reasons for belief. (4) However, philosophers have defended a distinct attitude—call it practical acceptance—whose constitutive standard is not truth but practical coherence or action-guidance. (5) By (1) and (4), practical reasons are right-kind reasons for practical acceptance. (6) Now, rational agents are subject to a principle of doxastic integration: if one rationally accepts p for practical reasoning over an extended period, and one has no countervailing epistemic reason against p, then one ought eventually to believe p. (7) So if a scientist has overwhelming practical reason to accept her climate model—funding, policy impact, institutional commitment—and she lacks decisive counter-evidence, then by (5) and (6) she is rationally required to come to believe the model is true. (8) But then the original practical reasons, though wrong-kind for belief directly, generate an indirect rational requirement to believe. (9) Hence there is no stable category of wrong-kind reasons for belief—any reason that can justify acceptance can, via integration, justify belief. (10) This seems to collapse the wrong-kind-reasons problem into a trivial point about directness.

I feel that something slips between (6) and (7), or perhaps in the formulation of (6) itself, but I cannot name the error. Is (6) false because acceptance and belief can be permanently compartmentalized? Or is the mistake earlier, in assuming that the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction applies cleanly to acceptance in the first place? Or is the argument actually valid, and we must reject (1) or (3) and pay the cost of allowing that practical considerations can sometimes be direct reasons for belief? I want to know which step to reject, why it initially looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs for our understanding of belief's normative structure—particularly whether belief remains uniquely governed by truth or becomes just one node in a unified practical-cognitive system. I would prefer to preserve the idea that belief is distinctively answerable to truth, but I am willing to abandon that if the cost of keeping it is too high.
