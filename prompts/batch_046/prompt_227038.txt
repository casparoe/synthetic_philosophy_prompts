I am a high school student writing a paper for an ethics elective, and I am trying to work out my own reflective equilibrium on whether ordinary social conventions carry genuine moral weight, especially for outsiders. Rather than asking for a bibliography, I want to test my intuitions against a principle. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: In a U.S. restaurant where servers depend on tips, leaving nothing feels morally wrong, not merely rude. Case 2: In Japan, leaving a cash tip can insult the staff; leaving nothing is correct, and leaving one feels wrong. Case 3: Jaywalking across an empty Berlin street still strikes me as wrong, though I cannot point to any harm I cause. Case 4: Failing to bow with the exact local form when entering a small Kyoto shop strikes me as a breach of etiquette, but not of morality. Case 5: In a town with a silent queuing convention—people reserve bakery spots by placing a folded umbrella on the ground—moving someone else’s umbrella to take the spot feels clearly wrong, even though no one is physically pushed. Case 6: A local resident who ignores that umbrella system seems to me more seriously at fault than a visitor who does the same, yet I also think the visitor should learn the system; I keep going back and forth on whether that “should” is moral or only prudential. Case 7: A visitor to Saudi Arabia who refuses to cover her hair in a public market feels to me like she is committing a real moral failure, not just being culturally insensitive—though I realize that conflicts with Case 4, since both are local dress and greeting norms. Case 8: In parts of Italy, not making eye contact during a toast is said to bring seven years of bad luck; as a visitor, I feel no moral obligation to observe this superstition, even if locals treat it as serious.

What principle, if any, captures this pattern? I have tentatively wondered whether conventions become moral obligations only when they function as coordination mechanisms that prevent collective harm, and whether visitors are bound only when non-compliance actively disrupts that coordination rather than merely marking them as outsiders. But that seems to struggle with Case 3 (no harm from jaywalking) and Case 7 (hair covering seems more about respect than coordination).

Please respond in American English and organize your answer in the following sections, with approximate lengths: (1) “Pattern Restatement” (about 100 words): summarize the verdicts I have offered and highlight the tension between Case 4 and Case 7. (2) “Candidate Principles” (about 250 words): present two or three precise principles that might capture the pattern, and for each state which case motivates each clause. (3) “Unconsidered Cases” (about 150 words): apply the leading principle to situations I have not mentioned—specifically, a tourist who refuses to remove shoes before entering a home in Seoul, and a local who refuses to stand on the correct side of an escalator in London. Give the principle’s verdict on each and explain whether it feels right. (4) “The Cost of Consistency” (about 150 words): identify which of my original verdicts—probably Case 3, Case 6, or Case 7—must be abandoned or revised to keep the best principle, and say honestly what makes that revision costly. (5) “Bottom Line on the Visitor in the Umbrella Queue” (about 100 words): state whether the visitor is morally required, prudentially advised, or neither, and say whether your answer depends on whether she knew about the system beforehand.
