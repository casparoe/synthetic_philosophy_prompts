I hold a qualified modernist position on nationhood, closer to Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented traditions and Anderson’s account of imagined communities, but modified by a claim I take to be under-explored: that modern nations are not fabricated ex nihilo out of print-capitalism and bureaucratic standardisation alone, but are rather “reassembled” from what I call dormant categorical residues—pre-modern ethno-linguistic, ritual, or territorial classifications that survive state collapse without themselves constituting nations, and that modern elites selectively reactivate rather than invent. On this view, the nation is irreducibly modern in its institutional and subjective form, yet its constituent fragments possess a kind of historical persistence that pure invention narratives systematically understate.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the familiar perennialist objections about ancient Egypt or medieval France possessing “national consciousness,” but the specific historical cases, conceptual distinctions, archaeological or linguistic findings, and interdisciplinary arguments that ought to move me. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which ones, if established, would compel me to abandon the “reassembly” claim and revert either to a stronger invention thesis or to a weaker perennialist one; which ones would merely demand further qualification. I also want an honest assessment of whether this qualified view has been insulated against refutation—whether the notion of “dormant residues” can absorb any counter-evidence by redescribing apparent continuity as mere dormancy, and whether that insulation is intellectually virtuous or vicious for a claim of this kind.

Please include: (i) what a committed defender of this qualified modernism would themselves concede as counting against them, not only what perennialists or ethnosymbolists say; (ii) whether there are predictions this view makes that neither pure invention nor pure perennialism make, and what it would take for those predictions to fail; (iii) whether the framework can coherently describe cases of nation-formation where no plausible “residue” exists, and if so whether that is a problem or a feature. Write your answer in British English.
