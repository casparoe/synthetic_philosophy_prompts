I'm writing a paper for my world history class and I'm stuck on this argument my roommate cooked up about societal collapse. It feels like it proves too much—either every big transformation is a collapse or none are—but I can't find the slip.

(1) If we call something a "collapse" we need an observable change, not just a value judgment, so losing writing, central government, and large-scale monument-building counts, but "failure" doesn't. (2) By that standard the Late Bronze Age, Classic Maya, and Easter Island all collapsed: they lost those instutions. (3) But they also changed—populations stayed, farming continued, new forms of organization emerged. (4) So if collapse is just institutional loss, then the fall of Rome, the Maya transformation, and even the shift from feudalism to nation-states are all collapses. (5) That makes "collapse" so broad it's useless for explaining why some changes feel catastrophic and others don't. (6) So we should say societies never really collapse; they just adapt to lower energy or complexity, and "collapse" is just a narrative we impose when we don't like the new form. (7) But then Easter Island's ecological crisis and the Bronze Age's widespread violence and displacement become mere "reorganization," which feels wrong. (8) So (1) must be false, and we need some normative or experiential component in "collapse."

My roommate thinks the problem is in (3)—that change and collapse aren't mutually exclusive, and losing writing is genuinely catastrophic even if people survive. I really want to keep (1) and (6) if I can, but giving up (1) seems to cost me the whole objective framing. Where does the argument actually break, and what's the price of fixing it? Please answer in American English, and don't just say it's all relative—I need a real diagnosis.
