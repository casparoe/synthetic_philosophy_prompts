I serve on my high school's Civic Studies Committee, and our Board of Trustees has charged us with drafting a formal opinion — for circulation before Thursday's meeting — on the future of our ceremonial "Student Sovereign," a non-voting representative selected by seniority who presides over assemblies, administers a modest cultural-events budget, and holds no legislative authority. The Board is genuinely divided: one faction maintains that retaining any appointment-based ceremonial office undermines the democratic ethos of our student government by institutionalizing unearned deference, while the opposing faction argues that, precisely as in the constitutional monarchies of the United Kingdom, Scandinavia, and Japan, a strictly delimited symbolic figure can stabilize collective identity and institutional memory without encroaching on democratic governance.

Please recommend a concrete course of action — retain with explicit structural reforms, transition to an elected ceremonial chair within a defined time frame, or abolish the office entirely — and ground the recommendation in criteria the Board can apply to analogous proposals in the future. The memo should engage the democratic objection seriously rather than dismissing it: specifically, it should examine whether symbolic authority can ever be fully neutral in a pluralistic school community, compare how British, Scandinavian, and Japanese arrangements manage the tension between democratic legitimacy and ceremonial continuity, and explain what features of those macro-institutional models do and do not translate to our micro-institutional setting. Avoid treating sovereignty as a purely legal category; instead, treat the question as one of democratic culture, asking whether the presence of an appointment-based figurehead shapes norms of inclusion, voice, and prestige in ways that elected structures alone do not.
