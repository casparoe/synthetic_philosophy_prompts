I've been working on a feature about how philosophers of social ontology are borrowing from institutional theory—Searle's line that money is just paper plus collective acceptance—to explain gender and race. There's this newer, kind of niche framing that treats gender not just as performed in the Butler sense but as a 'status-function' assigned through collective recognition, almost like a currency that only works if everyone agrees to cash it. Anyway, I was rewatching Twelfth Night the other night and it struck me: Viola doesn't just perform masculinity, she gets assigned the status of 'Cesario' by Orsino's recognition and Olivia's desire, and the plot only resolves when the collective 'currency' of gender gets re-issued. Could you read the play through that lens—where gender operates like an institutional kind whose power comes from collective assignment rather then individual expression? I'm especially curious whether the play treats Viola's disguise as a kind of counterfeit money that eventually gets redeemed, or whether it suggests the assignment system itself is arbitary. Which scenes—the Olivia/Cesario garden encounter, maybe, or the final reveal—show the idea best, and where does the play resist being reduced to a philosophy lesson? I'm trying to avoid writing something that makes Shakespeare into a grad student, so I'd like to know what the play actually adds to the debate that a paper on institutional gender wouldn't capture on its own.
