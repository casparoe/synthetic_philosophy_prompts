For an MA-level philosophy-of-mind take-home exam (48 hours, approximately 1,200 words), draft a single essay question on the extended mind thesis that cannot be answered by reciting Clark and Chalmers’s parity principle or by listing standard objections. Present a niche, concrete case: a master instrument-maker whose embodied skill relies on generations of unwritten workshop adjustments physically embedded in the tools themselves—jigs, worn surfaces, and calibrated tolerances that no single craftsperson fully articulates—so that the “knowledge” seems distributed across the maker, the tools, and the historical workshop rather than residing in any individual head. Ask students to determine whether this genuinely counts as extended cognition or merely as causal scaffolding, requiring them to navigate the coupling-constitution distinction and the cognitive-bloat worry without appealing to the familiar Otto-and-Inga template. Provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of about 800 words, and then a marking scheme totaling 30 marks built around five specific moves that separate an A from a B or C answer: for each move, state its point value, what the move must accomplish in relation to the craftsperson case, and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it (e.g., conflating reliable causal dependence with constitutive extension, or treating bloat as a reductio without addressing the functional-parity reply). The question should test whether students can construct the argument from the ground up for an unfamiliar scenario rather than reproducing textbook summaries.
