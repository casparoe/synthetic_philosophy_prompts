I am planning a ninety-minute seminar for fellow novelists—writers of multi-perspective political fiction with no training in formal epistemology—on the common prior assumption and its bearing on rational disagreement. I want them to grasp why two agents with identical evidence can persistently diverge in belief when their priors differ, and how this structural fact undermines any naive identification of narrative reliability with rational convergence. Design the teaching plan: specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, noting which dependency must be secured before disagreement can appear rational rather than merely obstinate; identify the single carrying example, drawn from fiction craft rather than economics, that demonstrates prior divergence producing opposite conclusions from shared information alone, and state precisely what features it must include and what confounding elements—bias, ignorance, emotional interference—it must exclude; catalog the misconceptions this audience will likely form, particularly the conflation of common priors with shared opinions and the assumption that rationality mandates belief convergence, and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; propose one diagnostic question that reveals whether they have understood the structural point rather than adopted a superficial relativism; and explicitly state what formal apparatus should be omitted, named as omitted, and replaced by conceptual paraphrase. The goal is conceptual precision suitable for intelligent non-specialists, not mathematical literacy.
