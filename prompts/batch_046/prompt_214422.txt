I am preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar on cross-cultural epistemology for students well-versed in Quine and Sellars but with no prior exposure to classical Indian philosophy. I need a teaching plan—not an exposition—for the Nyāya doctrine of the four pramāṇas (perception or pratyakṣa, inference or anumāna, comparison or upamāna, and verbal testimony or śabda). Plan the sequence: first, the dependency order in which the four must be introduced, with explicit reasoning for why perception cannot be treated as a self-standing given but must be articulated through its relation to inference and comparison, and for why testimony must come last rather than serving as the foundation; second, identify the one carrying example—specifically the misperception of mother-of-pearl as silver (śuktirajata)—and specify exactly what it must demonstrate about the Nyāya distinction between successful cognition (pramā) and erroneous cognition (bhrama), particularly how to prevent students from reducing it to a modern “sense-data mistake”; third, map the misconceptions this particular audience will import—such as treating Nyāya as Lockean empiricism in Sanskrit, collapsing anumāna into Humean induction, or treating śabda as mere hearsay—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; fourth, propose one diagnostic question to ask at the end that reveals whether students grasp why even apparently immediate perceptual judgments involve a qualified-qualificand (viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣya) structure rather than raw givenness; and fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as the detailed Nyāya-Sūtra bhāṣya tradition on the conditions for verbal cognition—and acknowledge that omission openly.
