In discussions of Aristotle's sea-battle argument in De Interpretatione, a persistent stalemate has divided philosophers for decades: one camp maintains that the threat to determinism arises only if one accepts bivalence for all future-tensed statements, and resolves the problem by restricting the law of excluded middle to present and past truths; the opposing camp insists that bivalence remains intact and that the genuine issue is whether present facts about past deliberations causally necessitate tomorrow's naval engagement. Both sides agree that the battle is genuinely possible rather than predetermined, and both claim to preserve "contingency," yet neither has moved the other. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about logic versus causation, but about the nature of modal openness itself: one side treats openness as ontological (there is literally no fact of the matter about the battle until it occurs), while the other treats it as purely epistemic (the outcome is already fixed but presently inaccessible). If that is correct, then much of the debate is verbal—both sides invoke "contingency" for different properties—though a hidden formal disagreement about the semantics of future-tense operators or about whether truth requires present grounds may also be doing work. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually commits to: do they differ about the metaphysics of time, about standards of explanation, about which cases count as data for a theory of possibility, or about bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for? And is the original question well-posed, or is it ill-posed in a way that explains the impasse? Finally, specify what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis—a formal result in temporal logic, a precise distinction between ontological and epistemic openness, a reframing of Aristotle's original argument, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
