Here are the notes and outline for my essay, tentatively titled Honoring What You Have Become: Sunk Costs and Identity-Constitutive Investment.

Standard economic and decision-theoretic treatments of the sunk-cost fallacy treat all past expenditures as fungible resources that a rational agent should disregard when choosing how to proceed. I want to argue that this model fails for a specific class of commitments that I call identity-constitutive investments. These are not merely large, irreversible, or emotionally charged expenditures, but structurally non-fungible commitments that have become partially constitutive of an agent’s diachronic practical identity. For example, years of sustaining a failing family enterprise not because it remains profitable, but because the agent’s conception of responsibility and loyalty is now inseparable from that care; or an artist’s refusal to abandon a technically failed medium because the struggle with its limitations has reshaped her aesthetic sensibility and her first-personal narrative of artistic development.

My central claim is that honoring sunk costs in these cases is not irrational, because the cost is not a separable resource that can be written off without altering the agent who writes it off. To abandon the commitment is not to save resources; it is to dissolve a part of the self that was formed through them. I distinguish this view from mere sentimental attachment or loss aversion by insisting that identity-constitutive investments must satisfy two conditions: (i) the commitment must be structurally non-fungible, meaning it cannot be replaced by an equivalent commitment without producing a practical discontinuity in the agent’s life; and (ii) the agent’s first-personal narrative of practical reasons must treat the commitment as a source of normative authority, not merely as a cause of emotional discomfort or status-quo bias.

The essay will then address the standard economic rejoinder: that even if abandonment alters the self, rationality requires maximizing future welfare, and a changed self is simply a new preference-set to be optimized prospectively. I want to reply that this rejoinder begs the question by assuming that practical rationality is always prospective and preference-neutral, whereas identity-constitutive investment reveals that rationality can also be retrospective and fidelity-structured—without collapsing into uncritical traditionalism or essentialism about the self.
