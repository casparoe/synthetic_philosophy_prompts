I am presenting next Tuesday at a joint metaphysics and philosophy-of-biology colloquium on a restricted answer to the special composition question. My thesis is that parts compose a genuine whole if and only if they enter reciprocal causal maintenance: each part’s continued functional existence depends on processes sustained by other parts within a shared boundary, generating a stability that no part could maintain in isolation. I apply this primarily to living organisms, but I also extend it—controversially—to certain synthetic tissue networks that achieve analogous interdependence, arguing that metaphysical composition should track organizational dynamics rather than spatial contiguity or biological origin. The audience of about fifteen includes three metaphysicians who lean universalist or pluralist and regard biological examples as metaphysical crutches; two philosophers of science in the mechanistic-explanation tradition who think talk of “composition” is redundant with organizational structure; one cell biologist skeptical that metaphysicians can say anything about living systems not already in her textbooks; and graduate students familiar with the standard literature on special composition but not with mechanist philosophy. I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Order the objections roughly as they will arrive—mechanists first, then metaphysicians, then the biologist—phrased in the vocabulary each group actually uses. For each, state what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the single reply that has to land if the synthetic-tissue extension is to survive. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody raises, with a two-minute damage-control reply and an honest assessment of where that reply is soft. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need material that works without online access.
