Here is where I have landed: (1) I accept the care-ethical critique of liberal individualism—moral reasoning should begin from concrete, asymmetrical relationships rather than from the standpoint of detached, rights-bearing agents; (2) I think that genuine care sometimes requires overriding the immediate expressed preferences of a dependent—say, insisting that a resistant elderly parent take medication—because relational autonomy is not the same as deference to every present desire; (3) I believe that direct care labor should be socially recognized and compensated, but I reject both market pricing and familial privatization, proposing instead a mandatory rotational civic-care year for every adult, administered through neighborhood assemblies rather than state bureaucracies; (4) I take emotional attentiveness and the management of others' feelings to constitute a form of practical moral knowledge, not merely an affective burden; and (5) I maintain that care ethics is fundamentally incompatible with consequentialist aggregation, since attending to the particular other precludes treating welfare as fungible across persons.

These five commitments feel right individually, but I am unsure whether they form a stable package. Meanwhile, (4) and (5) might pull in different directions about whether emotional labor can be measured or compared across contexts. I am not signaling which commitment I would drop if forced, and I do not know whether the tensions are real or only apparent. Could you audit the set? For each pair that is genuinely in tension—not merely stylistically awkward—please indicate how severe the conflict is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs in terms of the other commitments. Then rank the tensions by severity and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems at once. I want the assessment point by point rather than a general impression, and please do not treat all five as equally compatible just to be polite.
