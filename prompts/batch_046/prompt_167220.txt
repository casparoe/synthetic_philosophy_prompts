I am leading a fifty-minute discussion section for third-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished Mill's Utilitarianism and are now encountering the heterogeneity problem for hedonism in our value-theory course. The central puzzle is whether pleasures as disparate as the focused satisfaction of completing a complex proof, the passive warmth of sunlight on skin, and the quiet contentment of remembering a friend share any common phenomenal quality that makes them all pleasures—or whether hedonism is left with nothing more substantive than the claim that people pursue whatever they happen to pursue. I find that students consistently miss why this is a problem specifically for hedonism rather than a generic observation about mental diversity, and I want a teaching plan that treats the objection as a live conceptual wedge forcing a choice between phenomenological essentialism and attitudinal reconstruction, not as a historical footnote.

Plan the explanatory arc, not the explanation itself. Begin by mapping the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—likely between the evaluative thesis that pleasure is the sole good and the descriptive thesis that pleasure is a uniform mental kind—must be firmly in place before the heterogeneity objection can register as a threat rather than a category mistake, and then sequence the steps that lead from that distinction to the modern split between phenomenological and attitudinal hedonism. Next, specify the single carrying example or paired contrast that should bear the weight of the argument: it must present two states that are uncontroversially pleasures yet seem to share no introspectively accessible quality, but it must avoid examples that smuggle in moral evaluation—such as malicious glee—before the conceptual framework is established, since that causes students to conflate heterogeneity with the separate problem of whether bad pleasures are genuinely good. Clarify exactly what this example needs to include and what it must exclude. Then anticipate the predictable misconceptions: I expect an analog of "sense is mental image," where students imagine hedonists posit a single, identifiable "pleasure feeling" like a specific shade of mental color; I also expect confusion with Sidgwick's qualitative comparison argument, leading them to think the problem is about ranking pleasures rather than identifying them. Indicate precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and neutralized. Propose a final check that reveals whether students understand why heterogeneity undermines hedonism specifically—that is, whether they can articulate what is lost if pleasure turns out to be only a family resemblance—rather than merely repeating that pleasures are diverse. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as such—perhaps the neuroscience of reward pathways or the full phenomenology of intentionality—and do not provide any bibliography, reading list, or citation.
