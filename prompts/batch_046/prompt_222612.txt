As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science, I am organizing a single ninety-minute session for a small circle of fellow retired natural scientists—an engineer, a biologist, and a medicinal chemist—none of whom have formal philosophical training. I wish to lead them through the problem of causation by omission: specifically, the claim that failing to act can be a genuine cause rather than merely a background condition or counterfactual absence. Rather than a finished lecture, I need a pedagogical architecture. Please specify the exact order in which the conceptual dependencies must be introduced, beginning from the audience's ingrained commitment to mechanistic, positive causation and proceeding through the distinction between a mere absence and a normatively relevant omission, then to the criteria—whether counterfactual, normative, or explanatory—under which an omission acquires causal status. Identify the single concrete scenario that should serve as the carrying case: it should be drawn from scientific or medical practice so that the audience trusts its details, yet structured so that the omission performs indispensable explanatory work and is not reducible to the presence of some other factor. Map the specific misconceptions this audience is likely to generate—particularly the reduction of omissions to statistical correlations missing a variable, the confusion of omission with mere counterfactual dependence, and the reflex to treat absence as physically unreal—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Propose a concise diagnostic question or exercise that would reveal whether the participants understand the explanatory weight of an omission, not merely its logical form. Finally, state explicitly what should be left unaddressed at this level—perhaps legal liability, free will, or the metaphysics of negative facts—to prevent the discussion from diverging. Write this as a teaching plan, not as the explanation itself, and do not include reading lists or citations.
