Exam question: In his critique of the philosophers, Al-Ghazālī argues that the connection between fire and the burning of cotton is not necessary but is produced by God on each occasion according to His customary order ('ada). A recent interpretive proposal suggests that this occasionalism can be reconstructed as a "modal fragility thesis": causal relations are metaphysically contingent not merely in the weak sense that God could suspend them, but in the stronger sense that they are stable only within worlds that instantiate the exact same habitual order, so that in nearby possible worlds the same physical conjunctions might yield no effect at all. Evaluate this reconstruction. How does Al-Ghazālī's position differ from a Humean regularity account of causation? And how does the Ash'arite doctrine of kasb (acquisition) account for human moral agency without attributing independent efficient causation to the agent?

Student answer: Al-Ghazālī denies that fire possesses any intrinsic nature that necessitates burning; rather, God creates the burning on the occasion of contact, in accordance with His customary order ('ada). The modal fragility reconstruction captures this well: it frames the denial of necessary connection not as an epistemic limit—like Hume's claim that we never observe necessary connections in the mind—but as a metaphysical thesis that causal relations hold only within worlds structured by this specific divine habit, and that in nearby worlds the same conjunction might produce nothing without contradiction. This makes Al-Ghazālī's view stronger than Humean regularism, since Hume remains neutral on whether hidden powers exist behind the regularities we observe. On human agency, Al-Ghazālī adopts the Ash'arite doctrine of kasb: the human being exercises a real, though dependent, causal power that cooperates with divine creation, so that the voluntary act is truly the agent's while being simultaneously created by God. This cooperative model distinguishes him both from the Mu'tazila, who assert that humans possess an independent power (qudra) to generate actions, and from strict determinists, who deny that agents have any genuine role in producing their deeds. The reconstruction is therefore largely accurate, though it risks overstatement by treating Al-Ghazālī's theological framework as if it included a systematic modal metaphysics of "nearby worlds," which is anachronistic.
