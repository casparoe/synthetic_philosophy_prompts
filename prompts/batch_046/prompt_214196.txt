Design a realistic municipal charter scenario—no longer than one paragraph, with no science-fictional or fantasy elements—in which a city council amends its own amendment procedure using only the existing procedure, but the amendment introduces a recursive validity clause. The clause should state that the amendment is retroactively valid if and only if a specified condition (for example, voter turnout in a referendum exceeding sixty percent) is satisfied, and the authority to certify whether that condition was met should be vested in a board whose own authority derives entirely from the charter being amended. Engineer the case so that four theoretical positions deliver four distinct verdicts: first, a Hartian rule-of-recognition analysis accepts the amendment as valid because the ultimate social rule of recognition in the municipality recognizes it; second, a Kelsenian basic-norm analysis rejects it on the ground that the hierarchical chain of authorization contains a circular dependency that breaks derivation from the basic norm; third, a Ross-style analysis of legal self-reference treats the loop as logically harmless, because the clause is merely a conditional statement about validity rather than a direct act of self-authorization; and fourth, a nomic-constraint view—introduced here as the idea that constitutional rules function not as social conventions but as institutional analogues of physical laws that constrain official behavior—holds that the rule is valid as a binding constraint but generates an "authority vacuum" for all subsequent amendments, since the constraint binds the certifying board without conferring on it any legitimate power to validate. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate the reasoning for each of the four positions, state the judgment each produces, identify the exact structural feature that does the work (the coupling of retroactive self-validation to an evaluation performed by an authority whose legitimacy depends on the very rule being evaluated), and explain what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed—specifically, whether the case then collapses into a standard Ross-style paradox. Finally, present the strongest counter-attack available to the defender of the position that comes out worst—likely the Kelsenian—who might argue that the recursive clause is not a valid internal amendment but an ultra vires act falling outside the charter's scope, and assess whether that counter-attack succeeds without begging the question against the Hartian acceptance of the amendment.
