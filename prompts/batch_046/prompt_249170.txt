For my graduate seminar on political thought, the discussion of Hannah Arendt’s report on the Eichmann trial and her concept of the banality of evil has reached an impasse. Participants agree on the biographical facts of Eichmann’s role in the logistics of deportation, and they agree that Arendt intended to describe a particular kind of moral failure rather than to excuse it. Yet one camp insists that her claim was empirically falsified by subsequent archival research revealing Eichmann’s ideological zeal, while another camp maintains that this misses the point entirely because Arendt was diagnosing a structural feature of modern bureaucratic evil that does not depend on the subjective psychology of any particular agent. A third, smaller group suggests that the disagreement is not about Eichmann at all but about whether thoughtlessness, which Arendt termed Gedankenlosigkeit, should be treated as a cognitive deficit, a moral vice, or a historically specific mode of self-deception.

Please diagnose the kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it primarily an empirical dispute about Eichmann’s inner life that further archival evidence could settle? Is it a normative dispute about what counts as culpable moral failure, disguised as a historical argument? Or is it a conceptual dispute about whether the category of evil requires intentional malevolence, with Arendt proposing a revisionary taxonomy that her critics simply reject? In your answer, do not survey the interpretations as if they were entries in an encyclopaedia; instead, identify the precise point at which the arguments talk past one another, specify which distinctions (for instance, between explanatory and descriptive uses of banality, or between agent-based and system-based attributions of responsibility) would need to be clarified for progress to occur, and state honestly whether you think any conceivable evidence — psychological, historical, or philosophical — could actually resolve the stalemate, or whether the impasse signals that the participants are answering different questions under a single heading.
