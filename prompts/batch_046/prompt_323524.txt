I lead a first-year philosophy seminar whose students have read only the preface to On the Genealogy of Morals and have no background in nineteenth-century philology. I need a teaching plan for Nietzsche’s three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—and for the specific claim that each becomes poisonous to life when it dominates without the others. Lead with your bottom line: the single dependency that must govern the order of introduction and the one concrete scenario that carries all three modes. Then give the reasoning. Plan the sequence: the exact order in which to present the three modes and the “service to life” criterion, with the dependency that makes that order necessary; one carrying example—a specific municipal decision about a physical site or tradition—that must illustrate all three modes and show precisely how each turns disadvantageous when detached from life; the misconceptions students predictably import from popular talk about “learning from history” and “critically examining the past,” with the point in the sequence where each should be defused; a brief check for understanding that reveals whether they see that critical history is not automatically the healthiest mode; and what to explicitly leave out at this level (for instance, the full argument of the Untimely Meditations, the philological context, or any bibliography) and say so openly. The example should be specific enough that a student could apply the three modes to it immediately, but it must not depend on knowledge of any particular town’s actual history. No preamble, no restatement of the question.
