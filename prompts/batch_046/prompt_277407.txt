For an upper-level political-philosophy seminar of about fourteen students, ninety minutes, design a structured role-play simulation that makes participants feel the institutional logic behind the collapse of Roman republican norms—specifically the transformation of protective mechanisms (extraordinary provincial commands, prorogation of imperium, and tribunician intercessio) into instruments of factional paralysis and executive aggrandizement—without delivering this analysis in advance. Frame the exercise around an explicit analogical target: structural parallels to modern democratic backsliding through emergency powers, legislative gridlock, and the normalization of temporary exceptions. The students should not hear the phrase “constitutional hardball” or the modern comparison named until the debrief.

Specify roles (senators, tribunes, consuls, provincial commanders) with asymmetric information and divergent payoff schedules using only symbolic stakes—reputation tokens and future agenda-setting rights, not money. Structure three rounds (peacetime crisis, external military threat, internal deadlock) governed by rules that initially require unanimous consent for exceptional measures but allow “temporary” exemptions that accumulate across rounds. Arrange the incentives so that rational self-interest drives the group to approve successive exemptions, and design the information structure so that participants discover, through play, that collective safeguards have eroded before anyone labels the pattern as institutional drift.

In your deliverable, include: the full rules and materials (paper, tokens, timer only); a timeline for each round; the precise mechanism—whether a hidden payoff matrix, a secret role card, or an escalating cost schedule—that produces the intended failure mode; and a debrief that maps moments in the simulation onto specific Roman mechanisms and onto modern analogs (executive overreach, patronage-funded private loyalty, legislative obstruction), with an explicit note on which feature of the actual historical collapse—such as the multi-decade temporal scale or the personal clientela network—the simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Also predict what will go wrong on the first run (for example, one student dominating deliberation, or the group accidentally preserving coordination) and explain how to adjust facilitation or rules to preserve the intended breakdown. Make sure the core idea emerges from the rules and stakes, not from framing delivered by the facilitator, and confirm that no bibliographic references, citation lists, or outside research are required to run the activity.
