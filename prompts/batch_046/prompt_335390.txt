Construct a realistic, one-paragraph institutional scenario — not a historical case like dueling or slavery — in which a stable, harmful professional norm collapses: specifically, a ritual in which junior staff must publicly disclaim individual credit for collective work in front of clients, with the explicit purpose of demonstrating loyalty. Engineer the case so that an honor-based mechanism succeeds in the manner Appiah describes for dueling: a reversal in who bears the burden of shame makes continuing the ritual a mark of low status rather than virtue, and the collapse is rapid and widespread. Simultaneously, block the interest-based explanation: economic incentives, career structures, and external regulations remain entirely unchanged. Block the argument-based explanation as well: the moral and conceptual objections to the ritual had been available and articulated for years with no effect. The purpose of the case is to isolate whether honor dynamics alone can drive normative collapse when both interests and arguments are causally inert. After the scenario, demonstrate for each mechanism why it succeeds or fails, identify the precise structural feature — the reversal of the shame-burden — that does the explanatory work, state what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and show how a defender of the rationalist position, in the spirit of Bernard Williams's skepticism that moral reasoning drives large-scale change, would attack the case and how the honor-theorist could reply without invoking hidden economic interests.
