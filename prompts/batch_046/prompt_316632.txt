I am preparing a discussion session for a course on technology and human survival, and I keep returning to Pixar's WALL-E (2008) because it depicts machines operating centuries after humanity has abandoned Earth. Rather than treating the film as a straightforward fable about robots replacing people, I want to read it through John von Neumann's framework for self-reproducing automata—the idea that true technological survival requires not just continued operation (what von Neumann associated with kinematic persistence) but a universal constructor capable of evolutionary adaptation and error-correction. In the film, WALL-E and his fellow compactors maintain Earth through repetitive routines, while the Axiom's autopilot AUTO executes directives with absolute fidelity. Yet WALL-E himself develops what look like evolutionary deviations: he collects and curates artifacts, learns social behavior from a recording of Hello, Dolly!, and eventually revises his mission priorities through his relationship with EVE. Meanwhile, AUTO refuses to deviate even when the directive is obsolete.

Read the film through this lens: does WALL-E's deviation represent a malfunction that undermines his function as an automaton, or does it constitute the very transition from mechanical persistence to a form of logical self-replication—transmitting culture rather than copying hardware? Which scenes—WALL-E's hoarding of human objects, his interaction with the plant, AUTO's refusal to return to Earth—best illuminate this distinction, and where does the film resist it, perhaps by sentimentalizing WALL-E's personality in ways that obscure whether he actually reproduces anything? I want a reading that treats both von Neumann's technical distinction and the film's narrative choices seriously, and that concludes with whether WALL-E ultimately suggests that technological survival without evolutionary adaptation is merely slow decay.
