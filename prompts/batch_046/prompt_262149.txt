Over the past year, as I have watched a close family member gradually lose the coherence of a single, continuous self, I have found myself returning with unusual urgency to Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue in which Aunt Hillary and the ant colony serve as mirror-images for one another. I want to close a seminar I am leading on distributed cognition with a poem—roughly thirty-five lines, in free verse but architecturally organized as a fugue—that does not merely describe the problem but enacts it. Use four voices entering in imitation: a solitary worker ant, the queen, an external observer, and the colony speaking in the first-person plural. The subject should state that no ant believes or intends anything, yet the whole moves with unmistakable purpose. The counter-subject should press the objection that without a unified sensory manifold or a single point of view there is nothing it is like to be the colony, hence no subject for the verb "to think." Build through successive entries and episodes until a final stretto in which the four voices overlap grammatically: the pronoun "I" should become ambiguous between ant, queen, colony, and perhaps even the observer. The form itself should carry the argument: just as a fugue has no melody that exists apart from the interrelation of its voices, so the poem should ask whether a mind can exist apart from the parts that compose it, without ever answering yes or no. Please do not resolve the tension in the closing lines; let the voices fade into one another, leaving the audience with the formal fact of their overlap rather than a philosophical verdict.
