Construct a thought experiment engineered to test whether Émilie du Châtelet's conception of bonheur necessarily includes embodied, causally efficacious labour, or whether it can be satisfied by an equivalent mental state achieved without physical or productive effort.

Design the case as a single paragraph set in a realistic institutional environment—such as a museum conservation studio or a clinical calibration laboratory—where one agent faces a choice between two protocols for completing a complex, meaningful task (for instance, reconstructing a damaged scientific instrument or refining a therapeutic measurement). In Protocol A, the agent performs sustained manual and intellectual work over several days: she handles materials, corrects errors through her own reasoning, and produces the final result through direct bodily action, experiencing fatigue but also progressive mastery and social recognition. In Protocol B, an automated system executes every physical manipulation and computation; the agent receives the identical cognitive content—she observes and comprehends each reasoning step, understands the result fully, and receives the same external praise—while her body remains entirely at rest, with no risk of injury, no fatigue, and no causal contribution to the outcome. Both protocols eliminate physical suffering, provide the same intellectual stimulation, and yield the same social esteem.

Then analyse the verdict each framework delivers. First, state what du Châtelet's philosophy implies: argue that she would deny Protocol B produces genuine bonheur, on the grounds that idleness and the separation of mental vigour from bodily engagement corrupt the soul and that robust happiness requires active, productive occupation rather than passive reception. Contrast this with a hedonist view, which should treat the two protocols as equivalent because subjective experience and absence of pain are identical, and with a purely intellectualist view, which should approve of Protocol B provided the mental content is preserved.

Next, isolate the precise structural feature that forces the divergence—whether it is the agent's causal efficacy, the effort-induced sense of mastery, or the avoidance of ennui through active engagement—and demonstrate what happens if that feature is removed: for example, if Protocol B requires the agent to make trivial physical adjustments, does the philosophical disagreement collapse, and why?

Finally, present the strongest objection available to a defender of automated well-being: that du Châtelet's insistence on embodied labour reflects the material and gendered conditions of her historical context rather than a universal metaphysical requirement. Explain how a proponent of her view could reply to this charge without relying on anachronistic assumptions or on biographical details that require specialised archival knowledge.
