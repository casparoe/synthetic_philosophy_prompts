I am convening a single 90-minute philosophy-of-mind seminar for about ten participants—graduate students and a few rehabilitation clinicians—next month. The topic has become personally urgent rather than merely historical: three months ago my sister regained partial sight after a corneal graft resolved long-standing congenital opacity, and watching her attempt to align what her hands knew with what her eyes now report has made Molyneux’s problem feel like a live puzzle rather than an antiquarian thought experiment.

Please design a structured in-room activity—not a lecture—that makes the group feel the specific tension in Molyneux’s question before anyone names the philosophical positions. The session should run in two rounds. Round one uses geometric solids (cube, sphere, pyramid); round two uses functional objects (a cup, a chair, a key) where identity turns on an affordance rather than on form. In each round, assign participants to three roles: “haptic reporters” who learn objects only by touch behind a screen; “visual reporters” who see only static images or silhouettes; and “cross-modal adjudicators” who must decide, under a brief time limit, whether the two reports refer to the same thing, without ever experiencing both modalities themselves. The rules should forbid shared verbal shortcuts—no “the round one,” no “the drink-container”—and should include a small, non-monetary stake (for example, the group accumulates points that determine which interpretation they must defend in the final ten minutes).

Provide a precise timeline, the materials needed (household or 3D-printed objects, image cards, a divider screen), and explicit rules that prevent participants from smuggling in linguistic correlation where sensory integration is absent. The point is that participants should discover for themselves whether matching requires a shared cross-modal concept or merely a causal correlation, and whether geometric identity feels different from functional identity.

Then provide a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the philosophical landscape: Locke’s empiricist expectation that associative experience must link the senses; Leibniz’s rationalist suggestion that a shared idea of space might bridge them; and contemporary sensorimotor or enactivist claims that vision is not static image-recognition but exploratory action. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong—likely, visual reporters will over-describe geometric properties while haptic reporters emphasize weight and temperature, and adjudicators will conflate correlation with identity. Note which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce—probably the lifelong absence of visual experience in congenital blindness versus a temporary blindfold here—and say exactly how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

Finally, please separate what is empirically settled about post-operative visual recovery (for instance, that color and motion recognition often precede stable form binding in sight-restoration cases) from what remains genuinely contested in philosophy of mind (for instance, whether cross-modal failure implies modality-specific concepts or merely unintegrated sensory streams). Do not present a contested interpretation—such as the claim that failure proves concepts are inherently embodied—as if it were the consensus. Flag every point where the activity necessarily simplifies clinical reality or philosophical debate, and avoid requesting any bibliography, reading list, or specific citations.
