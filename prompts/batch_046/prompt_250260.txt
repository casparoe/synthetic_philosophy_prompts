At a workshop on collective action, two colleagues—Sam and Riley—disagreed about whether second-order multipolar traps should be analyzed with the same framework as first-order races to the bottom. By second-order, they meant competitive dynamics that target the rules themselves rather than the regulated activity: for example, jurisdictions racing to weaken environmental or labor standards in order to attract capital, where the competitive pressure operates on the regulatory baseline rather than on production levels within a fixed baseline.

Sam argued that applying the "Moloch" framework to both levels dilutes its critical force. In a first-order trap—say, firms overproducing to capture market share—every actor prefers the cooperative outcome (lower production, stable prices) but defects because unilateral defection is individually dominant. The failure is genuinely one of coordination: shared goals exist, yet individual incentives prevent reaching them. In a second-order regulatory race, by contrast, some jurisdictions actively prefer weaker standards because they value the competitive advantage more than the protected good. This is not universal self-defeat despite shared aims; it is a conflict over what the collective outcome should be, masked by competitive dynamics. Calling it a multipolar trap wrongly assumes a shared preference for stricter rules, turning a political disagreement about values into a technical failure of coordination. Sam added that the stability conditions differ: in first-order cases, a binding cooperative agreement is stable once achieved (no secret defector benefits, given enforcement), whereas in second-order cases, actors can reject the agreement's legitimacy and work to exit or undermine it—not merely cheat within it.

Riley replied that this distinction collapses under inspection. Even in second-order cases, the competitive equilibrium is typically Pareto-inferior to a coordinated regime: the "winning" jurisdictions that attract capital often suffer degraded conditions they would have preferred to avoid, and the strategic preference for lower standards is instrumental, not fundamental. The structural logic is identical—individually rational moves aggregate into collectively worse outcomes—so excluding second-order dynamics obscures the feedback loop between them: eroded standards intensify first-order competition, and first-order pressures then drive further regulatory erosion. Riley also challenged Sam's stability criterion, noting that classic first-order traps like arms races or climate emissions also involve actors who reject cooperation on principled grounds and constantly seek to weaken agreements; imperfect enforcement and partial dissent do not dissolve the trap, they just make it harder to solve.

Sam conceded the feedback loop but insisted that the conceptual point stands. If every competitive dynamic with negative externalities counts as a multipolar trap, the concept loses its specific bite, which is meant to highlight situations where rational agents are collectively self-defeating despite sharing goals. Where goals are not shared—where some actors genuinely endorse the competitive game—diagnosing the situation as collective irrationality mischaracterizes it. The appropriate framework, Sam suggested, might be distributive conflict or hegemonic competition, not coordination failure.

Riley countered that Sam is demanding an unrealistic purity of shared preference. In practice, first-order traps never involve perfect unanimity either; they involve enough overlap in preferences to make cooperation desirable for a critical mass, combined with incentive structures that prevent it. Second-order regulatory races meet exactly this condition: a critical mass of jurisdictions often expresses preference for stronger global standards, yet competitive pressures drive them downward. The presence of holdouts who genuinely prefer the race does not change the structural diagnosis for the system as a whole.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the conceptual distinction between first-order and second-order dynamics, about whether shared goals are necessary for a multipolar trap, and about whether stability conditions genuinely differ. Then say who is more right overall. Finally, identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a meaningful intermediate category between pure coordination failure and pure value conflict, and what that intermediate category might imply for institutional design.
