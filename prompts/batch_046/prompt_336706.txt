I have just come out of a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether human extinction is bad in ways that go beyond the deaths it involves. We were both drawing on the idea, familiar from Samuel Scheffler’s work, that much of the value of present activities depends on the expectation that future generations will continue them—an ‘afterlife conjecture’, if you like—but we kept disagreeing about what exactly follows.

Here are my verdicts on six cases. First, if humanity dies out suddenly after every person has lived a full lifespan—no extra deaths—this still strikes me as seriously bad, and the badness is not reducible to lost pleasure. Second, if the final generation knows it is the final one, I think their present projects suffer a particular kind of hollowness: not mere sadness, but a pointlessness that would not be there if they expected successors. Third, if we are replaced by digital minds that preserve our literature and science but do not genuinely experience or care about them, the loss is partly mitigated but still real. Fourth, if we are replaced by intelligent beings who actively reject our values, the situation seems to me just as bad as total extinction. Fifth, extending every human life to five centuries, but with the same guarantee of ultimate extinction and no successors, does not seem to remove the problem: the extra time does not compensate for the dead end. Yet, sixth, if extinction were to come only after humanity had completed some genuinely universal and irreversible achievement—say, the resolution of all fundamental scientific questions—my intuition is that the extinction becomes far less bad, perhaps not bad at all.

My friend thinks I am importing incompatible commitments: that I want future generations for their own sake in some cases, for the sake of present meaning in others, and for value-continuity in still others. He also pressed me on a case I keep wavering over: suppose we learn that extinction is inevitable in ten thousand years, but that with enormous present sacrifice we could extend our survival to one million years. Is the extension worth it?

Please adjudicate: is there a coherent principle behind my six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which verdict motivates each clause, and apply it to the ten-thousand-year case. If not, say which verdict should be overturned and why. Please write your answer in American English.
