I am working through an argument about the democratic boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—that seems to prove democratic self-government is conceptually incoherent unless we accept a global, intergenerational demos. I want to diagnose where the reasoning slips, and I want to understand what I pay if I reject that particular step.

(1) The boundary problem asks for a principled criterion that determines membership in a democratic demos.

(2) Any adequate criterion must satisfy two conditions simultaneously: it must be normatively justified (respecting autonomy or affected interests) and institutionally feasible (capable of actual implementation with accountability mechanisms).

(3) Citizenship fails normative justification: it arbitrarily excludes long-term residents and non-citizen subjects who are bound by laws and affected by policies.

(4) The all-affected-interests principle corrects this, but it fails institutional feasibility: climate, fiscal, and infrastructural policies affect people across borders and across generations, so the principle requires including outsiders and the unborn in every national decision.

(5) The all-subjected principle—membership for everyone subject to the coercive authority of the decision—seems more feasible, but it applies to tourists, temporary visitors, and future generations who will be bound by laws enacted today.

(6) To preserve feasibility, one might refine subjection to "habitual residence combined with a durable stake in the collective future."

(7) But "durable stake" is either too vague to block arbitrary exclusion (a distant foreign investor holds a durable financial stake in local policy) or too precise to capture all genuinely affected parties (a child born in another country next year holds a survival stake in present carbon regulations).

(8) Therefore every precise criterion is either normatively arbitrary or institutionally unworkable, and every workable criterion is normatively arbitrary.

(9) If there is no non-arbitrary boundary, then either the demos is co-extensive with humanity across time, or the concept of democratic self-rule lacks determinacy.

(10) A global, timeless demos is institutionally impossible—there is no mechanism for reciprocal accountability across centuries and borders—so democratic self-rule is conceptually incoherent.

(11) Yet democratic self-rule is practiced and generally regarded as legitimate.

(12) Hence one of (1) through (11) must be false.

The argument feels suspicious because its conclusion is absurd. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep the dual-requirement framework in (2) and the denial of incoherence in (12). Please identify the flaw—whether it is an equivocation between "arbitrary" in the normative and institutional registers, a hidden quantifier shift between "every criterion fails some test" and "no criterion can satisfy both tests together," or a false dichotomy in (9)—and price the alternatives. If I reject (8), must I accept that democratic boundaries are conventionally justified rather than fully principled? If I reject (6), do I lose the resources to distinguish citizens from transient visitors? Please separate what is relatively settled in democratic theory (for instance, that the boundary problem has both normative and institutional dimensions, and that pure affectedness faces feasibility objections) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether representation can substitute for direct inclusion of affected outsiders, or whether subjection should take priority over affectedness), and do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
