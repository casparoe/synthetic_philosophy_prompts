Hey, I've been reading some philosophy since I retired from the lab, and I keep running into the same argumentative move that feels off, but I can't tell if it's got a real name or if I'm just being picky. Here's the pattern. Someone wants to defend a simple rule—say, "scientists should always share raw data" or "doctors should tell patients everything"—and instead of testing it in real messy conditions, they imagine a world where everyone is perfectly rational, nobody misinterperts information, and there's no time pressure. They ask "would a reasonable person agree to this rule there?" and since yes, they say it's justified. But then they apply that same rule back in the actual world, where people are tired, biased, and will definatly misuse preliminary results. The weird part is that the mess they stripped away—bias, rush, misinterpertation—is exactly why you'd want a simple heuristc in the first place. If everyone were already perfect, you wouldn't need the rule at all. Is there an established term for this bait-and-switch, where the ideal conditions used to justify a norm erase the very problem the norm was meant to solve? Or is it just a generic misuse of thought experiments? I don't want just a bare label; I want to know if philosophers treat this as a distinct failure mode in justifying rules indirectly rather than case by case, how it relates to idealized deliberation, and where the line is between legitimate simplification and this kind of self-undermining idealization.
