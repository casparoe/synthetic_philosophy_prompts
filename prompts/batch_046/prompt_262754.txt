Here are some rough notes for an essay im trying to write about Darwin and Turing and how both of them flip normal reasoning on its head. The basic idea is that Darwin shows nature can build a well-adapted eye without any understanding of optics, and Turing shows a machine can compute correct outputs without grasping the math behind them. Both are cases of competence without comprehension, but I want to push further and say they share a deeper pattern: they invert the usual explanatory order where comprehension comes first and competence follows. Instead, competence is generated by a process that is blind to the very reasons that justify the result.

I then want to connect this to something I call "teleonomic drift" — the way evolutionary and computational processes accumulate functional behaviors that have no representational content about why they work. In Darwin's case, natural selection doesn't represent the environment; in Turing's universal machine, the tape symbols don't represent the computation being performed. But here's the niche part: I think both cases also involve what I'd call an "epistemic asymmetry" where the observer can recover reasons post hoc (we understand optics after seeing the eye; we understand arithmetic after running the machine) but the generating process never had them. So comprehension is retrospective, not constitutive.

My worry is that I'm overreading Turing — he was mostly interested in formal computability, not in epistemology. And maybe Darwin's theory doesn't actually require that organisms lack comprehension, just that the evolutionary process lacks it. So I might be conflating the process-level blindness with the organism-level blindness.
