I've been working through the sociology-of-science literature on psychiatric looping effects — the feedback loop in which classifying people with a disorder changes how they experience and present themselves, which in turn forces the diagnostic criteria to shift, altering the very phenomenon being measured. The standard examples feel well-trodden, and I'm trying to push past them into a stranger variant: what if the loop isn't an unfortunate distortion but the constitutive mechanism? That is, perhaps "major depressive disorder" isn't a natural kind that gets corrupted by classification; perhaps it is the pattern that emerges from the loop itself, like the problem in Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project — there is no hidden pure essence behind every instantiation of an 'a', only a family resemblance held together by style and recognition.

Write a short story about a clinical researcher in a near-future institution where diagnostic manuals are updated in real time by an algorithm that ingests patient self-reports, clinician adjustments, and behavioral telemetry. The researcher, who specializes in affective taxonomy, realizes that her own revisions to a proposed subcategory are being anticipated and performed by patients before the data even reaches her dashboard. The story should turn on the analogy to letterform identity: just as asking what makes every 'a' an 'a' reveals that style and category are inseparable, asking what makes a given episode "depressive" reveals that symptom and diagnosis are inseparable. But don't let the protagonist settle for easy social-constructionism. Instead, stage a concrete argument — perhaps over a specific case file — between her and a patient who has deliberately altered his symptom profile to match her proposed revision, debating whether his change represents authentic suffering adapting to a better map, or the map manufacturing the territory. Keep the interaction grounded in the particularities of that file, not in abstract philosophy. Avoid citing papers, naming living theorists, or requesting bibliographies; the aim is to make the conceptual tension live in the narrative itself.
