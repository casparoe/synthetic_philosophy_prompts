I am a sixth-form student preparing an essay for my philosophy coursework, and I have constructed an argument about advance directives that seems to prove too much, yet I cannot locate the precise fault. The argument concerns whether a competent patient can issue a binding refusal of treatment that applies to their later incompetent self.

(1) If a competent person P at time t1 can issue an advance directive that binds P at a later time t2 when P is incompetent, then P at t1 possesses normative authority over P at t2.

(2) Normative authority over a being at a given time requires either that the authority-holder is identical to that being at that time, or that authority has been validly transferred.

(3) At t2, P lacks the psychological continuity and rational capacities that ground present autonomy; this is precisely the justification for treating incompetent patients differently.

(4) Therefore P at t1 is not identical to P at t2 in the normatively relevant sense, so for the directive to bind, authority must have been transferred.

(5) But authority can only be transferred by a being that currently possesses it; P at t2, being non-autonomous, possesses none to transfer, and P at t1 cannot unilaterally seize authority over a future being who will then be separate in the relevant respects.

(6) Hence, if advance directives are genuinely binding, P at t1 retains authority over P at t2 without any present consent from P at t2.

(7) If such temporal retention of authority without present consent is permitted, then it ought also to be permitted across persons: I could sign a 'prospective continuity agreement' claiming special relational authority over my severely disabled sibling's future medical decisions, even against their present objections, on the grounds that I understand their narrative continuity better than they do.

(8) Yet we clearly reject that one adult can contract away another's present autonomy through such an agreement.

(9) Therefore, the initial premise must be false: advance directives cannot be genuinely binding; they are at best advisory evidence of past preferences.

I should like to preserve (1) and (8) if possible, since they capture the intuition that prior autonomy matters and that cross-person contracts are illegitimate. Where exactly does the chain break? I suspect there is an equivocation between 'normative authority' as it functions in medical ethics and as it functions in contractual law, but I cannot isolate it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, please explain what the precedent-autonomy view, the present-interests view, and the narrative-continuity view each have to pay to reject that step, and whether my invented 'prospective continuity agreement' genuinely parallels the temporal case or relies on a hidden disanalogy.
