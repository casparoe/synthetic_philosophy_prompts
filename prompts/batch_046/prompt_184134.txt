Suppose Charles Darwin—retaining his actual philosophical commitments about morality, natural selection, and the social instincts of human beings—were confronted with a debate that emerged only after his death: evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, which hold that learning the evolutionary origins of moral beliefs undermines their justification.

Frame the challenge with the following precise but non-standard distinction. Divide moral cognition into two clusters: a "cooperative-tracking" cluster, comprising norms shaped by inter-group selection for altruistic coordination and yielding broadly consequentialist judgments; and a "boundary-marking" cluster, comprising norms shaped by in-group purity and contamination-avoidance mechanisms and yielding deontological constraints. The debunking claim is applied asymmetrically: cooperative-tracking beliefs are alleged to be merely instrumentally reliable for fitness rather than truth-tracking, whereas boundary-marking beliefs are alleged to derive from pathogen-avoidance mechanisms that carry no representational aim whatsoever.

Predict Darwin's response. Would he deny that evolutionary genealogy bears on normative authority? Would he concede that one cluster is more vulnerable to debunking than the other? Might he argue that natural selection can calibrate cognitive faculties toward true moral properties, and if so, for which cluster?

Please answer in exactly the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:
1. Reconstructed Darwinian framework (~180 words): Summarize his genuine commitments regarding the evolution of morality, the relation between social instincts and reason, and whether he treated moral faculties as capable of tracking objective truths—without attributing to him any anachronistic positions.
2. The asymmetrical debunking scenario (~150 words): Present the two-cluster model and the differential debunking claim clearly and precisely.
3. Predicted position (~300 words): State what Darwin would likely argue about the relevance of evolutionary origins to justification, about each cluster separately, and about whether selection can produce truth-tracking moral cognition.
4. Internal tension (~150 words): Identify one specific point at which his predicted view seems to conflict with a well-known element of his philosophy, and either resolve the conflict or explain why it remains.
5. Limits of the exercise (~100 words): Note one formal or conceptual feature of modern evolutionary debunking—such as a specific epistemological premise about defeat or reliability—that Darwin lacked the apparatus to address, and explain how this restricts the prediction.
