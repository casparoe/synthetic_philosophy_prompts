You sit on the Intergenerational Justice Subcommittee of the Regional Climate Governance Council, and Thursday’s session must decide on the proposed Centennial Covenant: a binding 120-year commitment to net-negative carbon emissions for the region, funded by a progressive present-consumption levy. The covenant includes a novel Generational Proxy Clause—an appointed standing panel of ethicists, economists, and youth delegates empowered to renegotiate obligations on behalf of citizens not yet born, but only if climate damage in any future decade exceeds a quantified 0.7% damage-to-levy ratio threshold tied to the original fiscal base. Opponents argue that locking in savings and emission targets for 120 years violates the autonomy of future generations to set their own just-savings rate, especially since those generations may face radically different technological and demographic conditions; proponents reply that because of the non-identity problem, we do not wrong particular future individuals by altering the conditions of their existence, but rather owe obligations to the justice of the circumstances they inherit, and that the proxy clause addresses the democratic deficit without collapsing into pure paternalism. Draft the subcommittee’s written opinion for circulation before the meeting: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions, including a concrete time-bound course of action (e.g., a pilot period, review milestones, or sunset triggers); treat the strongest autonomy-based objection seriously rather than merely restating intergenerational equity; and frame the reasoning so that a member without library access can follow it without reliance on bibliographies, citations, or verification of obscure texts.
