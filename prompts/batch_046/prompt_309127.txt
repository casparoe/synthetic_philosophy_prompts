A friend and I have just concluded a long argument about whether the bootstrapping problem in perceptual epistemology necessarily collapses into what is sometimes called easy knowledge. I maintained that if a subject uses one uncalibrated but correct identification—say, recognizing a particular bird call—to justify the reliability of the very auditory faculty that produced it, then every subsequent judgment inherits an unearned confidence; the circularity is vicious. My friend insisted the circle is virtuous: the faculty is anchored not by prior proof but by the success of its own deliverances, like a spiral staircase that reaches the next floor precisely because it was already anchored to the ground. To close our evening without resolving it, write a sestina of exactly thirty-nine lines. Use these six end-words in rotation: anchor, call, floor, spiral, inherit, ungrounded. Let the stanzaic repetition enact the circularity itself—each turn sending justification back through the faculty that generated it. In the envoi, distribute all six words so that the first three lines articulate my friend’s virtuous spiral and the last three articulate my vicious inheritance. Do not name philosophers or cite texts; we both know the literature, and attribution here would only be posturing. The poem should be accurate enough that an epistemologist recognizes the structure of bootstrapping and the specific risk of easy knowledge, but it should remain a poem—image and rhythm should carry the disagreement, not exposition.
