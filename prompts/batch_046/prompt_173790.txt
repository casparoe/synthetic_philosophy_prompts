so my mom got diagnosed with dementia about eight months ago and ive been her main caregiver since then, which is why i suddenly care way more about whether ethical obligations can actually be infinite. im pitching a small workshop grant for a three-month part-time project. the question: does levinas's claim that responsibility to the other is literally infinite survive when the other is not an abstract face but a specific, dependent person who needs constant physical care, and does the asymmetry of that responsibility collapse once the caregiver is also depleted? the existant literature mostly treats infinite ethics as either a metaphysical claim about subjectivity or a normative claim that youre never done with obligations, but neither side has seriously engaged with long-term caregiving as a pressure-test. my method would be to take three phenomenological descriptions of caregiving exhaustion and run them against the asymmetry thesis, asking whether infinite responsibility needs a second-order condition of possibility for the self's continued responsiveness, or whether it transforms into something like mutual moral bankruptcy rather than pure one-way obligation. deliverable: a 30-page working paper mapping where the model holds and where it fractures, plus a short note on what a modified asymmetry might look like for people actually deciding on respite policies. failure conditions: if the argument just collapses into standard care-ethics reciprocity, or if im basically describing burnout in fancy language with no conceptual gain. i know this sounds personal but i think the caregiving context injects something the abstract debates miss.
