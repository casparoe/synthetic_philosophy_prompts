I was talking with my study group about abortion ethics and someone walked me into an argument I cant find the exit to. They start with Thomson's violinist - you can unplug because you never consented to be life-support machinery, even if he dies. Then they introduce this idea they call the dependency gradient: moral obligations to sustain another scale with how physically integrated the support is, from internal gestation down to occasional feeding. So pregnancy is high-integration, infancy is medium, and occasional babysitting is low. The principle is that at any point on the gradient, you can withdraw if you revoke consent, because there's no implcit contract just from initiating the dependency.

Then they lay out these steps:

(1) If sustaining B requires using A's body at integration-level L, then A may withdraw that support at any time, provided A never signed an explicit contract to continue through the full dependency period.

(2) Pregnancy operates at the highest integration level; infancy operates at a lower but still substantial level.

(3) In neither case does the dependent party acquire a claim to continued support that outlasts A's present consent.

(4) So if abortion is permissible because A can revoke bodily consent, then withdrawing care from an infant is also permissible, since both fall on the same gradient and differ only in degree, not in kind.

(5) But letting an infant die by withdrawal is clearly wrong.

(6) Therefore, either the dependency-gradient principle is false, or pregnancy and infancy are not on the same gradient.

The problem is, if I reject (4) by saying infancy is different because it's external care rather than internal bodily use, then I'm just smuggling in the conclusion that pregnancy is special. But if I reject (1), I seem to lose Thomson's core intuition about the violinist. I feel like there's an equivocation hiding in "using A's body" - in pregnancy it's literal organ-sharing, but in infancy it's labor and resources - but I'm not sure if that's enough to break the chain. Which step should I actually reject, and what does that rejection cost? Can I keep the violinist intuition intact without accepting that parents can abandon newborns? Also, where does Marquis's future-like-ours argument fit in - does it change the cost of rejecting (1) or (3)?
