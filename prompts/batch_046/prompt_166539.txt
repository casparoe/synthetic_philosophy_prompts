In a seminar I am currently taking on the political thought of the late Roman Republic and its modern receptions, I have noticed a recurring argumentative maneuver whenever classmates or assigned texts draw structural analogies between the collapse of republican institutions and present-day democratic erosion. The pattern unfolds in two stages. First, a speaker anchors the comparison in a specific institutional parallel—perhaps the gradual normalization of extraordinary magistracies that circumvent ordinary legislative procedures—presenting this parallel as the decisive historical lesson. When an interlocutor challenges the analogy by noting a significant disanalogy, such as the presence of modern constitutional courts with oversight capacities that the Roman senate lacked, the speaker does not defend the original institutional parallel on its merits. Instead, the speaker shifts the analogy’s weight to an entirely different structural feature, such as the capture of public discourse by privately financed rhetorical campaigns, and derives the same normative conclusion about impending democratic decay. The shift is presented not as a change of subject but as a clarification of the original point, even though the two analogies rely on incompatible causal mechanisms. I have observed this across discussions of populist leadership, legislative paralysis, and even the spatial symbolism of deliberative forums.

The move is not mere equivocation, because the vocabulary of crisis and institutional decay remains consistent throughout; nor does it fit the standard description of motte-and-bailey reasoning, since neither analogical version functions as an obviously defensible core protected by an obviously indefensible periphery—both are equally contested, and either can serve temporarily as the foundation. Is there a recognized concept in rhetoric, philosophy of history, or comparative political theory that captures this specific kind of analogical drift, where a historical comparison relocates its structural basis in order to preserve a predetermined normative conclusion? If the literature does not contain a precise term, I would like a careful definition that distinguishes the phenomenon from weak analogy, strategic ambiguity, and the motte-and-bailey structure itself, together with an indication of where its conceptual boundaries lie.
