Alright, here's a position I'm trying to defend in a seminar paper and I want to stress-test it properly. I think Strawson's reactive-attitudes framework gets misread when people apply it to institutional blame — like holding a corporation or a government minister responsible. My claim is that reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, etc.) only do genuine moral work when they're anchored in what I'd call 'direct participatory recognition' — basically, you have to be able to recognise the other as a specific agent in a shared normative space, not just a role-filler. So in big bureaucracies, what we call 'blame' is actually just a kind of vicarious reactive displacement: we're projecting attitudes onto instiutional nodes, but there's no real reactive attitude happening because the recognition condition fails. Critics usually say this is too restrictive and that we clearly blame politicians or CEOs all the time, but I think defenders of Strawson can insulate the view by saying those are just 'objectifying attitudes' misclassifed as reactive ones. My worry is that this insulation makes the position unfalsifiable — any counter-example gets reclassified away. So: what would actually count against my position? Not just intuitive counter-examples, but arguments or distinctions that would genuinely damage the claim that direct participatory recognition is necessary. Would it be enough to show that reactive attitudes scale without losing their moral force? Or that the objectifying/reactive distinction collapses under pressure? Also, am I right that this risks being unfalsifiable, or is there a real conceptual prediction I'm making that could turn out wrong? I'd like an honest map of where this is exposed, not just a list of objections.
