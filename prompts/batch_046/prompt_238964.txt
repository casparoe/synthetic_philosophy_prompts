I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind and have found myself persuaded that the conceivability of philosophical zombies—creatures physically and behaviourally identical to conscious beings yet lacking any phenomenal experience—establishes a genuine modal gap, and therefore that physicalism is false. Before I present this to my students, I want a rigorous assessment of what could genuinely defeat the position, not merely the standard repertoire of objections. Specifically, I am interested in whether formal results in modal epistemology undermine the inference from conceivability to possibility; in whether the analogy between the water/H₂O case and the consciousness/brain-state case breaks down at precisely the point where phenomenal concepts are involved; and in what empirical discoveries about the neural correlates of consciousness, if any, would render the zombie scenario not just implausible but metaphysically impossible.

Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed advocate of the conceivability argument would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as missing the point, and address whether retreating to a purely epistemic gap claim—where zombies remain conceivable but not metaphysically possible—insulates the view into unfalsifiability, and whether such insulation is a theoretical vice or virtue for an argument of this kind. I would particularly value an honest judgement about which of these challenges have arguably already arisen in the literature and which remain speculative, without requiring any bibliography or citation of specific authors.
