Several jurisdictions have recently expanded animal-welfare statutes to cover decapod crustaceans and cephalopods—requiring humane-slaughter protocols—while leaving insects, despite outnumbering them by orders of magnitude in biomass and individual count, entirely outside protective frameworks. The legislative reasoning typically appeals to an epistemic confidence threshold: protection is warranted only when sentience is judged "likely" or "proven," and insects fall below that bar. But this creates an asymmetry: if the moral case for protection turns on the risk of suffering multiplied by the number of affected beings, then excluding an enormous population because its sentience is merely uncertain—rather than near-certain—seems to invert ordinary precautionary logic. What philosophical commitments about moral risk, epistemic thresholds, and the aggregation of welfare does this regulatory practice presuppose? Does it implicitly treat sentience as an all-or-nothing property rather than graded, require near-certainty instead of weighing expected value, or allow taxonomic proximity to do hidden normative work? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, assess whether its parts are consistent, and say what would have to change in welfare policy if the confidence-threshold view were replaced by a calculus that weights probability of sentience directly against population scale.
