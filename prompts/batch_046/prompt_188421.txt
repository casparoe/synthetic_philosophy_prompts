I am serving as an external examiner for a graduate seminar in early modern intellectual history, and I need to calibrate my grading of one final essay before the marks are finalized. The assignment asked students to analyze Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz’s defense of women’s study in her Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, with particular attention to two elements: first, her deployment of biblical exempla—specifically Deborah, Esther, and the Queen of Sheba—to establish precedent for female intellectual authority; and second, her use of the Salamanca scholastic distinction between innate knowledge (infused by God) and acquired knowledge (gained through disciplined study). Students were also expected to discuss how her argument interacts with the institutional reality of the convent, and whether she ultimately challenges or reinforces the gendered boundary between speculative knowledge and practical, domestic knowledge.

Below is the submission from Student A. I am deliberately not signaling my own assessment; I want you to evaluate the answer as if you were a second reader. Please assign a letter grade (A through F) and then provide a point-by-point justification: identify what the student gets right about the biblical examples and the scholastic framework, flag the specific places where the reasoning slips—especially any misattribution of Sor Juana’s position, any anachronistic conflation with modern frameworks such as epistemic justice, and any mischaracterization of how she treats the speculative versus practical division. Note also whether the student correctly grasps the institutional limits of her defense. Do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need only the evaluative judgment with explicit reference to the text of the answer above. 

Student A writes: Sor Juana’s Respuesta advances a universal claim—because God distributes reason equally, every woman regardless of social rank possesses a natural right to study theology, law, and natural philosophy. She supports this with biblical exempla, citing Deborah, Esther, and the Queen of Sheba to show that women have historically exercised public intellectual and political leadership. Drawing on Salamanca scholasticism, she distinguishes innate knowledge, which is divinely given, from acquired knowledge, which must be cultivated through study; she concludes that denying women education violates natural law by blocking the acquisition of what reason makes possible. Student A then observes that Sor Juana deliberately restricts her defense to the convent: she does not demand admission to universities or public office, but instead presents study as a devotional aid for religious women. From this, the student infers that she preserves the gendered split between speculative knowledge, permitted in private, and practical knowledge, reserved for men in civic life. Consequently, although Sor Juana anticipates modern arguments for epistemic justice, her position ultimately reinforces the separation between women’s private intellect and men’s public authority.
