I am a retired physicist who spent three decades studying non-equilibrium thermodynamics, and I have recently turned to philosophy of economics and ethics with the enthusiasm of a latecomer. I am drafting a short essay on pure time preference—the view that goods in the future should be valued less than present goods solely because of their temporal location, not because of risk, uncertainty, or economic growth. The literature I have encountered treats this either as a psychological quirk or as an axiomatic feature of rational choice. I want to argue that pure time preference is conceptually unstable once we examine what physical time asymmetry actually implies.

My central claim is that treating temporal position as an intrinsic value-diminishing property has no analog in physics. The second law describes a statistical gradient in macroscopic systems, not a normative or causal weight that makes the present “better” than the future. To make this concrete, I propose an analogy to branching radioactive decay networks. In such networks, the present abundance of a parent isotope is not “preferred” over future daughter products; the proportions are governed by rate constants and branching ratios. If intergenerational social welfare is modeled as a decay chain—where institutions have heterogeneous “half-lives” and capital transforms across generations with different rate laws—then imposing a uniform exponential discount factor looks less like rational prudence and more like forcing a single decay constant onto a multi-phase system. I further suggest that evolutionary biology offers no support for a normative present-bias: natural selection filters on present reproductive success, but this is a causal mechanism without representational content, not a temporal preference that rational agents ought to emulate. Conflating the two seems to be a category error.

I would welcome focused comments on three points. First, have I misrepresented the standard philosophical objection—often associated with Frank Ramsey—that discounting distant futures is ethically indefensible—by framing it as a problem of conceptual coherence with physical asymmetry rather than as a direct ethical intuition? Second, does the decay-chain analogy genuinely illuminate intergenerational obligations, or does it obscure the distinction between describing a physical process and prescribing a normative distribution? Third, is there a defensible formulation of pure time preference that survives the separation between evolutionary present-focus and rational choice?

Please do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am working without library access and wish to test whether the argument holds on conceptual grounds alone. I am particularly interested in whether injecting multi-phase decay kinetics into the discounting debate produces a structurally new objection, or whether it merely repackages familiar worries about exponential discounting in scientific language. I also ask that you flag any place where my use of thermodynamic or evolutionary concepts seems to overreach into normative territory without adequate justification.
