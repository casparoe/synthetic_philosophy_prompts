After a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether Hofstadter's "Person Paper" actually dismantles sexist language or accidentally reinforces the very default assumptions it parodies, I need something concrete. I work as a technical editor at a university press, and I am trying to build a reusable fifteen-minute editorial procedure—something I can apply to manuscripts in philosophy, sociology, and cultural studies—derived from Hofstadter's core analysis of "default assumptions" and "unmarked categories." The goal is not to police language but to flag passages where ostensibly neutral terminology still encodes gendered defaults, and to do so without requiring linguistic expertise beyond what a generalist editor can apply.

Please design the procedure as an ordered sequence of textual tests: specific questions I ask of a passage, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a clear "no further action needed," the point at which it stops with a flag for revision, and the explicit points where the decision must be handed back to the author or to me as a values judgment rather than a factual determination. The procedure needs to survive three hard cases: a passage that uses generic "man" or "he" while explicitly arguing for gender equality; a passage that eliminates gendered pronouns entirely yet assigns active agency to male subjects and passive reception to female ones; and a deliberately ironic or satirical passage in the style of Hofstadter itself, which risks being misread literally by reviewers lacking the contextual frame. It must also be honest about its misfires—especially where authorial intent is inaccessible to textual analysis alone.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical excerpts: one drawn from a 1970s analytic philosophy paper on epistemology; one from a contemporary sociology methods section describing research participants; and one from a modern satirical essay written in direct imitation of Hofstadter's rhetorical strategy. For each, show what the procedure yields and whether that result should be trusted.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which biases lie outside its scope, which contextual judgments it deliberately refuses to make, and under what conditions an editor should override its output despite its recommendation. I need this to be something I can actually run repeatedly, sign off on, and defend to authors—so mark every step that is genuinely mechanical, and mark every step that is only a judgment call dressed in procedural clothing.
