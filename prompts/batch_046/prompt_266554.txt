I'm working through some intuitions about inductive projection with a friend in a philosophy of science reading group, and we're trying to see whether there's a principled boundary between legitimate generalization and ad-hoc curve-fitting rather than just a vague feeling of "naturalness." Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them, what it predicts about a scenario I haven't mentioned, and which of my judgments I'd have to abandon if I took that principle seriously.

Predicting that the next swan is white because all observed ones were, and that the next emerald is green, both feel fully justified. But predicting that the next emerald is both green and weighs under fifty grams feels noticeably weaker—weight seems like an accidental accompaniment rather than part of the kind. If I invent a predicate "vird"—green for objects observed before 2030 and violet afterward—and predict that a 2029 emerald is vird, that feels like cheating, not real induction, even though it fits every past observation. Meanwhile, observing that all ravens are black and that every non-black thing I've seen is a non-raven feels like it genuinely supports "all ravens are black," not like circular reasoning. A drug that cured all one hundred patients with a specific genetic marker, but was never tested on anyone without it, justifies predicting success for the next marked patient, yet I wouldn't extend the prediction to unmarked ones. By contrast, my roommate's observation that she received good news on every Tuesday she wore a blue shirt feels like pure superstition, not induction at all. There's also a synthetic polymer case: every sample tested was flexible at room temperature, but the synthesis protocol was accidentally varied in unrecorded ways each time; I feel somewhat justified predicting flexibility for the next batch made by the exact standard protocol, but not for one made with an undocumented variation. The case I keep returning to involves a historian who notices that every democracy adopting a particular constitutional amendment within its first fifty years survived past two centuries—only five instances, all survivors. Part of me treats this like the drug case, because there might be a structural link between the amendment and institutional resilience; part of me treats it like the Tuesday-blue case, because the pattern could be pure post-hoc selection.

What coherent principle distinguishes the legitimate projections from the spurious ones? Please state it precisely, indicate which clause handles each case above, say what it predicts for an unlisted scenario—specifically, whether a physicist should project that "all particles of this novel type decay within ten seconds" after observing fifty such decays in one laboratory but zero observations in a second laboratory using a different detector design—and be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and must therefore overturn. Also, if the principle relies on a distinction between "projectible" and "non-projectible" properties, explain how that distinction is drawn without circularly appealing to the very inductive practices it is meant to ground, and address whether my ambivalence about the historian's case reveals an instability in my own commitments or in the principle itself.
