I was recently appointed to an interdisciplinary oversight committee charged with specifying the normative reasoning framework for a deployed autonomous resource-allocation system. The committee is divided between two camps. One camp maintains that the system ought to reason as a utilitarian: it should maximize expected aggregate welfare across affected agents via a cardinal utility representation updated by predictive models of outcomes. The other camp maintains that it ought to reason as a deontologist: it should apply lexical side-constraints—treating certain allocations as impermissible regardless of aggregate welfare—with those constraints encoded as non-negotiable protocol rules rather than weighted penalties. Both camps accept that the system lacks phenomenal consciousness, operates under severe epistemic limitations regarding others' subjective states, and must maintain transparency and stability under perturbation.

Please draft the joint statement that these two camps could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration. Structure the document as follows: first, a numbered section listing the claims both sides endorse, including about the system's epistemic limits, the formal architecture of each proposed framework, and shared desiderata such as resistance to reward hacking and auditability; second, a disagreements table containing the precise formulations of their residual differences, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept—specifically addressing whether deontic constraints can be represented within a utility calculus without collapsing into consequentialism, whether intersubjective cardinal comparison is computationally and epistemically feasible for this system, and whether there exists a harm-threshold below which lexical constraints may be suspended; third, the identified cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would cause at least one side to revise its position; and fourth, for each crux, a concrete formal argument or test scenario that both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit prediction of what that case or argument demonstrates. Keep the tone constructive, avoid attributing straw-man positions, and do not include bibliographic references or citation requests.
