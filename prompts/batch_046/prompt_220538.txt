The Swiss militia system embeds lethal capacity in domestic space by requiring citizens to maintain service rifles at home between training periods; Sweden, after suspending conscription in 2010, reactivated and expanded a gender-neutral selective draft in 2018 that applies only to a lottery-filtered subset of each cohort; Finland, meanwhile, operates under a total-defense doctrine that legally binds civilian infrastructure sectors—transport, communications, energy—to maintain parallel wartime readiness protocols, dissolving the institutional boundary between soldier and citizen altogether. All three reject large standing professional armies and claim armed neutrality, yet they distribute the burden and ontology of violence differently: as a universal domestic possession, as a meritocratic civic filter, and as a distributed systemic property of the social order itself. What conception of citizenship, sovereignty, and the legitimate locus of violence does each architecture strictly presuppose? Do these three models rest on compatible philosophical anthropologies, or do they quietly presuppose rival and perhaps inconsistent views—republican, liberal, or communitarian—about whether readiness is an individual virtue, a selective duty, or a collective emergent feature? Identify which commitments are strictly necessary to license each practice and which are merely contingent fits, then say what institutional transformations would be required if we replaced all three with a purely professional, volunteer-based defense model: which implicit theories of the citizen-state relation would be abandoned, and which rival conceptions of political membership would such a replacement implicitly vindicate?
