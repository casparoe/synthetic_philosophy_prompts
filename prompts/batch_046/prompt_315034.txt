In our philosophy of punishment seminar we have spent several weeks on the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso regarding just deserts and moral responsibility, and the discussion has reached what feels like a stable impasse. Both reject libertarian free will and accept that human behavior is determined; both agree that suffering is bad and that social institutions need some mechanism for responding to harmful conduct. Dennett nevertheless defends a compatibilist account of desert: he holds that an agent can deserve blame or punishment in virtue of having the relevant rational capacities and being subject to appropriate social practices, even if those capacities are themselves determined. Caruso, arguing from hard incompatibilism, denies that determinism permits any form of retributive desert; he proposes replacing punishment with a purely forward-looking, public-health-style system of quarantine and rehabilitation in which outcomes—deterrence, protection, treatment—do all the normative work. What strikes me is that neither side seems to dispute any empirical claim about neuroscience or psychology, and neither offers a new argument for determinism, yet the disagreement persists despite agreement on the facts. I wonder whether the stall is not about free will at all, but about two different questions being asked under the name "justification of punishment": Dennett asks whether our existing practices of holding people accountable can be rationally reconstructed so that they remain legitimate, while Caruso asks whether any suffering inflicted on an offender is metaphysically fair given that she did not ultimately create herself. If that is right, then one side offers a process-based justification—grounded in the agent's capacities and the fairness of institutional procedure—while the other demands an outcome-based justification that makes no reference to desert. Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement verbal—turning on different meanings of "desert"—or substantive, resting on bedrock intuitions about whether institutional fairness can ever license suffering? Or is there a hidden formal question, perhaps about whether a justification must cite features internal to the agent rather than external social goals? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a sharper distinction between the allocative and expressive functions of punishment, an empirical result about how desert-talk affects recidivism, or a demonstration that one of the two questions is ill-posed? I am not asking you to adjudicate who is right, but to map the structure of the disagreement and say what kind of progress, if any, should be expected.
