You advise a national health-technology assessment board that allocates a scarce, life-extending therapy. For each candidate, the board must produce a baseline prognosis drawn from epidemiological reference classes, but staff have no systematic way to choose among the infinite classes to which any patient belongs—age cohort, disease subtype, genetic profile, geographic cluster, socioeconomic stratum—each yielding different survival estimates. The board needs a reusable decision procedure that translates the logical structure of the reference class problem into an operational protocol that non-philosophers can run in under thirty minutes per case, with clear sign-off points and explicit separation of statistical questions from value judgments.

Design the procedure as an ordered sequence of checks presented in plain language. For each step specify: (a) the exact question the staff member asks; (b) what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed; (c) whether the step produces a hard stop that returns “defer to clinical judgment,” “proceed with statistical forecast,” or “return for mechanistic justification”; and (d) an explicit flag marking where a value judgment, rather than a statistical fact, has entered. The procedure must embed a synthetic arbitration rule—predictive non-dominance binding: when two causally relevant reference classes yield conflicting forecasts, the narrower class may be adopted only if its predictions are not strictly dominated by the broader class across all clinically relevant outcomes; if dominance holds, the board binds to the broader class unless a documented mechanistic justification breaks the tie. Apply this rule inside the flow, do not treat it as an external citation.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a 58-year-old patient carrying a rare genetic variant of a common cardiac condition, where the genetic-subclass reference class and the general-condition reference class diverge sharply in predicted five-year survival. Second, a population screening proposal that requires forecasting outcomes for each screened individual treated simultaneously as a member of the general-risk pool and as part of a self-selected high-risk subgroup. Third, a case in which the procedure recommends denial of therapy based on a broad-class statistical forecast that conflicts sharply with the treating team’s clinical intuition—present the unwelcome recommendation and explain exactly what the procedure can and cannot say about that conflict.

Conclude by stating the precise limits of the procedure: which form of reference-class arbitrariness it eliminates, which form remains irreducible without importing normative commitments, and the practical conditions under which the procedure is likely to misfire. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable must be fully usable by staff without access to external literature or named theoretical texts.
