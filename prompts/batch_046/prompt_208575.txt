I've been trying to nail down my view on psychiatric classification and Hacking's looping stuff for a seminar next week, and I want to check if I'm being consistent or just helping myself to whatever sounds good. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Psychiatric kinds are interactive in Hacking's sense — getting labeled bipolar, autistic, etc. loops back and actually changes how you experience and present yourself, so the category partly makes the people in it.

(2) Because of that looping, I don't think these categories carve nature at the joints the way 'gold' or 'tiger' do. They're closer to social roles like 'genius' — partly constructed by the classification itself.

(3) Still, I think DSM-5 clusters track real, cross-culturally stable patterns of suffering and dysfunction. The looping complicates our access but doesn't erase the underlying reality.

(4) That's why I'm skeptical of diagnositc inflation — adding things like prolonged grief disorder or broadening autism risks manufacturing the very distress it describes, especially in vulnerable people.

(5) But I also think labeling is often genuinely liberating. For a lot of folks, an autism or ADHD diagnosis explains their experience, builds community, and gets them care. So looping isn't just distortion; it can be therapeutic.

(6) I think psychiatric science should still aim for the same third-person objectivity we want in cardiology. The social construction part is real, but good science should minimize it and focus on biomarkers and functional outcomes.

(7) And here's a weirder thing: I think some conditions show reflexive stabalization over time. Shared narratives and treatment protocols actually make the phenotype more uniform across generations, not less. So looping doesn't always mean chaos — sometimes it locks the pattern in.
