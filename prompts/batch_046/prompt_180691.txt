I am writing an extended presentation for my advanced high-school biology-philosophy course, and my teacher has shared her consistent judgments on several borderline cases of life. She classifies bacteria and synthetic minimal cells as genuinely alive; she judges viruses and prions not to be alive, despite their replication; she treats mitochondria as alive only derivatively, as parts of a cell rather than as individual organisms; she denies life-status to self-replicating computer programs and to sustained chemical oscillation patterns, even though both reproduce structures or maintain dynamic order; and she regards the recently developed cell-cluster “xenobots”—frog heart and skin cells arranged to move and gather loose cells into copies—as alive only in the same derivative sense as a tissue culture, not as autonomous organisms.

The new case I must analyze is a laboratory-constructed “autopoietic vesicle” built entirely from synthetic lipids and non-biological catalysts. It maintains internal chemical homeostasis, imports precursor molecules from its environment, divides upon reaching a size threshold, and produces daughter vesicles whose catalyst compositions vary slightly and compete for available resources—so there is selection and inheritance of compositional traits, yet there is no DNA, no RNA, no carbon-based metabolic pathway borrowed from biology, and no evolutionary history prior to its synthesis.

Predict my teacher’s verdict: would she count this vesicle as alive? Then identify at least two distinct theoretical principles—such as one centered on autonomous metabolic organizational closure and another centered on integrated hereditary evolutionary capacity—that could each consistently explain all of her earlier rulings. For each principle, state what it predicts about the vesicle, explain which one you think she is actually running and why, and propose one further hypothetical case that would clearly separate the two principles. Please distinguish what is broadly settled in biology from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of biology, and do not present a disputed theoretical position as if it were the established consensus.
