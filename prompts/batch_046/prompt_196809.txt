Assume David Hume has been fully briefed on the economic landscape of the early twenty-first century, with particular attention to two developments: the sustained negative nominal interest rates on Japanese and European sovereign bonds since the 2010s, and the complete absence of metallic or commodity backing for all major currencies. Drawing exclusively on the conceptual framework of his essays on money, interest, and trade—specifically his claim that the rate of interest is determined by the real supply of capital and the demand for borrowing rather than by the quantity of money, and his account of how trade imbalances self-correct through specie flows—reconstruct his likely assessment. Would he regard persistently negative rates as a natural market-clearing outcome or as an artificial suppression comparable to the mercantilist interventions he criticized? Next, propose how he might adapt his specie-flow reasoning to explain international capital movements and reserve accumulation in a pure fiat regime, and identify the precise point where that adaptation fails. Restrict your analysis to premises Hume actually held; do not invoke post-1800 macroeconomic theories or request external sources.
