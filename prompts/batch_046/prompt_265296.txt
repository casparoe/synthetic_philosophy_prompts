question: Hume argues beauty is in the mind, not the object, yet he proposes a standard of taste anchored in the verdict of refined critics. Is he secretly smuggling objectivism back in? And how should his view deal with taste drift—when a culture's aesthetic baseline shifts so gradually that even expert judges are calibrated to different norms without noticing?

student answer (Alex):

Hume's position is consistent because the standard of taste is intersubjective, not objective. The true judges arent averaging opinions like a poll; they're individuals who have developed delicacy of taste through practice and comparison, so their sentiments are more reliable. This means beauty is still a feeling, but we can rank feelings by the quality of the observer. As for taste drift, Hume's framework actually blocks it: since delicacy is a biological endowment rather than a learned skill, true judges across cultures share the same baseline human nature, which keeps the standard universal. So Hume allows minor disagreements about particular works—like fashion or local customs—but not deep cross-cultural splits over what is genuinely beautiful. He's not an objectivist; he's just saying that subjective responses converge when the subjects are properly calibrated.
