Here is a pattern I have noticed repeatedly, but I do not know whether it has an established name. In discussions of mathematical constructivism, an author will often establish a result within a very strict framework — for example, showing that you cannot always locate a root of a continuous function unless you already possess an explicit rate-of-change bound, or demonstrating that within a particular logical system certain statements are not provably true or false. Having secured that rigorous result, the same author (or their readers) will then invoke it in practical or philosophical contexts that violate the very conditions that made the proof work: citing the root-location result to justify standard numerical algorithms that never supply the required rate-of-change data, or appealing to logical undecidability to argue that scientific questions lack definite answers while elsewhere assuming those same questions are definitely settled one way or the other. When challenged on the purity of the framework, they retreat to the formal result and insist it carries no commitment to the looser practices; when challenged on the practical relevance, they point to the formal result as proof that the looser practices are "essentially correct" or "well-founded." The two contexts — the strict proof and the loose application — are treated as interchangeable, even though the conditions that legitimize the first are precisely what the second abandons.

I have observed this in debates over constructive analysis and in philosophy-of-science arguments about empirical decidability. It is not simple equivocation, because the two uses are genuinely related; nor is it exactly motte-and-bailey, since there is no single defensible core paired with an indefensible extension — rather, both the strict and the loose versions are presented as legitimate, and the slide happens through treating them as the same thing in different registers.
