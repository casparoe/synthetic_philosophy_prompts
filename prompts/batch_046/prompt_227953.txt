The audience is likely to arrive with two default assumptions: that the self must be either a Cartesian thinking substance or a purely fictional construct like Sherlock Holmes, and that any denial of the Cartesian ego amounts to eliminativism about personal identity. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture script itself—specifying the dependency structure of the explanation: which preliminary idea, such as the distinction between a physical center of gravity and a theoretical center of narrative gravity, must be secured before the main claim can be introduced without collapsing into metaphor; which single carrying example should do the explanatory work, with the constraint that it must illustrate distributed, coordinated behavior without a central controller while still preserving a sense of first-person perspective rather than reducing the self to an external observer's fiction; the predictable misconceptions that will arise in sequence—likely beginning with the claim that Dennett reduces the self to a mere story, followed by the inference that this makes the self an illusion with no causal power, and then the concern that moral responsibility evaporates—and the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved; a brief check-for-understanding that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the theoretical-construct view from mere discomfort with the absence of a Cartesian ego; and which elements of Dennett's broader framework, such as the intentional stance or heterophenomenology, should be mentioned only in passing or left out entirely so as not to overload first-year listeners. Please write the response in American English.
