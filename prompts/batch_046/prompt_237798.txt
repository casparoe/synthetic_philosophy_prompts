I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on population ethics, and last week we examined the procreation asymmetry—the view that there is a weighty moral reason not to bring into existence a person whose life would contain serious suffering, paired with the absence of any correspondingly weighty reason to bring into existence a person whose life would be genuinely happy. The discussion became unexpectedly personal over dinner with my partner, who is pregnant with our first child. She observed that if the asymmetry is correct, then our decision to conceive was at best morally permissible rather than positively good, which seems to conflict with the celebratory language we instinctively apply to planned parenthood. I suggested in reply that the asymmetry might still accommodate a weaker, non-obligatory reason in favor of creating happy lives without collapsing into full symmetry, but our exchange left me unsettled about how deeply this logical structure should reshape practical reproductive ethics.

More recently, I have been considering a less-discussed extension of the asymmetry into what might be called modal risk framing: rather than evaluating reproductive choices with reference to specific, identifiable future individuals, we might treat them as selections among possible worlds in which either suffering, happiness, or non-existence obtains. On this framing, the baseline against which we measure harm is literal non-being, which carries no interests or deprivations, whereas the baseline for measuring benefit remains equally empty—there is no corresponding deprivation of happiness for those who never exist. This generates an asymmetrical risk profile in which the potential error of creating suffering registers against a null moral baseline, while the potential success of creating happiness registers against that same null baseline, producing no counterbalancing moral gain. I worry that if this modal extension is taken seriously, reproductive decision-making could be pushed toward an extreme defensive conservatism, treating every potential life as primarily a risk of harm rather than an opportunity for value.

Please describe the most important implications of the procreation asymmetry, particularly when interpreted through this modal-risk lens, for how individuals ought to understand reproductive autonomy and the rhetoric of giving life. I am especially interested in whether this framing threatens to reduce all ethical evaluation of procreation to a calculus of harm avoidance, and what consequences that might carry for societal attitudes toward voluntary childlessness, genetic screening, and assisted fertility technologies. If the asymmetry indeed implies that there are no positive duties to reproduce, does it also undermine the idea that societies have any legitimate interest in encouraging births, or does it leave room for a distinct, non-moral category of social value?
