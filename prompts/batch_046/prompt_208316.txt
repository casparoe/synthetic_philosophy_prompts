As a novelist who has spent too much time with the archives of early American self-fashioning, I keep returning to Benjamin Franklin’s documented attempt to engineer his own moral perfection—the practice, described in his Autobiography and private papers, of tracking thirteen named virtues in a pocket ledger, marking failures with black dots, and cycling through them in weekly rotations. I want you to treat this not as quaint biography but as a live philosophical experiment testing Franklin’s own theory: that moral perfection is an asymptotic duty rather than an achievable state; that virtue can be decomposed into discrete, trainable habits; and that rational self-surveillance, not grace or social formation, is the primary motor of ethical improvement. Analyze the episode through these lenses. How do his recorded failures—particularly the persistent inability to master Order and the ironic difficulty with Humility—function within the framework? Do they confirm his claim that perfection remains necessarily just beyond reach, thereby protecting the agent from despair, or do they expose a structural flaw in treating virtue as a quantified, cyclical technology rather than as an integrated state of character?

Consider further what Franklin’s gradual abandonment of the formal ledger system, even as he insisted that the pursuit of perfection remained obligatory, reveals about the sustainability of his model. Does the episode ultimately vindicate his view that moral progress is incremental and self-directed, or does it suggest that a framework built on discrete habit-formation cannot accommodate the kind of unified moral transformation he professes to value? In your analysis, be disciplined about the mapping: identify precisely which feature of the ledger experiment corresponds to each element of his theory, say where the theory is silent, and address whether the case serves as confirmation, anomaly, or something the theory never anticipated. Do not treat the episode as mere illustration; let it test the theory in both directions.
