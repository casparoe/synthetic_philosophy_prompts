In comparing Niall Ferguson's institutionalist defense of the British Raj—emphasizing railway networks, legal unification, and administrative frameworks that persisted after 1947—with Shashi Tharoor's extractive critique—interpreting these same artifacts as mechanisms of capital extraction, deindustrialization, and political subjugation—I observe a structural stall despite shared empirical references. Both sides invoke famine mortality curves, textile export declines, irrigation acreage, and post-colonial institutional continuity, yet neither advances. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about the historical record but about what I will call the counterfactual ownership problem: whether an institution built under coercion can be credited to the coercive regime if its persistence after independence depends on the very extraction that funded its creation, and whether the proper evaluative baseline is an autonomous Indian development trajectory or the actual pre-colonial economic path. Given this framing, is the stall best diagnosed as a hidden empirical disagreement about which counterfactual is historically admissible, a normative disagreement about whether institutional inheritance can ever offset sovereignty loss, or a methodological divergence in weighing diffuse long-term capabilities against concentrated immediate harms? More precisely, does the debate split along a distinction between legacy accounting—treating post-1947 structures as inherited assets—and dependency accounting—treating them as path-dependent artifacts of extraction—such that the two sides answer different questions about empire under a shared vocabulary? What conceptual clarification, formal distinction, or change of framing could move the argument forward, and is there reason to conclude that no progress is possible because the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock moral intuitions about domination and development?
