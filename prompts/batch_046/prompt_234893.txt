As a novelist fluent in the vocabulary of narrative craft — plot architecture, character agency, dramatic irony, catharsis, narrative economy, and the tension between authorial design and the apparent autonomy of figures within a story — I ask you to restate Hegel's mature philosophical account of war in these terms alone. Focus on his claim that the state embodies the actuality of the ethical idea; that sustained peace risks a kind of moral stagnation comparable to narrative flatness; and that conflict functions as a necessary moment in which collective sovereignty is affirmed and private interest is transcended through exposure to mortal risk. Translate this faithfully, using concepts such as narrative arc, collective protagonist, dramatic necessity, and the economics of suffering in fiction. Do not smuggle philosophical assumptions into the vocabulary and present them as translation. Then report what survives intact, what translates only with distortion, and what possesses no counterpart whatsoever. In particular, does Hegel's idea that reason achieves its purposes through the unintended passions and sufferings of individuals correspond to anything in narrative theory beyond a redescription of dramatic irony, or does it depend on metaphysical commitments about history that have no analogue in storytelling? Does the notion that a state requires conflict for its ethical health map onto any coherent concept of a collective character's development, or does it collapse into mere anthropomorphism? Finally, indicate whether the untranslatable residue exposes a genuine limitation in narrative vocabulary, or whether it suggests that parts of Hegel's argument are less substantive than they appear. Present this in one sustained section of no more than 350 words, using British spelling and punctuation throughout.
