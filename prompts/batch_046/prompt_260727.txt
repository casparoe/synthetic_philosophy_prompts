As a novelist leading a weekend workshop for fellow fiction writers—most of whom write historical novels and have no formal philosophy training—I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself, for introducing Nietzsche's distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, with particular attention to his claim that each mode can either serve or damage present life. Lay out the dependency structure: which mode must be introduced first, and why, so that the normative criterion—that history is justified only when it expands present creative vitality—remains visible rather than seeming like an afterthought. Select the single carrying example drawn from the practice of writing fiction (not academic historiography) that can bear all three modes simultaneously; specify what this example must contain and what it must lack to keep the three modes distinct, and explain why a generic case of "a student studying the past" collapses them together. Map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form—especially the tendency to read antiquarian history as mere archival research, critical history as present-day political correction, and monumental history as simple hero-worship—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted before it hardens. Design one closing question that tests whether the writers have grasped the normative criterion (whether a given use of the past expands or constricts present imaginative capacity) rather than merely labeling the three types. Finally, state explicitly which part of Nietzsche's broader argument—likely the physiological diagnosis of modernity's excess historical consciousness—should be acknowledged as omitted at this level and left out of the session.
