I recently joined a cross-cultural research-ethics board where Dutch and Japanese colleagues have been deadlocked for months over peer-review feedback norms. The Dutch members treat indirect framing—saying "this might be refined" when the intended meaning is "this is wrong"—as a white lie that imposes an unfair interpretive burden and violates a norm of transparency. The Japanese members respond that explicit negation in a collaborative setting ruptures the honne-tatemae balance and that ambiguity functions not as deception but as a shared pragmatic contract that lets the listener preserve face and withdraw gracefully. Both sides concede the descriptive psychology and agree that respect, honesty, and scientific rigor are non-negotiable; yet neither has moved. My hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical dispute but a disagreement about where moral weight resides—whether in the speaker's intent to deceive or in the listener's experienced cognitive load—with each culture treating one pole as the bedrock moral fact that needs no further argument. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the stall actually verbal—two groups using "honesty" and "respect" for different normative functions—or even a sign that the question is ill-posed? Give me a committed diagnosis, not a menu of possibilities, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about interpretive burden, or a reframing of the question itself.
