Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for philosophical positions on the Gettier problem, designed as a diagnostic sorting instrument for an advanced epistemology seminar. The tree should lead a respondent from concrete intuitive verdicts on four engineered cases to a named position—or to an explicitly marked unoccupied branch—without requiring prior knowledge of the labels.

Specify four case nodes:
A. A classic false-lemma case: an agent derives a true belief through reasoning that essentially depends on a false justified empirical premise.
B. An environmental-correction case: the agent’s reasoning is internally sound and contains no false premises, but the belief is true only because an unknown external factor happens to correct what would otherwise have been an error; the “luck” is environmental, not doxastic.
C. A distributed-cognition case: a collective agent reaches a true conclusion because one subgroup’s false sub-report is accidentally canceled out by an independent error in the aggregation procedure, so no individual agent holds a false lemma.
D. A modal-false-premise case: the agent justifiably holds a false modal belief (e.g., that a certain outcome is impossible) and derives from it, via valid reasoning, a true empirical belief.

For each internal node, phrase the split as a concrete yes/no or either/or question about one of these cases—e.g., “Does this scenario lack knowledge?”—so that a respondent answers from intuition alone. The branching dimensions must include: whether knowledge requires the absence of false premises in the reasoning chain; whether environmental luck undermines knowledge when reasoning is sound; whether Gettier-style failures extend to collective or distributed belief; and whether false modal premises produce a distinct epistemic defect from false empirical ones.

The leaves must include at least: (1) an unqualified justified-true-belief position; (2) the no-false-lemmas view (knowledge fails precisely when justification essentially depends on a falsehood); (3) a modal-robustness or safety-oriented position; (4) a sensitivity-based position; (5) a virtue-theoretic or reliabilist position; (6) a pluralist or disunity view holding that Gettier cases do not share a single underlying explanation; and (7) a deflationary view that denies a unified “Gettier problem” exists (e.g., a dissolution via knowledge-first or contextualist strategies). For each leaf, provide the position name and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment.

Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate—such as safety and sensitivity on ordinary empirical cases—and indicate which case, if any, breaks the tie. Identify at least one logically possible combination of answers that corresponds to no named position, and diagnose the vacancy: is the combination incoherent, structurally unstable, or merely unexplored?

Keep every node labeled by the claim that splits its siblings, not by philosopher names. Do not include citation requests, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not ask “which philosopher do you agree with?” The instrument must classify respondents by what they believe about the cases, not by name recognition.
