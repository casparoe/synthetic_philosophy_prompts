Analyze the 2009 german schuldenbremse — the constitutional debt brake that caps structural deficits — as a Ulysses contract in constitutional precommitment theory. The story is pretty well known: parliament amends it's basic law to tie its own hands, but builds in an emergency escape clause, which gets used during covid. Then the government tries to shuffle leftover covid borrowing authority into a climate fund, and in 2023 the constitutional court strikes it down, saying emergency debt can't be repurposed once the crisis passes. Use the precommitment framework — who binds whom, whether future generations consent, whether escape clauses undermine the contract or just make it realistic — to map the episode. There's also this idea of "precommitment decay" where the binding erodes exactly under fiscal stress; does the suspension and the court's pushback illustrate that decay, or show the brake actually has teeth? Say what the theory gets right about democratic self-binding, where it misses the intergenerational consent problem, and whether this case proves constitutional precommitment works or just reveals that every Ulysses contract comes with a hidden exit door.
