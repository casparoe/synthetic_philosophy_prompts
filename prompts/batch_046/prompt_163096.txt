I am writing a dissertation chapter on explanatory pluralism in artificial intelligence, and a colleague in the philosophy of science offered the following gloss on Daniel Dennett's thesis about real patterns and his distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances. According to this summary, Dennett holds that a pattern is ontologically real only when it is indispensable to our best physical theory; the design and intentional stances are merely heuristic overlays that we apply when computational limitations prevent full physical simulations. Consequently, the summary concludes, Dennett's view entails that attributing beliefs or goals to an AI system is never more than pragmatic shorthand: with sufficient computational resources, the design stance itself would collapse into the physical stance, leaving no explanatory work for intentional vocabulary. The colleague further suggests that this makes Dennett an unwitting ally of mechanistic interpretability, since functional explanations of neural networks are always provisional approximations destined to be replaced by lower-level descriptions. Something about this strikes me as conflating Dennett's anti-reductionism with a kind of physicalist eliminativism, and I am unsure whether his criterion of predictive utility actually supports the claim that higher-level stances are dispensable in principle. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish which parts accurately capture Dennett's resistance to reductionism and his criterion for pattern reality, which parts subtly misrepresent his ontological commitments, and whether the application to AI interpretability introduces premises not present in Dennett's argument. I am particularly interested in whether the claim that the design stance collapses into the physical stance is a misreading of his view on the autonomy of explanatory levels.
