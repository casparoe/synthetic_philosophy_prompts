For a graduate seminar on historical epistemology, I am trying to formalize what it takes for a theory of distant-past knowledge to be adequate—specifically for what I call the “intermediate darkness,” the long stretch (roughly from the collapse of Bronze Age palace economies to the emergence of continuous state archives) in which neither living testimony nor purely material traces can carry the epistemic load alone. In this zone, access is doubly mediated: first by the preservation biases of the transmitting institutions (scriptoria, ritual reciters, royal chroniclers) and second by the modern reconstructive disciplines (philology, stratigraphy, radiometric calibration) that recover fragments. I want a precise specification of the desiderata that any adequate epistemology for this domain must satisfy.

Please list the requirements—phenomena it must explain, distinctions it must respect, results with which it must remain consistent—with the motivating case behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable versus which reflect the preferences of particular camps (for instance, hermeneutic continuity versus evidentiary minimalism). Crucially, identify any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable given the underdetermination of narrative reconstruction by material traces, and indicate which single requirement does the most work in excluding rival candidates. In framing these, treat Herodotean inquiry—cross-cultural comparison and reliance on reported speech—not as a naive precursor but as a live methodological option, and treat Thucydidean critical verification—suspicion of myth and demand for eyewitness proximity—not as fully achievable here but as a regulative ideal whose failure conditions must be specified. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want the criteria themselves, with only a brief scoring of how the main contending approaches (narrative-coherentist, trace-minimalist, and hybrid mediationist) fare against the list, keeping the weight on the requirements rather than on the scorecard.
