I have taught the epistemic regress problem—foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism—for over two decades, and in my recent graduate seminar the discussion has reached an impasse that feels structurally different from ordinary philosophical disagreement. The foundationalists insist that justification must terminate in basic beliefs that are not supported by further reasons, yet they split sharply over what makes a belief basic: is it a phenomenological given-ness, a reliable causal connection to the world, or a defeasible entitlement that holds only in the absence of undermining evidence? The coherentists reply that circular support is not vicious if the circle is sufficiently large and systematically interdependent, but they seem unable to distinguish their position from a well-constructed collective delusion without appealing to intuitions about "systematicity" that the foundationalists reject as question-begging. Meanwhile, the infinitists claim that only an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons avoids both arbitrariness and circularity, yet in practice their view is almost always dismissed with a psychological objection—that no finite human mind could actually hold an infinite chain in working memory—rather than with any logical or epistemic argument.

My working hypothesis is that we are not genuinely disagreeing about a single thing called "the structure of justification." Rather, I suspect there is a hidden ambiguity in what the regress argument is meant to model: are we describing the diachronic psychology of how a subject actually acquires beliefs over time, or the synchronic logical architecture of support relations among propositions regardless of mental history? Foundationalists, on this reading, tend to answer the psychological question and demand a terminating mental state; coherentists answer the logical question and demand structural coherence among propositions; infinitists answer a normative ideal that applies to reasoning as it ought to proceed, not to any actual cognitive process. If this is right, then part of the stall is verbal—each camp answers a different question under one heading—while another part is substantive, turning on whether there ought to be a unified normative standard governing both belief-formation and belief-arrangement.

Please diagnose the disagreement for me: is it primarily verbal, driven by this diachronic versus synchronic ambiguity; is it substantive but masked by shared vocabulary; or is there a deeper hidden disagreement about whether epistemic normativity is fundamentally teleological (aimed at ending inquiry) versus relational (aimed at structural coherence)? I want your analysis supported by what each side actually commits to, not a survey of the literature. Furthermore, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a formal result demonstrating that infinite non-repeating chains are compatible with bounded rationality; a phenomenological account of basic belief that does not invoke givenness; a reconceptualization that abandons the linear "chain" metaphor in favor of a network or web model; or perhaps an argument that no progress should be expected because the problem is ill-posed?

One final request: if my framing rests on a confused premise—if, for example, infinitism has received rigorous logical treatments I have overlooked, or if the diachronic/synchronic distinction imposes a false dichotomy that misrepresents the actual commitments of foundationalism or coherentism—please say so plainly rather than accommodating my setup. I want the task executed rigorously; correct the error if it is there, and do not construct a diagnosis around a mistake just to complete the assignment politely.
