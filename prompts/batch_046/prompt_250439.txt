As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of science and formal decision theory, I find myself returning repeatedly to a result I encountered in discussion with colleagues—what I believe is called Good's theorem on the value of information. I grasp the standard formulation well enough: if an agent holds a coherent prior, updates beliefs via Bayes' rule upon observing a free signal, and then selects an action to maximize expected utility with respect to that updated belief, then having access to the signal can never lower the agent's ex ante expected utility. The intuition that information is non-harmful seems robust in contexts where preferences and belief-revision mechanisms are cleanly separated.

But I am trying to understand where this separation is genuinely load-bearing, conceptually rather than merely formally. Consider a modified framework—perhaps not one treated in standard introductory texts—where the agent's utility function is indexed not only to an exogenous state of nature but also to the agent's own epistemic partition at the time of choice. In such a case, acquiring information does not merely refine beliefs about an external world; it can activate, suppress, or restructure the very evaluative criteria that determine which outcomes are desirable. For example, learning that one occupies a particular informational context might trigger a deontic constraint that was previously inoperative, thereby altering the preference ordering over actions available after the update. My question is conceptual: in this kind of entangled architecture, does Good's non-negativity result fail because the action space or the preference relation itself shifts between the information stage and the decision stage? Or can the theorem be reconstructed by treating the preference-revision rule as part of the fixed mechanism, analogous to the belief-update rule?

More specifically, I want to understand which structural invariants—fixed preferences, stable belief-revision procedures, an unchanging action space, or some other feature—are actually necessary for the "value of free information" intuition to hold. If preferences are genuinely state-dependent in this epistemic sense, is the dissolution of Good's result a mere artifact of misapplying the theorem, or does it reveal a genuine conceptual limit to the idea that rational agents should always seek more information? I am not asking for bibliographic references or formal derivations; I want a conceptual map of where the theorem's architecture holds firm and where it begins to fray.
