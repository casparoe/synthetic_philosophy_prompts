I am preparing a feature on logical empiricism's afterlife in contemporary ethics, and in a recent long-form essay I came across a summary of Quine's view on moral values that seems suspiciously neat. The author casts Quine as extending his naturalized epistemology straight into ethics: moral judgments belong to the web of belief, yet because they never appear in the canonical notation of a regimented scientific theory, they carry no existential commitments and are revisable solely on pragmatic grounds. Ethical disagreement, on this account, is not a clash over facts but a competition between different strategies for simplifying our overall conceptual scheme—analogous to choosing between logically equivalent formulations. The essay then applies Quine's indeterminacy of translation directly to normative language, suggesting that two societies with identical behavioral dispositions but radically incompatible moral vocabularies could both be fully correct, with no deeper fact of the matter to adjudicate between them. From all this, the author concludes that Quine dissolves moral philosophy into descriptive sociology and evolutionary psychology, leaving no space for genuine normative authority. Before I rely on any of this in print, I need an accuracy audit. Which parts genuinely reflect Quine's position on ontology, scientific revision, and translation; which parts conflate those positions or overextend them into ethics; and does the disappearance of normative authority actually follow from the parts that survive correction? I am especially uncertain whether Quine ever applied indeterminacy specifically to moral terms, and whether his comparison between ethical and scientific sentences can bear the weight the summary places on it.
