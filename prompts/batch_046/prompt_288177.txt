Exam question:
Compare Appiah's and Bernard Williams's explanations of how practices like dueling and slavery actually end. Is it rational persuasion, shifting economic interests, or a change in what counts as honorable? Then apply both views to the decline of foot-binding in China around 1900-1920. Which framework fits the Chinese case better, and what does the mismatch reveal about the limits of the other?

Student answer:
Appiah argues that moral revolutions happen when a practice becomes incompatible with the honor of the people who maintain it. With dueling, gentlemen didn't stop because philosophers convinced them it was immoral; they stopped because dueling started to look ridiculous and low-status, so the honor attached to it inverted. Bernard Williams extends this by saying that moral change always tracks social utility: once a practice stops being useful for coordination, people construct moral reasons to abandon it. So for Williams, slavery ended mainly because industrial capitalism made slave labor inefficient, and the moral arguments against it were just post-hoc rationalizations.

Applying this to foot-binding, I think Appiah's framework works better. Foot-binding was tied to elite Han status, and when modernizing Chinese elites wanted to signal cosmopolitanism, the practice lost its prestige. Williams's interest-based story is weaker here because foot-binding actually didn't conflict with factory labor—many bound-foot women worked in cottage industries—so economic interest doesn't explain the speed of the collapse. Therefore, the honor-shift model captures the Chinese case well, while Williams's utility model needs to be restricted to cases where material incentives clearly drive the change.
