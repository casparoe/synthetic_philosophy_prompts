Below are working notes for a short essay tentatively titled "Molyneux's Problem and the Non-Modularity of Spatial Representation." The standard formulation asks whether a person blind from birth, having learned to distinguish a cube and a sphere by touch alone, could immediately identify them by sight upon suddenly gaining vision. I want to challenge the implicit modularist premise that tactile and visual representations are delivered to a central, amodal comparison module; instead, I propose that spatial properties are encoded as modality-specific but cross-modally linked invariant structures—curvature gradients, topological relations, and affordance patterns—so that recognition is not a single event but a gradual calibration of shared detectors. On this view, Molyneux's problem dissolves from a conceptual yes-or-no puzzle into an empirical question about calibration speed, though it may re-emerge as a question about whether such invariants can exist without prior visual experience. I also want to ask whether this framing forces a rejection of all representational amodality, or whether it permits a weaker, emergent amodality built through sensorimotor interaction rather than innate central mapping. Please evaluate the coherence of this framing, its fidelity to the historical problem and to contemporary discussions of sensory substitution, and whether it genuinely dissolves the problem or merely relocates it; also indicate whether the argument risks being either trivial or implausible. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
