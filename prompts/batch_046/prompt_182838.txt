I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving active laboratory research, now serve on the Clinical Ethics and Research Oversight Board at a major teaching hospital. Our board is divided over a proposed study in clinical decision psychology that directly engages a question I have recently begun to take seriously: whether genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which an agent faces incompatible moral demands and remains culpable regardless of choice—are actually possible, or merely appear so because our normative frameworks are incomplete. The researchers propose placing experienced emergency physicians in high-fidelity simulated triage scenarios constructed from anonymized real case composites, but arranged so that two patients present with identically weighted clinical urgency, prognostic profiles, and ethical entitlements. Crucially, the protocol explicitly forbids the use of any tie-breaking principle—whether utilitarian, deontological, or clinical—so that the physician must select one patient without being able to justify that choice over the other by reference to any available moral framework. The study then tracks long-term psychological outcomes, specifically persistent self-blame and what the investigators describe as irreducible moral residue: regret that remains even when the physician fully recognizes that no correct choice was available. The proponents argue that if clinicians experience lasting culpability in the absence of any possible justification, this supports the reality of genuine moral dilemmas; opponents on our board contend that we are deliberately manufacturing psychological harm to settle a metaphysical dispute, and that any observed distress can be explained by epistemic uncertainty rather than by an actual conflict of undefeated obligations.

I need a formal decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval with specific safeguards—addressed to the full board and written in terms that the research team could respond to concretely. The memo should treat the philosophical issue with conceptual precision: it must not assume that persistent emotional distress proves the existence of a genuine dilemma, nor should it dismiss the possibility by definitional fiat, for instance by insisting that any resolvable conflict was never truly a dilemma. It should also include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation. Given my scientific background, I particularly value reasoning that distinguishes empirical measurement from conceptual proof, and I ask that the memo maintain that distinction throughout. Please do not include requests for bibliographies, citation lists, or literature reviews in your response; our mandate is institutional action, not scholarly compilation.
