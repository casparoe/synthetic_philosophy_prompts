I'm writing a column on symbolic causation and need to stress-test an argument I've sketched about a hypothetical apparatus called a careenium. In this device, spherical tokens—simmballs—roll along grooved tracks; their spatial configuration at any instant encodes a formal symbolic expression, such as a command, and observers report that the symbol "makes" a gate open. The argument runs as follows. (1) Every motion of every simmball is fully fixed by local mechanical forces—gravity, friction, collisions—with no physical remainder. (2) The symbolic properties of the arrangement supervene entirely on these motions. (3) If a symbol genuinely caused substrate change, it would have to alter a simmball's trajectory in a way not determined by those local forces. (4) That would violate the causal closure of physics. (5) Therefore, in a careenium symbols never push the substrate around; only mechanics does. (6) The brain is structurally parallel: neurons form a substrate on which mental symbols supervene, yet each firing is physically caused. (7) By parity, mental symbols never push neural substrate, making mental causation epiphenomenal and intentional explanation systematically false. (8) But the designers of the careenium rely on intentional explanations—beliefs, plans—to build and maintain the device; if (7) holds, those explanations are false too, undermining the very reasoning that generated the argument. (9) Hence the argument proves too much: it eliminates not only mental causation but also the rational agency needed to formulate it. Where is the hidden slip? Is it in (3)'s demand for non-physical force, in (4)'s closure principle misapplied to supervenient properties, or in (6)'s analogy? Name the weakest step, explain why it felt unavoidable, and say what we must concede—downward causation, a qualified closure principle, or a broken analogy—to keep symbolic efficacy intact in both careeniums and brains.
