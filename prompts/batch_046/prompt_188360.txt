I am preparing material for a philosophy of mind seminar on the temporal dynamics of conscious experience, specifically Daniel Dennett’s distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of phenomenal content—exemplified by phenomena such as color phi, in which the brain either retroactively rewrites the record of an experience or delays the construction of that experience until after subsequent stimuli have arrived. Rather than treating this as a settled taxonomy, I want to use Michel Gondry’s film *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* as a philosophical stress-test for the distinction itself. The film depicts Joel Barish undergoing a targeted erasure of memories involving Clementine Kruczynski, during which he attempts to conceal her within unmapped regions of his past—childhood scenes, pre-verbal impressions, and emotional residues that the Lacuna technicians have not indexed. Read the film’s treatment of memory erasure through the Orwellian/Stalinesque lens: when Joel becomes aware, inside the collapsing memory, that he is experiencing a reconstructed past and tries to resist its deletion, is he defending an already-completed experiential record against retroactive rewriting (Orwellian), or is the “memory” he fights for never fully constituted as conscious experience until the erasure procedure forces a retrospective binding that makes it seem as though it had always been present (Stalinesque)? Pay close attention to the visual logic of the erasure sequence, in which memories dissolve in reverse chronological order while Joel’s present-tense awareness seems to inhabit them simultaneously, and to the train encounter at the end, where Joel and Clementine behave as strangers despite the audience’s knowledge of their erased history.

More specifically, I want you to consider whether the film’s fantasy of selective amnesia maps cleanly onto the perceptual case of color phi at all. In color phi, a discrete stimulus is experienced as continuous motion; in the film, discrete emotional traces survive the erasure and re-emerge as inexplicable attractions. Does Joel’s attempt to “hide” Clementine inside pre-linguistic or childhood memories—memories that, on Dennett’s account, might never have been fully “broadcast” into the Cartesian theater—imply that some experiential content is structurally immune to Stalinesque revision because it was never available for revision in the first place? Or does that very immunity undermine the Orwellian alternative by showing that there is no stable “original record” to be rewritten? I am looking for a reading that takes both Dennett’s framework and the film’s narrative architecture seriously: point to specific scenes (the beach-house collapse, the office revelation, the final train meeting) and say exactly where the philosophical distinction illuminates the cinematic choice and where the film resists being reduced to illustration. In particular, address what the work adds to the debate about the timing of experience that a purely conceptual argument—lacking the film’s depiction of emotional persistence across erasure—cannot supply on its own, and be honest about where the analogy between perceptual color phi and narrative memory breakdown ultimately fails. I am writing this for a seminar discussion and do not want the reading to mislead students into thinking that the neuroscience of memory consolidation straightforwardly vindicates either the Orwellian or the Stalinesque view.
