Read Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon through the philosophical question of whether brain size is morally relevant, with particular attention to a distinction rarely made explicit in the debate: the gap between absolute cranial volume and allometrically scaled neural capacity. Charlie Gordon's brain does not physically enlarge, yet his cognitive and social standing transforms radically after the procedure. Does the novel treat moral status as tracking functional intelligence, invariant biological substrate, or something in between? Examine the bakery scenes before and after the operation, and Charlie's relationship with Alice Kinnian, to see where the text endorses, complicates, or resists a brain-metric account of worth. Finally, consider whether Keyes's portrayal implies a discontinuous jump in moral standing at a cognitive threshold, and what that would mean for views that ground consideration in encephalization rather than in sentience or relational capacity.
