I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and scientific representation, and over the break I started consulting for a small public-health NGO that distributes epidemiological briefings to multilingual, low-literacy communities in refugee camps. They want to move away from text-heavy reports and use purely visual statements—essentially reviving something like Neurath's picture-language idea—so that a reader with no shared language can still grasp the claim. The problem is that their designers keep producing infographics that smuggle in hidden syntax: temporal ordering implied by left-to-right layout, causal arrows that look like correlations, quantitative differences shown through icon size rather than repetition, and negative conditions represented by absence rather than by an explicit symbol. I need a reusable, step-by-step evaluation procedure—something a non-philosopher communications officer can run in about fifteen minutes—to decide whether a proposed visual statement is genuinely a valid pictorial claim in the spirit of Neurath's constraints, or whether it has collapsed back into a disguised verbal argument with decorative images. The procedure should specify the ordered tests to apply, what counts as passing each one, the point at which it returns a hard "do not publish," and the exact points where judgment about cultural interpretation or ethical framing must take over because the theory itself is silent. Please include worked applications to three proposed graphics: one depicting a drop in measles vaccination coverage using progressively smaller human figures; one depicting a causal claim about water-source contamination and diarrheal illness using arrows and paired symbols; and one attempting to represent statistical uncertainty or confidence intervals through faded color intensity, which the communications team strongly favors for readability but which may violate the requirement that every representational feature have a fixed, non-ambiguous referent. In at least one of these cases, the procedure should yield a rejection that the NGO staff will likely resist, and you should say exactly how to present that result to them. Be explicit about which distinctions from Neurath's framework—such as the difference between iconic quantity-representation and symbolic encoding—must survive the operational simplification and which can be dropped without damage, and flag any step that is essentially a judgment call dressed up as a technical check.
