Design a 90-minute workshop activity for sixteen advanced undergraduates or beginning graduate students in philosophy of science or historiography that lets them experience, rather than merely discuss, the collapse of historicist prediction. The group should come away feeling why Karl Popper insisted that what looks like a law of history is usually a conditional trend, and why the very act of predicting historical development tends to destroy the conditions on which the prediction rests.

Structure the session in three timed rounds with assigned roles, a hidden-information mechanic, and a debrief. In Round One (20 minutes), divide the room into four groups of four. Give each group an identical visible “societal profile” card listing economic base, technological regime, and institutional form. Their task is to formulate, in writing, a single predictive law that determines the next historical stage, with justification. Each group posts its law on the board.

In Round Two (25 minutes), reveal that every group was also holding one hidden “contingency card” (e.g., a specific leadership decision, a localized climate event, an unanticipated cultural transmission) that was invisible to the other groups and was meant to represent an unstated initial condition. Each group now sees that its law failed for the others, not because the logic was wrong, but because the initial conditions were never uniform. The facilitator should ask each group to revise its law into a “trend statement” qualified by explicit initial conditions, and then to vote on whether the revised statement still deserves to be called a law.

In Round Three (20 minutes), the groups must issue a public “historical prophecy” based on their revised statement. Once a prophecy is announced, the other three groups may each alter one visible parameter of the societal profile card in direct response to that prophecy—simulating reflexivity, where the prediction changes the behavior of the agents being predicted. The prophecy is then tested against the altered profile. Predictably, all prophecies fail.

For the debrief (25 minutes), map each round onto Popper’s critique: Round One to the historicist ambition of discovering laws analogous to physics; Round Two to the uniqueness of historical initial conditions and the impossibility of repeating experiments; Round Three to the reflexive effect of predictions on open social systems. Ask the group specifically whether they felt the failure in Round Two was a failure of method or a revelation about the object of study. Then be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: Popper’s deeper argument that the growth of scientific knowledge itself is unpredictable and therefore undermines any deterministic account of long-term historical development. That point depends on philosophical argument about knowledge, not on game mechanics, and the facilitator should supply it directly. Also note a practical failure mode: groups often try to “save” their law by adding ad hoc conditions, which is itself instructive—have the facilitator label this move in real time and connect it to the immunization strategies Popper criticized.

Include an honest note on which feature of Popper’s view the activity reproduces well (the dependency on unstated conditions and reflexivity) and which it cannot (the epistemological argument about unpredictability of knowledge growth). Specify the materials needed (profile cards, hidden contingency cards, alteration tokens) and a timeline with minute allocations. The rules and incentives—not the facilitator’s lecture—should make the participants experience the frustration of trying to legislate history.
