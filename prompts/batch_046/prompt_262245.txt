I am a journalist covering science and philosophy of biology, and I am drafting an essay on the procreation asymmetry—the idea that we have strong moral reason not to bring a severely suffering being into existence, but no parallel reason to bring a flourishing being into existence. Rather than treating this as an abstract puzzle about distant possible worlds, I want to apply it to synthetic embryo models: biological structures derived from stem cells that possess the developmental potential for sentience but are maintained in vitro and can be activated or terminated with precise technical control.

My argument proceeds in three steps. First, standard accounts explain the asymmetry by appealing to modal distance—the suffering being is “near enough” to generate a duty, the happy one is not. Second, synthetic embryos collapse that distance: the potential being is not in remote logical space but in an immediate technical branch, equally available for activation or non-activation. Third, despite this collapse, the asymmetry persists. A laboratory has a compelling duty not to activate a synthetic embryo that would develop into a suffering sentient organism, yet no comparable duty to activate one that would flourish—even though both are equally “possible” within the same substrate. I take this to suggest that the asymmetry is not about modal proximity at all, but about a directional constraint on agency: introducing a negative-value state creates a claimant with standing, whereas failing to introduce a positive-value state is merely a missed opportunity that creates no claimant.

I then consider the strongest objection: that synthetic embryos lack moral status altogether, so neither duty applies. I respond that if status is absent, we should not regulate suffering in comparable animal embryos either; yet we do, which implies that the potential for sentience—not actual personhood—is doing the normative work. I conclude that the synthetic case reveals the asymmetry to be a feature of how harms generate obligations regardless of prior existence, not a quirk of population ethics.

Please comment specifically on: (1) whether the move from “modal distance” to “technical branch” is coherent or conflates metaphysical possibility with laboratory feasibility; (2) whether my response to the moral-status objection holds up, or whether it illegitimately maps animal-embryo regulations onto synthetic-embryo ethics; and (3) whether the “directional constraint on agency” framing genuinely illuminates the asymmetry or merely restates it.

If this project rests on a confused premise—for example, if synthetic embryos do not actually alter the modal logic of the procreation asymmetry in any meaningful way, or if the task requires resolving an empirical dispute about sentience potential that I have not addressed—please say so plainly rather than complying politely with adjustments. Write in American English.
