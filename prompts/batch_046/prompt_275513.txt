I'm working through some material on the School of Names (Mingjia) for an independent study project on how classical Chinese logic treats category boundaries, and I keep returning to the white-horse dialogue associated with Gongsun Long. The argument, as I understand it from the received version, runs roughly as follows: horse denotes the form or shape of a horse, whereas white horse denotes that form together with the color white. Since a horse may be yellow, black, or brown, but a white horse cannot be any of those colors, the two descriptions pick out different ranges of instances. From this, the interlocutor concludes that a white horse is not a horse—because if they were the same thing, then whatever applies to one should apply to the other, yet the color constraints differ. The dialogue then seems to defend this conclusion against the obvious objection that a white horse is plainly still a horse.

What I find genuinely hard to parse is not the surface paradox but the exact logical move that licenses the slide from descriptive divergence to ontological separation. The standard gloss treats this as an ancient version of the universal-versus-particular problem, but that reading feels too quick: it assumes that whenever two descriptions have different extensional conditions, they must refer to distinct entities, which is precisely the principle in dispute. I am wondering whether the argument is best reconstructed as turning on a hidden ambiguity about predication—specifically, whether is a horse is being read as an identity claim between the kind named by the subject term and the kind named by the predicate term, rather than as an inclusion claim about an individual falling under a kind.

To complicate things further, I have been trying to map this onto a modern concern that comes up in conceptual engineering and in discussions of the frame problem: if we treat a category as defined by a cluster of features, does augmenting that cluster with an additional feature like color create a new category that is disjoint from the original, or does it merely specify a subcategory? The dialogue seems to presuppose the former, but I cannot tell whether that presupposition is a logical error, a deliberate provocation about the fragility of kind-terms, or a consequence of a specific metaphysics of forms that the School of Names held. Could you help me see exactly where the inference breaks—whether in the move from descriptions to kinds, in the treatment of color as essential rather than accidental, or in some other step—and whether there is a coherent interpretation that preserves the argument's force without committing to the claim that feature-addition always fractures category identity?
