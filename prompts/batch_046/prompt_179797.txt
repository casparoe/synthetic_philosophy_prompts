I serve on the interdisciplinary review panel of our national science funding body, and we are deadlocked over proposal QF-2024-118, “Embedded Conceptual Analysis for Quantum Error Correction.” The applicants—an experimental physics group paired with philosophers of science—argue that philosophy is not decorative here but instrumental: they claim that unexamined ontological commitments in quantum measurement theory, specifically the treatment of measurement outcomes as primitive rather than relationally defined, have caused researchers to misidentify noise sources in error-correction protocols. They contend that clarifying the ontology of measurement under conditions of informational inaccessibility is a precondition for empirical progress, not a substitute for it. Several panelists reject the premise entirely, invoking the familiar skepticism of Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman—Weinberg’s argument that philosophy does not furnish working scientists with the right conceptual tools, Hawking’s dismissal that philosophy has failed to keep pace with modern physics, and Feynman’s insistence that philosophical debate is a poor substitute for direct experimental labor. The skeptics worry that funding this project would institutionalize a category error, substituting conceptual debate for bench work and allowing philosophers to claim scientific relevance without producing falsifiable outputs, and they specifically warn of specification gaming: conceptual vocabulary being optimized to sound empirically consequential while remaining insulated from actual experimental constraint.

Please draft the formal recommendation memo I will circulate before Thursday’s vote. The memo should recommend a concrete course—approve full funding, approve a restricted pilot with empirically tied milestones, or reject with reasons the applicants could actually address—not merely summarize the disagreement. It must engage the strongest version of the skeptics’ concern: that ontology identification under informational inaccessibility is either already handled by physics’ internal modeling practices or is too abstract to constrain experimental design, and that embedding philosophers risks substituting conceptual refinement for measurable progress. At the same time, it must take seriously the applicants’ specific claim that misallocated resources in error-correction stem from an unexamined categorical framework, and it should propose conditions—if any—that would make the collaboration accountable to physical results rather than philosophical consensus. Write it as a document the full panel and, if necessary, the applicants could read: clear bottom line, reasoning that separates what is genuinely at stake from rhetorical framing, and no request for bibliographies, reading lists, or external citations.
