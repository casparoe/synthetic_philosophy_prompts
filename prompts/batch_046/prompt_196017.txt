Since Kripke's 1982 study of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations, the philosophical dispute over whether any fact determines that I meant addition by 'plus' has cycled through dispositionalist accounts, primitivist rejections, quietist dissolutions and normative-constitution theories, with objections now entirely familiar. The debate feels stalled: no position has won adherents outside its founding camp, and the literature increasingly reads like taxonomy rather than argument. I want a structural diagnosis, not another entry in it.

My working hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal—the sides talk past one another about 'fact'—but that a deeper problem is that they answer different questions under one heading. One camp treats the considerations as asking what metaphysically constitutes the content of a rule (a constitutional question), while the other treats them as asking what justifies a particular application given an already-fixed content (a question of epistemic entitlement). If that is correct, then trading counterexamples about dispositions or normativity will never resolve the dispute, because the explananda differ.

Yet I am unsure whether there is also a hidden formal issue at work: for instance, whether specifying any rule without circularity requires an infinite regress of interpretations, and whether that regress is vicious or benign. Or whether the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about naturalness—whether meaning must be reducible to physical or behavioural facts, or is sui generis.
