I hold a hybrid position drawn from Dennett’s and Caruso’s exchange on just deserts: punishment can be justified without basic metaphysical desert if it operates as a reason-responsive feedback mechanism that stabilizes cooperative norms, but only on the condition that we can maintain a strict distinction between (a) addressing an agent as a rational interlocutor capable of being held to account and (b) merely manipulating causal dispositions for social utility. My concern is that if Caruso’s skepticism about deep responsibility is extended consistently—not only to libertarian free will but to the thinner rational capacities required for genuine address—then Dennett’s pragmatic defense collapses into (b), leaving us with a sanitized form of behavioral engineering rather than justice. What would count against this hybrid view? I want the decisive conceptual distinctions, if any, that could salvage reason-responsiveness without smuggling in the very moral responsibility Caruso rejects; the empirical findings from moral psychology or neuroscience that would show whether the stabilizing feedback Dennett describes depends on, or can operate independently of, the rational-address conditions he requires; and an honest assessment of whether defenders of this position risk insulating it by redefining “reason-responsiveness” so broadly that it applies to any causally modifiable system. Rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of the hybrid should concede as genuinely damaging versus which they can plausibly dismiss, and say whether such insulation, if it occurs, undermines the view or is an acceptable feature of a pragmatically oriented theory of punishment.
