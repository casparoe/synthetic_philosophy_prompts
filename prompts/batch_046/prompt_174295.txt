Before drafting anything, spend a moment brainstorming the logical architecture of the disagreement: which parts are empirical, which are conceptual, and where the two sides might be talking past each other.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on insect sentience and the scale argument.

Camp 1, the Aggregationists, holds that with population estimates running into the quintillions, even a prior probability of sentience per insect in the range of 0.01% to 1% yields an enormous expected number of conscious beings. They argue that this expected-value structure gives serious moral weight to insect welfare and supports precautionary reasoning about large-scale ecological interventions.

Camp 2, the Reference-Class Skeptics, holds that base-rate reasoning across taxonomic boundaries is illegitimate without a validated reference class of organisms for which sentience is established. They maintain that observed insect neural architectures lack the specific integrative properties found in validated reference classes, so the appropriate prior should be near zero; multiplying a near-zero prior by a vast population still yields near-zero urgency, and treating “vast numbers” as amplifying weak evidence commits a category error between probabilistic physical traits and categorical mental properties.

The document should contain:
1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign—covering approximate population magnitudes, current neuroscientific uncertainty, the conditional moral stakes if sentience is present, and the agreement that this debate should not alone drive specific policy prescriptions.
2. A structured table of exact disagreements, with each camp’s position phrased in wording that camp itself would accept.
3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. I suspect central cruxes are: (a) whether the absence of particular neural correlates justifies a near-zero prior rather than a more agnostic distribution; (b) whether expected-value mathematics applies legitimately when the property in question may not be probabilistically distributed like a physical trait; and (c) whether our ecological predictions about large-scale effects are reliable enough to ground precautionary reasoning.
4. For each crux, identify one concrete empirical finding, theoretical argument, or comparative case both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence would show.

Ensure formulations are neither vacuous nor slanted. If part of the dispute turns on definitional differences—say, between “sentience,” “nociception,” and “valenced experience”—identify that portion explicitly, propose a shared operational definition for the remainder of the document, and note whether any residual disagreement is purely verbal. Keep the tone formal, focused, and constructive: the goal is a text both parties could put their names to, with the remaining divergence made precise.
