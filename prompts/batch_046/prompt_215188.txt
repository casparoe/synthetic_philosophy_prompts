After a long, exhausting argument with a friend about Kavka's toxin puzzle—specifically whether the rationality of forming an intention can be cleanly separated from the rationality of executing it when the reward is tied to a counterfactual mental state rather than to the physical act—I think the scenario is structurally absurd enough to be genuinely funny. Write a joke that turns on the actual mechanics of the puzzle: an agent receives a substantial reward merely for sincerely intending, at a specified time, to drink a toxin that will cause only mild, temporary harm the following day; there is no external mechanism enforcing consumption; and once the reward is secured, the agent faces an independent decision about whether to follow through. The punchline should depend on a feature that distinguishes this from ordinary commitment problems—perhaps the mildness of the harm, the irrelevance of the physical act to the reward condition, or the way the agent's second-order beliefs about their own future weakness of will enter the calculus. To prevent the joke from being a generic philosophy-nerd gag, embed a niche institutional twist: present the offer as administered by a bureaucratic ethics-review board that requires a pre-registered intention protocol, complete with a Bayesian credence update on the applicant's predicted failure of will, and treat the paperwork as if it were the genuinely hard part of the dilemma. The humor should come from the dry collision between the high-stakes rational-choice structure and the mundane administrative framing, not from named philosophers or slapstick. Keep the tone precise and relatively formal.
