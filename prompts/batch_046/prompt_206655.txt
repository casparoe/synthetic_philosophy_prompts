I've been reading about Ben Franklin's moral perfection scheme — the thirteen virtues he tried to master one at a time, marking his failures in that little grid each week. It's a neat idea, but I keep wondering what he'd think of modern 'moral biofeedback': wearable devices and apps that monitor your speech patterns, heart rate, and social interactions to flag when you're being dishonest or losing your temper, then nudge you back into line in real time. There's this slightly niche angle I've been chewing on — that Franklin's chart wasn't really about self-knowledge so much as about manufacturing a self-policing subject, a kind of internal panopticon where the surveilance is done by yourself for yourself. Given he was also pretty sceptical about human nature and knew people lie to themselves, do you think he'd see algorithimic moral coaching as the natural next step for his system, or as something that corrupts the autonomy that makes virtue worth having in the first place?

Would he keep the list at thirteen, or collapse some together? And what about this case: a device that can detect, with 90% accuracy, that you're about to say something cruel, and stops you — does that count as practising moderation, or as outsourcing your character?
