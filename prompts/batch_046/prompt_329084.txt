As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand a cluster of decision puzzles that involve a predictor running a simulation of an agent before the agent acts. I have worked out my intuitive verdicts in five concrete cases, but I am unsure whether they cohere. I would be grateful for help identifying the principle — or the competing principles — that best captures the pattern, and for an honest assessment of which verdicts must be abandoned to keep the account simple.

Case 1: There are two boxes on a table. Box A is transparent and contains £1,000. Box B is opaque; a highly reliable predictor (historically correct in 99 per cent of cases) has already placed either £1,000,000 or nothing inside, based on its forecast of whether you will take Box B alone or take both boxes. You may take Box B alone, or both boxes. My verdict: you should take both boxes. Even though I recognise that people who take only Box B usually leave with more money, the contents are already fixed; taking Box A as well cannot causally affect what is in Box B.

Case 2: The setup is identical, except the predictor is only 80 per cent accurate and its forecast is partly influenced by your physiological stress level, which you can lower in real time via a brief breathing exercise before you choose. The exercise takes thirty seconds and costs you nothing. My verdict: you should still take both boxes, but you should also do the breathing exercise, because lowering stress improves the predictor's accuracy in your favour without changing the causal independence of the boxes.

Case 3: You are running deterministic source code that will output "take one box" or "take two boxes". The predictor executes an identical copy of your source code in an isolated sandbox one minute before you run your own copy. You are permitted to edit your source code after the sandbox execution but before your own execution. My verdict: you should edit the source code after the simulation so that your live execution takes both boxes, but you should ensure the sandboxed version takes only one.

Case 4: The two boxes are filled simultaneously with your choice in a quantum-mechanical branching model: in branches where you take one box, Box B contains £1,000,000; in branches where you take both, it contains nothing. There is no prior moment of filling. My verdict: the causal argument collapses, so you should take only one box, because in that branch structure the correlation is direct rather than mediated by a prior prediction.

Case 5: There are now three opaque boxes (B, C, D) and the transparent Box A (£1,000). The predictor fills B and C with £500,000 each if it forecasts you will take exactly one of B, C, or D; if it forecasts you will take two or three, all three are empty. You may take any subset. My verdict: I think you should take exactly one opaque box together with Box A, but I am less certain than in Case 1.

Please answer in British English. Specifically:

1. Is there a single principle that delivers all five verdicts, or must some be revised? State the principle precisely, and for each clause indicate which case motivates it.

2. What does your principle predict for the following unlisted case: the predictor fills the boxes not by running your code, but by consulting a deterministic world-model that includes your brain state at the moment of choice, yet you have the option to inject random noise into your decision process at the last millisecond?

3. Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether overturning any of them makes the overall view more defensible.

Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am working without access to external sources and want an analysis I can evaluate on its own terms.
