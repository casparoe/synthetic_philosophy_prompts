I'm a high school senior in an advanced course on political philosophy, and I'm trying to work out my own view on political obligation and civil disobedience by testing it against concrete cases rather than accepting any textbook answer wholesale. I want to know whether a single coherent principle can capture all of my intuitions, or whether consistency requires me to abandon at least one of them.

Here are my judgments. First, on ordinary obligation: I feel genuinely bound to obey a democratically enacted carbon tax, even though I think the rate is too low and I could easily evade it without anyone noticing. Second, if the exact same tax were imposed by an unelected administrative board with no public debate or vote, my sense of moral obligation vanishes; I would pay only to avoid a fine. Third, I was too young to vote when my state passed a school-funding law, yet because I now attend those public schools and use the libraries funded by it, I still feel some duty to respect the law—though noticeably weaker than if I had actually cast a vote. Fourth, however, a recent zoning ordinance was decided by a vote open exclusively to property owners; my family rents our apartment, so we had no formal voice, yet we benefit from the improved parks and cleaner streets it produces. In that case I feel no moral obligation whatsoever, despite receiving the same kind of public benefits. Fifth, on civil disobedience: peacefully blocking the entrance to a government office to protest an unjust immigration policy, and willingly accepting arrest, strikes me not merely as permitted but as morally required. Sixth, yet secretly forging identification papers to shelter my own family from that identical policy, without accepting any legal consequence, feels clearly wrong—wrong in a different and deeper way, even though the law is unjust and the family need is urgent. Seventh, if a law required me, as a future teacher, to report undocumented students to immigration authorities, I believe I ought to refuse, regardless of how democratically it was enacted, because compliance would make me an active instrument of harm.

My tentative principle is that a law generates genuine moral obligation only when it issues from a decision procedure that grants fair standing to people with interests like mine, and that this obligation is nullified whenever obedience would require violating a more basic duty not to harm the vulnerable. But this principle seems strained under inspection. In the school-funding case I lacked a vote yet feel partial obligation, while in the zoning case I also lacked a vote and receive tangible benefits yet feel none at all; the distinction seems to depend on whether my exclusion was accidental (youth) versus structural (property ownership), which looks like an ad hoc repair rather than part of the principle itself. Meanwhile, my sharp distinction between open protest and secret evasion seems to turn on willingness to accept punishment, which my principle does not even mention.

Can you formulate one precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that delivers all seven verdicts? For each clause, please identify the specific case that motivates it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts must be overturned if I want the simplest coherent account, and defend that choice. Finally, apply your best principle to a case I have not considered: a law providing public goods I actively use, passed by a democratic majority but enacted through voting procedures deliberately designed to suppress participation by a minority group to which I belong, where the law's purpose includes disadvantaging people like me. Would I be morally bound to obey it?
