As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I have become preoccupied with what I think of as the observer-dependence problem in moral judgment—specifically, the question of whether hypocrisy undermines one's standing to blame. Rather than a generic survey, I would like a rigorous dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—let us call them Dr. Aris and Dr. Bell—who defend opposing accounts of this issue.

Dr. Aris holds that hypocrisy vitiates standing through a failure of normative calibration: just as a scientific instrument that applies different correction factors to identical measurements is unreliable, a blamer who applies stricter moral weights to others' conduct than to her own demonstrates a structurally analogous miscalibration. This undermines her authority to blame not merely because she is imperfect, but because her judgment lacks the consistency required for genuine normative authority.

Dr. Bell rejects this calibration model. She argues that standing is not a comparative property dependent on the blamer's record, but a direct normative relation between the wrongdoer and the wronged party—or, in appropriate cases, any moral agent addressing genuine wrongdoing. On her view, hypocrisy may diminish the blamer's credibility or invite legitimate counter-blame, but it does not automatically annul standing; only specific relational conflicts, such as having wronged the very same person in the very same way, can do that.

The dialogue must contain real argumentative exchange: Bell should directly challenge whether moral judgment genuinely requires the kind of quantitative or procedural consistency implied by Aris's instrument analogy, and Aris should respond to Bell's direct-relation claim by asking whether blame can carry authoritative force without at least minimal consistency in the blamer's normative commitments. Neither speaker should be reduced to a strawman, and each must address the other's actual points rather than talk past them.

Please structure the response so that it clearly distinguishes what is broadly settled in contemporary philosophy of blame—such as the distinction between having standing and the all-things-considered appropriateness of expressing blame—from what remains genuinely contested, including the precise conditions under which hypocrisy undermines standing and whether moral authority requires self-application consistency. Do not present either the calibration model or the direct-relation view as the established consensus. Where the scientific analogy relies on contested premises about the nature of moral judgment, note that explicitly. Maintain a formal, precise tone throughout, and do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations.
