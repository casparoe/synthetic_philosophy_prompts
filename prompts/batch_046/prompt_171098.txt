As a novelist constructing a narrative around vow-keeping and moral obligation, I have become preoccupied with an analogy that recurs in philosophical discussion: that making a promise is like promulgating a private statute, in which one simultaneously occupies the roles of legislator and subject, enacting a rule that binds only oneself and specifically in favor of another party. This comparison is frequently invoked to explain why promises remain obligatory even when breaking them produces no harm, no external punishment awaits, and the promisee may even have forgotten the vow; the authority of the self-enacted rule, it is said, requires no further justification. I want to subject this analogy to a rigorous audit. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the legislative body, the enacted text, the territorial jurisdiction, the citizen-subject, the mechanism of repeal or amendment, and the sanction for non-compliance? Second, identify the precise disanalogies: for example, whether the promisee's consent functions like ratification in a way that statutory subjects do not provide; whether a promise can be unilaterally revoked in ways that enacted law cannot; and whether the analogy silently imports a divided-self model — a legislating will distinct from an executive will — that may be metaphysically contentious. Third, assess whether the analogy smuggles in value-laden assumptions about autonomy, authority, or institutional legitimacy that do not apply to interpersonal commitment. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is meant to license — that promises bind independently of reliance, expectation, or consequential harm — survives once these fractures are visible, or whether the legislative framework was doing all the argumentative work. If a more accurate structural comparison exists — perhaps to a ritual performative, a relational contract with bilateral exit conditions, or a commitment device — propose it and explain which failures of the legislative model it avoids.
