I am leading a ninety-minute seminar for third-year comparative-politics students on how societies structure the memory of state violence, using Germany’s Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan’s postwar management of colonial and wartime responsibility, Turkey’s official denial and civic silence regarding the Armenian genocide, and the American South’s Lost Cause mythology versus its more recent counter-memorial shifts. Rather than surveying countries, I want to teach one analytical framework: the distinction between mnemonic integration, where culpability is woven into national civic identity and institutional practice, and mnemonic compartmentalization, where responsibility is quarantined into private, regional, or diasporic spheres while the official narrative remains intact.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, what idea must be in place before the framework can be introduced—specifically, how should I dislodge the default assumption that societies simply “remember” or “forget” along one dimension? Next, which single case or paired comparison should carry the framework, and what must that example possess and lack so that students see a structural distinction rather than a moral ranking? In what sequence should I move through the four cases, and at which point should I head off each predictable misconception: treating Germany as a completed moral success story, conflating Japan’s state reticence with popular amnesia, missing how Turkey’s compartmentalization operates through legal and bureaucratic codes rather than mere absence of knowledge, and reading the American South only through slavery rather than through federal-versus-state mnemonic divergence? Provide one end-of-session question that tests whether students have grasped the structural framework—not just whether they can label countries—and specify what a correct answer must include. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and why.
