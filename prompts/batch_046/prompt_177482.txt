I was arguing with my roommate last night over beers about whether 'Western values' is actually a coherrent concept or just a rhetorical cudgel people swing around in immigration and foreign-policy debates. I came across this idea—maybe in a podcast?—that if you trace things like individual liberty, secular governance, and gender equality back through history, they don't emerge from one shared tradition; they come from contradictory sources like medieval canon law, Stoic cosmopolitanism, Reformation theology, and 19th-century anti-clerical liberalism. So my take is that since there's no single philosophical lineage holding them together, treating 'the West' as if it has values is basically a falacy of reifiction—you're attributing beliefs to a fictional entity that doesn't actually exist. My roommate pushed back and said that even if the geneology is messy, the phrase still picks out a real functional cluster that democratic societies actually invoke—rule of law, pluralism, that sort of thing—so it's not meaningless just because historians can't draw one straight line back to Athens or whatever. I'm not convinced. If the category lacks historical coherence, doesn't that undermine any normative argument that appeals to it? Or is there some other way to defend the phrase without pretending there's one unified Western philosophy? I keep thinking about how 'Asian values' got torn apart in the 90s for being a manufactured tradition—feels like the exact same problem, but maybe I'm missing something.
