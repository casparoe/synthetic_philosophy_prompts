After a long argument with a friend about strategic voting and moral complicity, I've arrived at the following positions, but I want to know if they form a coherent package: (1) voting is fundamentally expressive — it communicates one's values to the polity rather than serving as an instrumental lever; (2) despite its causal inefficacy for the individual, there is a conditional pro tanto duty to vote in high-stakes elections, so long as the personal cost is minimal; (3) in a binary race where one candidate poses a genuine danger, one should vote for the lesser evil rather than abstain or vote for an unacceptable third option; (4) voting for the lesser evil does not make one morally complicit in that candidate's wrongs, because strategic voting lacks both causal contribution and genuine endorsement; (5) protest abstention is permissible only when the election outcome is not genuinely catastrophic; and (6) the decision should be modeled not as selecting a pure strategy but as assigning probabilities across one's acceptable options in a mixed-strategy framework, weighted by proximity to one's values. Do these hang together? Please audit the set: identify the genuine tensions (ordered by severity), point out any pairs that only seem to conflict but do not upon inspection, and specify what the cheapest repair is and what it costs — in particular, whether (6) can survive alongside (3) and (4) in a strictly binary race, or whether consistency requires dropping one of the others. State how confident you are in this audit, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
