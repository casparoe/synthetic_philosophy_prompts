I'm currently grading the final exams for a graduate seminar in social epistemology that I'm assisting with, and the prompt below was one of the long-form questions. The grading rubric emphasizes precision about the scope of conciliationist commitments. At the same time, I recently experienced a sustained disagreement with a colleague in an adjacent field—she works in empirical aesthetics, I work in philosophical aesthetics—about whether a failed replication of an emotional-response study should affect our shared theoretical framework. Reading these exam answers has made me acutely aware of how easily students, and perhaps I myself, slide between genuinely weighting peer opinion and merely splitting the difference.

Here is the exam question and one student's answer. I am grading this as an instructor or TA and do not wish to signal my own assessment in the request.

Question: Consider a case of peer disagreement in an interdisciplinary research group. Two collaborators—one a philosopher of science and one a cognitive psychologist—disagree about whether a recently published replication failure in concept-learning studies undermines a philosophical thesis about the poverty of the stimulus. Both have examined the same statistical reports and methodological critiques. The philosopher maintains that the replication failure is irrelevant because the philosophical thesis concerns normative competence, not descriptive performance; the psychologist maintains that the failure directly undermines the empirical premise on which the normative claim is based. They acknowledge each other as intellectual peers in the broader domain of cognitive science, though neither is an expert in the other's sub-discipline.

Explain the conciliationist position on peer disagreement and apply it to this case. Then present one serious challenge to conciliationism—specifically, the steadfastist reply that allows one to maintain belief in the face of peer disagreement—and evaluate whether that reply can avoid collapsing into mere dogmatism. Your answer should distinguish between disagreement about first-order beliefs and disagreement about how to weigh different kinds of evidence.

Student answer:
Conciliationism holds that when two epistemic peers disagree, both should revise their credences downward and adopt something close to the arithmetic mean of their prior positions. In the case described, since the philosopher and psychologist share the same evidence—the replication reports and critiques—they are peers about this specific claim. Therefore, both should reduce their confidence equally: the philosopher should lower confidence that the thesis survives, and the psychologist should lower confidence that it is undermined. The main challenge comes from steadfastness, which allows a party to maintain belief if they have independent first-personal access to the reasoning that produced it. The philosopher can remain steadfast because normative philosophy and empirical psychology operate with different evidential standards, so they are not truly peers on this particular issue; the psychologist's evidence does not reach the philosopher's domain. This avoids dogmatism because the philosopher is not ignoring the psychologist's view but is correctly judging that it falls outside the relevant peer group. Furthermore, conciliationism itself requires that peers share exactly identical bodies of evidence, which is satisfied here since both read the same reports, and steadfastness requires only that one have a meta-justification for trusting one's own reasoning process—a condition the conciliationist actually denies can ever be met without circularity.
