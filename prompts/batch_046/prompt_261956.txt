I've been chewing on Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle — you know, the Hamlet/Yorick scenario where Hamlet's brain gets copied and both bodies wake up claiming to be him. I want to defend a kind of hybrid view I'm calling embodied indexicalism: the idea that first-person reference is anchored not just to psychological continuity (like Parfit-style theories) nor just to the brain, but to a live sensorimoter loop between a specific brain-state and a specific body-state. So in the story, Hamlet-in-Yorick is briefly a real continuer, but once the original body also runs on the backup copy, neither has the privileged loop — they're both fragments, and the original Hamlet is genuinely dead, not split into two people. This saves the intuition that survival is a determinate fact without collapsing into pure brainism, but its anchored in a pretty specific claim about real-time integration.

What would actually count against this? I'm not asking for the standard fission objections or the usual psychological-continuity rebuttals. I want the defeaters that hit specifically at the loop idea: maybe results from split-brain patients, embodied-cognition experiments, or phenomenology of out-of-body experiences. Which ones are fatal, which are just awkward? And does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability — like saying 'well the loop was disrupted, so of course he died' — and is that insulation a problem for a metaphysical claim? Rank them for me, and say what a committed embodied-indexicalist would themselves concede is damaging rather than just what critics say.
