Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, with two refrains alternating through the tercets and meeting in the final couplet — in which the two refrains embody the etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, respectively. Let the first refrain assert that a trait's proper function is determined by the evolutionary history of selection that preserved it; let the second assert that a trait's proper function is determined by its current causal contribution to the organism's fitness. Choose one concrete biological trait — the heart's circulation, the crystalline lens's focusing, or the kidney's filtration — and let the poem's middle lines explore a case that strains both accounts together, such as a co-opted spandrel now performing a vital role without having been selected for it, or a malfunctioning organ that retains its causal effect despite breaking its historical mandate. The formal architecture should do conceptual work: because the refrains must return unchanged and finally stand side by side without merging, the poem's closure should illustrate that the two theories circle the same phenomenon yet remain conceptually adjacent rather than reconcilable. Write with enough technical precision — distinguishing selective history from present causal role, allowing for malfunction and exaptation — that a reader familiar with the philosophy of biology recognizes the stakes, but keep the language self-contained so that the poem requires no external references or citations.
