Lately I've been obsessed with eliminative materialism: the notion that beliefs and desires are going the way of phlogiston, and that neuroscience will eventually replace them with something cleaner. I can follow the broad stroke, but I find myself stuck on a practical question. If we really do scrap folk psychology, what exactly has to step in and do its job? I don't want a reading list — I'm working mostly offline and can't chase down obscure papers — but I do want the full specification of what any adequate replacement theory must deliver.

So here's what I'm after: a proper list of desiderata for whatever theory is meant to replace belief-desire psychology. For each item, tell me what case or consideration makes it necessary, and flag whether it's a hard constraint or just a preference of one camp. The theory should explain intentional behaviour — like reaching for an umbrella or cancelling a meeting — without treating those actions as caused by literal mental sentences. It must preserve distinctions folk psychology usually blurs, such as between being genuinely mistaken about the weather and deliberately misleading someone, or between a passing whim and a settled plan. It also has to sit comfortably with what we know about distributed neural processing — no hidden homunculi, please — and I'd especially like it to account for the predictive-error corrections we see in simple motor control, where your hand adjusts mid-reach because you misjudged a glass, but without framing that adjustment as the revision of a propositional attitude. I suspect some of these demands clash — maybe parsimony fights explanatory richness — so say if any subset looks jointly unsatisfiable. Keep the focus on the criteria rather than scoring existing views, though a brief word on how connectionist accounts or predictive-processing frameworks measure up wouldn't hurt.
