I'm taking a seminar on aesthetics and have been puzzling over an institutional asymmetry in how museums, markets, and conservation labs treat objects that are perceptually identical but differ in origin. Consider the standard practice: when a painting is revealed to be a skillful forgery—visually and materially indistinguishable from a lost original by the same master—it typically collapses in cultural and economic value, is sometimes destroyed, and if retained at all is relegated to study storage and insured only as raw canvas and pigment rather than as art. By contrast, a badly damaged but unquestionably authentic fragment of that same master's hand is preserved with extreme care, displayed with reverence, and valued far above any perfect replica. At the same time, museums now routinely present high-fidelity digital surrogates in virtual galleries and even in physical installations, without any institutional anxiety that these copies dilute the "aura" or value of the originals they reproduce. More recently, a few institutions have begun formalizing what might be called a "negative provenance" protocol: they catalog every object they reject as inauthentic, store the forgeries in climate-controlled conditions technically identical to those for authentic works, yet permanently exclude them from exhibition and from the category of "art" altogether. The result is a three-tier system—originals granted full status, hidden forgeries preserved as non-art, and freely circulating digital copies that leave original value untouched.

What philosophical commitments about authenticity, aesthetic value, and the ontology of art does this cluster of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require that value resides in a causal-historical relation to the artist, in a kind of "aura" that digital media cannot replicate but forgeries can parasitize, or in a social institution of recognition that draws lines arbitrarily? Say whether the practice is philosophically coherent, whether its parts actually presuppose consistent things—for instance, whether preserving forgeries with museum-grade conservation undermines the claim that they lack artistic value, or whether the unproblematic circulation of digital copies undermines the claim that causal history is decisive. And tell me what would have to change in museum, conservation, and market practice if we replaced the implicit view with one that treated aesthetic value as strictly a function of perceptible properties and interpretive experience, assigning no special weight to origin or to the social rituals of authentication.
