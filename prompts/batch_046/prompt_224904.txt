If one takes Buddhist ethics seriously as a resource for contemporary moral philosophy—without importing its full metaphysics of rebirth—several conceptual tensions with consequentialism become acute, especially regarding the aggregation of welfare in the absence of a persistent self (anatta). Suppose you have one year of research time and want to produce work that genuinely advances the debate rather than rehearsing familiar comparisons between karma and utility. Which of the following open lines should take priority: (a) whether anatta undermines the coherence of agent-neutral welfare aggregation altogether, or merely demands a non-personal metric; (b) whether Mahayana accounts of upaya (skillful means) can ground a form of moral reasoning that looks consequentialist but is structurally distinct from utility maximization; and (c) how Buddhist analyses of suffering (dukkha) and its cessation might reshape what counts as a good consequence in ways that standard consequentialist frameworks miss?

Rank them and defend the ordering. Specify the criteria you are using—tractability for analytic philosophy, potential to clarify other debates (for instance, moral status of artificial minds or preference aggregation), and what kind of conceptual progress in the next few years would cause you to reorder them. I am looking for a defended research agenda, not a literature review.
