Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar of 14 students. The goal is to make participants feel, through competitive prediction with tangible (but non-monetary) stakes, the practical tension between adopting Dennett’s intentional stance and adopting the design stance toward a conversational AI—specifically, to experience belief-attribution not as a metaphysical claim but as a predictive strategy that competes with architectural explanation, and that in certain edge cases the two stances diverge sharply.

Set up a hypothetical but concrete system for the game: a large language model fine-tuned for philosophical dialogue, equipped with a hidden “normative coherence layer” that suppresses outputs violating a specific logical consistency constraint—for instance, it rejects any answer that simultaneously affirms the moral relevance of sentience and denies that non-human animals can be sentient, yet applies this filter inconsistently across domains. Present students with transcripts of the system’s answers to three standard ethical thought experiments, then ask them to predict its answers to three novel, structurally analogous but unencountered cases.

Divide the class into two teams of seven: Team Intentional and Team Design. Team Intentional must predict outputs by attributing specific beliefs, reasoning dispositions, and desire-like states to the system (no architectural references permitted). Team Design must predict outputs solely by referencing the coherence-layer rules, the training distribution, statistical patterns, and filter mechanics—explicit mentalistic vocabulary is forbidden. Use a point system with no real money: each correct prediction earns 3 points; each incorrect prediction costs 1 point; teams may place one “double-or-nothing” confidence bet per round. The facilitator reveals pre-written “actual” outputs—consistent with the invented rules—after each round.

Structure the session precisely: 10 minutes for setup and rules; 30 minutes for Round 1 (three novel prediction cases with brief intra-team deliberation); 10 minutes for reveal and scoring; 20 minutes for Round 2, in which the teams switch frameworks (Team Intentional must now use design-stance reasoning, Team Design must now use belief-attribution) on two harder cases where the hidden filter behaves unpredictably; 10 minutes for final scoring and silent reflection; 10 minutes for debrief.

In the debrief, map moments in the game onto the intentional-stance framework: predictive success as the adoption criterion, the instrumental rather than metaphysical nature of the stance, the conditions under which mentalistic language fails and architectural explanation takes over, and the risk of “over-attribution” when belief-talk captures artifacts of the coherence layer rather than genuine cognitive structure. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the evolutionary and functional grounding of belief-desire psychology in biological agents with survival needs—and state clearly that the facilitator must supply this limitation verbally.

Also provide: a list of required materials (prediction cards, case descriptions, scoring sheet); the exact payoff rules; a minute-by-minute timeline; an honest prediction of what will go wrong on the first run (e.g., Team Intentional will smuggle design-stance reasoning into their belief-attributions, or students will conflate “predicting as if there are beliefs” with “the system literally has beliefs”) and how to correct it in real time; and a frank admission that the activity demonstrates predictive competition but does not resolve whether AI belief-attribution is ever literally true, only whether it is instrumentally useful in specific domains.

Write the response in formal, polished prose suitable for a dataset entry. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not depend on obscure theories outside standard philosophical and AI-literacy training. Keep the niche twist—the hidden coherence-layer rule—central to the scenario so the prompt is not a generic discussion of AI and mental states.
