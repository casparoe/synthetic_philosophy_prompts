In keeping with the rationalist habit of auditing a summary before accepting its conclusions, I want to check a characterisation I encountered in a philosophy-of-science discussion with a fellow student who is auditing a graduate course. She summarised Milton Friedman’s well-known 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics roughly as follows: the sole purpose of economic theory is predictive accuracy, which makes the descriptive realism of assumptions entirely irrelevant; indeed, the more unrealistic the assumptions, the better the theory, provided only that its predictions hold up. She added that this implies scientists ought actively to prefer false premises because they simplify computation, and that any model whose assumptions match observed behaviour is methodologically suspect, since it introduces unnecessary complexity. From this she derived a direct verdict on contemporary practice: climate integrated assessment models, which embed physically realistic carbon-cycle dynamics, are methodologically inferior to purely statistical black-box forecasting tools that treat emissions and temperature as unstructured correlations, because the latter rest on more unrealistic premises and therefore satisfy what she called the F-twist more rigorously.

I am inclined to think this misrepresents Friedman in several subtle ways—perhaps by conflating predictive success with explanatory irrelevance, or by turning a permissive claim about unrealistic assumptions into a prescriptive rule that realism is always a vice—but I lack the training to identify the precise distortions or to say whether the comparison with climate modelling actually follows. Would you analyse the summary with some care? Please go through the claims in order, distinguishing what a careful reader of Friedman would accept, what requires qualification or correction, and whether the argument about mechanistic climate models versus black-box statistical forecasts is licensed by anything he actually said. I would particularly value clarity on whether Friedman treats unrealistic assumptions as merely permissible or as positively desirable, and on whether his thesis is meant to apply to economics specifically or to scientific modelling generally. Answer in British English, please, and keep the tone formal and precise.
