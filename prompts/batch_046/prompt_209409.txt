I'm presenting my seminar paper next Tuesday in our graduate colloquium on nationalism and collective memory—mostly political theorists and modern European historians, plus one post-Soviet specialist who is skeptical of applying Western theory to Baltic cases, two peers who have published on Baltic memory politics and will push on empirical framing, a strict constructivist who thinks all continuity claims are equally fictive, and a comparative historian who suspects I'm over-theorizing ordinary state-building. My thesis is that Hobsbawm's invented traditions and Anderson's imagined communities are usually treated as parallel 19th-century accounts, but they should be read as structurally coupled: invented traditions supply the institutional substrate that lets an imagined community achieve temporal continuity. I argue this coupling produces a distinct mode I call retroactive continuity protocols—state practices (archival display, legal restoration clauses, museum curation) that suppress discontinuities rather than invent new rituals—and that post-1991 Baltic state-building exemplifies this, using display-based continuity rather than print-capitalism or ritual invention. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased as each audience type would phrase them: for each, note whether it has real force, what I should concede up front, where my reply is weakest, and what I should hold firm. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody raises—whether retroactive continuity protocols are actually distinguishable from Hobsbawm's invented traditions, since my defense depends on separating display-based suppression of discontinuity from ritual-based invention, and I'm not fully sure that distinction survives pressure. Keep it concise; the presentation is twenty minutes with fifteen for questions, and I need a prep sheet, not a full defense.
