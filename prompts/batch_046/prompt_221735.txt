I'm a phd student in bioethics and I've been going in circles about brain death versus circulatory death for a semenar paper. I came up with this argument that feels like it collapses everything, but I can't see the leak.

(1) If it's ethically permissible to harvest vital organs from a patient, that patient must be dead; killing a living person for organs is murder.
(2) Brain-dead patients are routinely used as organ donors, so medical practice treats them as dead.
(3) But brain-dead bodies still maintain homeostasis, heal wounds, grow hair, and in documented cases can gestate a fetus for months with only mechanical ventilation.
(4) So the biological organism is clearly still functioning as an integrated whole; it's alive in every biological sense except consciousness.
(5) Therefore if brain-dead people are dead, "death" can't mean the cessation of biological life; it must mean the irreversible loss of consciousness or personhood.
(6) But patients in a permanent vegetative state or with end-stage Alzheimer's have also irreversibly lost consciousness and personhood, yet we don't classify them as dead and we don't harvest there organs.
(7) There's no non-arbitrary line between brain death and PVS regarding consciousness—both lack awareness, both depend on others for basic needs, and both are irreversible in practice.
(8) So if we accept (5), we have to say PVS patients are dead too, which is clearly wrong.
(9) Therefore brain death isn't real death, and (2) is false—organ retrieval from brain-dead donors is killing living organisms.
(10) But that would make current transplant practice mass murder, which is absurd.
(11) So the argument seems to show that our concept of death is incoherent: either we expand death to cover PVS and dementia (too broad) or we deny brain death and condemn transplant medicine (too narrow).

Where is the flaw? I really want to keep (1) and (3)—they seem rock solid. Is there an equivocation between "the organism is alive" and "the person is alive"? Or is the problem in (7), that there's actually a neurological difference I'm glossing over—maybe something about the brainstem being the critical integrator? If I have to pay somewhere, I'd rather weaken (5) than (1), but I want to know what the standard positions—biological integrationists vs. higher-brain theorists—each have to pay to reject whichever step is bad. And please don't just say "it depends on your theory of personal identity"; I want to know which specific move in the chain breaks.
