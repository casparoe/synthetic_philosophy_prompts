I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. At a recent off-site, two colleagues—one in policy, one in product design—got into a sustained disagreement about whether conflicts between genuinely different moral value systems can ever produce real "gains from trade" through compromise. They asked me to referee point by point, and I honestly could not tell who was making the stronger move. Below is my best reconstruction of their exchange.

Maya (policy) began with an engineering analogy. She noted that when two technical teams have incompatible priorities—say, one optimizes for latency, the other for cryptographic rigor—negotiated trade-offs often yield architectures that neither side would have designed alone, yet both prefer to the winner-take-all alternatives. She proposed extending this logic to moral values: if Framework A treats individual privacy as paramount and Framework B treats institutional transparency as paramount, a compromise that requires transparent auditing alongside strong anonymization might allow both frameworks to realize more of their core concerns than either would achieve under the pure dominance of the other. She called this a situation of moral gains-from-trade, arguing that compromise is not dilution but a Pareto improvement across value systems.

Leo (product design) replied that the analogy collapses because latency and cryptography are both instrumental values serving a shared product goal, whereas privacy and transparency in Maya's example are frequently treated as deontological constraints—rights that should not be traded away regardless of aggregate outcomes. He argued that labeling a compromise a "gain" secretly assumes a meta-framework, likely consequentialist, that permits quantifying and exchanging moral goods. From within a rights-based framework, conceding ground on privacy is not gaining transparency-value; it is violating a constraint. So what looks like trade is actually one framework colonizing the other by forcing it to speak in consequentialist terms.

Maya answered that Leo was assuming values carry absolute, non-negotiable rankings, but real ethical reasoning is always comparative. Even strict deontologists make exceptions—lying to save a life, breaking a promise to prevent catastrophe—so values are already weighed internally. The compromise she described does not require either framework to abandon its internal ranking; it only requires each to accept less than full satisfaction of its highest value in exchange for partial satisfaction of another value it also recognizes, even if secondarily. She insisted this is structurally identical to economic trade, not conceptual colonization.

Leo conceded internal weighing but denied it supports her case. He said internal weighing occurs within a single normative system that already accepts all the values being balanced—medical ethics weighs autonomy against beneficence because both belong to the same practice. Cross-framework compromise, by contrast, involves values that one system does not recognize as legitimate at all, or recognizes only under conditions the other rejects. Without a shared normative language, he argued, there is no common currency for trade, only strategic bargaining. The "gain" Maya identifies is therefore an illusion projected by an observer who implicitly adopts one framework—probably a liberal-pluralist one—as the neutral measuring stick.

They turned to me and asked for a point-by-point adjudication. Is Maya's engineering-to-morality analogy sound? Does Leo correctly identify a hidden meta-framework in her reasoning? Is internal weighing genuinely different from cross-framework compromise in the way Leo claims? And overall, is there any coherent sense in which moral compromise between genuinely distinct value systems can yield gains from trade, or is the idea conceptually confused? Please explain who is right about each specific move, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
