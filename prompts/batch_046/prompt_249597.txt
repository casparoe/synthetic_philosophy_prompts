Here is my position on non-human forces in history, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. I hold:

(1) Pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic systems are not mere background conditions but genuine historical actors whose causal efficacy can rival human institutions in explaining large-scale change.

(2) The term "actor" here is strictly non-agential: attributing intentionality or goals to microbes or magma is a category error, and historiography should grant them narrative weight only as causal forces.

(3) Environmental determinism is false in principle—every effect of climate or disease on human societies is mediated by social structures, technologies, and political choices—so there are no "pure" environmental causes.

(4) Despite (3), in specific episodes—such as the abandonment of certain Norse settlements or the demographic collapse of particular premodern polities—the best explanation appeals primarily to volcanic winter or epidemic dynamics, with human institutional responses playing only a secondary, reactive role.

(5) The chief peril of environmental determinism is therefore not explanatory overreach but moral excuse: it lets human agents off the hook for suffering that was actually mediated by their own institutional choices.

Please trace which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real tensions by severity; for each, specify the cheapest revision and what it costs. Then identify the single change that would clean up the most conflicts at once. In particular, say whether (2) genuinely saves (1) from clashing with (3), and whether (3) and (4) can stand together without collapsing into determinism after all.
