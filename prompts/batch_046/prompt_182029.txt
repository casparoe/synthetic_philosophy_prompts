ok i'm trying to figure out my actual stance on remembering vs forgetting, partly cause i keep running into wierd edge cases. here's my gut on a bunch of scenarios, and i can't tell if there's one principel behind them or if i'm just inconsistent.

first, societies that went through mass atrocity should keep public memory alive — memorials, school curriculum, the works. that feels like a duty, not just an option. but if you're an individual survivor and you want to stop talking about it, burn your photos, live like it never happened, that's your right. no one can demand you carry it.

second, personal grievances. if someone betrayed me badly a decade ago and i still think about it daily, i feel like i should try to forget — not out of weakness, but because holding on is doing damage. so there's almost a duty to let go of private wrongs once they stop serving anything.

third, historical wrongs with no living victims or perpetrators. like, a town that was burned in 1843 and rebuilt. i don't think current residents have any special obligaton to remember that specifically. but then there's founding violence of a nation — say, a revolution that involved mass executions that shaped the legal system. even though everyone involved is dead, i still think there's a duty to remember that, not just as history but as acknowledgment of what built the present.

now here's the pair that bugs me. i think a family should be allowed to forget a personal injustice after a generation or two — like being cheated out of land — because the debt is dead. but i also think society should never forget collective injustice like slavery or genocide, even after centuries. what's the difference? scale? institutional legacy? but then why does the factory-collapse town (no institutional legacy) feel forgettable, while the founding violence (huge institutional legacy) doesn't?

i also made up this concept, maybe useless: mnemonic orphans — people or events with no descendants, no institutional heirs, no living claimant, but we know they existed. like a small sect wiped out with no writings, only census traces. do we have a duty to remember them specifically, or is archival existence enough? i keep flipping. sometimes yes, there's justice in not letting them vanish. other times no, memory needs a living link, else it's nostalgia for strangers.

and i keep going back and forth on this case: if a dying person asks about an estranged child who never called, and lying would comfort them, is there a kind of memory duty that requires telling the truth even if it hurts? or is allowing a false, comforting memory okay?

anyway, is there a coherent principle — maybe about institutional legacy, living claimants, or harm — that captures all this? which of my verdicts does it have to drop? and what does it say about the mnemonic orphan case?
