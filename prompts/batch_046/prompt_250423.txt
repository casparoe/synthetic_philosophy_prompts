I'm a retired physicist who got into philosophy of economics after retirement, mostly because I used to deal with distributed sensor networks and suddenly the socialist calculation debate looked eerily familiar. I was discussing with a friend who's into political philosophy and we got stuck on the Hayek/Mises vs. Lange argument — specifically whether Lange's trial-and-error pricing model actually answers Mises' 1920 challenge or just changes the subject.

Here's where I think the stall comes from. Mises and Hayek seem to say that rational economic caluclation requires real market prices formed by actual exchange, because knowledge is dispersed and partly tacit — you can't centralize what people don't even know they know. Lange, and later market-socialist economists, reply that a planning board can iteratively adjust prices using supply-and-demand equations until they clear, so calculation is technically possible without private ownership of capital. But then Austrians come back and say that misses entrepreneurship, discovery, and dynamic adjustment. Meanwhile, people like me notice that modern distributed computing can solve massive optimization problems in real time, which makes Lange's model seem more plausible technically, yet we still can't run an economy like a neural network. So the literature just loops.

My hunch is that this isn't really a factual disagreement about computation — it's about what kind of thing economic knowledge is. Is it information that can be aggregated, or is it embodied practice that only emerges through competitive exchange? But I'm not sure if that's right, or if there's a hidden formal question about convergence of iterative algorithms under radical uncertainty that neither side has pinned down. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal (they use 'calculation' differently), substantive about epistemology, or bedrock intuitions about freedom and markets? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical case study, or should we just accept the impasse? Please don't just pick a side.
