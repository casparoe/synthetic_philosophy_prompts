I had a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about escalation dynamics and the dollar auction, and I want to check whether my position actually holds together before I commit it to writing for a seminar. Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) The dollar auction is not primarily an illustration of individual irrationality; each incremental bid is locally rational given the current payoff structure, and the error—if there is one—lies in the failure to anticipate the escalation path at the entry stage, not in the bids themselves.

(2) Because of this, the sunk-cost fallacy is largely a misdiagnosis for what drives escalation in both the auction and in analogous real-world cases; the mechanism is strategic interaction within a specific payoff structure, not a cognitive bias about honoring past expenditures.

(3) In real-world contexts—organizational project continuation, geopolitical commitment, or even interpersonal disputes—the appropriate normative evaluation should be incremental: we ought to ask whether each further commitment is justified by correctly updated beliefs about future payoffs, not whether the agent should have withdrawn earlier.

(4) At the same time, I think a fully rational agent should adopt ex ante hard caps or credible precommitments specifically designed to block entry into auction-like dynamics, even if that means constraining actions that would be locally rational once inside the trap.

(5) The only stable resolution for fully rational agents facing this structure is common knowledge that neither party will escalate past the value of the prize; without that common knowledge, entry can be strategically rational if one expects the other to be either irrational or lacking anticipatory capacity.

I recognize that (1) and (4) might look like they conflict—if local steps are rational, why constrain them?—but I think that tension dissolves because the precommitment targets entry and the path, not the rationality of individual moves inside the path. What I am less sure about is whether (3) and (5) pull against each other: (5) makes the strategic failure one of entry and anticipation, while (3) directs normative attention to incremental updates in real-world cases where common knowledge rarely holds. There may also be a hidden tension between (2) and (4) that I am not seeing clearly—if sunk costs are genuinely irrelevant, is my defense of hard caps still well-motivated, or does it rest on a different psychological premise?
