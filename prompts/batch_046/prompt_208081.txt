I am running a 75-minute postgraduate seminar in moral philosophy with fourteen participants, and I want a structured, self-contained classroom game — with real, if minor, stakes and concealed roles — that lets the group experience the tension at the heart of prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection before anyone names it. Each student should receive a hidden card assigning them a baseline across three discrete dimensions — bodily health, autonomy of choice, and access to leisure — rather than a single cardinal utility score, so that the problem of interpersonal aggregation is tangible from the start. A common pool of points, convertible at the end into a shared reward (for instance, exemption from a brief administrative task or a collective refreshment budget), is then subjected to three successive redistribution mechanisms across two rounds with different pool sizes: a pure prioritarian weighting that assigns greater marginal value to gains for those with lower baselines; an egalitarian cap that trims surplus from the highest baselines without transferring it downward; and a levelling-down rule that collapses all baselines to the mean of the worst-off quartile, destroying surplus but producing strict equality. The rules and incentives must be arranged so that participants discover for themselves — particularly when the pool shrinks between rounds — that the prioritarian mechanism can leave the worst-off with no improvement while protecting the best-off, and that the egalitarian option makes every participant worse off in absolute terms than a state of moderate inequality; only after these reactions have surfaced should the facilitator map them onto the priority view and its critics.

The debrief should be explicit about which moments in the simulation correspond to which theoretical commitments — for example, the reluctance to destroy surplus as resistance to the levelling-down intuition, or the frustration at hidden baseline differences as an encounter with the non-comparability of multidimensional well-being — and should predict where the activity will misfire: participants will likely conflate prioritarian weighting with maximin, or treat the three dimensions as separately compensable rather than as an incommensurable bundle. The facilitator must therefore supply, verbally and without written citation, the feature that the game cannot reproduce: prioritarianism's explicit denial that equality has any intrinsic moral value apart from its effect on individual welfare. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; the prompt should stand on its own, and it should not invoke highly specialised formal apparatus — such as social welfare functions with continuous representations — that a small language model could not be expected to have internalised. Write the deliverable in clear, formal British English, specifying the timeline, the concealed information, the payoff structure, and the honest limitation of the exercise.
