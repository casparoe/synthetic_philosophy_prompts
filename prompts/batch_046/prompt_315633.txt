I am facing a choice between two mutually exclusive five-year commitments: a residency in Kyoto that would transform my practice from archival philosophy into public-facing translation, and a tenure-track position in Copenhagen that preserves my scholarly trajectory but requires dissolving my current cohabiting partnership. To cope with the paralysis, I have begun drafting unsent letters addressed to the self who takes the path I reject—not as fiction, but as correspondence with a real person who shares my origin but diverges. I notice that I assign this other figure not merely different circumstances, but different character traits: the Kyoto-version strikes me as more open to contingency, while the Copenhagen-version seems defined by institutional loyalty. One interlocutor insists that since only one path will be actualized, the other is merely hypothetical and carries no moral weight; another argues that if the divergence point is late enough and the psychological continuity strong enough, then the rejected path contains a genuine continuation of me, such that choosing Copenhagen is not just forgoing a life but suppressing a co-equal self.

I want to understand whether my intuition of guilt—that I am harming a real person by closing off the Kyoto branch—rests on a coherent metaphysical picture, or whether it collapses once we distinguish between being the same person across possible worlds and being a counterpart who is similar but numerically distinct. Specifically, if the Kyoto-branch and I share an identical mental origin up to the moment of decision, does that make her me in the same way that tomorrow's me is still me, only with different properties? Or does actualization create a hard boundary, so that she is at best a close analog—someone I ought not to confuse with my own future, but not someone whose existence I have a duty to preserve? I am less interested in practical career advice than in whether the structure of my worry reveals something about identity and modality: does the fact that I can coherently address her in the second person, and feel that she possesses values I do not, support the view that she is a distinct individual, or does it instead show that personal identity is loose enough to allow for branching without splitting?

How should I think about the relationship between the actual me and the unactualized branch, and does that relationship generate any genuine obligation not to "eliminate" her? If she is not me but only my counterpart, is my guilt a category mistake, or can there still be a moral reason to regret suppressing a life that is maximally similar to mine? And if she is me, does that imply I have contradictory obligations—to realize both the Kyoto and Copenhagen lives—or does it rather show that the concept of "the same person" cannot stretch across incompatible actualizations without breaking? I would like a careful analysis that addresses whether my framing makes sense, what distinctions need to be drawn to evaluate it, and where the argument for or against treating the rejected branch as a suppressed self ultimately stands or falls.
