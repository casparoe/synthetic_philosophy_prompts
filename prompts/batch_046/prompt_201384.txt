I am a doctoral student in philosophy of technology studying how institutional evaluation practices embed implicit theories of agency. In current AI development, several leading safety frameworks supervise large-scale systems through purely outcome-based signals—benchmark performance, automated reward modeling, and human preference judgments of final outputs—while systematically excluding process-level inspection of intermediate reasoning traces, internal feature attributions, or chain-of-thought representations. Curiously, these same institutions routinely require process-based transparency and step-wise justification for high-stakes human decisions in domains such as clinical medicine and criminal adjudication. What philosophical commitments about moral responsibility, epistemic access, and the ontology of artificial agents does this bifurcated supervisory regime presuppose? Does it tacitly endorse a form of methodological behaviorism restricted to non-human systems, or can it be sustained by a principled distinction between the kinds of accountability appropriate to artificial versus natural agents? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and say whether the regime is philosophically consistent with the transparency norms it imposes on human institutions—or whether that consistency can only be restored by abandoning either the outcome-only approach to AI or the process-based requirements for human decision-makers.
