I've been discussing with colleagues in a philosophy of law seminar how modern regulatory bodies treat rapidly evolving technical standards—think of carbon-accounting methodologies being finalized by private consortia, or AI safety benchmarking protocols that are revised quarterly—as if they already carry binding normative weight. Agencies cite these half-formed frameworks in enforcement guidance, grant them deference in licensing decisions, and penalize non-adherence even though adoption remains patchy, no clear coordination problem in the classical sense has been solved, and the standards themselves are still being rewritten by the very bodies that propose them. This strikes me as philosophically puzzling: what conception of convention and norm-evolution must such an institutional practice presuppose?

Specifically, does treating these emergent norms as authoritative require abandoning a strict Lewisian picture—where conventions are arbitrary equilibria sustained by common knowledge, mutual expectation, and settled precedent—in favor of something like an evolutionary-epistemic view, according to which norms gain legitimacy through iterative refinement and functional success rather than through fully established mutual coordination? Or can a Lewisian framework be stretched to accommodate pre-conventional norms by treating regulatory deference itself as the coordinating mechanism that bootstraps the equilibrium? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of convention embedded in this practice, carefully distinguishing what it strictly requires—for instance, whether it demands that conventions need not be fully arbitrary, or that they need not be common knowledge among all affected parties—from what it merely happens to accommodate, and to say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with both a modified Lewisian account and a richer evolutionary or normative-realist theory—or whether it quietly rules out one of them.
