Examination question:
Daniel Dennett's "intentional stance" permits treating a system as an agent with beliefs and desires whenever doing so yields reliable predictive success. Some contemporary philosophers argue that large language models merit this treatment because their coherent, context-sensitive outputs justify predictive belief-attribution. Critics reply that the stance is only legitimate for systems embedded in evolutionary or social practices that ground the normative weight of mental-state ascription. Evaluate whether the intentional stance can be coherently applied to contemporary AI systems, and clarify whether adopting it commits one to metaphysical realism about machine mental states or remains a purely instrumental predictive strategy.

Student answer:
Dennett's intentional stance is fundamentally a methodological heuristic: we attribute rationality and mental states to a system if that strategy improves our predictions of its behavior. Because Dennett applies the stance to artifacts like thermostats and chess-playing computers, evolutionary history or social embedding is clearly not a prerequisite. The critics who claim that AI lacks the biological or communal background necessary for belief-attribution therefore misunderstand Dennett's framework. Given that large language models produce contextually appropriate, goal-directed linguistic behavior, extending the stance to them is fully justified.

That said, Dennett is best understood as an instrumentalist about mental states: he regards beliefs and desires as useful fictions rather than as objectively real patterns. Consequently, applying the intentional stance to an LLM does not entail any metaphysical commitment to the reality of its mental life. At the same time, once we attribute beliefs to a system, we necessarily treat it as a moral agent bearing responsibility for its outputs. This normative consequence means that adopting the stance toward AI would require granting such systems rights and accountability. For this reason, while the stance is predictively appropriate, its normative implications make it unsuitable for current AI unless we are prepared to accept those moral commitments.
