I have been attempting to work out what I actually think about the norms of assertion, spurred by a seminar discussion that kept circling back to whether knowing that p is required, whether sincerely believing it is enough, or whether getting the truth right is what ultimately matters. Rather than defending a theory, I should like to test my own intuitions against concrete cases and see whether they point to a single principle, or whether I must sacrifice one judgment for the sake of coherence.

Here are my verdicts. When a meteorologist asserts that a storm will strike tomorrow, having personally analysed the models and knowing the prediction to be correct, her assertion is clearly in order. When a student asserts that Waterloo was fought in 1815, having learned it from a reliable textbook without any direct historical expertise, I still regard the assertion as permissible, though I am unsure whether he truly knows it or merely believes it well. When a clinician states that a treatment has a ninety per cent success rate, relying on a trial she has read but not herself scrutinised, I think her assertion is legitimate, but it seems to involve a kind of delegated epistemic authority rather than first-person knowledge. When a politician repeats a correct unemployment figure from a report he has not read and does not understand, asserting it purely for rhetorical advantage with no genuine belief, I judge him to have violated a norm despite the truth of his claim. When a friend asserts that a meeting is cancelled, having misread an automated notification, sincerely believing her error, I think she is blameworthy in a way that exceeds mere falsehood — she ought to have verified. More puzzling is what I call epistemic escrow: a barrister asserts in court that a contract is valid, not on the basis of her own reasoning, but because she has delegated judgment to a specialist partner whose analysis she trusts but cannot reproduce; the claim is true, she stands behind it professionally, yet she lacks both direct belief and direct knowledge. I am inclined to regard this as exemplary rather than defective. By contrast, a journalist publishes an allegation based on one anonymous source, believing it is probably true but admitting privately that she does not know it; the claim proves false, and I feel she has acted wrongly, though I cannot say whether the fault lies in lacking belief, lacking knowledge, or lacking adequate justification.

Is there a coherent account — perhaps one that distinguishes direct cognition from institutional delegation, or that separates the belief norm from a more complex knowledge-plus-role condition — that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what precisely does your proposed principle say about the barrister in epistemic escrow and about the friend who misread the notification? Please also tell me what your account predicts for a case I have not considered: a professional translator asserts that an ancient text means X, relying entirely on an opaque algorithmic translation system she cannot evaluate herself, vouching for the result sincerely in her official capacity, and the translation is in fact accurate.
