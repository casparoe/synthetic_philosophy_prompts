I'm trying to work out the actual norm that governs appropriate assertion, not by consulting theories but by testing my gut reactions across concrete cases and seeing whether they cohere.

Here are my verdicts. A meteorologist tells the public, "There will be a severe storm tomorrow," on the basis of an ensemble model she trusts but has not personally built or run; the storm arrives. This feels appropriate. A job applicant tells an interviewer, "I managed a team of twelve," when in fact she managed three but sincerely rounds up in her own mind because she felt responsible for broader operations. This feels inappropriate. A physician tells a patient, "This treatment will work," knowing the drug succeeds in about seventy percent of cases but having no specific reason to place this patient in that group; the patient takes the statement as a personal guarantee. This feels inappropriate. A friend tells you, "The meeting was moved to Thursday," because she misread an email; she sincerely believes it and is wrong. This feels inappropriate, though less severely wrong than the applicant's exaggeration. A person at a funeral tells a mourner, "Your father is at peace now," without any belief in an afterlife and with no evidence, purely to offer comfort. This feels appropriate, despite the absence of knowledge or even belief in the literal content. A historian writes in a standard textbook, "The treaty was signed in 1713," relying entirely on well-established secondary scholarship and never having checked the archival originals herself. This feels appropriate.

Now the tension. A physicist asserts at a conference, "Neutrinos oscillate between flavors," on the basis of experimental literature she understands conceptually but has never personally reproduced; within her field the claim is treated as settled, though it rests on indirect detection and statistical inference. This feels appropriate. But a philosopher asserts in the same kind of setting, "Moral realism is false," on the basis of her own conceptual analysis, knowing that equally trained philosophers reject the argument and that no empirical resolution is available. She is sincere and has reasoned carefully. This feels inappropriate, even though both speakers are domain experts making claims that lie at the edge of direct verification and that face significant peer disagreement.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving knowledge, justified belief, sincerity, audience-relative epistemic position, or the social function of the speech act—that captures all of these, including why the funeral consolation and the historian's textbook claim are acceptable despite lacking firsthand verification? What does that principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a financial advisor tells a client, "This portfolio will outperform the market over a ten-year horizon," citing back-tested models he sincerely accepts; he also knows that past performance carries no guarantee and that he earns a commission regardless of the outcome. Does the presence of a conflict of interest, or the statistical nature of the claim, or the client's reasonable expectation of personalized assurance, change the verdict?

If no unified principle saves every reaction, which verdict should I abandon to obtain the cleanest account? In particular, must I treat the physicist and the philosopher differently, or is there a principled reason—related to disciplinary norms, the kind of disagreement involved, or the speaker's epistemic authority—that explains why one expert assertion feels right and the other wrong?
