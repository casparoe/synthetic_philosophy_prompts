In my philosophy-of-physics discussion group, a debate about Gödel's rotating-universe metric has reached a frustrating standstill. One camp—call them the Global Eliminativists—argues that because the Gödel solution to Einstein's field equations admits closed timelike curves and lacks any global time coordinate, it demonstrates that temporal ordering is not intrinsic to spacetime; they take this as mathematical vindication of an idealist view of time, along the lines Gödel himself reportedly favored, in which local "before" and "after" are projections rather than fundamental features of the world. The opposing camp—the Local Structuralists—accepts every formal feature of the metric but insists it only relativizes synchronization, not reality: local causal structure remains intact, closed curves are topological curiosities with no empirical presence in our apparently non-rotating universe, and to leap from "no global foliation" to "time is unreal" is a category mistake that confuses geometry with ontology. Both sides agree on the mathematics, both concede that observational cosmology gives no sign of the required global rotation, and both have read the standard expositions; yet for three years neither has conceded a point.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really about physics at all, but about a hidden asymmetry in metaphysical explanation—whether local temporal experience grounds the spacetime structure or the structure grounds experience—disguised as a dispute over what the Gödel metric "shows."
