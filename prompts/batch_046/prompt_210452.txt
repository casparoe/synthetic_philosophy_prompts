At a recent seminar on applied ethics, two attendees, Maya and Jordan, debated which omnivorous choice—beef or chicken—causes less harm when one accounts for both farmed and wild animal suffering. Maya argued that if one's goal is to minimize the number of sentient lives ended, beef is the less harmful option: a single steer yields hundreds of servings, whereas obtaining an equivalent amount of protein from chickens requires killing dozens of individual birds, each of which spends its brief life in conditions of intense confinement. She added that this comparison holds even when one considers feed crops, because the relevant metric is direct intentional killing, not indirect ecological side-effects.

Jordan responded that this framing arbitrarily excludes wild animals killed during crop cultivation. Because beef has a far worse feed-conversion ratio than chicken, producing one calorie of beef requires substantially more acreage of feed crops—soy and corn—whose planting and harvesting kill field mice, rabbits, insects, and other wild creatures through machinery, pesticides, and habitat destruction. Therefore, he claimed, beef consumption generates a larger hidden toll of wild animal deaths per calorie, and the moral comparison should include these indirect casualties. He further noted that chickens, despite being numerous, live for only weeks, so the cumulative duration of suffering per calorie may actually be lower than that of a cow living for years.

Maya countered that Jordan's crop-death argument, if taken seriously, undermines not just beef but nearly all diets, including plant-based ones that also rely on monoculture agriculture. She said the comparison should therefore be internal to omnivory: since beef can be raised on grassland unsuited for crops, a meaningful portion of beef production avoids feed-crop deaths altogether, whereas chicken is almost entirely grain-dependent. She also questioned whether wild animal deaths should be weighted equally with the prolonged, intentional suffering of farmed animals.

Jordan replied that appealing to grass-fed beef is empirically marginal—most beef is finished on grain—and that Maya was now introducing an unmeasured qualitative distinction between wild and farmed suffering without justification. He insisted that if indirect deaths do not count, then the original comparison should be honest about ignoring them, rather than selectively invoking them when convenient.

They asked me to referee their exchange. Please explain, point by point, where each speaker is correct and where they err or overreach. Then say who is more right overall about the comparative ethics of beef versus chicken consumption under a suffering-minimization framework, and identify what important consideration both are neglecting.
