I've been thinking a lot about where creativity actually comes from, especially after playing with some image-generation tools and noticing how they seem to remix rather than invent. I think I've landed on something close to Douglas Hofstadter's notion of knob-twiddling — taking a core theme and varying its parameters — but I've pushed it in directions that feel right to me, and I want to know if the package actually holds together.

Here is where I stand:

(1) Real creativity is almost never ex nihilo; it is always variations on a theme, where the theme can be extremely abstract — not a melody or a plot, but something like the feeling of a familiar object becoming strange.

(2) The key skill in being creative is not having ideas but identifying which dimensions, or knobs, are the right ones to twist for that particular theme. If you twist the wrong knobs, you get noise, not art.

(3) Even works that seem to break their own theme — say, a painting that suddenly abandons its color palette — are still variations, because the break itself is just another knob turned to maximum. There is no true outside to the theme.

(4) At the same time, I think the audience does not need to grasp the abstract theme or the knobs consciously. A viewer can feel that a variation works through pure intuition, without being able to articulate what stayed constant.

(5) I also think that current AI systems can be genuinely creative in this framework, because they are excellent at identifying statistical regularities — which, in my view, is essentially identifying the theme's knobs — and then varying them. They do not need human-like understanding; they just need to map the parameter space.

(6) But I also believe that if an AI never surprises itself — if it only ever outputs variations that are predictable from its training distribution — then it is not really twiddling knobs creatively, but just replaying a fixed setting. True creativity requires that at least one variation be genuinely unexpected to the system itself.
