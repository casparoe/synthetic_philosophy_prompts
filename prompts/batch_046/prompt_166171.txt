You serve as acquisitions curator for a regional social-history museum. Every month you receive donated artifacts—garments, tools, ledgers, household items—whose provenance is partially documented, often through institutional records that obscure everyday use. You need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered flowchart in words with yes/no/unclear branches, to classify each artifact for interpretation: does it belong in a microhistorical frame (recovering ordinary, non-elite experience through exceptional but typical traces), does it necessarily belong to an elite or state narrative, or do the two frames irreducibly overlap?

Your procedure must operationalize three distinctions. First, the microhistorical priority of recovering lives that institutional archives tend to flatten. Second, an object-biography approach that tracks successive uses, repairs, and ownership transfers rather than treating the artifact as a static product of its origin. Third, the problem of silent provenance gaps: cases where documentation survives only because an elite institution preserved it, even though material evidence—wear patterns, hidden modifications, oral testimony—points to sustained everyday, non-elite function. At each step, specify what evidence is sufficient, where the procedure yields a definite classification, where it must hand the decision to curatorial judgment because the issue is normative rather than factual, and where it is prone to misfire—particularly when thick elite documentation drowns out recoverable everyday use.

Apply the procedure to three donated items:

- A mass-produced mill-worker’s smock, donated within a factory owner’s archive; the archive lists it only as a uniform specimen, but the fabric shows heavy personal repair and concealed pocket stitching.
- An 1892 county tax ledger, donated by the clerk’s family; its margins contain handwritten recipes and local-dialect debt notations, yet the volume is bound and catalogued as pure bureaucratic record.
- A ceremonial presentation sword with complete state gift-registry provenance but no record of the local forge; material analysis reveals extensive local repair and an unnamed artisan’s hilt modification.

One application should produce a classification you, in your curatorial role, would prefer to resist. Conclude by stating exactly what your procedure cannot decide, and describe the kind of artifact that would break it entirely. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or named theoretical texts; the procedure should stand on the distinctions given above.
