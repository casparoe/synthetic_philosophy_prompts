I'm a backend engineer who works in disributed systems and has exactly zero formal philosophy credits, but I occassionally fall down wikipedia rabbit holes at 2am. Lately I've been stuck on Aristotle's distinction between oikonomia - natural household management where you exchange wheat for shoes because you actually need shoes - and chrematistike, the unnatural art of accumulating coins for their own sake. He apparently hated interest especially, calling it tokos because money is barren and shouldn't breed offspring like a living thing.

Anyway, I want to apply this to the actual 2008 mortgage crisis, not in the lazy bankers-were-greedy sense but as a real conceptual map. So take the different actors - the mortgage originators, the ratings agencies, the banks packaging CDOs, and the institutional buyers - and say which parts of their behavior fit Aristotle's natural exchange category and which are pure chrematistike. But here's the niche twist I'm adding: in September 2008 there were massive automated fire-sales executed by algorithms that weren't even human agents managing households - they were just velocity-maximizing loops generating profit from spread without any oikos behind them. Does Aristotle's binary have any bucket for non-human agents that produce tokos without natural purpose? Or does the presence of automated, purpose-less accumulation break his framework? I want the analysis to run both directions - use Aristotle to illuminate the crisis, but use the crisis, especially the algorithmic part, to show exactly where his natural versus unnatural distinction gets silent, breaks, or needs updating.
