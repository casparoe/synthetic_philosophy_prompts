I serve on the acquisitions committee for a mid-sized museum of modern art, and our registrar needs a reusable decision procedure—ordered like a flowchart in words, runnable in about twenty minutes per object—to determine how to catalog, insure, and display works that fall between clear originals and clear forgeries. The objects we face are not obvious counterfeits but gray-zone cases: a painting whose original paint layer survives only in scattered islands beneath extensive conservator overpainting; an estate-authorized bronze cast produced decades after the sculptor's death from original molds; and a high-fidelity digital facsimile that reproduces every visible feature of a fragile original too light-sensitive to exhibit. I want the procedure grounded in what philosophy of art actually knows about the value of originals, but translated so that a non-philosopher can apply it using only documentation, material analysis reports, and curatorial comparison. Specifically, it should operationalize three values that can come apart: maker-causation value (being physically caused by the artist's intentional action), provenance-continuity value (having actually traveled through the documented historical trajectory attributed to the work), and aesthetic-functional equivalence (delivering the perceptual and interpretive experience the artist designed). For each value, provide a practical test, a rule for conflict resolution, and explicit flagging of where judgment cannot be eliminated. Include worked applications to the three cases above, with one yielding a recommendation our board will resist—likely that an object should be acquired for study but cataloged separately and not insured at original-market value. The procedure should also state its limits: what kinds of disputes it cannot settle and when it must defer to external expertise. Finally, please separate what is relatively settled in aesthetics (for instance, that a perfect visual copy does not automatically inherit the original's art-historical significance) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether maker-causation contributes to aesthetic value beyond perceptual access), and do not present any contested position—such as the view that only perceptual properties matter—as the consensus view.
