I'm designing the final exam for a graduate seminar in epistemic game theory, and I want an essay question that forces students to work with common knowledge of rationality in a setting that can't be answered by reciting Aumann's agreement theorem or summarizing the Muddy Children puzzle. To that end, please construct a 90-minute exam question built around a deliberately asymmetric visibility topology: three rational agents—A, B, and C—observe a shared noisy signal about an underlying state, but they do not share the same observational access. Specifically, A sees B's observation but not C's; B sees both A's and C's observations; and C sees only her own observation plus a single public announcement that B issues after round one. All agents know the payoff structure, all know that all are rational, and the visibility rules themselves are common knowledge, yet the topology prevents symmetric mutual access. The question should ask students to determine whether common knowledge of rationality is actually achieved after B's announcement, and to identify precisely where—if anywhere—the iterative elimination of irrational strategies stalls in this particular accessibility graph. They must distinguish, in this concrete topology, between mutual knowledge and common knowledge, and explain why A can still rationally maintain a different posterior about C's belief state despite knowing that B is rational and that B knows C is rational. Please include in your prompt an explicit prohibition on merely applying the agreement theorem or invoking symmetric-information puzzles.

Along with the exam question, provide a model answer of approximately 700 words and a detailed marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The marking scheme should separate an A-level answer from a B-level answer by naming five specific moves that a top answer must contain: correctly mapping the visibility structure onto epistemic accessibility relations rather than describing it informally; identifying the exact round or stage at which iterative reasoning breaks down; explaining why the standard Muddy-Children-style elimination argument fails under this asymmetry rather than saying it "doesn't apply"; showing why A's belief about C's belief remains rational despite B's public announcement; and diagnosing the specific misconception—such as assuming symmetric visibility or treating B's announcement as generating common knowledge for all three agents—that should cost marks if present. For each of these five moves, indicate the marks allocated, a one-line note on how students typically miss it, and the total distribution across the scheme. The difficulty should come from navigating the logical space of the visibility graph, not from recalling obscure literature, and the question should be fully answerable without any bibliographic references or external citations.
