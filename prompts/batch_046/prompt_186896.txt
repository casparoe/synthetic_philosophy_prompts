An essay I read recently summarizes the debate over whether the quantum wavefunction is real—what philosophers of physics call the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic distinction—in the following terms: the ontic view holds that the wavefunction is a direct representation of physical reality, comparable to a classical field, while the epistemic view holds that it encodes an observer's incomplete information about some deeper underlying state, analogous to a probability distribution in statistical mechanics. According to the essay, the PBR theorem demonstrated that if two distinct quantum states could correspond to the same underlying ontic state, then independent preparations of those states would generate correlations incompatible with quantum mechanics unless superluminal signaling were permitted. Because no such signaling is observed, and because even Spekkens's toy model, which reproduces a fragment of quantum correlations using hidden variables, is vulnerable to the same reasoning, the essay treats psi-epistemic models as empirically excluded. It then claims that QBism survives only by denying that quantum mechanics describes an external world altogether, which makes it a form of anti-realism rather than a true epistemic interpretation of the wavefunction; consequently, anyone who accepts quantum mechanics as a physical theory must treat the wavefunction as ontologically real, and the debate is effectively settled.

Something about this framing strikes me as too neat, particularly the move from the PBR result to the dismissal of all epistemic interpretations and the characterization of QBism. Could you audit this summary? Please go through it carefully: which parts are accurate representations of the standard positions and results, which parts subtly conflate distinct claims, which parts overstate what has been proved, and where the essay's conclusion fails to follow even from its accurate premises. I am especially interested in whether the PBR argument is correctly described—particularly its assumptions about preparation independence versus locality or signaling—and whether Spekkens's model is actually within the theorem's scope. I also want to know whether QBism is fairly characterized as anti-realist in the relevant sense, and whether there remain live epistemic interpretations that the essay ignores. In short, I want to know what a philosopher of physics working on foundations would accept, what they would wince at, and whether the essay's claim that the question is "effectively settled" survives correction.
