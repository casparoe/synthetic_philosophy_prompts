Please explain the conceptual tension between archival verification and narrative emplotment in historical explanation, using the dispute between Leopold von Ranke's ideal of recovering past events as they actually occurred, E.H. Carr's argument that historical facts are always already selected and structured by the historian's present concerns, and Hayden White's claim that historical writing is irreducibly governed by rhetorical tropes and narrative genres. In particular, address whether Ranke's demand for documentary evidence can verify not merely that a diplomatic event happened, but the counterfactual causal claim that it produced a long-term institutional change; whether Carr's scepticism about bare facts undermines causal explanation altogether, or merely relocates its justification to narrative coherence; and whether White's tropological analysis shows that causal language itself is just one mode of emplotment among others, thereby dissolving the disagreement between Ranke and Carr into a choice of rhetorical frame. Clarify whether, once White's position is granted, there remains a meaningful distinction between factual error and narrative framing, or whether the three positions collapse into a single problem about the conditions under which historical statements can be warranted.
