Consider a constitutional order built from four interlocking provisions. First, Article A establishes a formal amendment procedure—say, a two-thirds legislative vote plus regional ratification—through which any constitutional text may be revised. Second, Article B declares that certain core commitments, including human dignity and the democratic form of government, are permanently unamendable and bind all state authority. Third, Article C states that Article B itself may be altered, but only through a direct popular referendum conducted in strict accordance with the procedural rules then in force under Article A. Fourth, the constitutional court asserts jurisdiction to invalidate any amendment—including one that modifies Article B or Article C—if it violates the substantive limits of Article B, even though the court’s own authority to do so is established by the constitutional text it reviews.

This specific configuration, in which an eternity clause is simultaneously shielded and made amendable through a self-referential procedural loop, is assembled from familiar parts, yet its joint operation raises a sharp puzzle about the source and limits of legal authority. What philosophical commitments about legal self-reference, normative hierarchy, and the distinction between constituent and constituted power does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice require the view that some legal norms possess a meta-level authority that is not itself governed by the norms it regulates, or is it equally compatible with a purely hierarchical positivism in which every rule, including the rule of recognition, remains ultimately revisable? Identify which commitments are strictly necessary for the coherence of the design and which are merely congenial to it, and indicate what would have to change in the institution—particularly in the court’s role—if the underlying theory of legal authority were replaced by one that denied any form of non-derivative, self-grounding normative power.
