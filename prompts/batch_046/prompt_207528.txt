I've been drafting a short essay for my decision-theory reading group on what I'm tentatively calling budget Platonia. I take the standard Platonia dilemma setup — two agents, cooperate/cooperate yields 3 each, defect/defect yields 1 each, sucker gets 0 and defector gets 5 — and graft onto it the Luring Lottery framing, where each agent draws a ticket tied to prior confidence that the other is superrational. The niche twist I'm injecting is that I treat the draw not as exogenous randomness but as a computation-cost drain: every unit of processing spent modeling the other agent's reasoning architecture reduces your available reasoning budget for the actual choice. So if you're highly superrational and spend a lot computing correlation, you literally have less cognitive resource left to execute defection. I then argue that this collapses the usual counterfactual versus interventionist distinction — changing your belief about the draw changes your computational capacity to act — and that the dilemma dissolves not because agents agree but because they can't afford to disagree. I think it's interesting but I'm worried I'm smuggling in a category error between epistemic costs and causal powers, or maybe just reducing standard causal decision theory to extra steps.
