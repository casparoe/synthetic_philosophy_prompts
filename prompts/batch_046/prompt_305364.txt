I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I'm trying to clarify my own commitments about cognition, analogy, and AI before presenting them in a discussion group. I want a consistency audit, not a lecture, and I especially want you to flag it plainly if my framing rests on a confused premise or if the task can't be done well as posed.

Here is what I hold:

(1) I take the central claim of Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences seriously: human cognition is not symbol manipulation or statistical learning but analogical mapping—understanding a novel situation requires projecting its relational structure onto a familiar domain and evaluating the structural fit. Without such mapping, there is no genuine conceptual grasp.

(2) I believe large language models do not perform analogical reasoning in this sense. They compute token probabilities over surface statistical patterns in training data and never represent structural correspondences across domains.

(3) From (2), I conclude that current LLMs lack conceptual understanding and genuine reasoning, even when their outputs are structurally appropriate or useful.

(4) In my engineering work, I constantly generate design solutions through deep analogies—for instance, treating a distributed cache-invalidation problem as structurally corresponding to hardware cache-coherence protocols.

(5) At the same time, I observe that a large portion of my expert judgment—spotting a race condition, recognizing an antipattern, sensing performance degradation—operates through rapid, non-explicit pattern recognition that does not feel like conscious cross-domain analogical reasoning.

(6) I hold that for an analogy to be cognitively valuable, the reasoner must understand both source and target domains deeply enough to verify the structural correspondence; without this bidirectional understanding, the mapping is just heuristic borrowing.

(7) I also think that in rigorous software design, analogies should be formally checkable—ideally expressible as homomorphisms or functorial mappings between the relevant data structures—otherwise they remain informal metaphors without predictive force.

(8) Despite (2) and (3), I regularly prompt LLMs to propose architectural analogies for my codebase, and I evaluate those proposals using my own domain knowledge as if they came from a junior engineer.

My question is: which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity and specify, for each, which commitment(s) would have to give. I am particularly unsure whether (5) undermines (1), whether (8) is compatible with (2) and (6) together, and whether (7) is realistic given how I actually use analogies in (4). Please write in American English, and if my premises are muddled—especially given my lack of philosophy background—say so directly rather than accommodating them.
