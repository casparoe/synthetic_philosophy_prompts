I am preparing to moderate a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently proposed that Herbert Simon’s satisficing should be reconstructed not as a descriptive fallback from optimization, but as a primary normative framework for practical reasoning—specifically, one governed by what she terms “regret-sensitive aspiration levels.” On this view, a rational agent does not merely settle for the first option that clears a static threshold; rather, she dynamically adjusts her aspiration level in response to the epistemic costs of further search, while maintaining a “regret floor” below which she refuses to descend regardless of those costs. The idea has become personally urgent to me because, over the past year, I have had to make rapid, largely irreversible care decisions for a family member under severe informational scarcity, and I realized I was not optimizing outcomes, nor was I applying a fixed cutoff—I was calibrating my willingness to keep searching against a hard lower bound on acceptable results, and I want to understand whether that pattern is rational, arbitrary, or something in between.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of this reconstructed position rather than on biography or general exposition. I want at least two questions that press the difference between “regret-sensitive aspiration levels” and both standard satisficing and Simon’s own notion of procedural rationality; at least one that asks how the framework applies to collective or institutional deliberation where individual aspiration levels conflict; at least one that probes whether the “regret floor” covertly reintroduces maximization (for instance, by functioning as a lexicographic constraint); and at least one that addresses the arbitrariness objection—why this floor rather than another, and whether its selection is itself subject to the same bounded-rationality pressures it is meant to govern. For each question, include a concise follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for example, if she insists the floor is a boundary condition rather than an optimization target, ask how she distinguishes it from a lexicographic ordering without invoking the very comparative reasoning she wants to avoid; or if she appeals to procedural rationality, ask whether procedural checks themselves require unbounded computation to validate.

The questions should anticipate the standard objections that the literature presses—premature closure, the risk that aspiration levels are merely post-hoc rationalizations, and whether this is just bounded rationality redescribed with normative window dressing. Do not ask for reading lists, citations, or bibliographic recommendations, and avoid generic prompts like what drew her to philosophy.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the philosophical coherence of the reconstructed view I have described, and single out the specific point at which your confidence is lowest—whether in the fidelity of the interpretation of Simon’s procedural rationality, in the stability of the distinction between the regret floor and hidden maximization, or in the claim that collective deliberation can be governed by individually calibrated aspiration levels.
