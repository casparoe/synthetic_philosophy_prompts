If I take the seat, I could advocate for binding labor audits and perhaps redirect some of the donor's public-relations budget toward direct aid rather than reputation laundering; yet my name would appear in the firm's annual sustainability report as a community partner, a citation the company actively deploys to deflect criticism of its labor record.

One friend insists this is a straightforward moral trade: I exchange a measurable slice of my moral standing—specifically, my refusal to legitimize exploitation—for a concrete reduction in harm, and I should set the exchange rate rationally rather than sentimentally. I find this framing both clarifying and deeply unsettling, because it treats integrity as a fungible currency and seems to imply that all moral obligations might ultimately be priced and swapped. I am unsure whether my resistance reflects a genuine philosophical problem with reducing morality to transaction, or merely an emotional aversion to seeing my values quantified. I am taking a course on practical ethics, and while we have discussed situations where good people must work within corrupt institutions, none of the frameworks we covered quite capture the specific idea that one's own credibility can be deliberately spent like capital to purchase outcomes. Can you help me see what philosophical structure actually governs this case, and whether accepting the framing of a moral trade necessarily erases the distinction between permissible compromise and complicity?
