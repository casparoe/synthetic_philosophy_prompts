The engineers at Morton Thiokol had statistical records of O-ring distress on previous launches occurring at temperatures of roughly 53 °F; the meteorological forecast for launch day was approximately 31 °F, well outside the observed range. Management therefore faced a choice between delaying the launch—incurring certain costs in schedule, budget, and institutional credibility—and proceeding despite an unknown failure probability that was not merely risky (with a known distribution) but genuinely ambiguous, since no validated physical model linked low-temperature behaviour to catastrophic joint failure.

Please map this onto the Ellsberg structure: identify who the relevant decision-makers are, what counts as the ambiguous prospect versus the risky or certain alternatives, and whether ambiguity aversion predicts the engineers' recommendation to delay or, conversely, management's willingness to launch. I am particularly interested in whether you can apply what I shall call second-order ambiguity—uncertainty not about the failure rate itself, but about the applicability of the statistical model to unprecedented conditions—and whether this layered structure strengthens or weakens the descriptive power of ambiguity aversion for collective, rather than individual, choice.
