I am developing material for an upper-level philosophy seminar on recognition and intersubjectivity, and I want an exercise that isolates the logical skeleton of Hegel’s master-slave dialectic from its familiar narrative packaging. Rather than asking for an exposition, I need you to construct a custom thought experiment engineered to test one specific structural dependency.

Design a scenario with exactly three conscious agents—call them A, B, and C—arranged in a cyclical recognition structure: A recognizes B as a free self-consciousness, B recognizes C, and C recognizes A, with no direct recognition running in the reverse directions and no shared observer who validates the chain. The scenario must satisfy these constraints: none of the agents has engaged in transformative labor upon an external object-world; each risked biological existence in an initial confrontation, but all survived with consciousness intact; and the recognition each offers is explicit and intentional, not tacit. Present the scenario in no more than one paragraph.

Then, working strictly from the internal logic of Hegelian recognition—without invoking secondary literature—show which agent, if any, achieves the kind of self-certainty that Hegel associates with the master, and which remains in the position of the bondsman. Specifically, determine whether the cyclical arrangement allows any participant to receive recognition from a consciousness they simultaneously recognize as independent, or whether the absence of a direct dyadic asymmetry forces all three into an unstable equilibrium. Identify the precise feature of the case—whether it is the absence of labor-mediated objectivity, the circularity of recognition, or the symmetric survival of all parties—that prevents the dialectic from producing a stable lord-bondsman relation. Finally, specify what minimal alteration to the scenario (for example, introducing a single laboring agent, breaking the cycle with an external mediator, or making one recognition unidirectional) would collapse the triadic structure back into the standard dyadic outcome, and explain why a defender of the dialectic might argue that your original three-agent case violates a necessary condition for recognition rather than refuting it.
