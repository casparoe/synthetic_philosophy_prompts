Since I recently began consulting on institutional design for a regional economic community, debates about the European Union have become personally urgent rather than merely academic. In those debates, a familiar analogy is often deployed: the EU is like a bicycle — it must keep pedaling forward toward ever-closer union, or it will fall over. Proponents use this to claim that pooling sovereignty is not a contingent political choice but a structural necessity, and that the democratic deficit is therefore an acceptable cost of maintaining momentum. I want a rigorous audit of how far this analogy actually carries the argument. Give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the rider, the pedals, the forward motion, the fall, and the road or terrain? Then identify where the mapping breaks: for instance, whether a bicycle permits democratic brakes, multi-speed gears, or riders who can dismount without destroying the frame, and whether the analogy quietly imports an inevitability that ignores the EU's existing opt-outs and differentiated integration. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the sovereignty-and-legitimacy question from those that do not. Does the conclusion — that democratic deficits should be tolerated to avoid institutional collapse — survive once these breaks are visible, or was the analogy doing all the normative work? In particular, address whether treating sovereignty as a removable training wheel rather than as the frame itself alters the moral calculus. If the bicycle image fails, suggest a more accurate mechanical or institutional analogy. Please state how confident you are in this audit and single out the specific correspondence or disanalogy about which you are least certain.
