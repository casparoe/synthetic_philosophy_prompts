I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a conceptual final-exam exercise for an upper-level seminar on law, moral psychology, and collective violence that I've been following closely. I want one essay question students could answer in 75 minutes, together with a model answer and a marking scheme out of 25 points. The prompt should sit at the intersection of Raphael Lemkin's original conception of genocide—including his eight-field framework of political, social, cultural, economic, biological, physical, religious, and moral destruction—and the politics of naming atrocities, but it must not be answerable by reciting the standard summary of the 1948 UN Convention's Article II intent requirement or by listing familiar debates about politicide versus genocide. To force students past memorized labels, inject a constructed analytical framework—call it the "continuity-interruption standard"—that classifies an atrocity as genocide if and only if (a) there is targeted physical violence against members of a protected group, and (b) that violence is systematically coupled with institutional measures that sever the group's capacity to transmit identity across generations, regardless of whether the perpetrators subjectively intend the group's total disappearance. Ask students to apply this standard to three concrete cases: (1) a scenario in which large-scale killing occurs but cultural institutions—language, kinship rituals, religious practice—survive intact among survivors and diaspora communities; (2) a scenario in which direct killing is minimal but state institutions systematically dissolve kinship structures, ban language transmission, and erase collective memory, leaving the group's intergenerational continuity broken; and (3) a scenario in which perpetrators hold an explicit, documented intent to destroy a group entirely, deploy mass violence, yet fail, leaving the group's cultural and institutional framework largely undamaged. The model answer should evaluate whether the continuity-interruption standard improves on both Lemkin's broad cultural-genocide category and the UN Convention's narrow intent-based definition; it should state clearly which of the three cases the standard classifies as genocide, which it excludes, and which it leaves in an ambiguous middle category; and it should argue whether the politics of naming—who controls the label and to what moral or legal effect—should depend on perpetrator intent, victim experience of broken continuity, or the structural outcome of interrupted transmission. The marking scheme must identify five must-hit moves, each worth 5 marks: (i) precise articulation of Lemkin's original eight techniques of genocide and his reason for including cultural destruction; (ii) precise articulation of the UN Convention Article II intent requirement and its exclusion of purely cultural destruction; (iii) correct application of the continuity-interruption standard to all three cases with explicit reasoning for each classification; (iv) identification of at least one case where the standard produces a counterintuitive result relative to either ordinary moral judgment or the UN legal framework; and (v) a substantive argument about whether naming politics should track intent, experience, or structural outcome, with explicit reference to at least one of the three cases. For each move, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it—for example, conflating forced assimilation with cultural genocide, treating perpetrator intent as purely psychological rather than institutional, or describing the three cases without actually applying the standard to them. Finally, please state how confident you are in the model answer's classification of case (2), and single out the specific point in that classification about which you are least certain—specifically, whether minimal direct killing combined with complete institutional erasure of intergenerational transmission should count as genocide under the continuity-interruption standard, or whether that stretches the category beyond coherence. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; write in polished American English.
