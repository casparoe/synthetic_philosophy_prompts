For a graduate workshop in normative ethics, I am constructing a conceptual taxonomy of responses to the paradox of deontology — the tension that arises when honoring an agent-centered prohibition (for instance, against killing an innocent person) in a particular case either permits or causally enables a far larger number of violations of that identical prohibition elsewhere, whether through other agents, institutional mechanisms, or unchecked natural processes.

Please design a decision tree that maps the logical space of deontological positions on this problem. Build the tree from the answers to diagnostic questions rather than from a list of named theorists. Include exactly these four splitting distinctions, arranged in whatever order avoids circular dependence:

1. Scope of agency: Is the constraint violated solely by direct personal action, or can a deliberate omission that predictably allows replication of the harm also constitute a violation?
2. Override conditions: If direct action is required, is the constraint ever justifiably broken in order to prevent N > 1 violations of the same constraint, or does it hold absolutely regardless of the magnitude of N?
3. Ground of exception: If an exception exists, is its boundary determined by a lexical ranking of perfect over imperfect duties, by contractualist standards of reasonable rejectability, or by a scalar assessment of rights?
4. Non-override resolution: For views that reject any exception, is the paradox dissolved by insisting on a morally decisive act/omission distinction, or is it embraced as an acceptable consequence that morality may require allowing greater harm?

Every internal node should be phrased as a concrete yes/no or either/or question that a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to.

The tree should contain between six and eight leaves. Required leaves include: a strict agent-relative absolutist position; a threshold deontologist with lexical ordering; a contractualist resolution; and at least two additional positions drawn from the logical combinations (label them 'unnamed' if no prominent defender exists). Each leaf must state the position's name and, in exactly one sentence, its most awkward commitment — the specific implication that critics find hardest to accept.

Additionally:
- Identify one branch combination that yields no coherent position and briefly diagnose why it is unoccupied (e.g., incoherent, self-defeating, or conceptually unexplored).
- Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining which diagnostic split should distinguish them and why it fails to do so.
- Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, page references, or requests for further reading; the classification should depend only on conceptual reasoning.

Keep every node focused on what a position claims about cases and commitments, not on historical labels.
