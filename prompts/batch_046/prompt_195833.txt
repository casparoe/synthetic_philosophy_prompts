To determine whether I am being fair to her position, I want you to write a polished, formal dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them A and B—who have just finished an equally long argument in a café and are now trying to write down exactly where they disagree.

A should defend a refined baseline view: therapeutic intervention restores an individual to a species-typical range of functioning necessary to achieve a basic "threshold of agency," defined not as a statistical average but as a dynamically stable biological range that permits a decent set of human capabilities. A should explicitly concede that baselines are culturally mediated and that they shift across generations, but insist that this drift does not erase a categorical normative gap: removing an obstacle that prevents someone from reaching the current threshold differs in kind from installing a capacity that exceeds the present species-typical range.

B should attack this using the problem of "intergenerational baseline drift." B should argue that if parents enhance working memory or emotional regulation in embryos, the unenhanced children of the next generation do not merely fall below a statistical norm; they fall below a new functional threshold required for adequate participation in education, labor, and social life. B should press that A's "threshold of agency" is therefore circular: it is defined relative to a technologically mediated environment that prior enhancements have already altered. B should further ask whether restoring someone to that drifted baseline is not, relative to the pre-enhancement biological state, itself a form of relative enhancement.

Neither should be a strawman; each should state the strongest version of the opponent's position before answering it.
