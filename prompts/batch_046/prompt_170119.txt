I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced philosophy and bioethics seminar, and for my final project I am examining whether an advance directive retains moral authority once the person who signed it has undergone profound psychological change—specifically, when a patient with advanced dementia who previously refused artificial nutrition now appears genuinely content while being fed. Rather than simply rehearse the standard debate between precedent autonomy and present best interests, my instructor has encouraged me to construct my own conceptual framework, which I am calling the “narrative debt” view: the idea that the earlier self does not bind the later self through strict identity persistence, but rather leaves behind a kind of inherited obligation, analogous to a promise made by one chapter of a life to the next, where the debt is owed not to the same agent but to the continuity of the story itself. Alongside this framework, I want to evaluate four other positions: (1) the strong precedent-autonomy model, which treats the directive as an irrevocable exercise of self-rule that survives mental change intact; (2) the present-interest override model, which holds that any current subjective well-being automatically nullifies prior instructions; (3) the relational-identity model, which asks whether enough psychological continuity remains to justify treating the present patient as the same moral agent who signed the document; and (4) a modified threshold model, which permits the present self to override the directive only if its values have transformed beyond recognition rather than merely shifted in degree.

Could you rank these five positions—from the most defensible to the least defensible—specifically with respect to how well each resolves the tension between honoring past agency and protecting present welfare in the feeding-tube scenario I described? Please assign each a score out of ten, and in one or two sentences for each, identify its central strength and its most serious vulnerability, paying particular attention to whether the “narrative debt” view genuinely offers something distinct or whether it collapses into one of the other four once pressed. I am especially interested in where you think the weakest point lies in the narrative-debt account, since that is the position I am inclined to defend but need to test rigorously before presenting it to my seminar.
