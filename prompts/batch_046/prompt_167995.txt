I am writing a philosophical novel in which a surgeon-philosopher confronts a patient whose brain has been extracted, duplicated, and implanted into two separate bodies—one remaining in the original surgical location, one transported to a remote facility—so that both resulting persons claim, with equal subjective conviction, to be the original subject and to be asking where they are. Drawing on Dennett's brain-transplant scenario but extending it to include this branching duplication, I need you to specify the desiderata that any adequate theory of personal identity and self-location must satisfy to resolve the scene's central conflict. For each requirement—such as accounting for first-person authority against third-person attribution, preserving the distinction between numerical identity and psychological continuity without collapsing them, accommodating branching without arbitrary tie-breaking, and respecting the intuition that physical location is not metaphysically irrelevant—provide the motivating case or consideration behind it. Mark which criteria are non-negotiable for any viable account and which reflect camp-specific commitments, for instance those of physicalist continuity theorists, animalist embodiment theorists, or narrative-constructivist flexibility theorists. Note whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given standard no-collapse conditions on identity. Finally, briefly indicate how a brain-centered view, a body-centered view, and a relational-narrative view measure against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard, since the characters will debate the standards rather than apply them. Avoid citations, bibliographies, or appeals to textual authority; the argument must stand on conceptual grounds alone.
