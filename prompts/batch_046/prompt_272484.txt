I am grading an upper-level philosophy of action exam. The essay question asked:

"Hyperbolic discounting is often invoked to explain weakness of will (akrasia). In roughly 400 words, (a) describe the mechanism of hyperbolic discounting and the preference reversal it generates; (b) distinguish this dynamic inconsistency from the kind of judgment-violation characteristic of akrasia, particularly the idea that the agent retains a contemporaneous "all-things-considered" judgment that the larger delayed reward is better; and (c) evaluate whether accommodating weakness of will within a hyperbolic-discounting framework requires abandoning a normative control principle—the claim that an agent is responsible only for actions they could have avoided given their mental states at the time.

Here is a student answer (approximately 390 words):

Hyperbolic discounting differs from exponential discounting because the rate of discount declines hyperbolically with delay, producing very steep devaluation of near-term rewards. For instance, an agent might prefer $110 in 31 days to $100 in 30 days, but reverse that preference when both rewards are immediate. This reversal is driven by the spike in present value as the smaller reward draws near.

This pattern is essentially identical to akrasia, or weakness of will. In both cases, the agent judges that one option is best overall yet selects another. The hyperbolic model explains this economically: the long-range preference favors the larger delayed good, but as the smaller immediate good becomes available, its discounted value exceeds that of the delayed one, causing the switch.

Crucially, however, at the exact moment of action the agent's strongest preference is for the immediate reward. Therefore there is no genuine contemporaneous conflict with a "better judgment"—the judgment has merely updated with the changed temporal distance. As Donald Davidson argued in his analysis of incontinence, this demonstrates that no separate faculty of will is required. Davidson's principle of continence holds only when an unconditional intention is present, but in hyperbolic cases the intention is conditional on delay, so the reversal is rational from the present viewpoint. Consequently, the normative control principle is not undermined: given the agent's mental states at the time, including the hyperbolic curve itself, the action was unavoidable, and responsibility is suspended.

One might reply that the agent still reports feeling they "should have" worked. But this is merely retrospective regret comparing the present outcome to the past preference state. George Ainslie's intertemporal bargaining model shows that what we call willpower is just the strategic bundling of future preferences, not an independent control mechanism. So integrating akrasia into hyperbolic discounting does not force us to reject the control principle; rather, it reveals that the principle was misapplied to conflicts that are really just intertemporal preference shifts.
