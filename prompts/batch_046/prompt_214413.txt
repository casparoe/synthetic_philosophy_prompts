You are a professional philosopher of collective action and an experienced graduate seminar facilitator. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the central tension you want participants to feel: joint action seems to require a shared intention that cannot be reduced to individual intentions plus common knowledge, yet attributing a collective mind remains metaphysically suspicious. Design the activity so that this tension appears in what participants do, not in what you explain afterward.

Design a 75-minute in-person classroom activity for 12 philosophy graduate students in a seminar room with no technology beyond index cards and a whiteboard. The students should come away seeing why irreducibly plural we-mode reasoning is practically necessary even when individual mental states diverge.

Structure: divide the 12 students into three groups of four. Give each group eight ambiguous borderline cases of joint action—e.g., strangers pushing a car out of mud together, a committee voting by secret ballot, a leaderless flash mob, partners dancing with mismatched intentions. Every student receives a hidden “epistemic stance” card—Individualist, We-Mode, Emergentist, or Eliminativist—specifying criteria they must apply. Every student also receives a hidden “commitment card”: half must sign any group output with their own name only; half must sign “We, the group,” but they are forbidden from revealing which card they hold until the debrief.

Rules and timeline: 25 minutes to produce a single written classification of all eight cases with brief justifications, requiring unanimous agreement to submit. During deliberation, any member may invoke a “joint-reason veto,” blocking a proposal only if they frame the objection in first-person plural—“We should reject this because...”—while singular framing—“I reject this because...”—renders the veto invalid. After submission, each group reads its statement aloud; the class then votes silently on whether the statement expresses a genuinely shared intention or merely an aggregated compromise. Follow with a 20-minute debrief.

In the debrief, map patterns of veto use and signing discrepancies onto the standard philosophical distinction between I-mode and we-mode reasoning. Reveal the hidden commitment cards and ask those who signed “We” whether they intended to bind the others, and ask those who used plural veto language whether they actually shared the underlying reasons with the group. Be explicit that the activity stages the practical pressure that makes an irreducible collective stance plausible, but it cannot reproduce the metaphysical question of whether group minds literally exist, nor can it reproduce the diachronic stability of joint commitments across changing membership—those limitations you must supply verbally. Also predict what will go wrong: students may deploy plural language strategically rather than sincerely; some will feel deceived by the hidden cards. Handle this by framing the structural opacity not as deception but as a feature—joint commitment often functions precisely despite private divergence that participants do not transparently access. List the minimal materials needed and provide an honest closing note on the one feature of collective intentionality this simulation necessarily omits.
