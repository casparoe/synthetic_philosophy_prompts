i've kinda landed on a weird hybrid view about just war theory. i think the moral equality of combatants is best defended not through Walzer's independance of jus ad bellum and jus in bello, but through institutional role morality—once you enlist you've consented to enter a structure that authorizes lethal force, and that consent wipes out the usual self-defense asymmetry regardless of cause justice. it's not about ignorance, it's about having stepped into a role that suspends normal moral permissions. what would actually count against this? skip the standard "unjust soldiers are murderers" line, i already know that one. i want defeaters specific to the role-consent framing: what if the state is illegitamate or conscription isn't voluntary? are there anologies in medical ethics or corporate liability where similar role logic breaks? and is there some result about collective responsibility or delegated authority that makes this collapse back into Walzer anyway? rank them fatal to awkward. also, be honest—has this view been qualified into near-unfalsifiability with conditions about legitimate authority and informed consent? and if it has, is that actually a problem for a normative theory like this? i think the strongest challenge might be an institutional parallel where the logic clearly fails, so if you have one in mind, lead with that.
