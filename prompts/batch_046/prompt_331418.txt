I'm working through Schlick's distinction between activities that possess meaning (Sinn) as ends in themselves—what he associates with play (Spiel)—and activities that are mere work (Arbeit), oriented toward an external result. I have formed concrete verdicts about a range of cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, or whether coherence requires abandoning one.

Here are my judgments. A child building an elaborate sandcastle knowing the tide will destroy it within the hour is engaged in something fully meaningful; the absence of any lasting product does not diminish it. A scientist who pursues a problem solely to win a prize and secure tenure, feeling no curiosity about the phenomenon itself, is not engaged in meaningful activity, despite the intellectual demands. A monk who recites prayers mechanically because he fears punishment if he stops falls into the same instrumental category. A retired chess player who plays every morning with no intention of improving, competing, or being observed strikes me as clearly meaningful, analogous to the child. An open-source programmer who spends years refining code that almost no one downloads, receiving neither pay nor recognition, yet reports being in a state of complete absorption, also strikes me as meaningful.

But the harder cases create tension. An Olympic athlete who trains with excruciating discipline for a medal, yet genuinely loves every session and would continue much of it even without the competition, still seems meaningful to me; the external goal is present, but it does not seem to reduce the training to mere work. A musician who composes a symphony because she feels an irresistible inner drive, yet is also constantly aware that this work will secure her legacy and posthumous fame, also strikes me as meaningful, though the quality feels subtly different from the child's sandcastle. By contrast, a soldier who fights for a cause he sincerely believes in and derives visceral satisfaction from combat, but whose every action is rigorously subordinated to strategic victory, strikes me as falling on the work side despite the emotional intensity.

My tentative principle is that an activity is meaningful in Schlick's sense when its present quality of engagement constitutes its value for the agent, regardless of whether a separable outcome is also pursued. This saves the athlete and the musician, but it forces me to classify the soldier as meaningful too, since he also derives present satisfaction—yet my verdict is that he is not. Alternatively, I could require that the separable outcome not be the agent's justifying reason for participation, but then the musician's awareness of legacy becomes a disqualifying factor, which I am reluctant to accept.

So I ask: can Schlick's framework be refined—perhaps by distinguishing an accompanying goal from a constitutive purpose, or by introducing a condition about the reversibility of the agent's motivation—to accommodate all these verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to two further situations I have not mentioned (a surgeon who loves the craft but depends entirely on her salary to survive; a gambler who plays solely to feel an addictive risk-rush with no other end), and tell me honestly which of my original verdicts—especially about the athlete, the musician, or the soldier—I should drop if I want the simplest coherent account.
