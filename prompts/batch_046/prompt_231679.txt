In drafting a multi-generational novel, I am trying to resist the habit of treating earlier centuries as imperfect drafts of our present moral vocabulary; yet in craft essays on historical fiction I repeatedly meet the consolation that "the arc of history bends toward justice," invoked precisely to license that framing—to suggest that past disagreement, cruelty, or limitation can be narrated as necessary prelude rather than as fully inhabited worlds. Before I can guard my narrative against this corruption, I need to know exactly what is being claimed. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan might express, treating them as a menu rather than selecting one? For each reading, specify: whether the "arc" is meant as a descriptive statistical pattern, a teleological law, or a normative aspiration; whose conception of "justice" serves as the endpoint—liberal-procedural, cosmopolitan, local-traditional, or otherwise; over what temporal scale it operates—lifetimes, centuries, or the span of the species; and whether the bending is presented as inevitable, probabilistic, or merely hoped for. Then, for each formulation, say whether it is trivial, defensible on its own terms, or false, and—most important for my purpose—whether, even if true, it actually justifies writing the past as progress toward the present, or whether it fails to license the novelist's reduction of earlier characters to morally primitive stepping-stones. I am not asking for historical examples or a bibliography; I want the conceptual disambiguation with verdicts, so that I can see which version of the claim, if any, genuinely supports the Whig temptation I am trying to avoid.
