As a novelist who has spent the better part of a decade constructing an interlinked speculative fiction cycle—each volume governed by its own physical and metaphysical laws yet bound by an overarching authorial architecture—I have become preoccupied with debates I encounter in conversations with scientists about belief in unobservable worlds. Specifically, I want to understand the controversy over multiverse hypotheses arising from string theory, in which direct empirical access to other universes is impossible and defenders instead appeal to mathematical consistency, explanatory coherence, the absence of unique alternatives, and probabilistic reasoning across a landscape of vacua. I ask you to translate the core positions in this debate—the argument that internal coherence can substitute for observational testing, the claim that theoretical uniqueness confers epistemic weight, and the charge that unfalsifiability removes such theories from scientific status—into the vocabulary of narrative world-building, canonical consistency across installments, suspension of disbelief, authorial intent, and the aesthetic economy of fictional ontology. I am fluent in these narrative categories and in distinguishing canonical rules from fan-fiction expansions, though I have no technical training in physics or formal epistemology. For each claim, report whether it translates cleanly, requires distortion—perhaps forcing quantitative ensemble reasoning into qualitative judgments about narrative plausibility—or fails entirely, leaving a residue with no expression in fiction. Then tell me whether that residue indicates a limitation in narrative theory's ability to ground ontological commitment, or whether it reveals that the scientific debate was already smuggling in unstated aesthetic assumptions about coherence, closure, and the authority of an unseen architect—assumptions that physics pretends to reject and fiction openly admits.
