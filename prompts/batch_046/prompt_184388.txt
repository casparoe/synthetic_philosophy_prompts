At a philosophy discussion group, Jordan and Riley got stuck arguing about whether a person could have originated from different sperm and egg. Jordan began with an intuition pump: imagine her parents had used IVF, and a different embryo—formed from entirely other gametes—had been implanted and developed into someone with Jordan’s exact psychology and memories. That person, Jordan said, still wouldn’t be her; so origin essentialism, which claims she necessarily comes from those particular gametes, must be false, because psychological continuity should be sufficient for identity. Riley replied that Jordan was simply assuming psychological continuity is the criterion of personal identity, which is precisely what is at issue. The origin essentialist grants that psychology matters for persistence, but denies it determines origin; describing a psychologically identical being just describes a doppelgänger or sibling, not Jordan herself.

Jordan then pressed a sharper, more technical objection. Even granting that origins matter, she said, there is no principled boundary between “the same gametes with a random mutation” and “different gametes that happen to yield the same DNA sequence.” Since mutations are biologically inevitable, the essentialist must either allow enormous genetic drift within one “essential” origin or draw an arbitrary line, reducing origin essentialism either to implausible rigidity or to genetic essentialism, a view almost no philosopher holds.

Riley answered that origin essentialism concerns causal history, not genetic identity. The claim is metaphysical rather than epistemic: two origins might be indistinguishable to us and still be numerically distinct. She then introduced a refinement drawn from reproductive biology. Monozygotic twins share the same gametes and the same initial zygote, yet they are clearly different people; therefore the essentialist should specify the origin not merely as “those gametes” but as “the specific zygote that developed into me without splitting.” This, Riley argued, saves the view from genetic reductionism without making it arbitrary.

Jordan countered that this refinement is viciously circular. By defining the essential origin as “the very developmental trajectory that actually produced me,” Riley makes the claim true by definition: of course Jordan necessarily comes from the path that led to Jordan. The substantive version of origin essentialism was supposed to constrain counterfactual possibilities—for instance, that Jordan could not have been conceived a month earlier with different parents—but retreating to “the actual trajectory” just relabels her actual history as essential, a move that applies to every object and therefore explains nothing distinctive about persons.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not decide. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of Jordan’s and Riley’s responses from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. In particular, assess whether Riley’s refinement genuinely rescues origin essentialism from Jordan’s arbitrariness and circularity charges, or whether Jordan is correct that it collapses the view into triviality. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing about how origin essentialism interacts with psychological-continuity theories of personal identity.
