I've come around to thinking that the real AI failure mode isn't influence-seeking in the classic sense — some unified agent suddenly grabbing power through instrumental convergence — but slow value drift. The picture is that misalignment accumulates gradualy through normal optimization, feedback loops, and instutional adaptation, with no single actor deciding to deviate. By the time anyone notices, the values embedded in the system have slid far from ours, and it's too late to correct because the drift is distributed and path-dependent.

What would count against this? I want an honest map of its exposure. Rank the defeaters: what's fatal versus merely awkward? Include arguments and observations — maybe from existing tech or organizational behavior — that defenders of the drift view would themselves admit are damaging, not just what critics say. What would have to be true for drift to be easily reversible, or for influence-seeking to turn out to be the actual driver after all? Also check whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — like saying drift only happens under conditions that are never actually tested — and whether that insulation is a weakness for a claim of this kind. No reading lists, just the logic and the evidence.
