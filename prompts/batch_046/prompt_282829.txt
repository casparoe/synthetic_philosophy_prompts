Question: In his essay "Of Suicide," Hume argues that self-killing is not a crime against God, society, or oneself. Explain the structure of his argument against the charge that suicide violates divine providence, including the analogical reasoning he uses to show that human intervention in nature is not inherently impious. Then briefly indicate how he responds to the objection that suicide harms the agent.

Student answer:
Hume rejects the charge of impiety by appealing to general providence: God governs through universal laws of motion and matter, so human actions—including suicide—are governed by those laws rather than standing outside them. He compares suicide to diverting a river or stopping blood flow in one's veins: just as these acts redirect nature without breaking its laws, ending one's life redirects the course of existence without violating divine authority. If such redirection were truly sinful, then medicine and agriculture would be equally guilty, since both constantly alter natural outcomes. Regarding society, Hume claims that death usually benefits the community, particularly when the person was unproductive, because it frees resources and removes a burden. Against the charge that suicide wrongs the agent, he notes that after death there is no subject left to suffer the wrong, and beforehand the agent exercises rational autonomy in concluding that further life offers only misery. Throughout, Hume treats suicide not merely as permitted but as a Stoic virtue, recommending it nobly whenever existence becomes unbearable.
