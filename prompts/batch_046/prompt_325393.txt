I have recently begun caring for a parent with frontotemporal dementia whose daily routines have become mechanically rigid—repeating the same sequence of actions regardless of changed circumstances, and restarting from the beginning if interrupted—and this has made the philosophical problem of sphexishness suddenly personal rather than abstract. I came across an unnamed popular philosophy essay that uses the sphex wasp (Sphex ichneumoneus) to frame the relationship between mechanical rigidity, behavioral flexibility, and free will. The essay summarizes the classic observation like this: after paralyzing a cricket and dragging it to her burrow, the wasp always enters alone to inspect the interior before pulling the prey inside; if an experimenter moves the cricket away during this inspection, the wasp emerges, drags it back to the entrance, and repeats the inspection, cycling through this loop dozens of times without ever adapting to pull the prey straight in. The essay treats this not merely as an illustration but as decisive proof that the wasp lacks free will, because her behavior is governed by a fixed neural circuit in which the inspection reflex mechanically suppresses the dragging reflex until the loop completes. It then claims that neuroscience shows human decision-making operates through structurally analogous feedback circuits—merely longer and more complex—and concludes that human action is therefore equally sphexish. From this, the essay infers that free will can survive only if our loops are not deterministic, and since determinism is incompatible with breaking out of such loops, the sphex case ultimately undermines belief in human freedom rather than illuminating it.

Something about this feels like a sleight of hand—the wasp’s repetition is being made to carry far more philosophical weight than it should—but I cannot pinpoint where the slide occurs, whether in the entomology, the interpretation of the loop, or the analogy to human agency. Is this a fair summary of how philosophers actually deploy the sphex case, and does the essay’s conclusion survive correction? Do not merely map compatibilist versus libertarian options—argue for the correct reading and explain why the other interpretations fail here, particularly with respect to whether flexibility is sufficient for free will and whether determinism entails sphex-like rigidity.
