You are delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled "Who May Blame Whom: Standing, Hypocrisy, and Collective Agency," co-hosted by a philosophy department and a school of public administration. The audience is split evenly between moral philosophers who work on standing to blame—familiar with the puzzles of hypocritical blame, forfeiture, and collective agency—and senior civil servants, regulators, and NGO directors who have never read the literature but live inside these problems daily: agencies that sanction corruption while practicing opacity, or governments that condemn tax evasion while maintaining structural loopholes.

Write a five-minute address that uses a conceptual distinction invented for this occasion—the mirror-license problem—to structure the entire talk. Define the mirror-license problem as follows: an agent (individual or collective) demands that others hold up a moral mirror the agent refuses to face, but the refusal is not mere hypocrisy because the agent lacks the practical authority to enforce the norm upon itself. Explain this in language the practitioners grasp immediately, without naming philosophers or using technical terms like "second-personal" or "reactive attitudes," yet with enough precision that the philosophers recognize it as a substantive contribution to the standing literature rather than a gloss.

The speech must do three things structurally. First, open with a concrete institutional case—say, a municipal water authority that fines residents for illegal well-drilling while its own maintenance division has contaminated groundwater for years—showing why standard accusations of hypocrisy miss the point. Second, introduce the mirror-license problem as the reason the authority's blame is not merely hypocritical but structurally misdirected: standing requires not only moral consistency but a form of practical self-binding that collective agents rarely achieve. Third, close by framing the workshop's agenda: ask the philosophers to refine the conditions under which collective agents can acquire such self-binding, and ask the practitioners to identify institutional mechanisms that could create it, with the explicit caveat that if no mechanism exists we may need to accept that certain forms of institutional blame are permanently illegitimate.

Keep the tone formal but accessible, avoid all citations, bibliographies, and reading lists, and do not quote any published text verbatim. The philosophical idea should determine what can and cannot be said—if the mirror-license problem holds, then some institutional condemnations must be withdrawn not because they are false, but because the speaker lacks standing—rather than serving as decorative framing.
