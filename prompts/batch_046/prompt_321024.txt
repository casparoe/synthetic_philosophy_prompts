I serve on my university's Joint Committee on Digital Infrastructure and Research Ethics, and we face a binding vote next Thursday on a proposal to deploy an updated AI-assisted feedback system across all undergraduate writing courses. The administration and the vendor frame the update as a safe Pareto improvement: grading turnaround shrinks from ten days to two, feedback becomes uniformly granular for every student, no staff positions are eliminated, and licensing costs fall. Because every affected party—students, instructors, and the institution—receives strictly more of what they already value, they argue that there is no legitimate ground for objection and that our ethical review should be perfunctory rather than substantive.

I am drafting the committee's formal written opinion for circulation before the meeting, and I need it to recommend a concrete course of action with a clear timeline—approval, rejection, or conditional approval with specific safeguards—rather than merely raising concerns. The philosophical issue instantiated here is that the safe Pareto framework assumes we can rank and aggregate welfare changes across incommensurable dimensions—pedagogical autonomy, relational teaching, temporal efficiency, budget relief—without a shared metric, and it treats the absence of demonstrable complaint as proof of the absence of harm. Specifically, students whose learning depends on non-standardized, idiosyncratic instructor engagement may suffer a hidden comparative loss that never registers in the data, because the counterfactual personalized feedback is never delivered; they experience not an observable deprivation but a structural invisibility of what they lost. The memo should therefore engage the vendor's framing seriously, articulate the strongest reason it fails—particularly the problem of hidden comparative harm when goods are incommensurable and counterfactual outcomes are unobservable—and honestly acknowledge the strongest consideration on the losing side, whether that is the real efficiency and equity gains, the risk of bureaucratic overreach, or the practical difficulty of operationalizing incommensurability in institutional policy.

Please draft this memo so that the administration could respond to it directly. If you recommend conditional approval, specify the pilot design, opt-out protocol, and evaluation criteria; if you recommend rejection, state the principle that should govern future proposals of this kind. Additionally, please state explicitly what would change your recommendation—for instance, empirical evidence from a pilot showing no differential outcomes for opt-out students, a philosophical argument that multi-dimensional institutional comparison does not require a common metric, or a demonstration that the committee's mandate does not extend to metaphysical questions about value comparison. Finally, identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail, such as in the distinction between observable and hidden harm, or in treating pedagogical autonomy as a non-fungible good that policy must protect.
