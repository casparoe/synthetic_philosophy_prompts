Following a long argument with a friend about whether moral status admits of degrees—she maintained that either an entity is within the circle of moral concern or it is not, and that insects, plants, and thermostats all belong on the same side—I want a structured classroom activity for my graduate philosophy seminar (twelve students, 75 minutes) that makes the gradation of moral status feel unavoidable rather than sentimental. The design should place participants in a resource-allocation simulation: they receive cards representing entities that vary independently along dimensions of phenomenal sentience, autonomous agency, social embeddedness, and capacity for suffering, and they must distribute a scarce protective budget. The rules should be arranged so that imposing a sharp threshold feels arbitrary but distributing resources uniformly collapses practical reasoning. Provide the rules, card structure, payoff or constraint scheme that uses no real money, and the facilitator's script for the debrief, which must map moments of player resistance or confusion onto specific philosophical positions—including threshold theories, sliding-scale accounts, and the thermostat problem—predict what will fail in the first run, and be explicit about which feature of moral gradation this exercise cannot reproduce. The group should come away seeing that gradation is not merely quantitative but structurally multidimensional, and that any single-axis cutoff is philosophically costly. Please indicate how confident you are in the design, and single out the point about which you are least certain.
