I was recently appointed to the governing council of a scientific academy founded in 1923, and I am now confronting a motion that forces me to think about responsibility across generations in a way my ordinary moral intuition does not cover. From 1935 until 1965, the academy maintained an unwritten but rigorously enforced practice of excluding researchers from a specific colonial territory both from membership and from access to our archival collections; the exclusion never appeared in our bylaws, yet the minutes and correspondence leave no doubt about its systematic nature. Every living council member joined decades after the practice ended, but our current endowment—including the building that houses our archives—was accumulated in part through membership dues and institutional grants received during those thirty years. The motion before us asks the academy to issue an official declaration that the institution itself bears culpability for that historical exclusion, and to establish a reparative fund drawn directly from our present assets rather than from new donations alone.

Several colleagues argue that culpability is strictly personal and dies with the individuals who enacted the exclusion; since none of us were present, they say, the most we can coherently express is institutional regret or shame, but never culpability that attaches to the body itself. Others reply that the academy is a continuous legal and moral entity—same charter, same property, same accumulated prestige—so the wrong attaches to the institution regardless of current personnel. A smaller faction contends that even present members share in the burden because we actively maintain the organization, benefit from its reputation, and now hold the power to remedy the harm its resources helped to perpetuate. I find each view partly compelling, yet they seem to rest on incompatible assumptions about whether a collective can be a moral subject in its own right, whether benefiting from past wrongs transmits any form of blame, and whether responsibility can be borne jointly without being diluted into empty metaphor. Could you help me map out the philosophical commitments underlying each of these three positions, assess whether they can be reconciled, and advise what practical difference it would make—particularly for the framing of our official statement and for the ethical basis of drawing reparations from our existing endowment—if we adopted one conception rather than another?
