Essay sketch: The standard framing of Buridan's ass treats the problem as synchronic: at a single moment, reason offers no basis to prefer one bale of hay over an identical other, so choice appears impossible. Buridan's response—that the will can freely determine itself toward one option—solves this immediate deadlock. Yet I think this overlooks a deeper, diachronic form of the same problem. Even if the will breaks a particular tie, it does so without any prior comparative preference; the agent never undergoes a deliberative process that favors one bale. This raises a question about what kind of freedom is being exercised. I propose distinguishing between 'thin' arbitrary choice, in which the will operates as a bare causal switch, and 'thick' arbitrary choice, in which the act of choosing retroactively constitutes the preference that justifies it. Buridan's voluntarism, I suggest, tacitly requires the thick version: otherwise the will's decision is not genuinely self-determining but merely random. But defending the thick version demands a metaphysics of self-constitution that Buridan never articulates. So the sketch argues that the ass paradox is not resolved by appealing to the will alone; it is relocated into the prior conditions under which a will can form a preference ex nihilo.

Please assess: (1) whether the synchronic/diachronic distinction holds up under scrutiny; (2) whether attributing the 'thick' view to Buridan is historically and philosophically defensible; and (3) whether the sketch's conclusion—that the paradox is relocated rather than dissolved—is compelling or overstates the difficulty.
