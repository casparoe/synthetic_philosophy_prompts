A colleague in consciousness science has offered me her judgments about which systems possess integrated information in the sense that matters for consciousness, without explaining her underlying principle. Her verdicts are these: an awake human brain does possess it; the cerebellum does not, despite its greater neuronal count; a purely feedforward artificial neural network with billions of parameters does not; a small biological circuit of eight neurons with dense recurrent connections does possess it, though only minimally; an analog neuromorphic chip that physically replicates that same eight-neuron circuit in silicon also does; the identical circuit running as software on a conventional laptop does not; and a swarm of one thousand simple robots, each with a local recurrent loop and communicating wirelessly with ten-millisecond latency to solve a collective routing problem, does not.

Consider now a system composed of two such analog neuromorphic chips—each containing a four-neuron recurrent loop—linked by a dedicated fiber-optic cable that introduces a fixed fifty-millisecond transmission delay. Within each chip, feedback is instantaneous; between chips, a signal arrives too late to alter the receiving chip's current state, though over longer timescales the two chips do influence each other. Physically they are separate, but they are hard-wired together, and the combined architecture solves an integration task that neither chip can solve in isolation.

Predict whether she would say this hybrid system possesses integrated information. Identify at least two distinct rules that are jointly consistent with her seven verdicts, state what each predicts about the hybrid case, and say which of her earlier judgments best distinguishes the two rules. Then propose one further hypothetical system—describing its physical layout and informational dynamics—whose verdict would settle which rule she is actually applying. I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning that connects her track record to the prediction, and in the precise boundary that your proposed test case is designed to probe.
