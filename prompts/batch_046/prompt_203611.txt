Consider the position that the situationist critique of virtue ethics can be fully absorbed without abandoning virtue theory, provided virtues are reconceived not as global, cross-situational traits of isolated individuals but as distributed dispositions—relational properties emerging from the interaction between an agent's psychology and the social institutions that scaffold behavior. On this view, situationism shows not that virtues are fictions, but that their proper unit of analysis is the agent-institution pair: a virtue like honesty is stable only within specific institutional contexts (contract law, professional norms, reputation systems), and behavioral variability across situations reflects variation in scaffolding rather than absence of character. The claim is that this saves virtue ethics from empirical challenge while preserving normative core commitments: we still praise and blame individuals, yet recognize that cultivating virtue requires institutional design as much as personal effort.

Assess its exposure honestly: what observations, arguments, or formal results would have to obtain for this synthesis to be wrong? Which assumptions are load-bearing? Would empirical evidence that institutional scaffolding explains only a small fraction of behavioral variance be decisive, or merely awkward? Could the view collapse into pure situationism—treating individuals as passive nodes—or into institutional determinism that dissolves personal responsibility? What would proponents of this model themselves concede as counting genuinely against it—for instance, if cross-situational consistency turned out to be higher within uniform institutional settings, or if distributing virtue across agent-institution pairs made individual moral praise incoherent? Distinguish live escape routes from merely logical ones, and say whether the position is well insulated or whether its qualifications have rendered it unfalsifiable at the cost of emptiness.
