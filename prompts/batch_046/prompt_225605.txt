You are an expert in formal decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, with deep familiarity with diachronic coherence constraints, Dutch-book arguments, and the distinction between structural and substantive conditions on intertemporal preference. Before giving your answer, take a moment to brainstorm: list the main coherence norms that govern preferences across time—strict transitivity, dynamic consistency, immunity to money pumps, belief-sensitive plan revision, and any compromise principles that allow limited preference change without licensing exploitation. Then proceed systematically.

Consider the track record of verdicts delivered by a rational-choice counselor, Dr. V, to clients facing sequential decision problems:

Case 1: A client prefers A over B at time t, is offered a sequence in which she pays $1 to switch to B, then pays $1 to switch back to A, and ends with A and $2 less. Dr. V condemns this as an unambiguous money pump and rules the underlying preference structure irrational.

Case 2: The same client prefers gamble G1 (50% chance of $100, else $0) to G2 (40% chance of $110, else $0) in the morning, but reverses to G2 over G1 in the evening with no change in information or stakes. Dr. V permits the reversal, holding that risk attitudes can shift with context if the choices are not binding across the two moments.

Case 3: A client adopts plan P at t1, receives new evidence at t2 that alters her beliefs about outcomes, and consequently abandons P for Q. Dr. V approves the switch, treating belief revision as a legitimate ground for revising even firmly adopted plans.

Case 4: A client consistently prefers, in each isolated monthly evaluation, to spend an extra $100 rather than save it, yet over twelve months she saves nothing and later regrets the cumulative result. There is no external party extracting value, and her local preferences are stable under reflection. Dr. V still condemns the pattern as diachronically incoherent.

Case 5: The client is offered a commitment device that would lock in monthly saving, but she declines because she values preserving future flexibility more highly than the accumulated savings. Dr. V approves the decline, insisting that coherence does not require binding one's future self.

Now consider Case 6: A client exhibits cyclical preferences across three anticipated time slices—at t1 she ranks A > B > C > A; at t2 she ranks B > C > A > B; and at t3 she ranks C > A > B > C. She is fully aware of the cycle, refuses all commitment mechanisms, and states she will resolve each slice by "going with the flow" of her local ranking. There is no monetary exchange between slices, no cumulative objective loss measured in goods or welfare, and each local ranking is stable under careful reflection at that slice; the only "cost" is the structural fact that she traverses a loop.

Predict Dr. V's verdict on Case 6: does she condemn it as a diachronic money pump analogous to Case 1 or Case 4, permit it as legitimate preference evolution analogous to Case 2 or Case 3, or assign it to a different category altogether?
