After thirty years in condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun working through the philosophy of rationality and decision-making, and I find myself returning to the same conceptual knots I once navigated in experimental design. In a reading group devoted to bounded rationality, I noted that physicists routinely adopt effective Hamiltonians rather than full quantum-field descriptions—not out of cognitive laziness, but because the approximation is epistemically and practically superior at the relevant scale. A fellow participant dismissed this by saying, "the best is the enemy of the good," using the line to argue that my defense of approximation was really an endorsement of satisficing as a normative ideal, and that any insistence on globally optimal reasoning is therefore self-defeating.

I want to understand exactly what that sentence can amount to. Could you separate out the distinct precise theses hiding inside it—distinguishing, for instance, an empirical claim about the costs of optimization, a normative claim that "good enough" is intrinsically valuable, a psychological claim about the paralysis induced by perfectionism, and perhaps an epistemic claim about tractability—indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or simply false? I am particularly interested in whether there is a reading that genuinely licenses the analogy between scientific approximation (selecting an effective model over a fundamental one) and practical satisficing (accepting a satisfactory choice over the optimal one), and whether that analogy holds once we distinguish epistemic adequacy from practical rationality. Which of these readings was most likely being invoked to shut down my objection, and does that particular reading actually entail that bounded rationality should be embraced as a virtue rather than merely tolerated as a constraint?
