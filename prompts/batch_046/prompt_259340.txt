I am currently enrolled in an introductory metaphysics course, and during a recent discussion section a fellow student offered a summary of the problem of universals that the group seemed to accept without much scrutiny. Her account went approximately as follows. The problem arises because we routinely say that two particular objects are both red, or both round, or both taller than some third thing, which suggests that there is something genuinely shared between them—namely, the property of redness, roundness, or the relation of being taller than. Realists, she said, take this talk seriously: they hold that these properties and relations exist as universals, entities that can be instantiated by many particulars at once and that do not depend for their existence on being instantiated. Nominalists deny this; they hold that there are only particular things arranged in various ways, and that our general terms are merely useful labels for classes of resembling particulars, with no shared entity present in each member. She then described a middle position she called trope realism, according to which every particular object possesses its own unique, unshareable property—this particular redness in this apple, that particular redness in that rose—and that objective resemblance among these particular properties explains why we apply the same word to both objects. She concluded that trope realism is essentially a compromise that accepts the realist commitment to properties being genuinely in the world but denies that they are shared, and therefore it collapses the traditional distinction between realism and nominalism into a merely verbal disagreement. She added, almost as an afterthought, that this framework makes it especially difficult to account for structural universals such as the relation being to the left of, because if relations are also particularized tropes unique to each pair, then it becomes unclear how the very same relation can hold between different pairs of objects without being shared.

I have the impression that this summary is mostly coherent but subtly conflates distinctions that our lecturer has emphasized—particularly the difference between trope theory and resemblance nominalism, and the precise sense in which trope theories are or are not realist positions. I am also unsure whether her point about structural relations is a standard difficulty for trope theories or an improvised objection, and whether her framing of the entire debate around the notion of sharing obscures some other dimension that is actually central, such as the explanation of resemblance itself. Could you audit this summary claim by claim? Specifically, I would like to know which parts are accurate representations of the standard positions, which are common simplifications that obscure important distinctions, and which are genuine misrepresentations. Does trope realism in fact collapse the realism-nominalism distinction in the way she suggests, or does it belong more clearly to one side? Is the difficulty with structural relations genuinely a problem for trope theories, and is it usually formulated in terms of the relation failing to be shared across pairs? Finally, does her conclusion—that the debate reduces to a verbal disagreement—actually follow from the accurate parts of her account, or does it depend on the errors? I want to understand what a careful student should take away about the boundary between choice and circumstance in this debate, though here I mean the boundary between shared universals and particularized properties, and whether that boundary survives her analysis intact.
