I have recently become personally invested in longtermist ethics after a series of intense conversations with colleagues who argue that we ought to prioritize the far future—potentially at enormous present cost—to mitigate existential risk. One interlocutor countered by invoking the simulation argument: if we are likely simulated beings, she claimed, then our efforts to influence the physical far future are causally hollow, and fanaticism about distant outcomes is unwarranted. I find this counterargument superficially plausible but ultimately mistaken, and I want you to construct a rigorous case for the opposite view—namely, that the simulation argument, when analyzed with appropriate conceptual precision, actually strengthens rather than weakens the moral urgency of far-future reasoning.

To keep the argument from collapsing into well-worn rehearsals, please incorporate the following niche conceptual architecture. Distinguish sharply between ancestor simulations (computational reconstructions of a prior historical epoch) and branching simulations (models that share our present state but compute divergent future trajectories in parallel). Then argue that within a branching architecture, the moral weight of future beings existing in our causal branch is not cancelled by our lack of causal access to the "base" reality that runs the simulation. Furthermore, address whether nested simulations—simulations running inside other simulations—generate a regress that either collapses longtermist obligations or, counterintuitively, multiplies them across ontological layers.
