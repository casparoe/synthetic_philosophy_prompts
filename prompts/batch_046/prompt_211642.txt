My study group tried to formalize a constraint on institutional ontology that seemed obvious at first, and then it collapsed gender, race, and money into an impossible trilemma. I think there is either an equivocation between constitutive and causal dependence, or a hidden modal slide, somewhere in the middle steps, but I cannot isolate it. Here is the chain exactly as we numbered it.

(1) If a kind is genuinely socially constructed, then its membership conditions depend constitutively on collective practices, not merely causally on them.
(2) For such constitutive dependence to be well-formed, collective intentionality must apply to something independently identifiable; call this the bearer constraint—without a bearer, a status function has nothing to attach to.
(3) Money clearly satisfies the bearer constraint: physical tokens, digital records, or metal serve as independently identifiable bearers, and collective practices assign monetary status to those specific bearers.
(4) Social constructionists about gender typically deny that biological sex serves as the independent bearer; gender status is assigned directly to embodied persons without an intermediate biological kind acting as substrate.
(5) Race, on standard constructionist accounts, lacks any biological bearer altogether, since racial categories do not map onto intrinsic biological kinds.
(6) Therefore gender and race appear to violate the bearer constraint.
(7) So gender and race either are not genuinely socially constructed kinds, or they are not kinds with members at all—just free-floating collective ascriptions with no ontological weight.
(8) Meanwhile other institutional kinds, such as corporations and nations, are also distributed across people, land, and documents and lack a single physical substrate, yet we treat them as real.
(9) One might therefore reject (2) and allow distributed, substrate-free institutional kinds.
(10) But if there is no bearer constraint at all, then collective intentionality could in principle assign any status to any arbitrary collection without restriction—there would be no structural difference between constituting a nation and collectively hallucinating one.
(11) Thus keeping social constructionism about gender and race while avoiding ontological anarchy requires either finding hidden bearers for them or explaining why distributed kinds are still constrained.
(12) The argument therefore concludes that standard constructionism is unstable: it either eliminates gender and race as well-formed kinds, or it eliminates all constraints on institutional reality.

We want to preserve the constructionist denial of biological anchoring—steps (4) and (5)—and we want to keep money as a clear, unproblematic case in step (3). That leaves (2), (6), (9), (10), or (11) as likely suspects, though I am unsure whether the flaw is a single bad premise or a slide between different readings of "dependence."

Which numbered step should I reject? For that step, explain why it looked defensible and what the standard positions—institutionalist, strong social constructionist, and eliminativist about social kinds—each have to pay to reject it. I am especially reluctant to give up (4) and (5), since they capture the standard denial that sex or race serve as anchors, but I am open everywhere else. If the argument is actually valid and the cost must be paid elsewhere, say so and price the alternatives honestly.
