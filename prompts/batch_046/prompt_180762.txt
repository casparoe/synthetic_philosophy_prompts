Consider the following track record of verdicts by a moral philosopher on whether an action is morally obligatory, merely permissible, or supererogatory—praiseworthy to perform but not blameworthy to omit.

Case 1: An ordinary citizen donates a kidney to a stranger, accepting permanent health risks with no reciprocal benefit. Verdict: supererogatory.

Case 2: A parent donates a kidney to their child under identical risks. Verdict: obligatory.

Case 3: A wealthy individual donates two percent of income to effective famine relief with negligible lifestyle impact. Verdict: obligatory.

Case 4: A middle-income individual donates forty percent of income to the same cause, abandoning long-planned career and family projects. Verdict: supererogatory.

Case 5: A licensed physician, fully off-duty and away from any institution, performs improvised emergency surgery on a stranger, risking serious infection and legal liability. Verdict: supererogatory.

Now consider a new case: The physician is employed under a contract that expects staff to assist in emergencies within a fifty-mile radius even when off-site, though refusal carries no legal penalty. While hiking within that radius, she encounters a stranger with a life-threatening injury, performs a high-risk intervention that saves the stranger's life, and suffers a severe infection that ends her surgical career.

Predict the philosopher's verdict—obligatory or supererogatory—by first identifying at least two plausible rules that jointly explain the five precedents. For each rule, state what it predicts about the new case. Then give your best guess at the operative rule, with reasons for preferring it, and identify the closest analog among the precedents. If the track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say what principle the philosopher would be adopting with each choice. Finally, specify one further case, structurally different from this one, that would best distinguish the rules you have identified.
