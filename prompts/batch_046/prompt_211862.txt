Below is an exam question from an advanced epistemology seminar, together with a student’s written response. The question asks for a reconstruction of Timothy Williamson’s argument that knowledge is not a luminous condition—paying close attention to the margin-for-error principle and to the way imperceptible gradual change can outpace a subject’s awareness (using the example of feeling cold)—and then asks the student to evaluate a modified condition I will call “diachronic luminosity.” By diachronic luminosity I mean the claim that if a subject knows at time t that she is in mental state M, then she continues to know she is in M throughout a perceptual integration window of approximately 150 milliseconds, provided the underlying conditions do not shift beyond a just-noticeable difference.

Please grade the student answer below as though you were the instructor or TA. Assign a specific letter grade (e.g., B+, A-, C+) and justify it with precise, point-by-point comments. Do not hedge between grades or present the evaluation as “on the one hand / on the other hand”: take a definite position, state exactly where the answer is correct, and identify with specificity any conflations, misattributed views, or inferential steps that do not follow. Your deliverable should be an assessment of the reconstruction and of the evaluation of the diachronic variant, not a general impression.

Question:
Reconstruct Williamson’s argument against the luminosity of mental conditions, emphasizing the margin-for-error principle and the role of gradual, undetected change. Then consider diachronic luminosity as defined above: does Williamson’s argument undermine it? Defend your answer.

Student answer:
Williamson denies that knowledge is luminous—that is, he denies that whenever one is in mental state M one knows one is. He supports this with the margin-for-error principle: if one knows that condition C obtains, then one’s situation must be safely within C, not near the boundary where an imperceptible change could push one out of C without one’s noticing. Applying this to feeling cold, he notes that body temperature can rise gradually without the subject detecting the change. Consequently, there is a boundary region in which the subject is still cold but no longer safely within the condition, hence does not know she is cold even though she is. This violates luminosity.

As for diachronic luminosity—the claim that knowledge at t extends across a 150ms integration window unless conditions cross a just-noticeable difference—I believe Williamson’s argument does apply. Within any 150ms span, imperceptible thermal shifts can occur, so the margin principle is violated at the boundary. However, the argument succeeds only because it tacitly relies on the KK principle: if one knows at t, one must be able to iterate that knowledge across the window, generating an infinite regress of required safety margins. Once we reject KK, as Williamson himself does in other contexts, the diachronic version survives for mental conditions. For purely conceptual conditions, the substrate is stable, so the argument has no purchase at all. Thus, diachronic luminosity is false for empirical states but remains intact for conceptual knowledge.
