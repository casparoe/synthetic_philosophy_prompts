I am a retired ecologist who, since leaving full-time research, has taken up philosophy with some seriousness but without formal institutional training. Next Thursday I am presenting a short paper to a philosophy of science seminar—eight analytic philosophers, mostly epistemologists unfamiliar with ecology, plus two scientific realists and a philosopher of physics who treats metaphysical interpretation of models as decorative excess. My thesis is that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, standardly discussed in moral philosophy as the gap between reasons justifying an act and reasons justifying the agent’s motivation, has a precise structural analogue in the epistemology of scientific modelling. I contend that predictive accuracy and computational tractability supply the right kind of reasons for adopting a model instrumentally, yet they are the wrong kind of reasons for ontological commitment to its posited entities; conversely, mechanistic transparency and explanatory coherence provide the right kind of reasons for realism, but often fail to justify adoption in practice. The audience knows the moral literature well but has not encountered this extension. I need a Q&A preparation sheet, not a general defence. Please order the objections by the damage they are likely to do, phrase each as the relevant faction would phrase it—the epistemologists worrying about a category mistake, the realists invoking inference to the best explanation, the physicist charging pseudo-problem. For each, indicate what I ought to concede immediately, what I must hold, and where my reply is softest. Identify the single reply that has to land if the proposal is not to collapse, and flag the one question I most hope nobody raises: whether my ecological background confers any authority to redraw the boundary between instrumental and realist reasoning, given that I claim none beyond analogical argument. Finally, point out the weakest structural point in my position that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope they miss—the assumption of a sharp distinction between predictive and explanatory virtues, which recent philosophy of science has tended to erode.
