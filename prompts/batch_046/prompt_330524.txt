draft the joint statement for an adversarial collab between strict heterophenomenologists (dennett-style: first-person reports are texts, never authoritative, always need third-person interpretation) and moderate-access phenomenologists (reports carry defeasible but real evidential weight about qualitative structure, especially spontaneous ones from trained observers). they've been argueing past each other on whether heterophenomenology saves phenomenology from behaviorism or just hides the gap elsewhere, and on split-brain left-hemisphere reports of "nothing there."

format: numbered agreed-claims section, then disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions.

agreed claims should cover: what heterophenomenology is as a method (not an ontology of consciousness), the formal role of first-person reports in scientific practice, that split-brain cross-cueing data exists and needs explanation, that trained vs naive reporters differ systematically in consistency and detail, that neither camp denies consciousness exists (they disagree on epistemic access), and that silent/non-reporting subjects pose a shared puzzle rather than a settled case.

disagreements table: (1) whether any first-person report can be authoritative about qualitative content, with each side's wording they accept; (2) whether heterophenomenology relocates the explanatory gap or dissolves it, phrased neutrally; (3) whether reports of "there is something it is like but i can't describe it" (the intermediate report problem) should be treated as meaningful data, confabulation, or noise, with each camp's precise formulation.

cruxes: one is whether spontaneous reports correlate more tightly with neural markers than theorized reports (both agree this bears on it — HP predicts correlation is artifactual or equal across conditions, moderate-access predicts stronger spontaneous correlation). another is split-brain left-hemisphere denial reports: both agree interhemispheric suppression and cross-cueing experiments bear on this, with HP predicting literal interpretation or confabulation and moderate-access predicting a genuine absence-report that needs explaining. a third is extending the method to non-linguistic report architectures (trained button-press or preference protocols in non-human systems): does this generalize the textual-interpretation core or abandon it? both should predict what happens if such protocols show systematic divergence from human-style reports.

also include a short section identifying whether part of the dispute is verbal — specifically around "access" and "interpretation" — and say exactly which part and why.

write it casual, like a brief for a working group. don't ask for bibliographies or citations. don't quote anyone verbatim unless you can fetch the exact text. include the intermediate report problem as a concrete case both sides must address. make sure each crux has a concrete prediction recorded for both sides.
