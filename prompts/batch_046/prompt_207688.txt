Exam question: Compare the counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation. How does each framework analyse redundant causation — cases where two independent conditions are each sufficient for an effect? Furthermore, discuss whether Judea Pearl's do-operator can be accommodated within a David Lewis-style modal framework.

Student's answer:

The counterfactual account, developed by David Lewis, defines causation as counterfactual dependence between distinct events: C causes E if, in the nearest possible worlds where C does not occur, E does not occur either. Where redundant causation occurs — say, two fires both sufficient to burn a house — removing one fire still leaves the other, so the counterfactual test seems to fail for both. Lewis resolves this difficulty by appealing to causal chains: he traces a continuous chain of counterfactual dependencies linking cause to effect, which allows the overall process to count as causal even when individual links are overdetermined.

The interventionist account, associated with Judea Pearl, replaces possible-world talk with structural causal models and the do-operator. Applying do(X = x) represents an external intervention that fixes a variable's value and severs its incoming causal edges. To analyse redundancy, one examines whether the effect survives do-interventions on each cause separately. Because Pearl's method relies solely on algorithmic manipulation of equations, it avoids modal machinery altogether. Therefore, the do-operator cannot be interpreted within Lewis's similarity-based semantics; the two frameworks are conceptually incompatible, since one operates syntactically on graphs and equations while the other depends on metaphysical relations between worlds.
