I've noticed that when people compare the ethics of beef versus chicken, they almost always talk about how many farmed animals die per pound of food, and they advise switching to chicken—or to plants—to reduce that number. What strikes me is that this comparison almost never includes the wild animals—field rodents, insects, small birds—that are killed by tractors, pesticides, and harvesters in crop cultivation. I want to understand what view of moral harm or animal welfare this counting practice quietly assumes. Does it presuppose that only direct, intentional killing counts? That wild suffering lies outside the scope of human responsibility? Or that we should minimize only the suffering we can trace in a straight line back to our plate? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind the beef-versus-chicken ranking, say whether it can be made consistent with a broader concern for all sentient life, and tell me what practical dietary recommendations would have to change if we replaced its hidden assumptions with a view that weighs wild crop deaths equally. Please also identify the weakest step in your reconstruction and say what argument or empirical finding would lead you to reject the implicit theory you've laid out.
