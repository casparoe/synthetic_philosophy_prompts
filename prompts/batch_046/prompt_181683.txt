I was talking with some friends about Goodhart's law in the context of institutional design, and one of them walked me into this argument that feels like it proves social coordination is basically impossible. Here's how it goes: (1) Whenever a measurable proxy M is tied to rewards, people eventually optimize for M rather than the underlying value V it was meant to track. That's standard Goodhart. (2) Now consider "social trust" - call it V_t. Any explicit institutional metric for V_t, whether it's survey scores, audit results, or participatory budgeting turnout, is vulnerable to gaming. (3) So any society that tries to improve V_t through explicit reward structures tied to M_t will, over time, destroy actual trust and replace it with perfomative compliance. (4) But wait - you might think we can fix this by measuring the gaming itself and penalizing it. Let's call that meta-metric M_m. (5) The problem is that M_m is also a proxy for "genuine cooperation," so optimizing against detected gaming just creates a second-order game: people learn to game in ways that evade M_m. (6) This sets up an infinite regress. At every level n, the metric M_n for "not gaming M_(n-1)" becomes the new target of optimization. (7) Therefore, any large-scale institution that relies on explicit evalutation mechanisms is structurally self-defeating: it can never stabilize real cooperation, only deeper layers of performance. (8) But democracy, legal systems, and markets all depend on explicit evaluation. So if (7) holds, they should all eventually collapse into pure theater with no underlying cooperative value.

That conclusion feels way too strong, but I can't tell if the mistake is in (5) - maybe meta-monitoring works differently? - or in the jump from (3) to (6), or somewhere else. I want to keep (1) and probably (7) if I can, but I'm willing to drop (4) if that's the weak link. Can you tell me exactly which step is the problem, why it looked convincing, and what it costs me to reject it? Don't just say "human motivation is complex" - I want to know if there's a specific logical slip, like a scope error or a hidden assumption that every metric behaves the same way.
