I have been working through a philosophy of mind seminar that treats phenomenal consciousness not as a primitive datum but as a systematically misleading representation produced by hierarchical predictive processing—specifically, the view that qualitative experience possesses no intrinsic phenomenal properties, and that first-person reports of what-it-is-likeness are essentially confabulatory glosses on the brain's transparent self-modeling of attentional states. The instructor frames this strong illusionism as a direct descendant of mechanist physiology, yet one that denies even the minimal Cartesian intuition of an immediately present thinking substance. Suppose René Descartes had lived to encounter this position, having thoroughly absorbed modern cognitive neuroscience but retaining the core metaphysical commitments articulated in his published metaphysical writings and in his correspondence with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia.

How do you think he would respond? In particular, would he treat the illusionist denial of a Cartesian theater—understood here as the claim that predictive architecture only simulates an internal observer for behavioral regulation, never actually generating one—as a vindication of his own distinction between res extensa and res cogitans, on the grounds that mechanistic explanation can fully account for bodily behavior without touching the non-extended substance? Or would he argue that the illusionist commits a category mistake by treating the clear and distinct perception of thinking as itself merely another predictive artifact, thereby undermining the very foundation of his epistemology? Please lay out the reasoning he might offer, including how he might reply to the illusionist's charge that his indubitable awareness of thought is precisely the kind of transparent self-deception that a well-calibrated generative model should be expected to produce.
