I'm writing up some ethical commitments I actually hold after going through end-of-life decisions for my mom, and I want a consistancy check before I say anything dumb in a seminar. Here they are:

1. I'm a negative utilitarian in the basic sense: reducing suffering is the only fundamental moral duty. There's no corresponding obligation to create happiness, it's just not on the scoreboard.

2. I think suffering and happiness are genuinely incommensurable — there's no exchange rate — and I also hold a kind of discontinuity view: any suffering, however small, generates a moral priority that no amount of happiness can override.

3. I reject ant-natalist conclusions for net-positive lives. Having a child who'll mostly be happy isn't wrong, it's just not required.

4. I think rights like "don't kill" are purely instrumental — they prevent terror and social breakdown, not because death has intrinsic moral weight.

5. I also think that if we ever build artificial minds, we should avoid ones that can suffer. Specifically, I think only systems with a unified phenomenal field (like a global workspace) can suffer, so distributed computing is safe, but a unified AI with pain modules would be a moral catastrophe to create. Building happy-but-painless unified minds, though, is ethically neutral.

The doctors kept framing mom's care in terms of "quality-adjusted life years," which is exactly the kind of tradeoff thinking I reject. That experience is why this is personal now.

So: do these hang together? I feel like 1 and 2 together might destroy 3, and 4 might not survive extreme cases with 1, but I'm not sure if 4 and 2 are actually in tension or if I'm just confused. What's the least costly revision? Rank the real tensions by severity, and point out any obvious ones I'm missing. Also tell me which tensions only look bad but dissolve — I don't want to abandon a view just because it sounds weird next to another one.
