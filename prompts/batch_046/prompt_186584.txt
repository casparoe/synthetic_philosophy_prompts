I just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about which variety of free will is actually worth wanting, and she left me with a chain of reasoning that I cannot find the exit from. She introduces a condition she calls recursive authorization — the claim that an agent possesses free will worth wanting only if, at the precise moment of decision, the agent's own normative framework genuinely accommodates an alternative course of action that the agent could endorse without internal contradiction. From this she builds the following case.

(1) Recursive authorization is necessary for free will worth wanting.
(2) For an alternative to be genuinely accommodated within an agent's normative framework, it must be physically available; accommodating a physically impossible path is counterfactual fantasy, not normative reasoning.
(3) In a deterministic universe, no physically available alternative exists at the moment of decision, since every neural event is fixed by prior physical states.
(4) Therefore, deterministic agents lack recursive authorization.
(5) If we instead allow quantum-level neural indeterminacy, then physical alternatives become available.
(6) But if those alternatives arise from stochastic noise rather than from the agent's reasons, accommodating them within the agent's normative framework is impossible: one cannot endorse a path that bears no rational connection to one's values.
(7) Therefore, in indeterministic worlds, the available alternatives are normatively unendorsable, so recursive authorization still fails.
(8) Hence, whether the world is deterministic or indeterministic, free will worth wanting is impossible.
(9) Yet moral responsibility — the practice of holding people accountable — clearly presupposes that agents possess free will worth wanting.
(10) So either moral responsibility is systematically illusory, or the concept of free will worth wanting is incoherent.

She insists the argument is valid and that I must reject one of the premises, but I cannot see which step slips. I am particularly reluctant to give up (1), since recursive authorization captures exactly what distinguishes mere behavior from genuine agency, and I am also reluctant to abandon (9), because giving up moral responsibility seems to cost us too much in our ordinary social practices. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does rejecting (2), (5), or (6) each cost for the broader debate about determinism, indeterminism, and the varieties of free will worth wanting?
