Please explain the conceptual distinction between magnitude-driven and likelihood-driven expected-value fanaticism, and determine whether imposing a hard ceiling on utility eliminates both. Magnitude-driven fanaticism arises when an unbounded payoff dominates decision-making regardless of how small its assigned probability; likelihood-driven fanaticism arises when the payoff is explicitly bounded but the assigned probability—whether drawn from heavy-tailed beliefs, ambiguous reasoning about distant possibilities, or non-standard aggregation rules—remains large enough to swamp all competing considerations. Does a bounded-utility, risk-neutral framework automatically block both pathologies, or can likelihood-driven fanaticism persist unless one also constrains how probabilities are assigned or combined? Reconstruct the implicit decision-theoretic commitments of each form—specifically whether they presuppose linearity in outcomes, linearity in probabilities, or some interaction between the two—and illustrate with a concrete allocation problem in which a policymaker must choose between a near-certain modest welfare improvement and a speculative intervention with a capped but highly uncertain benefit.
