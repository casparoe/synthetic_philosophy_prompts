You are a philosopher of religion and comparative ethics with particular expertise in Buddhist metaphysics and moral philosophy. Before answering, take a moment to think carefully and outline the key distinctions at play—especially between karma (karman) as volition (cetana) and karma as external result, and between the Abhidharma analysis of action and the Mahayana doctrine of upaya-kausalya. Then address the following.

Here is an argument I constructed after working through Abhidharma literature on mental factors and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to erase the practical heart of Buddhist ethics. I would like you to diagnose exactly where the reasoning slips, if anywhere, and to price the alternatives.

(1) In classical Abhidharma, karma is defined essentially as cetana—volition or intention—rather than as bodily or verbal behavior. The ethical quality of an act (whether it is kusala or akusala) is determined entirely by the mental factors accompanying that volition, such as non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion, or their opposites.

(2) The external results of an action—its fruits (vipaka/phala) in the world—do not retroactively change the ethical quality of the original volition. A well-intentioned act that accidentally causes harm remains kusala in its karma; a malicious act that accidentally helps remains akusala.

(3) Therefore, ethical evaluation of an agent's action is complete once the volition has been assessed. The actual outcome plays no direct role in determining whether the karma itself is skillful or unskillful.

(4) At the same time, Buddhist ethics—especially in Mahayana accounts of the bodhisattva path—places enormous weight on upaya-kausalya (skillful means), which requires adapting one's actions precisely to reduce dukkha in the world. The tradition warns that even compassionate actions can cause harm if executed without wisdom, and praises actions that effectively liberate beings.

(5) If (3) holds, then a bodhisattva's deceptive or harmful act performed with pure compassion has exactly the same ethical quality—kusala—whether it succeeds in saving lives or backfires and causes mass suffering, provided the initial volition is identical.

(6) So Buddhist ethics seems to contain two incompatible evaluation schemes: one that judges actions solely by internal volition (making outcomes ethically irrelevant to the karma's quality) and one that judges actions by their effectiveness in reducing suffering (making outcomes central).

(7) One might reply that the volition fixes the karma's quality, while the outcome fixes only the strategic wisdom of the agent, so there is no contradiction. But if strategic wisdom is part of ethical evaluation—if a bodhisattva who causes harm through poor execution is ethically worse than one who succeeds—then the outcome does enter ethical assessment. That means two acts with identical volitions receive different overall ethical evaluations based solely on result, which undermines the claim in (1) that ethical quality is determined entirely by volition.

(8) Consequently, Buddhist ethics must either abandon the claim that karma is essentially cetana, or abandon the practical, outcome-sensitive orientation of upaya. Since it clearly does neither, the position is contradictory.

(9) By analogy, this would mean that in Buddhist ethics there is no meaningful distinction between a skillful lie that saves a life and an identical lie that accidentally kills—yet the tradition clearly wants to maintain that distinction.

If you must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than (5) or (8), but I want to understand exactly what I am paying if I reject one of the others. Which step should be given up, why did it look plausible, and what do the standard ways of resolving the tension—perhaps by distinguishing paramattha-sacca from sammuti-sacca, or by separating moral evaluation from soteriological assessment—each cost?
