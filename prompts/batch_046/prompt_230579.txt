There's this thing in heritage preservation law where a building stays legally protected — same listed status, same restrictions — even after like 90% of the physical material has been replaced over centuries. Think of those Japanese shrines they tear down and rebuild from scratch every 20 years, or the Warsaw old town reconstructed from rubble after the war. The law treats it as literally the same object, same rights, same protected identity. What view of material constitution does that practice presuposses? Does it need a four-dimentionalist picture where the building is a spacetime worm made of temporal stages, or can a three-dimensionalist who cares about form or social function make it work? And is the practice actually coherent, or does it flip-flop — like if you replace parts slowly it's the same building, but if you bulldoze and rebuild from scratch it's suddenly a copy? Reconstruct the implicit metaphyics, say whether rival views could license the same law, and tell me what preservation practice would have to change if we went full statue-and-clay: every replaced beam kills the original and the protection dies with it.
