Write a poem of six stanzas, each exactly six lines long in unrhymed iambic pentameter, structured so that every stanza divides into a "Given" (lines 1–3) and a "Construction" (lines 4–6), mimicking the form of a Euclidean proposition. Take as your subject Thomas Hobbes's conviction, articulated across De Corpore and the Introduction to Leviathan, that civil philosophy ought to imitate geometry: that reasoning is computation with names, that geometric figures are generated by the motion of bodies, and that a commonwealth can be demonstrated from first principles as rigorously as a triangle. Weave into this the fact of Hobbes's own failed attempt to square the circle and his long dispute with Wallis, treating that failure not as mere biographical irony but as a structural feature of the method—human construction always risks error even when the form of demonstration is perfect. Let the poem's repetition of the Given/Construction split across six stanzas enact the iterative promise of geometric proof, while the content of the final Construction leaves unresolved whether the quadrature's failure undermines the political application of the method or merely exposes the fallibility of the geometer. Do not resolve the tension with a sermon about humility or a triumphalist defense of science; end on an image—perhaps of a circle drawn in sand, or of a sovereign constructed from lines that do not quite meet—that holds both aspirations in suspension. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of early modern philosophy recognizes Hobbes's materialist ontology of geometry and his political project, and should work as a poem for a reader who knows none of this.
