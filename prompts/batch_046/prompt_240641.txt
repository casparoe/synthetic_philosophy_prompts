I'm working through what it actually means for a technology to "survive," using Von Neumann's framework of self-replicating automata and technological inheritance as background, but my judgments refuse to line up neatly with physical replication alone. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I'm hoping either for a coherent principle that captures them or for an honest statement of which ones I should abandon.

A computer worm that propagates across networks, rewriting its own copies without human intervention, clearly survives in the strong, Von Neumann-like sense—this is near the self-replicative ideal. By contrast, the precise manufacturing technique for Damascus steel disappeared in the eighteenth century despite extensive written descriptions remaining in archives; I judge that it did not survive, which suggests that archival preservation—call it mnemonic stasis—does not count. The QWERTY keyboard layout persists globally despite being demonstrably suboptimal; I say it survives, but through institutional lock-in rather than replication or active transmission. The legal doctrine of consideration in contract law also persists, yet through continuous judicial reinterpretation rather than physical copying; I feel it survives, but in a way closer to a living tradition than to QWERTY's inertia. Meanwhile, a fully specified nuclear reactor design that exists only in secured digital files and has never been built strikes me as not having survived, analogous to Damascus steel. The abacus, still taught and used in specific educational contexts, survives, but only where there is active pedagogical transmission; if all schools stopped teaching it, I think it would lapse into mnemonic stasis and cease to survive, even if copies remained in museums.

The tension I keep returning to is between contract law and Damascus steel: both depend on human carriers and continuous interpretation or practice, yet I judge one to have survived and the other to have failed. Meanwhile QWERTY and the abacus both persist without being self-replicating, but QWERTY feels like dead weight while the abacus feels like genuine survival, though I cannot say exactly why. I have been toying with a distinction between autopoietic survival—systems that maintain their own functional form, whether through physical replication or self-correcting institutional practice—and heteropoietic persistence, where an external framework keeps the form intact without internal self-maintenance. On this sketch, the worm is autopoietic; contract law might be; QWERTY and the abacus might be heteropoietic but with different outcomes; and mnemonic stasis is heteropoietic failure. Does any refined version of this distinction actually deliver all six verdicts, or does it collapse? Which of my judgments—especially about contract law versus Damascus steel, or about QWERTY versus the abacus—would a cleaner principle force me to overturn, and what would the repaired account predict for a technology like a language that survives only in automated translation databases with no native speakers?
