I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about zombie conceivability arguments, going case by case, and I'm all over the place. Here are my gut verdicts.

Case A: The standard philosophical zombie — molecule-for-molecule identical to me, behaves exactly the same, but has zero phenomenal consciousness. I can conceive this pretty clearly, so I take it that if conceivability implies possibility, physicalism is in trouble.

Case B: Gradual silicon swap. Every neuron gets replaced one-by-one with a chip that copies its input-output profile, no behavioral change. By the end the brain is all silicon, behaves identically. I still think I can conceive this version having no inner life, though I'm a bit shakier than with A.

Case C: The localized gap. Same physical human except one cortical column in V4 is just missing — not replaced, but the rest of the brain rewires so behavior is totally normal. The person navigates fine, reports nothing wrong, but genuinely has no color experience at all. Here my intuition flips: I don't think I can genuinely conceive this without some hidden trace or deficit. The absence feels like it should leave a footprint somewhere.

Case D: The inverted-spectrum twin. Physically identical, but every color quale is swapped. Conceivable, sure, but that's about qualitative content, not absence.

Case E: The historical zombie world. Everything physically identical to ours, evolutionary history and all, except consciousness never emerged; every creature is a zombie, yet they write philosophy papers about minds. I can conceive it, but it feels heavier, less imaginatively stable than A — like the sustained global absence should collapse.

My waver case, the one I keep circling back to: Case F, the modal-proximity zombie. A being with a brain physically identical to mine in every respect except that in its world the gravatational constant is 0.0001% different. The brain develops the same, behaves the same, but consciousness never arises. Sometimes I think this is just Case A with a hidden parameter; other times I think it's cheating — if consciousness depends on the actual physics of my brain, tweaking a constant should either change the brain or change nothing, not selectively erase experience.

So is there a principle that captures when I treat absence-of-experience as conceivable versus not? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop if I want it simple? What does it predict for F? And please be honest about where the reasoning is weakest — especially whether the problem is in my case judgments or in the conceivability-to-possibility bridge itself. Also say specifically what argument or finding would change your mind: for instance, if someone showed that localized neural absence always produces some detectable behavioral difference, or if the modal-proximity case turned out to be incoherent.
