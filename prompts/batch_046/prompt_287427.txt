I have no formal training in ethics or ecology, but I have been working through by myself whether eating beef is morally worse than eating chicken once one counts not only the farmed animals but also the wild creatures killed in growing feed crops and the land-use effects of grazing. Rather than collecting arguments I have seen repeated elsewhere, I want to know what standards any adequate moral theory of this comparison must actually satisfy. Please write out the full set of desiderata that a theory comparing beef, chicken, and crop-based diets ought to meet — with the specific case or consideration behind each one. Among other things, address whether the theory must distinguish direct killing from indirect crop-field mortality; whether it must incorporate what I call the pasture-shadow problem, namely that grazing land may displace wild herbivore populations whose lives and deaths would otherwise occur; whether it needs to take a position on invertebrate sentience in agricultural soils; whether it must remain neutral between consequentialist and rights-based frameworks; and whether it has to deliver precise quantitative rankings despite limited data. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. If any group of these requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — for example, requiring both exact suffering-per-calorie calculations and strict agnosticism about which organisms are moral patients — state that explicitly, since that tension is precisely what I want to see mapped. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim, and identify the single point about which you feel least certain.
