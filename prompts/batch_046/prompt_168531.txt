I have recently begun working with a local governance NGO in a region where officials routinely receive small gifts from citizens seeking administrative services, and where these exchanges are widely interpreted as expressions of respect and reciprocal obligation rather than as bribery. This experience has made me deeply unsure whether our organization's strict, Western-derived anti-corruption framework is ethically appropriate or whether it risks imposing an alien institutional morality on practices that function as legitimate social capital. I want you to write an essay addressing the controversy over whether clientelism and routine gift-giving to bureaucrats should be classified as corruption, or whether Western legal-rational standards improperly universalize a particular historical model of public office.

Your essay should weigh the view that corruption necessarily involves a breach of formal, impersonal rules—so that clientelism counts as corruption only when it systematically subverts merit or equality of access—against the view that corruption is best defined functionally, as any exchange that erodes public trust or creates dependency, regardless of local normative framing. You should also consider whether a stable intermediate position exists: for instance, that reciprocal gift practices can be legitimate when they remain diffuse, non-exclusionary, and subordinate to formal procedures, but become corrupt once they harden into predictable, exclusive channels of privilege. Reach and defend a clear conclusion—whether Western standards should be applied with only modest adjustments, rejected as parochial, or qualified by a context-sensitive criterion—and justify it with reasons rather than intuition.

Please distinguish carefully between what is conceptually settled in this debate—such as the idea that corruption involves some diversion of public authority for private or particularistic ends—and what remains genuinely contested, including whether cultural legitimacy can ever excuse systematic favoritism, whether corruption requires an explicit quid pro quo, and whether liberal institutional norms are genuinely universal or historically contingent. Do not present any of these contested normative or empirical claims as if they represent the consensus among philosophers, anthropologists, or practitioners who study institutional ethics.
