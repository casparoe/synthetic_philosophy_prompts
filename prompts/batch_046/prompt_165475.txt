Please provide a rigorous conceptual explanation of moral luck, with particular attention to the distinction between circumstantial and resultant luck. To avoid a purely abstract treatment, apply the framework to the following constructed case: a public-health administrator approves the emergency deployment of a vaccine whose adverse-event profile is well-characterized except for an unanticipated interaction with a rare genetic variant; one recipient with that variant suffers irreversible harm, even though the decision was ex ante optimal, fully informed by available data, and endorsed by standard risk-benefit protocols. Examine whether this produces the asymmetry characteristic of moral luck—specifically, whether the administrator is subject to agent-regret in Bernard Williams's sense despite lacking culpable ignorance or negligence. Then consider the deeper metaphysical question: is the moral significance of the outcome anchored in the counterfactual stability of the agent's internal character across possible worlds, or does the actual result retroactively constitute a feature of the agent's practical identity, regardless of psychological change?
