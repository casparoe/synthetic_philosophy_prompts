I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about Fitch-style knowability, going by my gut on specific scenarios rather than teh formal proof. Case 1: there's a true but unobserved cosmic background fluctuation in a region we'll never build a telescope to see — I think it's knowable in principle (someone could have built one) but not actually known, and that doesn't bother me. Case 2: there's a true statement "no one in this room knows this statement is true" — I feel like if it were true and someone knew it, it'd be false, so maybe it's not genuinely knowable, or at least not knowably-knowable. Case 3: a mathematician proves a theorem but dies before writing it down, and the proof is lost forever; the theorem is true, was known for a moment, but now isn't known by anyone. I want to say it was knowable then and remains knowable now (someone could rediscover it), but there's something wierd about saying a fact is "known" if all traces vanish. Case 4: an AI system computes a correct weather prediction but has no concept of belief or justification — is the fact known? I lean yes if knowledge just needs accurate representation plus reliability, but no if it requires a subject with understanding. Case 5: there's a hidden fact about my own mental state that I could never access due to a structural blindspot, not psychological but logical — I think this is genuinely unknowable for me, not just unknown.

The hard pair for me is Case 1 vs Case 5: both involve barriers, yet one feels unknown-but-knowable and the other unknown-and-unknowable. Meanwhile Case 3 makes me waver: if knowledge can vanish completely, does that affect whether the underlying fact was "knowable" in the Fitch sense? Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a distributed network where each node knows part of a fact but no single node grasps the whole? And honestly, which of my verdicts do I have to drop if I want a coherent account?

Please also say what would change your mind — like which argument or consideration would push you toward thinking all unknown truths are in fact unknown-because-unknowable, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
