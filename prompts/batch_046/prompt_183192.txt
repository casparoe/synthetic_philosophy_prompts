Following a long and rather exhausting argument last night with a close friend about whether destroying a user's persistent avatar-asset in a shared virtual environment constitutes a real harm, I find myself unable to shake a particular confusion. My friend argued that because virtual objects lack spatiotemporal location and are reducible to server-state configurations, they cannot bear the kind of ontological weight required for genuine property rights or damage; at most, virtual harm is a psychological injury mediated by representation, structurally no different from distress caused by a fictional narrative. I resisted by noting that virtual objects exhibit persistence, intersubjective accessibility, and internal normative governance—users treat them as owned, exchange them, and organize collective projects around them—yet my friend replied that these features are merely patterns of human behavior projected onto data, not intrinsic properties of the objects. This exchange has left me with a nagging suspicion that my own position rests on a false presupposition. If I claim that virtual harms are real because participants experience them as meaningful losses within the world's internal logic, have I not already conceded that virtual reality is purely conventional or subjective, which is precisely my friend's conclusion? Conversely, if I assert that virtual objects possess a non-physical but genuinely ontological status—analogous perhaps to institutional entities—I must then explain why the destruction of a virtual artifact feels closer to property damage than to the dissolution of a corporation by collective vote. Is my mistake the assumption that real harm must be grounded either in physical causation or in institutional ontology, thereby overlooking a distinct persistence condition that applies specifically to virtual entities? Or is the deeper error my belief that harm can be evaluated independently of the metaphysical status of its object?
