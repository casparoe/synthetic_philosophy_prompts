I have recently been appointed to an advisory board for a national archive that is restructuring its permanent exhibition on state formation. The board is divided over how to characterize the age and continuity of our national community. Some members invoke Benedict Anderson’s imagined communities and Eric Hobsbawm’s invention of tradition to argue that the nation as we know it is essentially a modern, nineteenth-century construct—an imagined solidarity made possible by print capitalism and elite-manufactured rituals—while others insist that pre-modern ethnic, linguistic, and religious continuities provide a genuine substrate that modern nationalism merely politicized rather than invented.

The controversy has become sharper since a historian on the board proposed distinguishing synchronic invention—the horizontal, simultaneous imagination of strangers as compatriots—from diachronic retrojection, the vertical projection of present institutions onto a selectively reconstructed past. She contends that Hobsbawm and Anderson are often conflated, but that the first describes a temporal fabrication (making the present seem ancient) while the second describes a spatial fabrication (making distant strangers seem intimate). If this distinction holds, then asking how old nations are is ambiguous: it could mean asking when people began to imagine strangers as fellow nationals, or asking when present elites began to claim ancient legitimacy. Write an essay examining whether this distinction strengthens or weakens the constructivist thesis about national age. Weigh the argument that pre-modern kingdoms, religious communities, or linguistic networks already contained proto-national elements against the claim that these lacked the specific institutional and cognitive architecture of modern nationalism. Reach a definite conclusion: are nations best understood as old communities wearing modern institutional masks, or as modern institutions wearing old cultural masks? Justify your verdict with reference to the mechanisms of collective identity formation rather than with a mere catalog of historical examples. Limit your response to 600 words or fewer.
