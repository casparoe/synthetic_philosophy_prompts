I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been trying to articulate a conceptual tension I keep encountering in my team's engineering culture. In our profession, we enforce an almost radical skepticism toward first-person reports about external systems: if a developer says "it works on my machine," we treat the claim as epistemically worthless until it is reproduced by logs, automated tests, or a standardized build environment. We have institutionalized a kind of hygiene that treats subjective observation of physical or digital processes as unreliable by default, and we organize our entire quality-assurance infrastructure—continuous integration, telemetry, reproducible deployments—around the idea that only third-party-verifiable data counts. Yet when the object of observation is our own mental state, we suddenly invert this posture. We practice rubber-duck debugging precisely because articulating our internal confusion is supposed to reveal structural errors that no log can capture; we prioritize sprint planning around self-reported "blockers" and "cognitive load"; and we will authorize expensive refactoring because a senior engineer reports that a module "feels fragile," even in the absence of performance metrics or failure rates. The institutional norm appears to be that introspection is deeply untrustworthy about the world but authoritative about the mind.

What philosophical commitments does this bifurcated practice actually presuppose? Does it require a view of mental states as enjoying a special first-person authority that physical processes lack, or can it be explained by purely practical considerations—say, that mental telemetry is technically impossible and verbal report is the best tractable proxy? Is the practice internally coherent, or does it quietly depend on rival theories of knowledge and mind that pull in opposite directions—for instance, a physicalist ontology that should, in principle, treat brain states as no more transparent than server states? Furthermore, if we were to extend the skepticism we apply to "works on my machine" uniformly to cognitive self-reports—treating "I feel confused by this abstraction" as no more authoritative than "the server feels slow"—what would have to change in how we organize debugging rituals, retrospectives, and technical decision-making? I am especially interested in whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: whether several incompatible theories of introspective reliability could all license the same engineering norms, or whether the norms actually commit us to one view and rule out others.
