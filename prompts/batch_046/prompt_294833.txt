I have been working through Dennett's brain-transplant scenario—in which a subject's brain is extracted, copied into different substrates, and eventually destroyed, leaving potential successors in a repaired body and in a computer system—alongside some recent discussions of distributed cognition, and I have arrived at a specific position that I want stress-tested rather than defended. The view is that the puzzle does not reveal a hidden metaphysical fact about which substrate houses the real self, but rather exposes a category mistake in assuming that personal identity must be a binary, all-or-nothing relation anchored to a unique spatiotemporal path. Instead, I propose that identity in such cases should be understood as a graded property of narrative-functional coherence: a later stage counts as part of the same person to the degree that it maintains the right causal-informational and autobiographical links to earlier stages, and this relation can genuinely hold across branching copies without requiring uniqueness. If two post-branch substrates both integrate the pre-branch narrative and can each justify treating the earlier stage as part of their own autobiography, then both stand in a real, if partial, identity relation to the original—there is no further fact that selects one branch as the sole legitimate successor. This dissolves the anxiety the scenario produces, but at the cost of allowing that personal identity can be genuinely shared and comes in degrees, which I take to be a theoretical virtue rather than a reductio.

What would have to be true for this graded, distributable account to be wrong? I am not asking for a restatement of standard physical-continuity objections or for a repetition of the uniqueness worry; I want to know which specific observations, conceptual commitments, or results in other domains—say, about the persistence conditions of artifacts, institutions, or biological organisms—would genuinely undermine the framework. Which predictions does this view make that strict brain-continuity or standard psychological-continuity accounts do not, and what evidence or argument would show those predictions to fail? Please rank the defeaters from fatal—those that would force abandonment of the graded-identity model—to merely awkward adjustments, distinguishing between objections I should concede as damaging and those I could dismiss as resting on pre-theoretical intuition. Be explicit about whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a theory of this kind. I am particularly interested in whether pressure from non-personal persistence conditions makes treating persons differently untenable, and whether, once all necessary qualifications are added, the view collapses back into one of its rivals.
