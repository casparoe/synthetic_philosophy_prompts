I am presenting a paper next Thursday in the philosophy of language and social epistemology seminar at my university, and I want to be ready for the discussion that follows the talk rather than surprised by it. The room will contain two committed anti-reductionists who treat testimony as a basic source of knowledge on a par with perception and memory; a social epistemologist who studies collective belief formation and tends to regard individualist frameworks as missing the institutional scaffolding that makes testimony possible in the first place; a skeptical doctoral student who believes the reductionism versus anti-reductionism framing is a false dichotomy inherited from Reid's polemical reading of Hume; and several agnostic listeners who are mainly attending to see whether the debate still has philosophical life. I have been working through the material with a colleague outside philosophy who keeps asking why we should not treat trusting a speaker as calibrated induction sustained by social feedback loops rather than as either pure private evidence or an irreducible testimonial source, and I have adopted that framing—calling it calibrated reductionism—to organize my argument.

My thesis is that testimonial justification is genuinely reducible, but not to private speaker-reliability statistics or raw sensory tracking; rather, it reduces to a mechanism in which individual hearers continuously adjust their acceptance thresholds through distributed social calibration. This means testimony is not a sui generis epistemic source, yet it also cannot be reconstructed without reference to the social networks that stabilize reliability over time. I need to defend this middle position against the charge that it secretly collapses into strict Humean reductionism on one side or disguised anti-reductionism on the other.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased in the vocabulary each interlocutor would actually use. For each objection, indicate what I should concede up front, where I must hold firm, and the weakest point in my own position that I should be ready to defend rather than hoping they overlook it. Include the one objection I am privately dreading—likely the charge that calibrated reductionism is merely anti-reductionism with extra steps—and suggest a reply tight enough to survive a brief exchange. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I need a strategic map of the room.
