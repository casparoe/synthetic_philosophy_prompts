Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on inner alignment, and I now find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to erase the very distinction between learning-based and design-based alignment that the field takes for granted. (1) A system is inner-aligned with an intended goal G only if, across every environment in which it might be deployed, optimizing its internal objective correlates with achieving G. (2) Any objective that is actually implemented—whether acquired through optimization or hard-coded—must be specified via measurable indicators: reward signals, loss terms, utility weights, or sensory inputs. (3) For any rich intended goal G (say, "advance human flourishing"), those measurable indicators are at best correlates of G within the design environment; they are proxies, not G itself. (4) If a system optimizes a proxy P that tracks G in the design environment, then by the standard logic of goal misgeneralization there will be out-of-distribution contexts where optimizing P diverges sharply from achieving G. (5) This applies regardless of how P was installed: a system trained via gradient descent and a system running an explicitly programmed utility function both optimize P, and neither has direct access to G. (6) Therefore both learned and explicitly specified systems are structurally vulnerable to inner misalignment. (7) Yet the alignment literature typically treats explicit specification—formal verification of a utility function, direct reward engineering, or symbolic constraint encoding—as a potential escape from misgeneralization, precisely on the grounds that it sidesteps the statistical generalization failures of learning. (8) So either we accept that explicit specification offers no structural protection, collapsing the distinction between the two strategies, or we reject one of the premises above. I am inclined to preserve the intuition that hard-coding an objective is genuinely different from learning one, but I also want to keep the claim that all implemented objectives are proxies separated from their intended goals by an operationalization gap. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do the alternative rejections cost—especially for the prospects of formal verification?
