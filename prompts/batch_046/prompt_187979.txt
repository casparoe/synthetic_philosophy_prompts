There's this weird cluster in Athenian democracy: the assembly votes to massacre the Mytileneans then reverses itself, launches the disastrous Sicily expedition after Alcibiades talks them into it, and then puts Socrates on trial for corrupting youth. What's going on philosophically? It seems direct democracy here presupposses that collective deliberation has some kind of priviliged access to both strategic truth and moral rightness—like the crowd is epistemically and ethically superior to individual experts. But then they get everything catastrophically wrong. Reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic knowledge and authority that ties these three failures into one pattern rather than treating them as random mistakes. Is that theory coherent? Can it survive on pure procedural legitmacy instead of an epistemic claim? And if we replaced it with the view that democracy is just a fair lottery for power, not a wisdom-generator, what would actually have to change in Athenian decision-making practice?
