I have noticed a recurring pattern in how psychiatric categories evolve once they are framed as spectra rather than discrete entities, and I am unsure whether it has a specific name or falls under an existing concept that I am misapplying. Consider a diagnosis initially defined by a severe, relatively homogeneous clinical picture—say, a condition marked by pronounced dissociative episodes and marked functional impairment. Over time the category is reconceptualized as lying on a continuum with ordinary experience, and diagnostic criteria are broadened to include milder, subclinical variants. The usual story here involves diagnostic inflation or the horizontal expansion sometimes discussed under the label of concept creep: more people qualify, prevalence estimates rise, and the category's social meaning shifts. But I keep seeing something more specific and, I think, less thoroughly discussed. As the spectrum expands, clinicians begin to describe even the severe, classic presentations differently—not merely as statistically rarer, but as somehow altered in kind—because the assessment instruments, therapeutic vocabularies, and patient self-narratives developed for the milder end become the default framework for the entire taxonomy. Meanwhile, individuals near the new boundary do not merely seek the label for practical reasons; they begin to inhabit and perform emotional and behavioral patterns associated with it, not out of malingering but because the classification offers a culturally available script for organizing ambiguous experience. The result is a kind of double drift: the category pulls outward, absorbing adjacent experiences, but it also pulls inward, displacing the clinical and phenomenological center of the original severe form. This seems related to Hacking's looping effects, yet Hacking's account focuses primarily on how being classified changes the classified individual; here the loop also runs backward, altering the recognition conditions and the lived reality of the very cases that originally anchored the category. It is not quite the same as ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustment or the kind of immunizing strategy Popper criticized, because the change is not a defensive theoretical patch but a structural transformation of the taxonomy driven by its own expanded membership. Is there an established term—perhaps within philosophy of psychiatry, science studies, or classification theory—for this particular two-directional dynamic, in which spectrum expansion reshapes both the boundary cases and the core cases through interacting looping mechanisms? If the phenomenon is recognized but lacks a precise label, I would welcome a careful delineation of what exactly it comprises, what it is not, and where its edges lie—distinguishing it clearly from diagnostic inflation, concept creep, and standard looping effects—along with a proposed name and definition if none exists.
