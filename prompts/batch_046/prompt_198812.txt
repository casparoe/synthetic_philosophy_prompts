I will defend, at next week's philosophy of mind colloquium, a non-standard interpretation of Dennett's multiple drafts model directed against the Cartesian theater. Rather than claiming consciousness is merely distributed, I argue that the core error is temporal, not spatial: phenomenal unity is produced by a post-perceptual editorial stage that binds disparate drafts into a coherent narrative only after a latency window on the order of hundreds of milliseconds. I call the result retrospective phenomenal coherence. On this view, there is no central observer, but neither is there raw, unbound multiplicity; unity is a representational achievement that misrepresents itself as immediate because the consolidation process is itself inaccessible to introspection. I can therefore concede that first-person reports of a single stream are largely accurate in content, while denying that they reveal a metaphysically unified subject or an instantaneous present.

The audience is specific and skeptical: two phenomenologists committed to a minimal, pre-reflective self in the Zahavi and Gallagher tradition, who will say Dennett confuses the absence of a homunculus with the absence of givenness; three cognitive scientists who accept global workspace theory but reject any elimination of phenomenal unity, because they think it undermines the empirical study of conscious access; and one visual neuroscientist who works on temporal binding, backward masking, and flash-lag effects, and who will immediately ask whether my latency window has any neural correlate beyond speculative timing.
