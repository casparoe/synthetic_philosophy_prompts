I was arguing with my flatmate about Hacking's looping effects after a philosphy of psychiatry lecture, and I tried to formalise my worry into an argument that seems to prove far too much — but I can't see the exact slide. Here it is:

(1) Hacking claims psychiatric categories are human kinds rather than natural kinds, because being classifed can loop back and change people — they alter their self-understanding, behaviour, and even symptom presentation, so the kind is partly constituted by the classification rather than just discovered.

(2) But imagine a proposed diagnosis called algorithmic attention dysregulation (AAD), where clinicians label people whose attention patterns have been reshaped by reccomendation-engine loops. Once diagnosed, patients start using blockers, joining identity groups, and reframing distractibility as illness — clear looping.

(3) Type-1 diabetes works the same way: diagnosis makes patients monitor glucose, adopt a diabetic identity, and radically change habits — looping happens here too.

(4) So if looping is what prevents a kind being natural, diabetes isn't natural either; it's as socially constructed as AAD or autism.

(5) But diabetes tracks an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic cells that exists regardless of anyone diagnosing it, so (4) is false.

(6) Therefore looping can't be the marker that separates human kinds from natural kinds, which collapses Hacking's distinction — psychiatric categories are either all natural kinds, or the framework is useless.

Every step feels plausible, but the conclusion is obviously too strong. Which premise or inference should I give up? I'm tempted to say there's an equivocation between looping that reshapes the underlying condition and looping that just reshapes how people cope with an unchanged condition — but then I worry I'm just assuming psychiatric looping is deeper without argument. What's the standard reply here, and what does each option cost?
