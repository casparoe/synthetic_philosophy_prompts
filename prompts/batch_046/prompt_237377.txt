I'm taking a philosophy-of-language seminar and have been arguing with friends from a rationalist forum about whether Orwell's critique of political writing applies symmetrically to modern technical jargon. The standard reading of essays like "Politics and the English Language" treats Orwell as attacking euphemism and dead metaphor because they protect ideology from scrutiny—think of "pacification" standing in for the bombing of civilians. But there's a stronger, less-discussed interpretation: Orwell may be advancing a general epistemic principle that any abstraction away from concrete, everyday language is an obscuring of thought, regardless of the writer's motives. If the stronger reading holds, then the neologisms common in AI alignment and decision-theoretic discourse—terms coined to describe causal mechanisms that ordinary language cannot cleanly separate—would fall under the same condemnation, not because they hide moral horror but because they replace visible reality with verbal machinery.

Write an essay on whether this extension is legitimate. You should distinguish carefully between a euphemism, which conceals a moral fact that should be seen, and a technical term, which attempts to expose a structural feature that common sense misses. Weigh both interpretations of Orwell—targeted critique of ideological defense versus general prohibition on non-windowpane abstraction—and defend one. Your conclusion should take a clear stand: either that technical vocabulary is innocent when it tracks distinctions with real explanatory power, or that Orwell's standard genuinely demands concrete paraphrase even in specialized reasoning, and that rationalist discourse should reform its vocabulary accordingly. As part of your verdict, address the reflexive problem: Orwell himself invented terms like "doublethink" and used "proles" in his fiction. Are these exceptions that prove the rule because they illuminate power relations, or do they show that Orwell's principle was always selectively applied?
