In common-law appellate practice, courts routinely treat longstanding statutory interpretations as carrying weight that exceeds their current perceived correctness, requiring more than mere disagreement with prior reasoning to justify overturning them. What philosophical account of accumulated institutional knowledge does that practice presuppose? Must it rest on a Burkean conviction that judicial tradition encodes tacit wisdom that individual reasoners cannot fully articulate, or is it equally compatible with an instrumental view that precedent is valuable only as a coordination device? Read through the logic of Chesterton's fence — that one ought not dismantle an institutional barrier before grasping precisely why it was erected — does the doctrine require judges to reconstruct the original legislative purpose before departing from it, or can it survive on a thinner evolutionary theory of common-law growth? And if the same legal system treats stability as decisive in statutory interpretation but largely discounts it in administrative-rule challenges, do those two branches share a consistent philosophical commitment, or do they quietly presuppose different theories of value?
