I'm drafting the conceptual framework for a comparative institutional-ethics paper that I began after visiting a Scandinavian correctional facility last month and then spending several weeks comparing it with the American carceral system. I need the normative criteria first, before I apply them, and I want them sharp enough that they can actually adjudicate between radically different designs rather than just restating familiar slogans.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of penal purpose and prison design must satisfy if it is meant to cover both Norway's Halden Prison—where architecture follows the principle of normality, guards are trained as social workers rather than security personnel, and the explicit institutional aim is to preserve and restore the prisoner's capacity for ordinary social life—and the American model of mass incarceration, with its supermax units, mandatory minimum sentencing structures, and explicit institutional emphasis on security, separation, and retributive proportionality.

For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or phenomenon it must explain, mark whether it is non-negotiable or belongs mainly to a particular camp (retributivist, utilitarian, restorative justice, abolitionist, or institutionalist), and note what theoretical cost attends giving it up. Be sure to include at least these dimensions: (a) an account of why deprivation of liberty is the specific punishment rather than some other harm, and whether the theory permits that deprivation to be accompanied by conditions that closely mirror free life; (b) a resolution of the tension between expressing moral condemnation of an offense and preserving the offender's civic and psychological capacities for eventual reintegration; (c) an explanation of the epistemic asymmetry between institutional designers and incarcerated people—how a theory addresses the fact that those who design prison regimes do not live inside them; (d) a stance on whether prison conditions must be consistent with the prisoner's continuing moral status, or whether that status can be partially suspended during punishment; and (e) an explanation of how the same theory can justify, without contradiction, either Halden-style normalization or American-style incapacitation and degradation of conditions, if indeed it can justify both.

Crucially, examine whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether the requirement that punishment express communal moral condemnation can coexist with the requirement that prison conditions maintain the prisoner's capacities for democratic citizenship and economic participation after release. If there is a genuine conflict, identify it precisely, state which camp must pay the price, and say whether the conflict undermines one camp or merely forces it into a qualified version of its view. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating institutional cases rather than on a scorecard of named theorists, though you may briefly note which mainstream positions most clearly fail which desiderata if that illuminates the criteria themselves.

Structure your response as follows:

- Conceptual framing (approximately 150 words): define what counts as an adequate theory in this domain and explain why the Halden versus American contrast makes the problem conceptually acute rather than merely empirical.
- The desiderata (approximately 500 words): the list with motivating cases, negotiability markers, theoretical costs of rejection, and explicit attention to the five dimensions above.
- Joint unsatisfiability assessment (approximately 200 words): explicit analysis of which subsets conflict, with special attention to the condemnation-reintegration tension, and a clear statement of the implications for theory choice.
- Brief illustrative scoring (approximately 150 words): concise notes on how retributivist, utilitarian, restorative, and abolitionist approaches fare against the list, used only to illustrate the criteria rather than to rank theories comprehensively.
