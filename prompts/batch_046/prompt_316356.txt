Two friends of mine, Maya and Jonah, got into a long disagreement after visiting a museum that displayed what it called a reconstituted original — a 17th-century still life destroyed in a fire, rebuilt molecule by molecule using archived chemical analyses and high-resolution scans. The pigments, canvas weave, and even the craquelure were reproduced to be physically identical, and the museum presented it as the painting itself, restored through reconstruction.

Maya said this was essentially a perfect forgery with excellent marketing. The actual object was destroyed; what hangs there now was made by different hands, in a different century, with different intentions. She argued that we value originals not just for how they look but for their specific causal history — the painter's brushstrokes, the accidental aging of varnish, the uninterrupted chain of existence — and this reconstruction possesses none of that history, regardless of its appearance. Jonah replied that if every physical property is restored, including at the microscopic level, then the causal history is actually continuous at the material level: the painting just is that specific arrangement of matter, and arranging the same matter in the same way restores the object rather than copying it. Maya then introduced a comparison to watch repair: if you disassemble a watch, clean the original surviving parts, and reassemble them, you still have the original watch — but she insisted there is a crucial difference between repairing parts that endured and manufacturing entirely new replacements for every atom that was lost. Jonah countered with an analogy to organ transplantation: if we reject reconstructed paintings because they contain replacement matter, shouldn't we also reject a heart-transplant recipient as not fully the same person, since their body now integrates tissue that was never originally theirs? Maya said that comparison misses the point because a body is a functioning biological system, whereas a painting's value is partly symbolic and historical, not merely functional — so the standards for being the same object should differ between them.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not decide. Please go through their exchange step by step and adjudicate: rate Maya's causal-history argument, Jonah's material-continuity reply, Maya's watch-repair distinction, and Jonah's organ-transplant analogy — from clearly mistaken to clearly right — with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both sides are missing, especially about whether being the original might involve several different criteria that do not always line up. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your overall verdict, and where is your reasoning most vulnerable to failure?
