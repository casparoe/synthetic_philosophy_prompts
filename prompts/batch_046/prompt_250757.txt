In discussions of Berkeley's master argument, I keep noticing a particular defensive move that I cannot place in the literature. A critic attempts to defend the conceivability of matter existing unperceived by constructing a mental scene — for example, a study with a desk and a lamp — and then imagining themselves and every other mind removed from it. They claim that what remains is the very same study, now existing independently, which shows that we can genuinely think of objects outside all perception. But when pressed to describe what actually remains, they invariably invoke properties that presuppose a perceiver: the desk is still brown, the lamp still emits warmth and light, the air still carries a particular scent. If one objects that colour, heat and smell are not intelligible without reference to sensory experience, they often retreat to saying these are merely physical correlates of experience, or they invoke scientific descriptions. Yet they never succeed in describing the remaining scene in purely non-perceptual terms; the content either collapses back into observational language or dissolves into an empty logical placeholder — the world with all observers absent — which they then treat as having determinate features. In some cases they try to avoid this by proposing that they are not picturing the scene at all but only entertaining a bare logical possibility, though they continue to speak of it as if it contained specific furniture and specific properties. Is there an established concept or label for this precise failure mode — the attempt to conceive of unperceived matter by subtracting the observer from a mental image while retaining perceptual content, together with the subsequent collapse into contradiction or abstraction? If so, how does it differ from the separation problem and from the charge that Berkeley confuses the act of conceiving with the object conceived? If there is no established name, could you coin one that captures both the initial subtraction and its failure mode, specify its boundary conditions, and distinguish it clearly both from a simple failure of imagination and from the claim that every conception requires an idea? Please write your answer in British English.
