I have been tracking the persistent disagreement between proponents of grand narrative history—those who construct sweeping causal accounts of human development across millennia, as in the frameworks associated with Harari's Sapiens and Diamond's geographical determinism—and their recent anthropological critics, above all the counter-narrative advanced by Graeber and Wengrow. Despite extensive engagement, the dispute seems to have stalled: both sides invoke the same archaeological and ethnographic record, yet they arrive at diametrically opposed conclusions about determinism, Eurocentrism, and whether macro-scale explanation is even legitimate.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily empirical. Rather, it concerns what I will call the compression threshold: the point at which reducing complex, regionally variable social processes to a unified causal arc shifts from legitimate abstraction to misleading teleology. The defenders of grand narrative appear to treat compression as an explanatory virtue, while the critics treat it as an epistemic vice that necessarily erases human agency and local contingency.

Is this the right diagnosis? Or is the disagreement verbal—centered on competing uses of the word explanation, with one side demanding predictive generality across civilizations and the other demanding interpretive fidelity to local context? Might the two sides be answering different questions altogether—one asking why certain large-scale outcomes occurred, the other asking whether the narrative form used to describe them distorts the moral and political significance of those outcomes? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about which cases count as decisive data, masked by shared vocabulary about evidence?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually says about method, the standards of historical evidence, and the purpose of writing about deep time. In particular, consider whether a formal distinction—between macro-causal explanation and narrative framing commitment—would resolve anything, or whether the question which grand narrative is correct? is itself ill-posed because it conflates causal and normative judgments. And tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a new archaeological discovery that breaks the symmetry, a conceptual clarification about the aims of anthropology, a shift in evidentiary standards, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
