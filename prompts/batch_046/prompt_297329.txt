I've been thinking alot about death lately after reading some old chinese philosophy, specifically this guy Zhuangzi. His basic take seems to be that dying is just like changing clothes or a caterpiller turning into a butterfly—nothing is actually lost, just transformed into something else. So the fear we have is basically a mistake, like being sad that ice melted into water, because we're clinging to a specific shape instead of seeing the bigger flow. I've kinda adopted this view and it actually makes me feel better, but I want to be honest about what could actually break it, not just emotional stuff like "you'll miss your kids."

Specifically, if death is genuinely just a transformation with no real loss of being, then it seems like I shouldn't care more about my own death than about a tree falling in the forest—both are just rearranging matter. But I do care, and not just because I'm attached to my form. There's something about the first-person point of view, like there's a specific "me-ness" that gets interrupted, not just reshuffled into soil or air. So what would have to be true for Zhuangzi's transformation view to actually be wrong? Would it require showing that consciousness depends on a specific organizational structure that gets destroyed rather than continued? Or that there's an asymmetry between not existing before birth and not existing after death that transformation can't explain?

Also, if the view holds, does it commit me to thinking grief is always irrational, since the dead person just changed form? And is there a middle ground where transformation happens but something genuinely important—maybe not a soul, but a particular narrative thread or relational position—just ends? I'd like the defeaters ranked from "this totally kills the view" to "this is just awkward but you can patch it," including whether the view risks being unfalsifiable because any death can just be called another transformation. I don't need citations, just straight reasoning about where the view is actually exposed.
