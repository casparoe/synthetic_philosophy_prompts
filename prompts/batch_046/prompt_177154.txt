Consider Dennett’s brain-transplant scenario in its full branching form: the original brain is extracted and sustained in a vat, a functionally identical copy is implanted in the original body, and both are activated simultaneously. From the first-person perspective, there are now two candidates each with an equal claim to being the original subject, neither of whom can determine by introspection alone whether she is the brain in the vat or the brain in the body.

Write the complete list of desiderata that any adequate theory of personal identity must satisfy to resolve this case without collapsing into pure bodily continuity or pure psychological continuity. For each requirement, identify the specific feature of the branching scenario that motivates it: for example, whether the theory must yield a unique answer to the locational question, whether it must preserve first-person epistemic access to one’s own position, whether it must permit fission without logical contradiction, and whether it must explain why the pre-operative subject’s rational anticipation should target one survivor rather than both or neither. Mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, such as psychological-continuity theorists, animalists, or those who treat identity as holding only between temporal parts. Then provide a brief, honest scorecard of how the psychological-continuity view and the animalist view fare against your list—indicating which criteria each satisfies, which it must reject, and which it can only accommodate by reinterpretation. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable is the specification itself and the logical relations among its parts.
