As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I am trying to understand the conceptual foundations of the consent mechanisms I build and deploy every day. In clinical and research ethics, valid consent is typically understood to require adequate disclosure, actual comprehension by the consenting party, freedom from coercion, and decision-making competence. By contrast, standard software onboarding—clickwrap agreements, cookie banners, and terms-of-service flows—treats a single unreflective click as fully binding, even when the user has not read the text, has been guided through deceptive interface patterns, and often lacks the legal or technical background to understand the obligations being assumed.

This divergence raises a conceptual puzzle. Does the institutional practice of software consent presuppose that consent is essentially a procedural or performative act—an allocation of risk or waiver of rights—rather than an expression of autonomous authorization that depends on internal mental states? Or could the same practice be justified under an autonomy-based view if we reinterpret comprehension and coercion in digital contexts? I have also been considering whether a distinction I have been sketching between what I call "procedural consent" (the observable act of agreement) and "substantive consent" (the informed mental state of authorization) helps explain the divergence, or whether it merely renames the tension without resolving it.

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of consent embedded in these software practices. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its parts are internally consistent, and compare it with the stricter biomedical model. If one were to redesign software onboarding around an autonomy-centered theory—requiring demonstrated comprehension, eliminating deceptive patterns, and evaluating user competence—what structural changes would be necessary? And would such a design still fulfill the commercial and legal functions that the current practice serves?
