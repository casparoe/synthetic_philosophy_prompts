I've been messing around with Sen's liberal paradox and now I'm spooked because I think it extends to show rational people cant plan across time. Here's the chain my study group built and cant kill.

(1) Sen's minimal liberalism says that if each person in a society is decisive over at least one pair of social outcomes, then Pareto efficiency has to fail — thats the classic paradox.

(2) Now model a single agent over her lifespan as a "society" of temporal stages, each with its own preference ordering.

(3) Give each stage minimal liberal authority over its immediate local choice — Monday-you decides Monday-you's schedule, Tuesday-you decides Tuesday-you's, and so on.

(4) If we also require intertemporal Pareto efficiency — never force a plan that every future stage considers worse — then applying (1) to this society-of-stages means some stage's local right will block a change that all later stages prefer.

(5) So any agent who respects both present autonomy and future welfare gets locked into dynamic inconsistency, constantly vetoing her own best path.

(6) But people clearly do make stable long-term plans without constant self-sabotage, so this result cant be right.

(7) Therefore either minimal liberalism collapses inside one head, or rational intertemporal choice is impossible. I'd really rather keep (3) — present autonomy feels real — but I'm open on the rest. Which step is the fraud, and what does fixing it cost?
