After a long argument with a friend about lobbying and campaign finance, I keep returning to an analogy he pressed: he said that giving money to a politician or PAC is essentially like tipping a waiter—you do not purchase the meal (the vote or the statute), you merely reward attentive service, and capping tips only drives the server to ignore the table. He used this to defend the view that lobbying and large donations are democratic signaling devices rather than mechanisms of corruption or access-buying. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy: what corresponds to the waiter, the customer, the meal, the tip, and the restaurant management in the political system; where the mapping breaks, especially regarding fiduciary obligations, collective action, and the public-good nature of legislation; which normative assumptions about consumer markets versus representative democracy it quietly smuggles in; and whether, once those disanalogies are explicit, the analogy can still support the claim that money in politics expresses preference intensity without distorting policy outcomes.
