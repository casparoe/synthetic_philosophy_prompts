I am preparing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on how ancient paradoxes resurface in contemporary philosophy of religion and logic. In my research I came across an unnamed contributor’s summary of the Death in Damascus thought experiment, posted in an essay on an online philosophy forum. The summary runs roughly as follows: the classic story describes a merchant who meets Death in Damascus, flees to Aleppo to escape his fate, and finds Death waiting there, who explains that he was always destined to be in Aleppo that day. According to the contributor, the paradox demonstrates that if an omniscient being knows the future, then every future action is already fixed, which means causal determinism is true and libertarian free will is impossible. The author argues that this inference fails because foreknowledge is epistemic rather than causal—knowing that an event will occur does not bring it about—so the merchant’s flight remains freely chosen even if predicted. To complete the resolution, the summary adopts an open-future semantics, holding that propositions about contingent future events lack a determinate truth-value until the moment they occur, thereby removing any contradiction between freedom and foreknowledge; it presents this as the standard view among contemporary philosophers of religion. Finally, the contributor introduces what they call indexical fatalism: the claim that the paradox only arises if we mistakenly treat Death’s belief as anchored to the agent’s present spatio-temporal location (Damascus) rather than as a timeless modal fact about where the meeting will take place, and that dissolving this indexical anchoring eliminates the appearance of inevitability altogether.

Something about this feels too tidy, particularly the move from the story’s logic to causal determinism being false, the presentation of open-future semantics as settled doctrine, and the introduction of indexical anchoring as if it were a recognised variant rather than a novel framing. Before I write about it, I would like an accuracy audit.
