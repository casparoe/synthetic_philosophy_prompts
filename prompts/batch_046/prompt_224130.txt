I'm trying to formalize the inefficacy objection across climate emissions, meat consumption, and voting, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to dissolve all individual moral obligation in large-scale contexts. (1) If an act is morally wrong, it must make a counterfactual difference to some harm: had the agent not acted, either the harm would not have occurred or its severity would differ. (2) In climate change, factory farming, and democratic elections, any single contribution—one vote, one meal, one year's emissions—falls below the threshold that changes the aggregate outcome. (3) So by (1), no individual acts wrongly by emitting carbon, eating meat, or abstaining from voting. (4) The same reasoning applies symmetrically to collective goods: if my charitable donation is one among millions funding famine relief, and relief succeeds regardless of my gift, then my donation makes no counterfactual difference either. (5) Therefore (1) implies that in any large cooperative or harmful context, individual moral reasons vanish entirely, leaving obligations only to collective agents. (6) But that is implausible—surely an individual can wrong someone or fail in duty even within a crowd. (7) So (1) is false and the inefficacy objection collapses. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd prefer to keep (2) because the empirical claim about thresholds seems correct, and I'd like to keep (6) because the disappearance of individual morality feels wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for how we think about climate, diet, and civic duty? Please answer in American English.
