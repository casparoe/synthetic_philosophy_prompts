I cover the governance of emerging technologies for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am leading a ninety-minute seminar for a reading group of science journalists and editors who have no formal philosophy background but regularly report on AI liability. Rather than delivering the familiar narrative about who to blame when a self-driving vehicle collides with a pedestrian, I want to introduce them to the philosophical problem of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems—specifically, the structural difficulty that arises when a system exercises operational autonomy at the moment of action while its decision-making process remains epistemically opaque both to its designers and to the operators monitoring it in real time. I need a teaching plan for this session, not the explanation itself: an architecture for how the concept should be delivered so that it lands as a philosophical problem rather than a legal ambiguity or a technical debugging exercise.

Please structure the plan as follows. First, the dependency order: which conceptual pieces must be in place before the audience can understand why this is a gap in responsibility rather than merely a failure of engineering oversight, and in what sequence should they be introduced so that the gap feels inevitable rather than artificial? Second, identify the single carrying example or scenario that should anchor the entire discussion—one that involves a system capable of adaptive or distributed decision-making, but that avoids the clichéd autonomous-vehicle collision or surgical-robot malfunction that every reader has already encountered; the scenario should also be constructed so that it lacks an obvious negligent engineer or manufacturer to scapegoat, forcing the group to confront the gap itself. Third, map the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form—such as the belief that full algorithmic transparency automatically closes the gap, or that assigning legal liability to a corporation resolves the underlying moral problem—and specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose a concrete check for understanding at the end that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the structural, relational nature of the gap rather than merely memorizing a list of potential blame-targets. Fifth, state clearly what should be deliberately omitted at this level—whether particular theories of moral agency, specific regulatory frameworks, or fine distinctions within the philosophy of action—and explain why leaving each out preserves conceptual clarity rather than simplifying the idea into error.

Because this is a group of journalists who think in terms of narrative and institutional accountability, I want the plan to incorporate one conceptual framing that is not part of the standard textbook treatment: frame the responsibility gap through the lens of behavioral drift, in which an autonomous system's decision boundaries shift post-deployment through sustained interaction with its environment in ways that neither the original training conditions nor the real-time human supervisors can fully anticipate or reproduce. Use this framing to shape both the carrying example and the misconception map, but do not allow it to substitute for the underlying philosophical structure. I will not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations for this session; what I need is a reusable conceptual scaffold that a non-specialist can follow, question, and eventually apply to the stories they cover.
