In AI safety evaluation, a well-established practice treats adversarial success differently from benign absence: a red-team is said to have demonstrated, or even to know, that a system is dangerous only when they can actually elicit harmful behaviour with a specific input. After extensive benign testing, however, reviewers refuse to say anyone knows the system is safe, treating absence of harm as epistemically empty. At the same time, engineers routinely claim to know that a text-only model will not generate harmful images, solely on the basis of knowing its architecture lacks vision modules, without testing every possible image-related prompt. What implicit theory of knowledge makes this asymmetry coherent? Does the practice presuppose a sensitivity or tracking condition — that knowing p requires that one would not believe p in nearby worlds where it is false — so that one failure-world destroys knowledge of safety, while architectural knowledge tracks the absence of capability? Or does it presuppose that knowledge is not closed under entailment, since knowing the architecture does not automatically yield knowledge of every behavioural consequence? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, say whether it is internally consistent, and describe what evaluation norms would have to change if we replaced this view with one that accepts epistemic closure together with a purely probabilistic account of knowledge. Please write your answer in British English.
