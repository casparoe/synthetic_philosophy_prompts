In discussions about online rhetoric and belief formation, I keep encountering the line that "costly signals are honest because they are expensive to fake," usually invoked to explain why people adopt visibly extreme or self-damaging ideological positions. I want to understand what that slogan actually commits to. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it might be expressing—from the tautological (that a signal's expense correlates with its reliability) to the evolutionary (that handicap mechanisms stabilize honest communication in non-human animals) to the psychological (that humans intentionally burn social capital to demonstrate loyalty or group membership)? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, an empirically supported thesis, an unsupported extension, or actually false; and tell me which of these readings, if any, actually licenses applying the handicap principle to human belief-expression and ideological commitment, as opposed to requiring extra premises about group selection, identity signaling, or strategic self-handicapping that the slogan itself does not supply. I am especially interested in whether any reading genuinely explains why someone might maintain a belief that is costly in epistemic rather than material terms, or whether that application collapses into a different theory altogether.
