I serve on the evaluation committee of a national research-funding body, and we are required to sign off on any quantitative performance indicator before it is tied to grant renewal or institutional funding. I need a reusable decision procedure—a sequence of ordered tests with explicit stopping rules and required evidence—that applies the conceptual distinctions among Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and the AI-alignment literature on specification gaming (including reward hacking, proxy misalignment, and goal misgeneralization) to determine whether a proposed metric is likely to corrupt the research behavior it measures.

The procedure must be executable in a single meeting without access to external literature, and it must distinguish three internal failure modes we have defined for this purpose: "surface gaming" (optimizing the measurable feature without changing the underlying construct), "structural displacement" (reallocating effort away from unmeasured but valuable activities), and "adversarial optimization" (deliberately exploiting ambiguities in the metric's formal definition). For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what documentation I should request from the proposing office, where the procedure stops with a clear rejection, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than predictive fact.

Then apply the procedure to two proposals: first, making "percentage of publications with linked open-access data repositories" a mandatory condition for grant renewal; second, introducing "interdisciplinary co-authorship rate weighted by field-distance scores" as a bonus funding criterion. One of these applications must yield an answer that the affected research community will strongly dislike. The procedure should also flag the cases it cannot settle—such as when the underlying construct is itself contested—and state explicitly what it cannot do, including predicting the magnitude of gaming, distinguishing benign adaptation from corruption, or resolving disputes about which research values should take priority.
