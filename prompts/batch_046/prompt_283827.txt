I'm a third-year philosophy student in a course on reduction, emergence, and scientific explanation, and I've been thinking about a new institutional policy at my university that seems to embody a particular metaphysics of learning without acknowledging it. Our philosophy department recently replaced its traditional capstone thesis and synthetic comprehensive exams with a "modular competency architecture": the major now consists of roughly two hundred discrete, atomized micro-credentials—things like "apply the law of non-contradiction in a formal proof," "identify a modal fallacy," or "distinguish intrinsic from instrumental value"—each tested in isolation via automated and peer-reviewed drills. There are no holistic essays, no integrative oral defenses, and no assessment of whether a student can weave these skills into sustained philosophical judgment; the administration's explicit rationale is that philosophical understanding just is the complete aggregation of these atomic competencies, with nothing genuinely emergent left over.

I'd like to understand what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Does it necessarily commit the institution to what Dennett calls greedy reductionism—the view that higher-level capacities can be fully dissolved into lower-level parts with no residual explanatory work for the whole—or can a "crane" picture, where complex reasoning is genuinely built up incrementally yet remains an irreducible explanatory target, license the exact same modular design? Relatedly, does the policy quietly presuppose the absence of any "skyhook"—whether that be innate philosophical intuition, a non-decomposable gestalt of critical thinking, or some holistic faculty that cannot be trained piecemeal? And there is an apparent tension: the university's marketing still advertises "critical thinking" as an irreducible, graduate-level attribute that distinguishes our alumni, even though the credentialing system treats it as nothing more than the summed checklist. Is that tension merely rhetorical, or does it reveal that the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with both reductionist and emergentist views of philosophical knowledge, depending on how one interprets the aggregation?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of reduction and explanation embedded in this policy, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and tell me whether replacing that implicit view with its rival would actually require changing the institutional practice, or whether the modular architecture could stay intact while the metaphysics shifts beneath it. I'm writing this for a course essay on institutional epistemology, so I'm especially interested in whether practices like this can be philosophically neutral even when they look like they aren't.
