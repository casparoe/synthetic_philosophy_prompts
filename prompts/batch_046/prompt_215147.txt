As a novelist drafting a scene for a literary novel, I need a polished, formal speech written in American English. In the scene, my protagonist—a retired hospice physician—delivers a five-minute address at a university symposium honoring her career. The room is divided: half are care ethicists and clinical philosophers who will catch every conceptual move, and half are her family members, who have no philosophical training. The speech must take as its structural backbone the tension within care ethics between relational autonomy and what I will call the asymmetry of vulnerability—the fact that the caregiver's direct knowledge of suffering often exceeds the patient's own articulated preferences, and that honoring the caring relation sometimes requires acting against an explicit refusal without collapsing into simple paternalism. Rather than merely describing this tension, the speech should argue, in language accessible to non-specialists, why it is structurally unavoidable in end-of-life care rather than a remediable failure of practice. It should comfort and honor a life of service without invoking religious consolation, and the philosophical idea must do real structural work: it should shape what can and cannot be said, not serve as decorative framing. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, references to specific scholars by name, or quotation marks around any external text. Write it in the first person, as if spoken aloud by a physician who has spent thirty years at bedsides.
