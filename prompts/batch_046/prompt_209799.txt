I have come to accept a strengthened form of the separateness of persons objection: utilitarian aggregation fails not merely because it distributes burdens unfairly, but because it treats the boundary between persons as normatively porous, allowing harms and benefits to flow across subjects as if they were compartments of a single life rather than irreducibly separate moral addresses. What would have to be true for this claim to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure—observations, formal results, or reconceptualisations that would show either that interpersonal boundaries are indeed porous in the relevant sense, or that utilitarianism can preserve them without abandoning aggregation—sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with an assessment of whether committed defenders should concede each point.
