I'm a doctoral student in economic history, and in a recent seminar on comparative industrialization we kept circling back to the Britain–China pairing. I noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I do not have a label for, and I am unsure whether it corresponds to an established critique of contrastive historical explanation or is merely an ordinary reasoning failure wearing period costume.

Here is the pattern in concrete form. A speaker proposes that Britain’s industrial takeoff depended on a specific institutional feature—secure property rights, accessible coal, or competitive labor markets. A respondent then observes that parts of Qing China, particularly in the Lower Yangtze, exhibited comparable conditions: coal deposits existed near manufacturing centers, and certain property arrangements were not obviously less secure. Rather than treating this overlap as grounds to abandon the institutional comparison or to expand the comparison set, the original claim is refined—property rights must be of a particular contractual kind, or coal must coincide with a specific energy market—so that Britain retains the feature and China does not. Meanwhile, other regions that also lacked the refined feature but eventually industrialized—France, parts of Germany, Japan—are treated as confirmatory near-misses rather than as counterexamples that should have been included from the start. The canonical pair Britain/China remains fixed, the causal variables are trimmed after observing the outcome, and the question “why Britain and not China?” survives every qualification intact.

This feels different from a routine auxiliary hypothesis, because the adjustments are not testing a theory against fresh evidence but protecting a pre-selected divergence narrative. It also seems more specific than generic selection bias, since the canonical pairing itself determines which qualifications count as relevant and which alternative cases can be set aside. Is there an established concept in the philosophy of history or in comparative method that captures this exact structure—this post-hoc trimming of explanatory conditions to preserve a fixed contrastive pair? If so, how does it relate to standard critiques of counterfactual dependency or to the well-known framing problems of the Needham question and the Great Divergence literature? And if there is no precise name, how should we distinguish it from ordinary confirmation bias and from the broader charge of teleological case selection, and what term might capture its edges?
