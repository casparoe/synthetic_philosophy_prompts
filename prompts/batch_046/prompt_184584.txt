Since I recently became the primary decision-maker for my aging parent's medical and logistical care, I have tried to adopt satisficing rather than optimizing every choice, but I have constructed an argument that seems to show bounded rationality undermines itself. (1) Satisficing is rational whenever the expected improvement from further deliberation is smaller than the cost of that deliberation. (2) Deciding whether to apply this stopping rule is itself a decision that requires deliberation. (3) So by (1), one should apply the rule to that meta-decision only if the gain from the meta-deliberation exceeds its cost. (4) But justifying the stopping point at the meta-level requires a meta-meta-decision, and so on, generating an infinite regress of justification. (5) To avoid the regress, a fully rational agent must either refuse all deliberation from the outset or accept an unjustified, arbitrary stopping point. (6) Yet bounded rationality is meant to explain why ordinary agents halt at reasonable, defensible points, not to eliminate deliberation or render it arbitrary. The argument therefore concludes that the cost-benefit defense of satisficing collapses into either total paralysis or unprincipled cessation. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would strongly prefer to preserve (1) and (6), but I want to know whether that forces me to deny (2), to block the regress in (4) by some other means, or to pay an unexpected price in (5).
