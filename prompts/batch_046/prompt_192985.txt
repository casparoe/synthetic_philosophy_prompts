I've been trying to work out how Hacking-style looping effects should guide psychiatric classification, and I want to check whether my intuitions form a coherent position or just a mess. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, removing homosexuality from the DSM was correct, and the looping effect—where being labeled disordered caused distress that then seemed to confirm the disorder—was part of the reason.

Second, merging Asperger's syndrome into autism spectrum disorder in DSM-5 was a mistake. By then looping effects had built a distinct social identity, self-advocacy networks, and characteristic patterns of self-presentation; erasing the category destroyed something socially real that the loop had made.

Third, adding prolonged grief disorder in recent DSM revisions is risky. The label can loop back and shape bereavement into a clinical identity, increasing prevalence not by revealing hidden cases but by generating them. That loop is a reason to resist addition.

Fourth, the sharp rise in adult ADHD diagnoses clearly involves looping—social media awareness, identity communities, diagnostic self-selection—but I do not think it undermines the category, because there is an independent neurodevelopmental substrate. The loop amplifies identification without inventing the condition.

Fifth, imagine a proposed category called algorithmic dissociation for people who report depersonalization after prolonged interaction with recommendation systems. I think we should reject it, because the loop here is especially vicious: the diagnosis gives sufferers a medical narrative that reinforces the very withdrawal and alienation it describes, and there is no independent biomarker to anchor the category.

The tension is obvious. In case two I want to preserve a category because looping effects made it valuable, but in case three I want to reject one partly because looping effects make it harmful. In case four I tolerate loops because of biological anchoring, yet in case five I reject them for lacking it—so perhaps my hidden principle is just that loops are permissible when there is an underlying biology? But then why was removing homosexuality correct? There was no biological marker for disorder there either; the loop was harmful because the classification was normatively loaded, not because it lacked biology.

So: is there a coherent principle that captures all five verdicts, specifying precisely when looping effects argue for retention, removal, or non-adoption? Or must I drop one? And what should I say about major depressive disorder, where looping is undeniable—diagnosis shapes symptom expression, help-seeking, and even neural plasticity—yet the category is clearly clinically useful?
