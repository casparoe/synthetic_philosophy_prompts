I am a novelist who has been asked to deliver the retirement toast for my former mentor, a philosopher of aesthetics who spent forty years at a small college. In her early career she defended a strict hedonism — that every pleasure, however different its source, contains an identical “hedonic tone” — and she wrote at length against the heterogeneity objection. In her later essays she quietly abandoned that view, turning instead to cataloguing the irreducible textures of pleasure: the satisfaction of catching a false rhyme in a draft, the relief of cold water after long thirst, the peculiar warmth of seeing an old enemy forgive someone else. Her departmental colleagues know this arc intimately; her grandchildren and neighbors in the room do not. Write a five-minute toast that takes the heterogeneity problem as its structural spine: the speech must honor her intellectual journey without reducing those catalogued pleasures to one shared feeling — it must not claim they are all “really the same” — yet it must also offer the non-philosophers present something genuinely consoling rather than a lecture. Let the philosophical tension determine exactly what the toast can and cannot claim about her legacy.
