I just spent the better part of an evening in a long argument with a friend about whether deterministic chaos genuinely undermines determinism or merely exposes the fragility of our predictive practices. We circled the usual examples—sensitive dependence, Laplace's demon, quantum noise—without ever deciding which conceptual distinctions actually do the philosophical work. To move past this, I want you to rank the following five positions by how effectively they reconcile deterministic dynamics with the practical failure of prediction. Assign each to a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—and in one sentence explain what makes it land there. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am asking for your direct judgment.

1. The strict epistemic/ontic divide: chaos is entirely a limitation on our knowledge and measurement, leaving the underlying determinism untouched.
2. The discrete-state injection claim: if physical state space is ultimately granular at the Planck scale, then continuous chaos is merely an approximation and true determinism operates only on discrete jumps.
3. The "predictability debt" framework I proposed to my friend: global determinism remains intact, but local predictive authority decays exponentially with Lyapunov time, so rational agents ought to treat near-future macrostates as effectively open for practical reasoning, even if they are closed in principle.
4. The quantum-decoherence bridge: chaos amplifies microscopic quantum indeterminacy to macroscopic scales, meaning determinism fails not at the quantum level alone but at the observable level precisely because chaotic dynamics magnify it.
5. The modal-structure defense: determinism is a claim about the modal relation between laws and initial conditions, not about epistemic access; since chaos introduces no new modal possibilities, it is philosophically irrelevant to determinism's truth.

Please rank them and justify briefly.
