I run a philosophy seminar for about 14 grad students and some advanced undergrads, 90 minutes, and we're trying to get past the usual "should you turn in your dad" exercises in Confucian role ethics. I want an in-class game or structured role-play that makes them feel why role ethics isn't just "apply universal rules with extra steps" — specifically the part where the norm isn't sitting outside the relationship waiting to be applied, but is partly constituted by the performance of the role itself. To keep it from being something they've seen in every intro text, inject a somewhat weird modern framing: each student gets assigned not just a classical Confucian role (parent, minister, friend) but also a parallel "digital steward" role — like a curator who owes obligations both to the original creators of an archive and to the algorithim system that now determines access — so they have to navigate when the two role-sets pull in opposite directions without any meta-rule to appeal to. The activity should have clear rules, some small real stakes (maybe they have to physically reorder cards or allocate limited points that affect a shared outcome), a point where universalist reasoning clearly collapses and they have to fall back on something like "this is just what this relationship demands," and then a debrief that connects that moment to the idea of zhengming (rectification of names) and the difference between li and yi. Also tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it — probably someone trying to meta-game by inventing a universal principle — and be frank about which feature of role ethics the game can't really reproduce, like the long-term cultivation of virtue over years. No lectures up front, and don't name the view until after they've played, and please include a note on how to handle the student who insists they can just recieve the rules from outside.
