Exam question: Consider a modified experience machine — call it the reflection chamber — in which a subject lives a fully simulated life of artistic mastery, but receives a brief, lucid reminder every seventy-two hours that her achievements have no causal effect on the outside world, after which she may remain or exit. Using hedonism and objective-list theories of well-being, assess whether choosing to enter and stay can be rational for someone pursuing a good life.

Student answer: Hedonism defines well-being purely in terms of pleasure, so the reflection chamber is clearly beneficial: it delivers intense, sustained artistic satisfaction without real-world risk. Because the subject receives regular reminders and still chooses to stay, her enjoyment is fully authentic rather than illusory, which removes the usual objection that simulated pleasure is somehow counterfeit. The philosopher who originally proposed the experience-machine argument was mainly worried about deception, and since deception is absent here, that objection collapses. Turning to objective-list theories, thinkers like Parfit maintain that achievement only has value when it is causally linked to actual external outcomes, so they must reject the chamber because the art produced does not exist outside the simulation. Although some versions of objective-list theory allow purely internal goods, the dominant view demands real-world impact. Therefore, hedonism endorses remaining in the chamber, while objective-list theory rejects it due to the absence of genuine achievement.
