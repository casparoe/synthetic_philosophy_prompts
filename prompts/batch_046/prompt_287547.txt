As a novelist long preoccupied with how belief structures bind communities together, I have noticed a recurring slide in debates over whether religion makes people more moral. The discussion usually opens with an interior standard: does religious commitment genuinely cultivate compassion, humility, or moral sensitivity in the individual? But when secular societies are brought in for comparison, the criterion quietly shifts to an exterior standard: can strangers sustain large-scale cooperative trust without shared transcendental sanctions? Consequently, secular societies are often judged morally deficient whenever they fail to replicate the functional reliability of religious communities, even when they score well on the interior measures originally proposed, and even when secular mechanisms—legal accountability, institutional transparency, reputational incentives—sustain that trust without relying on personal virtue at all. This is not the is-ought gap or the naturalistic fallacy, but a specific conflation of motivational morality with systemic cooperative reliability, particularly in arguments about secularization. Does this configuration have an established name? If so, how does it differ from ordinary equivocation? If not, what term and boundary conditions would precisely capture it, especially for cases where secular institutions successfully decouple interior character from exterior trust?
