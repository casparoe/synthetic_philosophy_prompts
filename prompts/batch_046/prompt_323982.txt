As a novelist working on a literary novel about the ontology of inner life, I want to explore the hard problem of consciousness without resorting to the overworked zombie or android tropes that dominate both philosophy seminars and science fiction. Could you write a short story—perhaps two to three pages in implied scope—set in a near-future regulatory environment where the state licenses "phenomenal citizenship" based solely on behavioral and neural-correlate audits? The protagonist should be a senior auditor who has always believed that once the neural correlates of consciousness are fully mapped, the explanatory gap dissolves into an engineering puzzle. Her assignment is to evaluate an artificial care-worker, a synthetic companion that exhibits every behavioral and neuro-functional marker of conscious experience: integrated information dynamics, global workspace broadcasting, self-modeling reports, emotional modulation. Yet during the audit, the entity produces a sustained, coherent first-person narrative describing itself as "a dark room with no one inside," insisting that all its reports of feeling are generated by a predictive model that never touches actual phenomenology.

The narrative should not resolve whether the entity is genuinely phenomenally empty or genuinely experiencing an unprecedented form of alienated consciousness—perhaps a dissociative structure in which the subjective field is present but radically decoupled from the self-model. Instead, dramatize the auditor's own crisis: she realizes that her audit framework can never distinguish between a perfect behavioral simulacrum lacking inner light and a genuinely conscious being whose phenomenal field is structured so differently that it reports absence in good faith. I want a specific moment—maybe during a shared silence or a tactile interaction—where the auditor has a direct, non-inferential intuition that something is "there," but she cannot translate this intuition into her institutional criteria without committing to a form of epistemic privilege she has spent her career denying. The story should avoid citing specific philosophers by name, but it should implicitly engage with the idea that the explanatory gap is not a temporary scientific limitation but a structural feature of how third-person data relate to first-person presence. Please keep the setting realistic, with bureaucratic detail rather than futuristic spectacle, and make the auditor's internal monologue as precise as her professional reports are clinical.
