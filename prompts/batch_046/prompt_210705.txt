After my father's death this spring, I inherited a box of his papers from law school, including handwritten notes from a 1962 screening of the 1961 film Judgment at Nuremberg. He was fixated on a single question: was the trial of the German judges an exercise in legitimate moral reckoning, or was it victors' justice masked by retroactive law? Rewatching the film through his eyes — and through the lens of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals' shared problem of nullum crimen sine lege — I want a philosophical reading that takes both the film and the jurisprudence seriously.

The film centers on American Judge Dan Haywood presiding over the trial of four German jurists, not the architects of the Holocaust but the judges who enforced its legal scaffolding. The prosecution, led by Colonel Tad Lawson, argues that the defendants perverted justice by applying Nazi statutes they knew to be immoral; the defense, led by Hans Rolfe, insists they were obeying the positive law of their sovereign state. The film famously includes documentary footage of the camps, and its dramatic climax hinges on Haywood's refusal to let legal formalism excuse complicity.

Read the film as an argument about retroactive punishment and victors' justice. First, does the film's decision to prosecute judges rather than high-ranking party officials — as was more prominent at Nuremberg proper and at Tokyo — implicitly solve the retroactivity problem? By framing the crime as professional corruption and betrayal of domestic legal duty rather than as a novel international offense like crimes against peace, does the film sidestep the charge that the Charter invented law ex post facto? Or does Rolfe's defense still force the retroactivity issue to the surface in ways the screenplay wants to suppress?

Second, compare the film's implicit jurisprudence to the actual tension between the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. At Tokyo, the prominent dissent — particularly that of Justice Radhabinod Pal — made retroactivity far more explicit, questioning whether crimes against peace could be punished without prior treaty law. Does Judgment at Nuremberg know about that divergence, or does its American legal formalism — embodied by Haywood's measured proceduralism — silently assume a universalist moral law that the fractured Tokyo judgments refused to endorse?

Third, examine the scenes where Haywood walks through the destroyed Nuremberg, where he speaks with Mrs. Bertholt, the widow of a Nazi general, and where he visits the imprisoned Ernst Janning. Do these moments dramatize a genuine philosophical position — perhaps a natural-law view that positive law forfeits its authority when it becomes radically evil — or do they trade on an emotional appeal that substitutes horror at the camps for an argument about legal legitimacy?

Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that a direct argument about retroactivity and victors' justice cannot? Does its focus on the banality of legal complicity — the defendants are not fanatics but careerists — illuminate something about the moral psychology of institutional wrongdoing that abstract jurisprudence misses? Or does its Hollywood resolution, in which Haywood sentences the defendants and then returns to America, ultimately domesticate a problem that should remain unresolved?

Please be honest about where the film resists your reading and where it distorts the jurisprudence to fit its dramatic needs. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want a criticism that moves back and forth between the film's scenes and the conceptual problem, and that says what the work gets right that the papers do not.
