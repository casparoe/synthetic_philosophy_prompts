I've been working through some arguments with a friend in my philosophy-of-value reading group about whether G. E. Moore's claim that human friendship and the appreciation of beautiful objects rank among the highest intrinsic goods actually holds up, and more specifically whether his principle of organic unity — that the value of a whole can exceed the sum of its parts in a non-additive way — explains anything distinctive about these goods or just renames the intensity of well-coordinated experience. To avoid rehearsing the standard classroom objections, we constructed a comparison that isn't in the textbooks: on one side, a subject who encounters aesthetic value only through fragmented, asynchronous sensory streams that are individually pleasant but never synthesized into a unified perceptual gestalt; on the other side, a long-distance friendship sustained entirely by delayed, text-only exchange lacking spontaneous reciprocity or shared presence. My friend, playing the reductionist, argues that in both cases what is missing is merely a higher-order coherence of subjective well-being, and that Moore's framework illicitly reifies this coherence into a mysterious property of the whole.

I want you to write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors — call them Alix and Bren — who take this comparison seriously and actually respond to each other's points rather than talking past one another. Alix should defend the Moorean view that friendship and aesthetic experience involve an irreducible, organically unified good that is not captured by aggregating momentary pleasures or instrumental contributions, and should address Bren's challenge that this sounds unfalsifiable or metaphysically extravagant. Bren should push back by asking exactly what empirical or conceptual difference organic unity makes: if mediated friendship still produces the same pattern of care and mutual knowledge, and if distributed aesthetic input still produces the same emotional trajectory, on what grounds does Alix deny them the status of full goods? They should discuss whether technological mediation destroys organic unity or merely transforms its conditions, and whether Moore's hierarchy gives us reason to privilege direct presence over distributed connection. The dialogue should be substantive, with real back-and-forth, neither character acting as a strawman.
