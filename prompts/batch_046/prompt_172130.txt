I'm trying to get my commitments straight on scientific priority and the inevitability of discovery, and I'd like an audit before I present them in a philosophy of science reading group. Here is where I've landed:

1. I think that major theoretical breakthroughs are structurally inevitable—given the accumulated anomalies and available methods, an equivalent formulation would have emerged within a generation if the actual discoverer had not existed—so exclusive priority claims carry weak moral weight.
2. I think that in the calculus case, Newton and Leibniz made genuinely different discoveries: Newton's method of fluxions was conceptually anchored in physical motion and limiting ratios, whereas Leibniz's differential calculus was a formal algorithm operating on infinitesimal differences, carrying distinct ontological commitments. So attributing a single "discovery of calculus" to one party is misleading.
3. I think that in the natural-selection case, Darwin and Wallace arrived at essentially the same theoretical mechanism, so simultaneous publication should not diminish Darwin's primary credit, though Wallace's independent arrival deserves formal recognition.
4. I think that scientific credit should be distributed according to a counterfactual-robustness criterion: a researcher deserves primary credit only if the specific conceptual framing, evidentiary base, and rhetorical form they provided were not substitutable by the near-simultaneous alternative.
5. I think that Darwin's specific institutional position, rhetorical strategy, and extensive empirical documentation were causally necessary for the rapid acceptance of evolutionary theory—without him, acceptance would have been significantly delayed or reshaped—so for historical explanation we cannot treat discoveries as fully anonymous.
6. I think that for the purpose of scientific rationality—deciding what to believe—the identity of the discoverer is entirely irrelevant; only the evidence and argument structure matter.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look tense but dissolve once you distinguish domains, and what the cheapest revision would be for each real conflict. Order the tensions by severity, and for each say exactly which commitment has to give and at what cost. Don't just flag every possible friction; discriminate between deep inconsistencies and apparent ones. I'm especially unsure whether my counterfactual-robustness criterion in (4) can accommodate my verdict in (3) about Darwin, and whether (1) conflicts with (5).
