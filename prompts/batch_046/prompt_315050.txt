I'm in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of computation and moral psychology—about sixteen graduate and advanced undergraduate students, one ninety-minute session. I want a runnable, rules-based activity that makes us feel the boundary problem for moral attribution in distributed computation, not just discuss it. The focus should be on which computations matter morally: specifically, the tension between attributing standing to globally integrated processes versus to locally efficacious sub-computations, and the further asymmetry that arises when redundant or causally disconnected sub-circuits mirror the very processes we treat as sufficient.

Design a structured simulation or role-play with hidden roles and a non-monetary payoff scheme. Before any framing lecture, assign each of us a functional node in a multi-stage pipeline—feedforward feature extraction, recurrent binding, global broadcast, motor output, and one "shadow" node that redundantly replicates the binding computation but is causally isolated from the broadcast and output stages. Give us decision rules and small stakes—say, collective veto tokens over a shared pool of "protection points"—that force the group to vote, before roles are revealed, on exactly which nodes in the pipeline count as proper subjects of moral concern if the system produces harm or benefit.

The mechanics should be arranged so that we construct the boundary ourselves: the vote should happen under conditions of partial ignorance about our own positions, and the payoff should reward consistency across cases but also tempt strategic self-protection. After roles are revealed, we should discover whether our own node fell inside or outside the boundary we endorsed, and whether the shadow node—functionally identical but causally inert—was included or excluded.

Please include: a precise timeline and rule set for the ninety minutes; the payoff and voting mechanics; instructions for how to stage the hidden-role reveal and the shadow-node disclosure without breaking the simulation; a debrief that maps each voting pattern and each moment of surprise onto the philosophical distinctions between global-workspace, integrated-information, and causal-role theories of sentience; an honest admission of which feature of the boundary problem the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion; and a frank prediction of what will go wrong the first time—whether strategic voting, role identification, or conflation of causal efficacy with phenomenal presence.

Do not provide a bibliography or name the theoretical positions until the debrief begins. The idea should emerge from what happens in the room, not from what is said beforehand.
