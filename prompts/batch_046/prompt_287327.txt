You are marking an undergraduate examination in philosophy of science. The question was: "Explain Neurath's transformer principle in ISOTYPE pictograms, and discuss one philosophical limitation he acknowledged regarding the use of pictures for scientific communication."

Here is a student's answer:

Neurath, working with Gerd Arntz, designed ISOTYPE to communicate economic and social statistics across language barriers. The transformer principle requires that quantities be shown by repeating identical icons rather than altering their size—so fifty workers are depicted as fifty small figures, not one enlarged figure. This prevents scale distortions and keeps the symbols readable regardless of reproduction size.

Neurath believed pictograms were more objective than verbal language because they bypass linguistic ambiguity and speak directly to visual perception. Nevertheless, he recognised that pictures cannot easily convey negation, conditionals, or abstract theoretical relations; you can display that unemployment has risen, but not that "if industrial output falls, then unemployment will not rise." Consequently, ISOTYPE was intended to accompany verbal reports rather than replace them.

Additionally, the system employed bright, emotive colours and stylised figures to make data more persuasive and engaging for a general audience.
