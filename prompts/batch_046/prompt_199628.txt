I'm giving the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez next saturday at the university club. She's a plant taxonomist who spent about thirty-five years arguing that species aren't natural kinds — basically that science doesn't carve nature at its joints, it just builds practical fences where the terrain allows.

Roughly half the room will be evolutionary biologists and philosophy-of-science people who know her papers; the other half are family and old neigbors who mainly remember her for gardening tips. The toast has to actually do philosophical work, not just decorate: it can't praise her for discovering nature's true categories, since that'd directly contradict her view, and it can't fall back on she's watching over us now because she's a committed materialist.

I want to center it on a framing she used in her later lectures, which she called the carpenter's fallacy — the error of thinking that because you had to put a joint somewhere, the tree grew one there. Can you write a five-minute toast that uses that idea to honor her career, explains to the non-experts why her work mattered without dumbing it down, and lands as affection rather than a lecture? It should feel warm and personal, maybe mention that she always said taxonomy is more like navigation than like map-making, and avoid any essentialist language about real kinds.
