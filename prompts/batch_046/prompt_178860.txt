I've been trying to think clearly on my own about how human categories work—race, gender, and money—without relying on any academic framework I don't fully understand. I've come up with a kind of three-bucket system to keep them straight, and I want to check whether my commitments actually hang together or whether I'm just patching over contradictions. Here is my profile:

1. Race is what I'd call a "line kind": it draws boundaries across continuous human biological variation purely to organize power and resources. The differences between so-called racial groups are negligible, and the category does its work socially, not naturally.

2. Gender is a "mask kind." People take on gender the way they take on a role or artistic persona. The internal feeling of being a particular gender is real, but the category itself is made up, so changing gender is more like changing one's religion or persona than like changing race.

3. Money is the purest "paper kind"—value exists only because enough people agree to treat paper and numbers as valuable. If that collective agreement collapsed, the physical objects would have no special status.

4. "Socially constructed" never means "fake" or "easy to undo" for me. Some constructed things—especially line kinds like race—become embedded in institutions and bodies over generations, so they are extremely real in effect and very hard to dismantle.

5. Biological sex, by contrast, is a genuine binary natural kind. It exists independently of human societies and is not constructed in any of the three ways above.

6. Because gender is a mask kind and money is a paper kind, both depend on collective agreement. So if we can redesign a currency system—moving from cash to digital credit, for example—we should be able to redesign gender categories with roughly comparable institutional effort: multiply them, merge them, or make them optional.

7. For race, since it is a line kind with no natural basis, the right response is to abolish the categories entirely—stop counting people by race, stop using racial labels in law and medicine, and treat the lines as errors.

8. I also hold that recognizing something as constructed should generally make us more willing to change it, not less—except that with line kinds the harm comes from the line itself (so erase the line), whereas with mask kinds the harm comes from restricting which masks are permitted (so add more masks rather than remove the idea of masking).

Please audit these commitments pair by pair. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only look tense but dissolve once you apply my three-bucket distinction (line, mask, paper), and which single revision—giving up one commitment, or altering one—would clean up the most conflict overall. For each real tension, rate its severity: is it a direct contradiction, a hidden contradiction that needs an extra premise I'm not supplying, or just an awkward fit? Say what the cheapest fix is and exactly what it costs me—whether that's abandoning the three-bucket framework, admitting that gender change is not like religious conversion, conceding that biological sex might not be as clean a binary as I think, or something else. Don't lay out a menu of options and leave me to choose; take a definite position on which pairs are broken, which apparent tensions are harmless, and what one change I should make. I want a committed answer, not a balanced survey.
