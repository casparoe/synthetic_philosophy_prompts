As a journalist covering decision science and AI policy, I keep encountering the maxim "the best is the enemy of the good" in debates over hiring algorithms that stop searching once a candidate clears a fixed threshold, rather than ranking the full pool. Proponents call this satisficing in Herbert Simon's sense and claim that exhaustive optimization is not merely costly but conceptually wrongheaded. Before I interview them, I need the slogan disambiguated: what are the distinct precise claims it could be expressing—say, a psychological claim about cognitive limits, an economic claim about diminishing returns to search, a normative claim about acceptable risk, or a stronger thesis that maximization is incoherent? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible, whether it actually licenses stopping at a threshold without comparing two "good enough" options, and whether it survives if "good enough" is defined not against a static utility curve but against a dynamic "regret budget" that updates as the search space is revealed. Structure the answer as a menu with verdicts, not a single interpretation, so I can map which version each engineer is actually defending.
