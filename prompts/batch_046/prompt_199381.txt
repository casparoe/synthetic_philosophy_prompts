I just finished a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend who has recently embraced the modern Stoic revival not as casual self-help but as a complete philosophy of emotional life. She defends what I take to be the strong version of the revived dichotomy of control: Epictetus’s distinction between what lies within our power and what does not is not merely therapeutic but a exhaustive normative taxonomy of emotional appropriateness. On her view, any sustained emotional disturbance—grief, outrage, anxiety—directed at externals such as other people’s actions, institutional outcomes, bodily health, or past events is always rooted in a false judgment of value; and cultivating perfect equanimity toward all such externals is rationally mandatory, not just psychologically beneficial. She allows that we should act to improve the world, yet insists our emotional investment must remain strictly confined to our prohairesis, the integrated faculty of moral choice and character, and that any affective spillover beyond it is a cognitive error. You are an expert in ancient Stoicism, moral psychology, and the philosophy of emotion. Before delivering your assessment, please begin by brainstorming the major categories of objection, then organize your response with explicit structure and careful reasoning.

What I need is not the familiar complaint that Stoicism ignores systemic injustice or encourages quietism, but a rigorous map of what would genuinely count against this position. Which specific intuitions about emotional life, which concrete cases—perhaps involving collective moral injury, parental grief in the face of unavoidable harm, or warranted anger at structural cruelty—and which distinctions or results in adjacent fields—say, moral luck, collective responsibility, or the ontology of emotional reasons—stand in tension with the claim that all externally directed affect is cognitively erroneous? I want you to examine a conceptual pressure point my friend glossed over: the modern revival often collapses Epictetus’s technical notion of prohairesis with the broader psychological idea of an internal locus of control, thereby erasing what I will call structural co-determination—domains where individual volition is neither sovereign nor irrelevant, but causally embedded in collective processes that partly determine outcomes. How damaging is this disanalogy? Which defeaters would a committed revivalist concede as genuinely damaging, which would they dismiss as a failure to grasp the distinction, and which risk rendering the view unfalsifiable—for instance, by interpreting any lingering emotional disturbance as proof that one has not yet achieved true Stoic understanding? Please rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude with an honest judgment: is the position well-insulated against criticism, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice?
