Having retired after four decades in experimental physics, I have lately immersed myself in moral philosophy and now sit, somewhat unexpectedly, on my university’s Committee for Commemorative Ethics. Over the past eighteen months we have rendered five judgments that I believe hang together, though I am no longer certain by what thread. We retained a bronze statue of a nineteenth-century naval surgeon who pioneered antisepsis yet conducted non-consensual experiments on prisoners, adding only an interpretive plaque; we removed a street name honoring a textile industrialist whose fortune rested on child labor, despite his having endowed public libraries; we preserved the name of a bridge dedicated to a civil engineer whose 1962 design error caused a fatal collapse, on the explicit rationale that the name now memorializes the ethical lesson rather than the man; we rejected outright a proposal to name a lecture hall for a living Nobel laureate in chemistry; and we approved, by narrow vote, the redesignation of a municipal park after an anonymous collective of 1920s sanitation workers, represented solely by a recovered numerical designation and archival photographs, with no individual identities restorable.

The case now before us is stranger, and I should value your help in seeing whether our record genuinely constrains it. A private consortium has proposed a “living memorial” in our central plaza: a garden of bioluminescent plants genetically modified so that segments of their DNA encode the names of undocumented laborers who constructed the city’s water system in the 1950s. The plants would glow in patterns readable as text, but the genetic archive would remain proprietary to the biotechnology firm maintaining the garden in perpetuity, and no public registry of individual names would exist outside that corporate database. The proponents argue that the installation honors precisely those whose labor was erased; critics reply that it converts commemoration into a proprietary spectacle and that using engineered organisms as gravestones violates a dignity we have previously recognized. If we wish to be consistent with ourselves, what should our verdict be, which of our five precedents is the closest analog, and—if our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome—what principle would we be adopting with each choice? I am especially interested in whether the implicit rule we have been running concerns moral character, domain-specific contribution separable from biography, procedural constraints on living or anonymous subjects, or something else entirely; and I should like to know which further case—say, a proposed open-source digital monument whose algorithm curates the narrative—would best distinguish the competing interpretations.
