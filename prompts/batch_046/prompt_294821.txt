You serve as the lead ethics mediator for the neuro-palliative service at a large academic hospital. Your unit is required to adjudicate conflicts between previously executed advance directives and the contemporaneous preferences or behaviors of patients whose decision-making capacity is now compromised or fluctuating—most commonly in advanced dementia, severe traumatic brain injury with intermittent lucidity, or profound psychiatric disorder that alters evaluative structures. You must design a reusable clinical decision procedure that translates the philosophical problem of diachronic authority—whether, and under what conditions, an earlier capacitated self retains normative control over a later, transformed or incapacitated self—into an operational protocol your team can run in real time.

The procedure should not collapse into either "the directive always governs" or "present preference always governs." Instead, operationalize a three-layer framework: (1) an authenticity and provenance layer that verifies the directive's origin conditions, specificity, and absence of revocation triggers; (2) an axiological drift layer that assesses whether the patient's current expressed preferences represent a pathological distortion of, or an authentic structural revision to, the value hierarchy that originally grounded the directive—assessed by coherence with underlying critical interests rather than surface agreement with the directive's literal instructions; and (3) a transformation threshold layer that determines whether the patient's current cognitive-affective architecture is so discontinuous with the authoring self (for example, total loss of autobiographical integration coupled with inversion of previously ranked values) that the directive's authority is suspended not because the patient changed their mind, but because the substrate capable of endorsing or repudiating the directive is no longer present.

In constructing the procedure, specify: the ordered sequence of tests or questions; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; the precise points where the procedure halts with a binding recommendation versus where it must hand the decision to a values judgment (explicitly marking those nodes); the time and expertise constraints (decisions must be renderable within 48 hours by a mediator with clinical-ethics training but not necessarily a philosophy doctorate, with sign-off required from the attending physician, a designated family or surrogate representative, and legal compliance); and the hard cases the procedure must accommodate, including conflicts involving experiential welfare versus long-term narrative coherence.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

A. A patient with behavioral-variant frontotemporal dementia who now consistently seeks social interaction and accepts spoon-feeding, but whose advance directive—executed five years prior—explicitly refused artificial nutrition and hydration in the event of advanced dementia. The family argues that the patient's current smiling and engagement represent a restored will to live.

B. A patient with intermittent locked-in syndrome who, via eye-tracking communication during lucid windows, directs the withdrawal of mechanical ventilation, contrary to a directive executed two years earlier that requested continuation of ventilation should such a state occur. The treating neurologist believes the current refusal is reactive despair rather than an authentic value revision.

C. A case in which your procedure yields an outcome—specifically, that the directive should be overridden and life-sustaining treatment continued—that you, as the mediator, will find ethically undesirable, and explain what the procedure is doing in that case and where it offers no further guidance.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: identify the metaphysical conditions of personal identity over time, predict future capacities or value changes, or substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal adjudication.
