The Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals prosecuted individuals for aggressive war and crimes against humanity under statutes enacted after the conduct occurred, and they applied command-responsibility doctrines that made leaders liable for subordinates' actions without proof of direct orders. That pairing—retroactive penal law combined with a direct, state-bypassing assignment of individual liability—looks philosophically unstable unless some deeper commitments are doing hidden work. What theory of legal obligation does it presuppose: that international morality already bound individuals independently of any enacted statute, so the tribunals were merely recognizing pre-existing duties? Or that retroactive legislation can be legitimate if the wrong was manifest? And does the command-responsibility framework presuppose a particular metaphysics of collective agency—treating organizational hierarchies as extending individual authorship—without which distributed liability collapses? Reconstruct the implicit package of commitments about justice, sovereignty, and responsibility; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and say whether an institution that denied both natural-law premises and collective-agency analogies could have produced trials recognizably like these, or whether the entire architecture would have to be rebuilt on different foundations.
