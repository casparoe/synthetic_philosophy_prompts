I have recently become involved in discussions of institutional reparations and transitional justice, which has made the question of collective national responsibility feel urgently personal rather than abstract. I find myself persuaded by a view drawn from the Fischer controversy: if documentary evidence reveals centralized state intent to initiate and expand a conflict—deliberate escalation, annexationist aims, strategic bureaucratic planning—then the nation-state can be held morally responsible in a direct, non-metaphorical sense. The justification, as I see it, is structural rather than psychological: the state operates as a continuous institutional agent, and bureaucratic intent recorded in official channels is not erased by subsequent elections, constitutional ruptures, or generational turnover. Holding a nation responsible for the First World War is therefore analogous to holding a corporation liable for board-level decisions; the legal and moral personality persists through changes in membership. I want a committed, critical assessment of what would genuinely defeat this position. Does it depend on an unjustified analogy between corporate and national personhood? Could a principle of diachronic dilution—the idea that democratic transformation and population replacement dissolve the continuity required for blame—undermine the argument without challenging Fischer's empirical claims about intent? And if defenders retreat to defining the nation purely as the enduring institutional framework, is that retreat a conceptual strength or an unfalsifiable qualification that insulates the view from counterevidence? Please take a definite stance: is this position genuinely exposed to refutation, or has it been rendered immune?
