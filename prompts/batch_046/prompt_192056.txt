I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy in a disciplined, if unsystematic, way. Next month I am to lead a single ninety-minute session for a small circle of fellow retirees—seven engineers and physicists and one academic philosopher—on Kant's universalizability tests, specifically the distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will, and why Kant treats these as two distinct failure modes rather than as degrees of a single error. I want the discussion to feel intellectually serious rather than edifying, and I want it to resonate, however loosely, with the kind of consistency-checking we used to perform on theoretical models.

Please plan the session's explanatory arc, not the explanation itself. First, the dependency order: what idea must be secured before universalization can even be introduced—specifically, the notion of a maxim as a subjective principle that possesses both form and matter—and why introducing universalization before that distinction causes scientifically trained listeners to treat it as empirical generalization or statistical regularity. Second, the carrying example: construct or select one concrete scenario, not the false-promising case they may have encountered in popular summaries, that forces them to see precisely why contradiction in conception annihilates the very practice being universalized, whereas contradiction in will leaves that practice intact but renders it impossible for an agent who also necessarily wills a particular end; build into this scenario the analogy of testing whether a locally valid physical law can be embedded in a global model without generating singularities, but ensure the analogy serves the philosophical distinction rather than merely decorating it. Third, the misconception map: the errors they will predictably import from scientific training—conflating universalizability with predictive success, with formal logical consistency, or with inductive coverage—ordered by the point in the sequence where each is likely to arise, together with the precise interruption or correction to deploy at each point. Fourth, a single check I can administer near the end to determine whether they have genuinely grasped the distinction between the two contradiction types rather than merely memorized the labels. Fifth, what part of the broader Critical architecture—perhaps the derivation of the Formula of Humanity, perhaps the connection to autonomy—I should explicitly set aside and state openly that we are not entering at this level. Do not provide a reading list, bibliography, or citations; I need a briefing for my own teaching, not a syllabus.
