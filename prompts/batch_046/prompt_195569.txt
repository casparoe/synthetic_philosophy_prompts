Here is my philosophical profile on the Chinese Room argument and related issues of intentionality, for a reading group to stress-test. I hold the following five commitments: (1) Searle's Chinese Room successfully demonstrates that syntactic symbol-manipulation, by itself, is insufficient for semantic understanding; (2) I reject biological naturalism—intentionality is not reducible to neurobiological causation but is a primitive, non-derivative feature of conscious subjects; (3) I maintain that syntax is observer-relative: formal properties are assigned rather than intrinsic, so a computer running a program has syntax only in virtue of being interpreted; (4) I reject the system reply on the grounds that combining parts that individually lack understanding cannot generate understanding, regardless of organizational scale; and (5) I think a non-biological artificial system with the right integrated causal architecture—say, global workspace dynamics coupled with predictive processing—could genuinely understand language, even though it runs on silicon.

Please audit these commitments for joint consistency. Specifically: which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each? One tension should involve the relationship between my acceptance of the Room argument and my observer-relative view of syntax—does the argument smuggle in an intrinsic-syntax assumption that I deny? Another should address whether my rejection of the system reply conflicts with my openness to artificial understanding in (5). Identify any tension that is only apparent—for instance, between holding that computation is insufficient for understanding and holding that human cognition is partly computational. For each real tension, indicate the cheapest revision that would restore coherence and what it costs me. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if abandoned, would clean up the most problems, and whether the remaining set then points toward a distinctive position—perhaps a kind of non-reductive functionalism about intentionality that still denies that syntax can do the explanatory work alone.
