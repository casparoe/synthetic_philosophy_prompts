I serve on an institutional review board that oversees non-human primate protocols at a research university, evaluating three to five proposals monthly. Each decision requires a written, auditable justification, and I need a reusable procedure—something my colleagues can run in about thirty minutes without philosophical training—grounded in Kant's ethical framework. Specifically, the framework holds that rational beings are ends in themselves and direct objects of perfect duty; non-rational animals are not direct objects of moral duty, but cruelty or neglect toward them is indirectly wrong because it erodes the moral disposition that rational beings owe one another.

Please construct a practical decision checklist that translates this indirect-duty view into ordered steps. The procedure should open with factual questions about the scientific objective, the animal's experiential state, and the availability of alternatives; then move to value-laden judgments about "moral corrosion risk," which I define for this board as the specific mechanisms by which a protocol might habituate researchers to disregard suffering in rational beings—namely bureaucratic distancing (suffering hidden behind automated data collection), instrumental normalization (animals treated as pure replaceable data sources), and affective suppression (training to ignore distress signals). The checklist must distinguish instrumental harm (using the animal as a means) from character-corrupting harm (procedures that risk degrading the researchers' moral character), since these function differently under an indirect-duty account.

Your deliverable should include:
- A sequence of no more than six ordered tests, each with clear sufficient/insufficient criteria and a defined stop point.
- Explicit flags marking where the procedure must halt and hand the decision to human judgment—particularly where it requires weighing the moral weight of potential human benefit against a quantified corruption risk, or where contested interpretations arise (for example, whether psychological distance between researcher and subject counts as corruption, or whether a high-benefit protocol can justify a high-corruption risk).
- A clear statement of what the procedure cannot do, such as determine the exact comparative moral weight of animal suffering relative to human benefit, or resolve whether indirect duties ever generate obligations to minimize suffering for its own sake.
- Worked applications to two proposals:
  1. A protocol involving invasive neural implantation in macaques to study motor recovery after spinal injury, with high clinical-translation potential but requiring months of physical restraint and post-surgical isolation.
  2. A protocol involving frequent, low-pain blood draws in chimpanzees for a non-critical biomarker study with minimal direct suffering but high bureaucratic distance—data is collected by automated systems, and researchers rarely observe the animals.

For each case, show where the procedure yields a clear recommendation and where it forces a board judgment. Include at least one case where the Kantian framework delivers a recommendation that conflicts with intuitive welfare concerns; explain precisely why the indirect-duty logic produces that result, and identify the point at which a board member might legitimately invoke judgment to override the procedural output.
