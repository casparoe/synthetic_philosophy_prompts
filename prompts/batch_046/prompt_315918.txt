I'm putting together a piece on whether generations are actually real kinds, or just boxes we invent for marketing reports. My study partner sent me this argument and I can't find the hole.

(1) If 'Gen Z' or 'Millennial' names a real kind, there has to be some non-arbitary property that all members share, like H2O being water.

(2) The usual candidates are birth-year brackets or shared formative experiences — smartphones, 9/11, the 2008 crash.

(3) But birth years are conventional; nothing biologically or psychologically flips on Jan 1, 1997. So the boundary is arbitary.

(4) And shared experiences aren't actually shared. A kid in Lagos and one in Ohio both technically grew up with TikTok, but the economic context is totally different. So there's no uniform causal profile.

(5) So generations fail both conditions for being a real kind: non-arbitary boundaries and internal homogeneity.

(6) That means generational labels are statistical artifacts — useful for surveys, maybe, but ontologically empty, like zodiac signs with better PR.

(7) But here's the problem: demagraphic predictions based on cohort labels actually work. 'Gen Z' tracks mental-health trends, voting patterns, and labor-market entry in ways pure age or income don't capture.

(8) If the category were purely fictional, that predictive success would be a miracle — like phrenology accidentally predicting personality.

(9) So either generations are real kinds after all, or sociology runs on lucky correlations.

(10) But (9) is absurd; no one thinks demography is pseudoscience.

(11) Therefore generations are real kinds.

(12) But (5) showed they can't be. Contradiction.

I'm stuck between keeping (5) and keeping (10). I feel like I'm smuggling in an assumption that social categories have to work like chemical elements, but I can't name the slip. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather not say sociology is just astrology with spreadsheets.
