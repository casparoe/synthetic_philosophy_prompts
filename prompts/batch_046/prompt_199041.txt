Here's a puzzle I cooke up for a seminar and now it seemngly proves way too much. There's this classic headache for hedonism: if pleasure is the only intrinsic good, every pleasure must share some feature F that explains why it's good, and F should be present in pleasures but not in other states.

1. For hedonism to be substantive, there is one intrinsic property F shared by every pleasure and only pleasures that makes each good.
2. F can't be a specific felt quality — warmth, smoothness, a buzz — because the pleasure of seeing justice done or grasping a hard proof doesn't feel like a warm bath at all.
3. So F must be relational: say, being desired for its own sake.
4. But malicious pleasure — genuinely enjoying a rival's failure — is also desired for its own sake, yet it isn't good. So F has to be "desired for its own sake and non-malicious."
5. But "non-malicious" is just "good" in disguise. So F collapses into "being a good instance of being-desired-for-own-sake."
6. That makes hedonism circular: pleasure is good because it's pleasure, where pleasure just means good-desire-state.
7. Now apply the same rescue to any rival theory. Say knowledge is intrinsically good. Define knowledge as "being-desired-for-own-sake and being-non-ignorant." By parallel reasoning, knowledge collapses into pleasure too.
8. So either hedonism fails because there's no shared F, or — if we save it with the circular move — every intrinsic good becomes pleasure. That's absurd; it wipes out the difference between hedonism and knowledge-based theories, justice-based ones, etc.
9. Therefore the heterogeneity problem doesn't just hurt hedonism; it either kills it or proves too much by absorbing every rival value.
