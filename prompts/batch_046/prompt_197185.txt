In a recent discussion about moral change, I have been trying to articulate a position that blends Bernard Williams’s well-known emphasis on honor and shame with a structural condition I want to call status-monopoly collapse. The idea is that practices such as dueling and Atlantic slavery persisted long after powerful arguments against them existed because those arguments were structurally inert: the honor sustaining each practice was protected by a monopoly on dignified status—only aristocrats could duel without vulgarity, and slaveholding was woven into the economic and social recognition available to a specific class. The revolution, on this account, occurs not when reasoning finally prevails or when interests align, but when that monopoly fractures—when dueling becomes accessible to those without aristocratic standing and therefore laughable, or when slaveholding comes to signal backwardness rather than gentility. Once the monopoly breaks, shame becomes available, and arguments that had circulated for generations suddenly appear compelling. Economic shifts and explicit advocacy matter, but primarily as accelerants after honor has already moved.

What would genuinely count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure, not just the standard objections. Which details in the history of dueling’s decline in Britain or in the abolition of slavery would be decisive if they showed that legal and economic change clearly preceded—rather than followed—any collapse in honor? Are there cases of rapid moral revolutions, such as the end of foot-binding or certain blood sports, where no comparable status monopoly existed, yet the practice collapsed with surprising speed? Could evidence show that defenders of dueling or slavery experienced profound internal shame before any external loss of standing, reversing the causal arrow I am proposing? And does the view risk being insulated into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be redescribed as a hidden honor-shift—such that it explains everything and therefore risks nothing? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with particular attention to what a committed defender of the honor-collapse thesis would themselves concede as damaging rather than only what critics would press.
