I'm taking a graduate seminar on temporal logic and the philosophy of time, and during our discussion of future contingents my study partner constructed what she calls the Retroactive Fixation Argument. She works within a branching-time framework that includes a privileged actual branch — a thin red line of realized history running through the tree of possibilities — and she introduces a principle she terms Backward Modal Lock: if a future-tensed proposition is true on the actual branch, then at every prior node along that branch it is not only true but modally fixed, because the actualization of that branch already excludes the live alternatives that would have made it false. From this she reasons: (1) Tomorrow there will either be a sea battle or there will not, and on the actual branch one disjunct is already true. (2) By Backward Modal Lock, that true disjunct is modally fixed at every earlier node, including today, so its negation is impossible now. (3) Therefore whether there is a sea battle tomorrow is already necessary today. (4) The same reasoning applies to every future event, so all apparently open futures are in fact closed. (5) But this eliminates genuine contingency, which contradicts our pre-theoretic conviction that present choices can shape an open future. (6) Hence either bivalence fails for future contingents, or the privileged-branch framework is incoherent, or Backward Modal Lock must be abandoned. She insists that preserving contingency requires rejecting bivalence, but I suspect the error lies between (2) and (3), or in an equivocation between truth-on-a-branch and modal status-at-a-node, though I cannot articulate exactly where the slide occurs. Which step should I reject, and what does each rejection cost? Specifically, if I want to keep both bivalence and the privileged actual branch without collapsing into supervaluationism, can I block the argument by refining what "modally fixed" means across nodes, and what does that do to the semantics of present choice?
