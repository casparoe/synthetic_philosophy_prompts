As a journalist who covers philosophy and public life, I am preparing a feature on speeches in which a philosophical idea does real structural work rather than serving as ornament. I need you to take the role of an expert speechwriter with formal training in political philosophy. Before you begin drafting, please spend a moment brainstorming how the tension between liberal individualism and communitarian identity can actually constrain — not merely decorate — what a speaker may and may not say on a particular occasion.

Please write the opening address for the rededication of Millbrook Common Hall, a municipal building rescued from private sale. The audience is deliberately mixed: local residents who campaigned to preserve the hall as shared heritage, and visiting academics in philosophy and law who will catch every theoretical move. The hall now operates under a new charter that introduces an invented legal concept — "narrative co-ownership" — which prohibits naming any individual or family as the primary benefactor or author of the building's history, and requires that the internal archive be curated collectively with no single editor credited.

Your speech must treat the liberalism-versus-communitarianism conflict as architectural, not thematic. Show how liberal frameworks of individual rights, named gratitude, and clear attribution conflict with the charter's prohibition on singular authorship, and how communitarian celebrations of shared identity risk erasing the specific, individual labor of the people who physically restored the structure. You must inject at least one genuinely niche conceptual move — for example, asking whether narrative co-ownership is best understood as a right distributed across separate individuals or as a good that exists only at the group level — and allow that distinction to shape the actual language of thanks, responsibility, and remembrance in the address.

Do not resolve the tension with a tidy synthesis; end on a practical ambiguity that the audience is asked to inhabit rather than solve. Keep the speech to a maximum of 600 words. Write the response in British English, using American spelling and punctuation conventions only in the prompt instructions themselves. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or named philosophers unless their ideas are paraphrased without quotation marks. Begin with a brief outline of your structural choices, then deliver the speech.
