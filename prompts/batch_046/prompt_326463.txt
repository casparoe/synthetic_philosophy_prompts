I'm finalizing a position paper for a graduate seminar on the ethics of war and want an internal consistency audit before I present. My commitments are as follows:

(a) The moral equality of combatants holds in its standard form: soldiers on opposing sides possess the same moral permission to kill one another in combat, regardless of whether their respective causes satisfy jus ad bellum, provided they observe the in bello restrictions.

(b) I am a methodological individualist about moral agency: soldiers are not fungible instruments of collective state will; each combatant retains full responsibility for the lethal acts they personally perform, and this responsibility cannot be wholly subsumed under institutional authorization.

(c) Combatant privilege is not an unconditional status but a revocable, epistemically conditioned authorization. A soldier's license to target enemy fighters depends on their holding a reasonable, justified belief in the justice of their cause.

(d) There exists what I term an "epistemic fracture point"—the moment when a soldier acquires compelling, non-negligible evidence that their war is unjust. At that point, the authorization described in (c) collapses immediately, and they may only continue to use lethal force in direct self-defense against immediate threats, not in pursuit of strategic objectives.

(e) Non-combatant immunity is grounded not in a general principle of innocence but in a direct-threat liability condition: only persons who are actively contributing to an unjustified rights-infringement may be made targets.

(f) In actual conflicts, soldiers almost never reach the epistemic fracture point described in (d), because institutional information structures are designed to prevent the acquisition of such evidence; therefore, (a) functions as the appropriate default rule for practical ethics, even though (c) and (d) govern the theoretical structure of privilege.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision that would restore consistency? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, specifying for each what exactly must be given up—whether (a), (b), (c), or (d)—and what that revision costs me conceptually. Be specific about whether the tension lies between the theoretical structure of privilege and its practical default, or between individual agency and collective authorization.
