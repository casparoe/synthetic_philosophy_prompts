I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working on metaethics and the philosophy of science, and I'm trying to fix the agenda for my dissertation on evolutionary debunking arguments. Much of the existing literature cycles through a predictable dialectic: evolutionary explanations of moral judgment are offered, realists reply with third-factor correlations or pre-established harmony, and anti-realists insist that contingency undermines justification. Rather than extending that exchange, I want to know which unresolved questions in this space are genuinely upstream of the others, which are tractable for a philosopher without empirical evolutionary biology training, and which implications for moral practice actually follow if debunking succeeds.

Specifically, I'm weighing four lines of inquiry that seem to differ sharply in their dependency relations and practical payoff. First, whether evolutionary debunking targets only moral knowledge—an epistemic undermining—or whether it also erodes the rational authority of moral reasons themselves, a practical undermining that might survive even if knowledge is recovered through some externalist maneuver. Second, whether third-factor responses require an implausible cross-niche correlation between reproductive fitness and normative truth, or whether they can be reformulated using only local ecological stability without global tracking claims. Third, a line I think is underdeveloped: whether debunking applies asymmetrically to deontological and consequentialist commitments, on the grounds that categorical, rule-based representations may be evolutionarily older and more rigid—hence more exposed to genealogical contingency—while consequentialist calculations rely on flexible, domain-general reasoning that is less vulnerable to origin-based critique. Fourth, whether the standard self-defeat objection—that evolutionary debunking undermines the belief in evolutionary theory itself—can be refined specifically for normative beliefs, such that the structure of moral reasoning (e.g., its reliance on consistency and universalizability) makes it uniquely susceptible to genealogical undermining in ways that scientific reasoning is not.

My request is for a defended prioritization, not a literature survey. Rank these four (and add or replace any that you think I'm neglecting) according to two criteria: upstream dependence—solving which ones makes the others moot or substantially easier—and tractability for someone with formal epistemology training but no laboratory access to evolutionary data. For each ranking, explain the trade-off: what you gain in theoretical leverage versus what you risk in empirical unsupportedness. Then, distinguish which practical implications genuinely hang in the balance—say, the legitimacy of reflective equilibrium, the treatment of deep moral disagreement, or the standing of moral expertise—from those that are commonly asserted but don't actually follow. If you think my framing of the asymmetry between deontological and consequentialist commitments is conceptually confused or redundant with existing distinctions, say so and propose the restructuring, but defend that restructuring by showing how it changes the dependency map among the remaining questions. I'm writing a long-form piece and want to focus my energy on the two or three questions that would actually change how philosophers and practitioners think about moral authority if settled one way or the other.
