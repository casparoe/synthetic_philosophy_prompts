I am mediating a dispute between two partners dividing a shared inheritance that consists of a family business and liquid assets. One partner has spent fifteen years running the business and reports that losing control would diminish her well-being more severely than any financial compensation could restore; the other partner has no emotional attachment to the enterprise, describes his preferences as easily satisfied by cash, and insists that fairness demands an equal division of market value because comparing "how much each cares" is like weighing pain in different currencies. Beneath the surface, the disagreement seems to turn on whether welfare can be compared across persons at all, and whether allowing such comparisons risks systematically favoring people with intense, socially cultivated preferences—what philosophers sometimes call adaptive or expensive tastes—while penalizing those with modest, flexible ones. Without requesting any bibliography, citation, or reading list, explain the precise philosophical problem embedded in this disagreement, lay out the main positions on interpersonal utility comparison that bear on the case, and advise how one might reason about a fair division without collapsing into either an implausible denial of subjective experience or an uncritical acceptance of every reported preference intensity as a valid metric of welfare loss.
