As a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and biology, I have watched the teleosemantics debate settle into a peculiar kind of paralysis. For decades, naturalists such as Ruth Millikan, David Papineau, and Karen Neander have argued that mental content can be grounded in the biological function of representational mechanisms, refining accounts of proper function to address misrepresentation and the disjunction problem; meanwhile, critics from Jerry Fodor onward have insisted that any selection-based story either presupposes intentional notions or collapses into bare causal covariance, leaving the normative dimension of error unexplained. The literature now circulates through the same canonical examples — the frog's snap-response to small dark moving objects, the heart's pumping, the eye's representing distance — and the same formal objections are met with the same structural adjustments, yet neither camp has shifted its foundational commitments in a generation.
