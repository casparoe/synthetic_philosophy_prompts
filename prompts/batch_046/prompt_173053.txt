I sit on the appeals panel of the State Architectural Licensing Board, and we must issue a ruling this week on whether to uphold or vacate a professional sanction that raises a foundational jurisprudential issue. The case involves Ms. Chen, a licensed architect sanctioned for failing to retrofit a 1965 building in violation of Section 14 of a recently adopted amendment to our building code; the amendment was produced by an automated rule-engine and formally enacted through our standard rule-of-recognition procedures. The problem is that Section 22 of the same text defines 'historical certification' in terms that automatically apply to every pre-1970 structure, which renders Section 14 a dead letter in practice, and Ms. Chen argues that this internal contradiction violates Fuller's desideratum of non-contradictoriness—part of the internal morality of law—so the rule never achieved legal validity ab initio. The board's counsel counters with a Hartian separation thesis: formal enactment and publication are sufficient for validity, and any logical incoherence is a moral or practical failure, not a legal nullity that removes our authority to sanction.

I need a formal panel opinion recommending a concrete, actionable decision—uphold the sanction or vacate it—with reasoning serious enough that the losing side cannot claim it was ignored, especially regarding whether minimal formal morality is a condition of legal validity even within a positivist framework. Please present the opinion as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbering; the first three should map the jurisprudential conflict onto the specific contradiction in the amendment, the fourth should address the practical implementation stakes for the board, and the fifth should state the final recommendation with a brief implementation note.
