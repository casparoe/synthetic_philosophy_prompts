Assume that John Stuart Mill is alive today and has been asked to evaluate a contemporary variant of the ticking-time-bomb scenario that has arisen in security ethics debates. In this variant, a state agency uses algorithmic risk profiling—acknowledged to carry substantial false-positive rates—to single out a detainee who may know the location of an imminent explosive device. Rather than conventional physical torture, the proposed method involves sustained sensory modulation and deliberate disruption of sleep cycles, calibrated to produce severe cognitive disorientation and anxiety without leaving physical marks. The government contends that this constitutes an exceptional, other-regarding harm-prevention measure permitted under Mill’s harm principle, comparable to the coercion Mill allows to stop direct injury to others. Drawing on Mill’s distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct, his account of liberty as protection against both state and social tyranny, and his broader concerns about institutional corruption and the erosion of moral character, reconstruct the position Mill would likely take. Specifically, consider whether he would regard the detainee’s psychological integrity as falling within the self-regarding sphere that even security imperatives may not invade; whether the epistemic uncertainty introduced by algorithmic profiling undermines the claim that direct harm to others is being prevented; and whether he would judge the normalization of such techniques as a greater long-term social harm than the risk of the attack itself. Present your analysis in formal American English, without providing citations, bibliographies, or references to specific modern commentators.
