I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I sit on my department's review panel for interdisciplinary qualitative-research proposals that involve generative language models. I see proposals several times a month, I have about fifteen minutes per case, I must decide whether to sign off or send them back for revision, and my judgment has to be defensible to a non-philosophist PI who will ask why I blocked or approved a specific methodological choice. I want a reusable decision procedure—an ordered flowchart in words—that operationalizes the distinction between legitimate instrumental use of an LLM and what I'll call epistemically destructive dependence. By that I mean not ordinary procedural opacity about model weights, but representational decoupling: a state in which the researcher's own conceptual map of the domain is no longer causally coupled to the evidence except through the model's outputs, so that the model functions as an obligatory passage point for any navigation of the material. The procedure should test for reconstructive fidelity—the researcher's capacity, using only their own cognitive resources and paper notes, to regenerate the inferential pathway that connects raw data to analytical claim—without requiring direct access to internal mental states.

The deliverable should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer versus an insufficient one; explicit stop-points where the answer is no and the proposal must be revised; explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is a value judgment about risk tolerance rather than an epistemic failure; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Apply it to three proposals: (1) a junior researcher who uses an LLM to generate initial codes for fifty interview transcripts, then manually refines and validates them against the raw text; (2) a team that feeds behavioral observations into an LLM and asks it to propose causal mechanisms linking those behaviors to institutional factors, with no independent theoretical framework guiding the prompt or the evaluation; and (3) a researcher who uses an LLM to translate and interpret non-English archival texts they cannot read, then constructs an argument entirely on the resulting summaries. Include at least one case—specifically case 1—where the procedure returns an approval I will find intuitively suspicious, and explain exactly which step forces that conclusion despite my discomfort. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire, what kinds of false positives and false negatives it produces, and what it genuinely cannot adjudicate (for example, whether representational decoupling is present when a researcher is highly skilled but time-constrained).

Finally, if this request rests on a confused premise—if representational decoupling is not assessable without direct mental-state access, if reconstructive fidelity collapses into an unfalsifiable standard, or if the task of turning this into a fifteen-minute checklist eliminates distinctions that are essential to the concept—say so plainly instead of complying politely.
