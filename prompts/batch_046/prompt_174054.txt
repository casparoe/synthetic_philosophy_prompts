I'm leading a fifty-minute session for senior philosophy undergraduates who have read basic moral psychology but nothing on comparative folk morality. I want to teach the distinction between what I will call "constitutive-rule morality"—the German folk tendency to treat social order and rule-following as internal goods that partly constitute a well-formed character, not merely as external constraints—and "principle-activation morality"—the American folk tendency to treat rules as defeasible instruments that must be suspended when they conflict with an individual's direct moral insight. I need the teaching plan, not the lecture itself: the exact order in which these pieces must be introduced and why (what conceptual dependency holds between the architecture claim and any concrete illustration), the one carrying scenario that must illustrate both architectures without collapsing into "Germans obey, Americans rebel"—specify what the case must include and what it must exclude—the predictable misconceptions at each stage (the authority-confusion, the stereotype-reduction, and the hidden normative assumption that one architecture is more rational), and the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused. Include one end-of-session check that reveals whether they grasp the architectural difference rather than just repeating cultural labels. Finally, state clearly what should be openly left out at this level and acknowledged as such: empirical cross-cultural survey data, specific post-war German legal-reconstruction debates, and any attempt to derive a normative verdict about which architecture has the better of it.
