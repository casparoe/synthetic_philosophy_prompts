I have been working through the implications of acausal trade since leaving my laboratory, and I find myself caught in an argument that seems to prove far too much. I present it here with numbered steps and ask where the slip occurs, and at what philosophical cost.

Consider a rational agent embedded in a deterministic or branching physical substrate—say, the Everettian multiverse or a Bohmian pilot-wave ensemble—who accepts that her decision procedure is itself a computable algorithm. Define her logical neighborhood as the set of all causally disconnected instantiations of algorithms with Kolmogorov complexity within some bounded distance of her own, weighted by the Solomonoff prior over programs. The argument proceeds as follows.

(1) Rational choice requires maximizing expected utility integrated over one's entire epistemic state, including counterfactual dependencies that are not mediated by causal influence.

(2) Within the logical neighborhood defined above, there exist instantiations that are perfect algorithmic twins—running the same code—whose local utility functions are anticorrelated with mine: if I gain, they lose, and vice versa, by the structure of the shared logical constraint that binds our outputs.

(3) There also exist adversarial variants—programs of nearly identical complexity but with inverted cooperative predicates—who will defect precisely when I cooperate, because they share my reasoning about acausal correlation but apply it with reversed preference orderings.

(4) Because the Solomonoff prior assigns non-zero measure to both twins and adversarial variants within any finite complexity bound, the expected utility of any cooperative policy must be computed over the mixture: the gain from twins multiplied by their prior weight, minus the loss from adversarial variants multiplied by theirs.

(5) For any cooperative strategy C with non-zero exposure, one can construct an adversarial variant whose prior weight is arbitrarily close to that of the cooperative twin, by adding only a constant-sized inversion module to the program.

(6) Consequently, the net expected utility of cooperation is non-positive for every C that is not maximally defensive; the adversarial terms dominate or cancel the cooperative terms in the integral.

(7) The maximally defensive strategy—call it universal defection—is invariant across causal and acausal domains, since it reveals no information about one's cooperative predicates that adversarial variants could exploit.

(8) Therefore, acausal decision theory implies that rational agents should defect universally, not only across cosmic separation but also in ordinary causal interactions where cooperation is typically advantageous.

(9) But if universal defection is rational even within one's causal future, then acausal reasoning has collapsed all distinction between local and global cooperation, rendering standard causal decision theory moot and producing a reductio of the entire framework.

I feel the error lies somewhere between (4) and (6), possibly involving an illicit quantifier shift—treating the existence of an adversarial variant for each strategy as if it were a single adversarial presence against all strategies simultaneously—or an equivocation on expected utility across modal spaces that do not share a common measure. Yet I am unsure whether the cost of repairing the argument is merely technical, such as redefining the integration measure, or whether it forces us to abandon either the Solomonoff-weighted logical neighborhood or the premise that rationality integrates over all counterfactual dependencies.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly attached to (1) and (7), though I recognize that retaining both may be exactly the source of the tension.
