I am preparing for a tutorial on nineteenth-century metaphysics of art, and I have been working through Arthur Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Representation, specifically the long discussion in Book III where he distinguishes music from the other arts. In one particularly dense passage—rendered in an older English translation that retains much of the original German syntax—he appears to claim that whereas painting and poetry copy the Platonic Ideas that are themselves objectifications of the Will, music is not a copy of an Idea at all, but rather an immediate and direct copy of the Will itself. He then seems to map the various grades of the Will's objectification onto musical structure: the bass voices correspond to the inorganic realm, the harmonic middle voices to the vegetative and animal kingdoms, and melody—especially in human song—to the highest grade of objectification, namely human consciousness with all its striving and suffering.

What I find difficult to reconcile is the precise metaphysical status of this claim within Schopenhauer's broader Kantian framework. If music truly bypasses the realm of representation—if it is not mediated by the Ideas and therefore not subject to the principle of sufficient reason—then in what sense can it still be experienced as ordered temporal succession, with harmonic relations, rhythm, and melodic development, all of which seem to depend on the very forms of representation (time, space, causality) that Schopenhauer insists govern the phenomenal world? Furthermore, does this direct access to the Will mean that music offers a kind of metaphysical insight that painting or poetry cannot, or does it simply make us more acutely conscious of the same blind, striving Will that underlies all existence, thereby deepening rather than alleviating our suffering? I should be grateful for any help in unpacking the logic of this passage without importing later Wagnerian interpretations that are not in the text itself.
