Having spent the better part of three decades in experimental physics before turning, somewhat unexpectedly, to philosophy of mind, I find myself returning repeatedly to the paradox of fiction with the impatience of someone who expects mechanisms rather than mere taxonomy. The puzzle, as I understand it, is how we can undergo apparently genuine emotional responses — grief at a character's death, suspense at a narrative crisis — whilst simultaneously knowing that the objects of these responses do not exist. I am familiar with the broad outline of Walton's make-believe theory, according to which engagement with fiction involves a kind of pretence or game of make-believe in which we experience not full-blown emotions but quasi-emotional analogues. More recently, however, I have been struck by an idea drawn from my former discipline: predictive processing models treat cognition as inference under precision-weighted prediction errors, and one might extend this framework to propose that fiction operates by globally suppressing the precision of reality-monitoring signals, thereby allowing affective predictions to update our internal states without triggering the belief-formation mechanisms that would normally veto them. Compare these two accounts — Walton's make-believe framework and this predictive-processing framing of fiction — with particular attention to three questions: whether each treats our emotional responses as genuinely affective or merely simulated; how each explains the well-documented phenomenon of lasting moral or attitudinal change induced by fictional narratives; and whether the predictive account can preserve a meaningful distinction between being moved by a novel and being moved by a vivid daydream, or whether it risks collapsing the two in an unhelpful manner. I should be grateful if you would present your answer in British English, and I would ask that you refrain from offering bibliographies or reading lists, since I am working without ready access to a library.
