At a philosophy of economics seminar, two colleagues — Maya and Leo — disagreed about what the backward induction paradox in the centipede game genuinely demonstrates. Maya argued that the paradox largely collapses once we recognise that "rationality" within game-theoretic analysis describes structural properties of strategies rather than transparent psychological states that agents recursively model in one another. Once we stop treating rationality as common knowledge of mental contents, the step-by-step unravelling loses its normative force; cooperative outcomes remain available to bounded agents who do not perform infinite epistemic iteration, and the failure to reach them is a descriptive limitation, not an internal contradiction. Leo replied that this dissolves too much: the paradox genuinely exposes an instability in common-knowledge reasoning about rationality, with concrete institutional consequences. He cited long-horizon collective-action problems — for instance, multi-decade climate agreements — where the defection point is not fixed exogenously but shifts endogenously with each party's model of the others' temporal discounting and credibility constraints. In such settings, standard backward induction fails descriptively, not because rationality is opaque, but because the game tree itself is shaped by the very beliefs being modelled. Maya countered that Leo conflates descriptive failure with normative paradox: even if real agents avoid immediate defection, this shows only that they violate the axioms of common-knowledge rationality, not that those axioms are incoherent.
