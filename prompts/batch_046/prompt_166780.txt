I am drafting a winter scene for a novel in which two senior academics—Marcus, an economist, and Elena, a political philosopher—debate a departmental vote that occurred earlier that afternoon, and I need their disagreement to be intellectually rigorous without sounding like a lecture. The faculty had used pairwise comparisons to choose a new chair from three candidates, and the sincere preferences cycled: a majority preferred A to B, B to C, and yet C to A. Marcus insists this is the Condorcet paradox manifest in real institutional life, and that Arrow’s impossibility theorem proves collective rationality is unattainable without one actor functioning as a dictator. Elena replies that transitivity is merely a formal constraint, not a moral requirement of democracy, and that practical institutions survive such cycles through mechanisms like rotating agenda control, so the paradox exposes a mathematical curiosity rather than democratic failure. Marcus then recounts how the dean actually sequenced the pairwise ballots to exploit the cycle, ensuring his own preferred candidate won despite a different sincere ordering among the voters; Elena distinguishes this strategic manipulation from the paradox proper, arguing that Marcus is conflating agenda-setting corruption with preference inconsistency, but Marcus counters that the framing of each comparison—whether voters were asked to weigh “vision” against “experience” or to choose “candidate A versus B”—already constructed the preference, making the notion of a stable “sincere” baseline illusory. Elena then shifts ground: she proposes that if voting is understood not as the aggregation of private preferences but as an epistemic procedure for identifying the best candidate, then the cycle does not reveal democratic incoherence but rather that the group lacks a shared evidentiary standard, which is a failure of collective knowledge rather than of collective choice. Marcus answers that if there is no shared standard to pool, then the epistemic framing collapses back into the original problem—there is nothing stable for the group to know together. They turn to me and ask for an adjudication, and since I am writing their voices I need to understand precisely where each stands: Is Arrow genuinely activated in this specific three-candidate meeting? Does rotating agenda control solve the problem or merely displace it? Is the dean’s sequencing an instance of the paradox or an external corruption of it? And does Elena’s epistemic turn salvage collective decision-making, or does it only rename the failure? Please go through their exchange move by move, rating each claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right, identifying what both miss—particularly whether either account can explain why several voters reversed their pairwise rankings when the ballot was reframed around “vision versus experience” rather than named candidates—and say who is more right overall.
