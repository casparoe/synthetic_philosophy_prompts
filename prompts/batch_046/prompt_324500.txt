I have recently inherited a modest multi-family property in a neighborhood now targeted by aggressive upzoning proposals and intense anti-development organizing among residents who have lived there for decades. As I weigh whether to support the rezoning or to join the opposition, I am torn between two moral pictures: one that treats continuous habitation as generating a proprietary-like claim over the character of a place, and one that treats the city as a commons in which exclusionary zoning is an unjust barrier to newcomers. Write an essay examining whether long-term residents possess a moral right—distinct from legal property rights—to resist zoning changes that would alter the density and social fabric of their neighborhood. In your analysis, weigh the idea that occupancy rights rooted in lived spatial memory and intergenerational community investment carry independent moral weight against the right-to-the-city argument that urban space should be governed by universal access and distributive fairness rather than by the preferences of current occupants. Address the charge that NIMBYism is merely disguised class protection, but also consider whether blanket upzoning ignores a legitimate form of place-based identity. Reach and defend a clear conclusion: either that residents do possess such a moral right and specify its limits, or that they do not and that universal access takes precedence.
