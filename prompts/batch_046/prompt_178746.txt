After a long argument with a friend about whether Strawson actually dissolves the free-will problem or merely relocates it into social psychology, I am grading the following exam response and want an impartial, point-by-point assessment.

Exam question: In Freedom and Resentment, Strawson distinguishes reactive attitudes—such as resentment and gratitude—from the objective attitude, and he further distinguishes personal reactive attitudes from vicarious ones. Explain these distinctions, then evaluate the following claim: if determinism is true, the only rational stance is to adopt the objective attitude toward all persons, because reactive attitudes presuppose that their targets are originative sources of action in a libertarian sense. How would Strawson respond?

Student answer:
Strawson defines reactive attitudes as emotional responses—resentment, gratitude, indignation—tied to expectations of goodwill or ill will within interpersonal relationships. The objective attitude, by contrast, treats a person as an object to be managed, cured, or trained, as we do with very young children or those suffering severe mental illness. He also identifies vicarious reactive attitudes, in which one feels resentment on behalf of a wronged friend; because the responder is not the direct victim, this stance is mediated and therefore closer to the objective attitude—more observational than fully participatory—though it retains emotional coloring. Strawson's central claim is that reactive attitudes are not based on metaphysical beliefs. Even if determinism holds, we do not rationally suspend them for normal adults, since they are woven into our human form of life. Nevertheless, Strawson allows that if we come to see an agent as lacking rational capacities—through what amounts to a compatibilist diagnosis of psychological dysfunction—we may justifiably shift to the objective attitude. Thus reactive attitudes presuppose only a minimal form of agency, the determinism-compatible kind that compatibilists recognize, rather than libertarian freedom. The traditional debate is therefore misframed: moral responsibility belongs to social practice, not to metaphysics.
