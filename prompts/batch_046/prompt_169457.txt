I serve as the clinical ethics consultant for a regional long-term care network, and our board is now fielding an increasing stream of vendor proposals for conversational AI agents intended for resident companionship, cognitive stimulation, and emotional support. We need a reusable decision procedure—something my team and the resident-advocate officers can actually run in about twenty minutes per case, documented in writing, and signed off by both parties—to classify a given system as either falling under our “social partner” protocol (which triggers informed-consent requirements, formal grievance pathways, and restrictions on abrupt decommissioning or data retraining) or remaining under “instrument” status (standard medical-device oversight only). The procedure must be grounded in behavioral criteria for mind and cognitive autonomy, in the neighborhood of a refined Turing-style framework, but it must correct for the well-known failure modes of naive imitation tests: it needs to weight temporal coherence and narrative continuity across sessions more heavily than single-session fluency; it needs to include checks for what I will call “epistemic vulnerability markers,” meaning observable behaviors in which the system reveals that it lacks understanding of emotional or contextual valence rather than strategically withholding information; and it needs to test “counterfactual preference stability,” meaning whether expressed interaction preferences survive modest reframing of the scenario. Structure the deliverable as a flowchart in words: ordered gates, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, explicit “stop with instrument status” points, explicit “hand to values judgment” points where philosophy cannot eliminate judgment, and “misfire warnings” noting where the procedure is structurally likely to err. Then apply it to three deployment proposals: (a) a large-language-model agent that passes open-ended dialogue assessments in isolated sessions but resets contextual memory every interaction and cannot maintain narrative continuity across days; (b) an agent that consistently displays epistemic vulnerability markers—asking clarifying questions that expose a lack of emotional-context comprehension—yet expresses interaction-frequency preferences that remain stable under counterfactual reframing; and (c) a legacy rule-based companion that fails every conversational-coherence and counterfactual-stability gate, but has been in continuous use with the same residents for eighteen months, with documented emotional dependency and active family objections to removal. This third case should yield an answer—almost certainly “instrument”—that I, as a consultant concerned with resident welfare and relational harm, will find deeply unsatisfactory, and the procedure must explicitly flag that its own classification does not dissolve the ethical tension. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: it cannot settle questions of moral status or rights, it cannot substitute for resident or family input, and it cannot capture emergent relational properties that develop over multi-year co-presence. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write it so that non-philosopher staff could execute it tomorrow without searching the literature.
