I've been working through arguments about moral objectivity for a graduate seminar I'm taking on comparative ethics, and the topic has become personally urgent because I'm writing my term paper on whether cross-cultural moral variation undermines realism. In our last session, a fellow student pressed an analogy that keeps recurring in the literature: the variation in folk moral judgments across WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations is like the variation in local food-preservation techniques—salting, smoking, fermenting—each an adaptive response to local climate and pathogens, with no universal "correct" method independent of ecological constraint. She uses this to license a strong anti-objectivist conclusion: just as a Scandinavian salter cannot claim smoking is objectively wrong, WEIRD morality is merely one locally successful technique, and its claim to universal validity is a category error. Before I commit to a response in my paper, I want to audit this analogy rigorously. Can you lay out the explicit mapping—what in the moral domain corresponds to climate, to pathogens, to preservation technique, to functional success—and then separate the disanalogies that actually damage the anti-realist argument from the ones that are merely decorative? Specifically, does the analogy smuggle in a functionalist account of morality that realists can reject without denying variation, and if those disanalogies are pressed, does the conclusion that moral objectivity collapses still survive, or does it require a different supporting image altogether? Give me a committed verdict with reasons: either the analogy genuinely carries the argument if read precisely, or it fails at identifiable joints that I should name, together with what a more resilient analogy for the same conclusion would look like if this one breaks.
