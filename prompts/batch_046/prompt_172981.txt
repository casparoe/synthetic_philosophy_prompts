Philosophers have argued for centuries about Leibniz's thesis that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds, yet the disagreement shows little sign of resolution despite advances in modal logic and the philosophy of religion. One camp reads the claim as an empirically reckless optimism—effectively falsified by the scale of natural and moral evil—while another treats it as a tautological consequence of divine choice within a pre-given modal space, and a third interprets it as a normative assertion about the optimal balance of simplicity and phenomenon that never required comparison with unactualised worlds. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is largely conceptual: the sides operate with incompatible norms of evaluation—some framing "best" as a global maximisation over all compossible worlds, others as a local satisfiability constraint compatible with divine simplicity—yet they present themselves as disputing one and the same metaphysical fact. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the stall driven instead by a disguised formal disagreement about compossibility relations, or by an unacknowledged empirical claim about the distribution of evil? I want an analysis that engages what each position actually asserts rather than surveying interpretations, together with an honest assessment of whether a new formal result in optimal-world semantics, a precise normative distinction—perhaps between Pareto-optimality across worlds and satisficing within actualist constraints—or a reframing of the question itself could break the deadlock, or whether the dispute is fundamentally ill-posed.
