I've been thinking alot lately about whether dreams are actually experiences, especially after a weird late-night argument with my roommate where he swore he "experienced" falling off a building in REM sleep and I told him that's not the same kind of thing at all. I went back and read up on Malcolm's argument that dreams can't be experiences since there's no verification condition available during sleep, and Dennett's cassette theory where the dream gets loaded like a tape only when you wake up. Now I'm trying to defend this hybrid position I'm calling the "retroactive binding view" — basically, dreams don't carry real phenomenal content while you're asleep, but they do leave a kind of pseudo-phenomenal trace that only gets bound into a narrative upon awakening, and this trace is real enough to matter for practical and ethical purposes without being a full-blown experience in Malcolm's stricter sense.

I'm pretty convinced, but I want to stress-test it properly. What would actually count against this view? Not just the standard Dennett objections or Malcolm's verificationist moves, but which specific intuitions, which hypothetical cases — say, lucid dreamers sending real-time eye-movement signals, or someone recalling dream-content without any waking narrative at all — and which results from other areas, maybe about memory consolidation during sleep or about what counts as experience in non-human animals, it stands or falls with? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a position of this kind. In particular, tell me which of these defeaters a committed retroactive-binding theorist would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they'd dismiss as missing the point.
