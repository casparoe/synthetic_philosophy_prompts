I have spent the last eighteen months coordinating intake assessments at a community mental-health clinic, and I have watched patients adopt diagnostic language so thoroughly that their symptom reports begin to mirror the manual's examples almost verbatim. This experience made Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects—where classifying people changes the people classified—feel less like an abstract philosophical thesis and more like a daily operational hazard. I am now drafting internal guidelines for our team, and I want to be precise about the mechanisms involved.

To that end, I have sketched four distinct dynamics that seem to operate in our setting:

1. Classic interactive looping, in Hacking's sense: the category provides a script, patients enact it, and clinicians observe the enacted behavior as natural evidence for the category.
2. What I am tentatively calling reflexive criteria drift: clinicians gradually broaden a category's boundaries because patients who have adopted the label present modified symptom profiles, and these modified profiles are then incorporated into revised assessment protocols without being flagged as artifacts of the labeling process itself.
3. Stigma-driven concealment, where awareness of a label's social cost suppresses certain symptoms in some populations while exaggerating them in others seeking validation or access to care, distorting prevalence data without necessarily changing the underlying phenomenon.
4. Therapeutic overidentification, where a clinician's commitment to a particular explanatory framework leads them to interpret ambiguous behaviors as instances of that framework's central kind, independently of any patient self-conception.

Please rank these four mechanisms—assigning each a score from 1 (least distorting to epidemiological and clinical judgment) to 10 (most distorting)—specifically with respect to their impact on long-term epidemiological tracking in community mental-health settings over multi-year timelines. I want your reasoning to show, for each mechanism, how it interacts with institutional record-keeping and patient self-report, not merely a bare ordering.

Crucially, separate what is well established in the philosophy and sociology of psychiatry from what remains genuinely contested. Do not present Hacking's looping framework as the uncontested explanation for all classification-related change; note where critics argue that underlying biological or social realities drive the observed patterns instead. Likewise, treat my proposed distinction between reflexive criteria drift and classic looping as provisional—explain whether it holds up or collapses into existing concepts, and flag any part of my taxonomy that relies on speculative rather than documented dynamics. Do not present any contested view as the consensus view.
