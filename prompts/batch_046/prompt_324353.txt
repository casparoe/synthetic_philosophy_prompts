I am a high school junior preparing an interview for our school’s philosophy podcast, and next week’s guest is a scholar who defends John Dewey’s experimentalist thesis that democratic politics should be modeled on collective inquiry rather than on the aggregation of fixed private preferences. I do not want questions that ask her merely to restate Dewey’s view; I want questions that probe its actual structure, press where the literature presses, and include a follow-up keyed to the defense she is most likely to offer.

Please draft six interview questions in a tone that is serious and accessible to an educated general audience. Each question should be paired with a brief follow-up that engages the standard reply anticipated in Deweyan scholarship. I ask that the sequence cover at least the following four points:

First, whether the analogy between scientific inquiry and democratic deliberation is intended as structural or only heuristic, and specifically what democratic legitimacy looks like when citizens lack the specialized training and peer review that define scientific expertise.

Second, how Dewey’s rejection of fixed political ends avoids collapsing into pure proceduralism or relativism—particularly, what non-arbitrary criterion distinguishes genuine “growth” from mere historical change, and whether that criterion can be specified without smuggling in the very fixed ends the view rejects.

Third, whether the emphasis on education as preparation for democratic inquiry risks an elitist or state-controlled conception of citizenship, and how the view can maintain that inquiry is genuinely democratic rather than technocratic.

Fourth, how experimental democracy addresses deep value conflicts that do not appear resolvable by shared empirical evidence—whether treating moral commitments as “working hypotheses” survives cases where disagreement is not about predictive failure but about incommensurable goods, and whether such conflicts expose a limit to the inquiry model itself.

Please do not include generic biographical questions, do not ask for a bibliography or reading list, and do not frame any question around highly obscure secondary debates that assume familiarity with recent specialist literature. I want the questions to be answerable by a defender working from the core conceptual framework alone.
