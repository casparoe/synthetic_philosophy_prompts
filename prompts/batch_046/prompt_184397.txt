You advise a regulatory science board that must evaluate predictive models submitted for policy use. Many submissions rest on assumptions that are known to be false—homogeneous mixing in epidemiology, rational expectations in macroeconomics, linear climate feedbacks—and reviewers disagree about whether such unrealism disqualifies a model. Operationalize the methodological principle that unrealistic assumptions are not fatal per se, but become dangerous precisely when they remain load-bearing in the domain where predictions are actually being made rather than being isolated or empirically calibrated away. Write a reusable decision procedure—a sequence of questions with stop conditions, judgment-call flags, and required expertise—that a non-specialist reviewer can apply to determine whether a model's false assumptions corrupt its policy-relevant outputs. The procedure must distinguish assumptions that are analytically harmless, those that are structurally load-bearing but shielded by calibration within the tested domain, and those that drive predictions into untested territory. Apply it to three cases: an SIR model with homogeneous mixing used for a spatially clustered outbreak; a DSGE macroeconomic model with rational expectations used during a behavioral-panic crisis; and a climate-sensitivity model using constant linear feedbacks applied near a tipping-point regime. For each case, show where the procedure gives a clear verdict and where it forces the board into a values-based judgment about risk tolerance. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle—for instance, whether empirical calibration in one domain licenses prediction in another, and how to weigh false negatives against false positives when the stakes involve public harm.
