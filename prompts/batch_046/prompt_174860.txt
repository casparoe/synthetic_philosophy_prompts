I'm a high school student writing an ethics essay, and I've noticed a recurring pattern in debates about fairness that I don't know how to classify. Here are two concrete instances. First, a school district can either keep every school at the same moderate funding level or allow one magnet school to reach exceptional funding while leaving all others exactly unchanged; opponents often say the exceptional school shouldn't be allowed, not because anyone is harmed or because equality is intrinsically good, but because the visible gap makes it easier for officials to ignore the unchanged schools, as if moral urgency were diluted by disparity. Second, in global health discussions, some argue that developing a very expensive cure for a rare condition should be delayed—not because the money would instead fund basic care, but because the cure's existence creates a stark disparity that, in their view, weakens the moral pressure to provide that basic care for everyone. In both cases, no one is made worse off by the improvement at the top, yet people treat that improvement as a kind of obstacle to justice for the bottom. I know this sits near prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection, but it doesn't seem to match either: nobody is proposing to reduce the well-off to match the worst-off, and the concern is not envy or strict equality but something like the idea that a prominent top tier competes with or obscures our duty to the worst-off. Does this configuration have an established name? If so, how exactly does it differ from the leveling-down objection and from standard prioritarian reasoning? If not, how would you define and bound it, and what distinguishes it from plain rhetorical distraction? Please answer in American English.
