I am trying to determine what I actually think about material constitution by examining my reactions to concrete cases, and I want to know whether a coherent principle unites them or whether I must drop at least one verdict. Here are my judgments. If a clay statue is squashed into a formless lump, the statue is destroyed but the clay remains. If that clay is melted and reshaped into a vase, the clay is the same object but the statue is not. If a statue is repaired gradually over decades until every original atom has been replaced, I still take it to be the same statue persisting through time. If it is fully disassembled, its parts stored untouched in a warehouse for a decade, and then reassembled precisely, I lean toward saying it is the same statue, though I am unsure. If an ice statue is kept in an unchanging shape for years by a refrigeration system that continuously substitutes melted water with new frozen water, I think one statue continues rather than being replaced daily. But if an artist takes the same clay each morning, forms it into an identically shaped statue, and dissolves it back into formless clay each evening for a month, I do not think there is one statue with intermittent existence; I think there are thirty successive statues. And if the statue is split in two, each half melted and reshaped into a miniature replica of the original, I judge that the original is destroyed and that neither miniature is it, despite together containing all the original material. Is there a precise principle—perhaps about spatiotemporal continuity, causal connection, or potential for reassembly—that captures this pattern, and what does it predict for a case I have not listed, namely a statue that is scanned, destroyed, and rebuilt elsewhere from entirely different material using the scan? Please identify which clause of the principle each case motivates, state honestly which of my verdicts the principle must reject if it is to remain simple, and present everything in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
