Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, Mara and Leo, debating the paradox of tragedy—why audiences actively seek art that evokes grief, dread, or despair. Mara defends a modified predictive-processing account: tragic narratives deliver high-precision updates to our social-emotional models in a safe, other-referential frame, and the pleasure derives from the successful reduction of prediction error about human suffering rather than from the suffering itself. Leo counters with a view he terms axiological decompression: the enjoyment is not in the negative affect but in the rare moral clarity that follows when a narrative strips away everyday ethical compromise; tragedy is valued as a cognitive reset, not as an emotional workout. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical. Leo should press Mara on why we do not seek equally precise negative updates about our own health or finances, and Mara should reply by distinguishing self-referential from other-referential error minimization. Then Mara should challenge Leo with interactive tragedies in which the audience directs the protagonist's fatal choice—if Leo is right that we value post-tragic clarity, such agency should deepen the pleasure, yet these experiences are typically disturbing rather than rewarding. Leo should concede that agency complicates his account, and Mara should concede that her framework needs a boundary condition for when other-referential updates become aversive. Keep the dialogue to about 400 words, do not invoke named scholars or specific texts, and ensure the philosophical idea does structural work—determining what each speaker can and cannot claim—rather than serving as decoration.
