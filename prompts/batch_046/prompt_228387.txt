The long-running dispute about Holocaust uniqueness, modernity, and comparability—running from the Historikerstreit through Bauman's claim that modern bureaucracy made industrial genocide administratively possible—seems stuck in a loop: one camp insists that comparing the Holocaust to other mass killings flattens its specific moral horror, while another insists that isolating it obscures the modern organizational and technological conditions that made it possible. Both sides appear to accept Bauman's sociological premise and both reject any normalization of the event, yet they talk past each other. Is the deadlock a hidden disagreement about what comparability entails—structural analogy versus moral equivalence—or about whether explaining an event via modern rationalization automatically functions as an exculpatory narrative? Or are historians and moral philosophers answering different questions—one about causal explanation, one about commemorative obligation—while using the same vocabulary?
