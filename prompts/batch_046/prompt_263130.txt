I am trying to understand a passage from René Descartes’ Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), in the First Meditation, where he considers whether he might now be dreaming. He describes having often been convinced in sleep that he was sitting by the fire in his dressing gown, only to realise upon waking that he was lying undressed in bed; he then asks whether there are certain signs by which he could distinguish being awake from being asleep, and finds none.

I want to read this through the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreams are experiences. Malcolm argued that reports of dreaming are not descriptions of mental episodes that occurred during sleep, since there is no independent behavioural or physiological criterion for such episodes apart from the report itself; Dennett defended, then revised, an account on which dreams involve the playing of a “cassette” of experience during sleep, with narrative confabulation occurring upon awakening.

My confusion is whether Descartes’ sceptical use of dreaming presupposes exactly the kind of “experience” that Malcolm denies. Descartes treats the dreamer as having genuinely experienced being by the fire, with false content, and then corrects this upon waking. But if Malcolm is right, there may be no “experience of being by the fire” during sleep at all—merely a disposition, upon waking, to tell a story. Does Descartes’ argument therefore depend on a Cartesian theatre model of consciousness that both Malcolm and Dennett reject?

I have tried to frame this using what I call a “retrospective authority” principle: the idea that upon waking, a subject has privileged access to the fact that a prior mental state was an experience with determinate content. This seems necessary for Descartes’ move, yet it looks incompatible with Malcolm’s criterion-based scepticism and with Dennett’s distributed, narrative-based account. Could you help me see whether Descartes’ sceptical point can be reconstructed without this principle, or whether the First Meditation implicitly commits him to a theory of dream experience he never defends? Please stay close to the texts and do not invent positions for either philosopher.
