I am preparing an assessment for my advanced high school philosophy seminar on comparative theories of the self, and I need an exam question that is rigorous enough for an upper-level introductory college course but does not depend on memorized summaries. The domain is Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, yet I want the prompt to inject a specific conceptual tension rather than ask for a general overview: students should be required to compare the Ubuntu claim that full personhood is constituted through sustained communal relationship with the capacity-based framework used in contemporary bioethics, which assigns moral status according to internal cognitive or sentient capacities regardless of social context. Specifically, the essay question should present a hypothetical case—such as a being who retains complete rational capacities but has been permanently isolated from all human community, or an artificial agent with no social history—to ask whether such a being counts as a person in the full Ubuntu sense, whether this exposes Ubuntu to a version of the marginal-cases objection that individualist theories face, or whether relational personhood actually resolves those cases more coherently than capacity-based accounts.

Please provide the following in formal American English:
1. The exam question itself, designed for a 60-minute essay.
2. A model answer of approximately 500 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 points, divided into at least five specific analytical moves that a top answer must contain, with point allocations and a brief note on the common error that should cost marks for each move.

The question must be unanswerable by merely reciting that personhood is constituted through others or by summarizing standard descriptions of Ubuntu; it should force students to apply the relational framework to the capacity-versus-relation tension, identify one genuine strength and one genuine vulnerability of the Ubuntu position, and clearly distinguish Ubuntu from social-contract individualism. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, as the answering model may not have online access. The language should be polished and formal.
