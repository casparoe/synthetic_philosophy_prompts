As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I keep encountering an analogy that treats honoring sunk costs like running a refrigeration cycle with a broken compressor: the work already expended to compress the refrigerant is dissipated and irrecoverable, so a rational controller ought to respond only to the present temperature gradient, never to the lost energy. Proponents deploy this to claim that any weight given to past investment is as irrational as trying to reclaim dissipated heat, hence honoring sunk costs is always fallacious. Audit this analogy precisely. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the correspondence table — compressor, dissipated work, gradient, controller — mapped onto decision-theoretic counterparts; second, which features of thermodynamic irreversibility genuinely parallel irrecoverable economic or psychological investment; third, the disanalogies that matter for the normative claim, especially path-dependent values such as loyalty, identity, or completed phases that have no thermal analogue; fourth, whether the conclusion that honoring sunk costs is always irrational survives once the breaks are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument; fifth, whether a different physical analogy, or none at all, better captures when honoring past commitment is rational rather than fallacious.
