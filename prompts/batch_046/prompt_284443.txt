I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on functional explanation in synthetic biology, and I'm stuck on what seems like a tension between etiological and causal-role accounts. I want to make sure I'm not smuggling in a false dichotomy.

Consider this case: a synthetic metabolic pathway is engineered de novo into E. coli using modular enzyme parts, then optimized through iterative in vitro selection for degrading a novel xenobiotic compound. Once integrated, it contributes to cellular homeostasis exactly as a naturally evolved detox pathway would—complete with feedback regulation and fitness improvement in the presence of the toxin.

My intuition is that a causal-role theorist along Cummins-style lines would grant it the function of detoxification immediately, since that is its actual contribution to system capacity. An etiological theorist, however, seems compelled to deny it has that proper function, because its presence is explained not by ancestral natural selection in wild populations but by human design and artificial selection in a bioreactor. Yet if the synthetic pathway acquires a mutation that disrupts feedback inhibition, we still want to say it is malfunctioning, not merely behaving differently. This suggests that etiological accounts need causal-role standards to ground normative judgments about synthetic artifacts. But if etiological theorists respond by allowing laboratory selection to count as legitimate selection history, then the distinction collapses: any currently useful contribution could be retrofitted with an artificial selection narrative.

So my question is whether the etiological account is inherently restricted to wild-type evolutionary history, rendering it inadequate for synthetic biology, or whether there is a principled reason—other than merely expanding what counts as selection—to maintain that this pathway lacks the proper function of detoxification despite its causal efficacy.
