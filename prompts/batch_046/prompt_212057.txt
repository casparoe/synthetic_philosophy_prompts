After a long argument with a friend about why museums treat some historical speculations differently from others, I keep returning to a concrete curatorial asymmetry. In most archaeology and natural-history museums, reconstructed ancient technologies—a Roman concrete mixer, a Neolithic irrigation layout—are displayed without any "hypothetical" warning, even though the reconstruction often relies on fragmentary texts and analogical inference rather than direct physical evidence. Yet if an exhibit proposed a Fomenko-style compressed chronology, or suggested that certain pre-Columbian monuments were engineered with guidance from non-human agents, the same institution would either exclude it outright or quarantine it in a marked "pseudohistory" gallery labeled discredited. The evidential gap does not obviously justify the difference in treatment: mainstream reconstructions frequently rest on equally indirect reasoning. What philosophical theory of demarcation does this labeling practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to a sharp science-versus-pseudoscience boundary grounded in method, in social authority, or in some epistemic virtue like explanatory coherence? Or is the practice actually compatible with a continuum view of historical knowledge, with the asymmetry functioning merely as sociological gatekeeping? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether it is philosophically underdetermined—licensable by several rival demarcation criteria. Then tell me what a museum genuinely committed to fallibilism and continuous evidential weight would have to change: not only in how it presents fringe chronologies, but in how it labels its own mainstream reconstructions.
