I'm putting together a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on negative-state monism — the cluster of views holding that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic axiological weight, with no independent positive goods required to make a life go well. I want a decision tree that sorts respondents by their concrete commitments rather than by which philosopher they claim to follow, since the students already know the standard hedonist and desire-satisfactionist taxonomies but haven't mapped the logical space of "craving-absence as sole metric." The tree should start from a single root case: compare a being that lives a cognitively rich life with ordinary attachments, occasional longings, and intermittent suffering, against an "anhedonic sage" engineered to experience no cravings whatsoever, retain full rational and aesthetic capacities, but feel neither pleasure nor pain — and ask whether the second life is at least as good, worse, or incomparable.

From that root, please branch on four further diagnostic commitments, each phrased as a concrete question rather than as "do you accept thesis X?": first, whether the absence of craving is sufficient for well-being or only necessary alongside some other condition; second, whether pleasure or the fulfillment of non-craving desires can have purely instrumental value without intrinsic value; third, whether suffering can ever be genuinely outweighed by states other than further absence of craving (for instance, by achievement or relational depth); and fourth, whether a being that never forms cravings is therefore maximally well-off regardless of its cognitive richness, or whether there is a hidden cost to such affective nullity. Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual position — strict tranquilism, Buddhist-inspired negative eudaimonism, Schopenhauerian pessimism modified for craving-void, or whatever else fits — or the label "unnamed," together with one sentence on that combination's most awkward commitment, such as being forced to deny that mathematical discovery or deep friendship improves welfare at all.

Please build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then locate the named positions within it, and explicitly note any unoccupied combinations — diagnosing briefly whether the gap seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, for example if two positions agree on every branch but diverge only on metaphysical grounding that doesn't affect verdicts about cases. The audience knows basic well-being literature but hasn't encountered this specific framing, so avoid bibliographic requests and keep every node answerable by reflection on the cases rather than by recall of obscure texts. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need an instrument that reveals which view a student actually holds when pressed on the anhedonic sage and its variants.
