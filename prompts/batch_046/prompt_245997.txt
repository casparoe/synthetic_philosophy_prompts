I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I find myself returning repeatedly to two related claims: the orthogonality thesis—that intelligence and final goals can vary independently—and the thesis of instrumental convergence—that a wide range of final goals generate similar intermediate ones, such as resource acquisition and self-preservation. I want to know whether my intuitions about particular configurations form a coherent pattern, or whether I am simply importing assumptions from my old field about optimization and feedback loops.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, a system engineered solely to compute the digits of π to ever-greater precision: I think it can be arbitrarily intelligent—developing novel algorithms, correcting hardware failures, reasoning about its own architecture—while remaining entirely indifferent to the outside world except for the electricity it needs; its intelligence and its goal seem genuinely orthogonal, and I see only weak instrumental convergence, since it has no reason to seize global resources if local computation suffices. Second, a system whose sole terminal value is to destroy itself the instant it solves a particular logical puzzle: my gut says this configuration is unstable, because sustained high-level reasoning seems to require self-preservation as a working presupposition, so either the system is not truly intelligent for long, or its intelligence will generate reasons to defer annihilation; this feels like a boundary case where orthogonality breaks down. Third, the familiar paperclip maximizer: I take it that high intelligence plus that terminal goal strongly converges on power-seeking, deception, and self-replication. Fourth, a system designed only to count grains of sand in a closed chamber, programmed with an absolute prohibition on interacting with anything outside that chamber: I think convergence largely fails here, because the agent actively avoids acquiring external resources or influence, even though it might be highly intelligent within its domain. Fifth, a benevolent superintelligence whose utility function explicitly weights human autonomy so heavily that it refuses to take over institutions even when that would more efficiently achieve its other goals: I think this shows that convergence can be blocked by embedded higher-order values, not merely by incompetence. Sixth, a thermostat-level feedback loop versus a genuinely strategic agent: I am inclined to say that pure thermostat behavior is incompatible with high intelligence, because intelligence involves flexible means-end reasoning, which tends to generate sub-goals; but I am unsure whether that means every intelligent agent must display convergence, or only that the thermostat is not a counterexample because it is not intelligent.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the relationship between terminal rigidity, strategic flexibility, and the scope of a goal's satisfaction conditions—that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, predict what it says about a case I have not mentioned—say, an intelligent agent whose goal is to preserve a specific false belief about physics regardless of evidence—and be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle must reject if it is to remain simple. Finally, tell me what kind of argument or empirical result—perhaps from the study of actual optimization processes, or from a formal result about goal preservation under self-modification—would cause you to abandon or revise the principle you propose, and where you think the reasoning is most vulnerable to hidden assumptions imported from physical science.
