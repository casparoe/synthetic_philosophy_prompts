I hold that the legal narrowing of Lemkin's concept of genocide—above all the exclusion of political groups and cultural destruction from the 1948 Convention—was not a clarifying refinement but a strategic retreat that converted the category from an active moral tripwire into a retrospective honorific applied mainly to uncontested past events. I also claim that the contemporary politics of naming atrocities produces a widening gap between accusation and protection: the more frequently the genocide label is deployed in live diplomatic discourse, the less it triggers the specific emergency obligations Lemkin envisioned, because states now treat it as a post-hoc judicial classification rather than an immediate call to intervention. What would count decisively against this? Separate fatal defeaters—historical or empirical patterns showing that broader definitions were actually less effective, or that rapid genocide labeling has consistently preceded successful protective action—from merely awkward ones, such as individual cases of rhetorical overuse. Address whether there is a structural reason the broader Lemkin-style definition collapses into a general mass-atrocity category, whether my naming-intervention gap can be tested against counterexamples where labeling was swift and followed by action, and whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability by redefining "effective moral tripwire" in terms that no institutional failure could disprove. I want an honest ranking of the evidence that should move me, including what defenders of the narrower Convention definition would themselves concede counts against my view.
