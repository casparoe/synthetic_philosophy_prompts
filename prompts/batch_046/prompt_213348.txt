As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to the philosophy of logic, I am preparing a 75-minute seminar for eight graduate students with no prior formal training in the subject. The session concerns Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which Achilles and the Tortoise discover that every inference rule invoked to justify a deduction can itself be demanded as an explicit premise, producing an infinite regress. I do not want to lecture on the standard reply that rules of inference are not premises; instead, I want the students to feel the asymmetry between premises and rules through the mechanics of a game.

Design a runnable classroom activity that occupies roughly 40 minutes of gameplay followed by a 35-minute debrief. Specify the exact rules, the physical materials (cards, tokens, or assigned roles), and a payoff scheme that relies solely on symbolic stakes—such as a collective score that can be depleted and that resets unpredictably—so that no real money or grades are involved. The core structure should be as follows. One student plays Achilles and attempts to defend a simple syllogism. Another plays the Tortoise and may object only by demanding that any unstated inference rule be written out and placed into a designated premise pool. The remaining six students serve as custodians of two distinct containers: a premise box and a constitution box. A card placed in the premise box functions as an explicit premise open to further challenge; a card placed in the constitution box governs subsequent play but is thereafter immune to challenge, though placing it there costs the group a fixed number of points and requires unanimous silent assent from the custodians. Achilles wins if the conclusion is reached while the collective score remains above zero.

In the debrief, map each critical moment—such as the first move to the constitution box, the first unanimous silent vote, and the first realization that further challenge is blocked—onto the logical distinction between object-level premises and meta-level rules of inference, and onto the philosophical problem of whether adopting such a rule can itself be justified. State precisely what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run, for instance if students treat the constitution box as merely another premise source or if the Tortoise plays the role with excessive aggression, and explain how to correct these failures without dissolving the experiential point. Be explicit about what feature of Carroll's regress the activity can reproduce—the pragmatic pressure to halt justification by adopting a rule—and about what feature it cannot reproduce: the normative question of whether that halt is rationally warranted, which must be supplied in discussion rather than simulated.

The design should make participants discover the problem themselves before it is named, should avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, and should not depend on students recognizing specific texts or authors beyond the dialogue already mentioned.
