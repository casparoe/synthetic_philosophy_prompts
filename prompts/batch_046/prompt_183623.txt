I was sitting with two colleagues after a philosophy of art lecture when they became stuck in a disagreement about whether a particular hypothetical artwork is genuinely one object or ten separate ones. The case they were discussing is an imagined piece called the Distributed Inscription: ten identical bronze plaques, each bearing one-tenth of a continuous poem, installed in ten different cities. The artist’s original contract specifies that the plaques are never to be sold separately and that any replacement of a damaged plaque must match the original dimensions and text exactly, so that the work “remains intact” regardless of which physical tokens exist at any given time. One colleague, who tends toward mereological nihilism, argued that there are simply ten concrete metal objects scattered across space with no physical connection or causal interaction among them; he said that treating them as one thing is merely a convenient fiction, comparable to treating Orion as a single constellation, and that if someone moved one plaque to a private garage they would not have “broken off a part of the monument” but only relocated one of ten independent objects. The other colleague replied that composition cannot require spatial contiguity, since even a human hand is mostly empty space held together by continuous molecular bonds, and that the artist’s specification functions as a genuine principle of unity—much as a corporation is one legal entity despite dispersed offices—determining which replacements count as parts of the same work over time.

Their exchange continued for several rounds with genuinely decent points on both sides. He challenged her by proposing a counterexample: if he writes a contract declaring that his left shoe and the Eiffel Tower form a single artwork called “Shoeffel,” has he thereby created a real composite? She responded by distinguishing ad hoc declarations from historically anchored, functionally integrated specifications: the monument’s unity derives from a single creative act with a fixed aesthetic and semantic purpose, not from an arbitrary pairing, and any replacement is constrained by that original design in a way that “Shoeffel” is not. He countered that “historically anchored” is just a label for “the cases I intuitively prefer,” and that she has effectively conceded that composition depends on human intentions and social practices rather than on mind-independent relations among parts; if the artist had never existed, he asked, would there still be one object, or only ten plates? She admitted that without the specification there might indeed be only ten plates, but insisted that this does not render the composite fictional—rather, it shows that some objects are essentially constituted by normative or intentional structures, just as a chess game is one game even when played on different boards with different pieces. At this point she introduced a more specific middle position she called functional-mereological essentialism: an object exists when there is a non-arbitrary functional specification that determines part-replacement conditions, provided that specification is anchored in a singular historical origin and is not extendable at will.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right rather than declaring only an overall winner? In particular, assess whether the nihilist’s appeal to scattered objects like Orion is a fair analogy, whether the functional-mereological essentialism she sketches is genuinely different from an arbitrary convention or merely saves her intuition, and what both disputants are missing about whether special composition can be restricted by intentional or normative conditions without collapsing either into full-blown universalism or into a purely conventionalist view. If you think their disagreement ultimately turns on a deeper issue—such as whether any mind-independent, non-arbitrary criterion for restricted composition is available—say so explicitly, and identify what further case or principle would best settle it.
