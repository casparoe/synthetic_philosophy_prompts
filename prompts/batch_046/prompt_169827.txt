Since my grandfather's recently declassified service records confirm that he served in a reserve police unit in occupied Poland during 1942, I have been trying to understand whether to interpret his actions through Christopher Browning's situational framework—peer pressure, bureaucratic fragmentation, and incremental escalation—or through Daniel Goldhagen's emphasis on deep, culturally embedded eliminationist antisemitism as a necessary condition for participation. In working through this, I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious: it seems to prove that only Goldhagen's model can preserve moral responsibility, yet that conclusion feels like an overreach driven by hidden premises. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Browning's explanation of Reserve Police Battalion 101 holds that ordinary social-psychological dynamics—conformity, authority deference, career concerns, and the routinization of violence—are causally sufficient for participation in mass killing, without requiring any prior, distinctive ideological commitment of the kind Goldhagen describes.

(2) If these situational dynamics are causally sufficient in Battalion 101, then they are also sufficient in any modern institutional context that shares the same structural features: hierarchical command, division of labor, and incremental moral distancing.

(3) Where situational dynamics are causally sufficient, the agent's own beliefs and character traits do no independent explanatory work; they are replaced by variables that could apply to anyone placed in the same position.

(4) Moral responsibility attaches to an agent only for actions that flow genuinely from the agent's own beliefs and character traits, rather than being fully determined by replaceable situational variables.

(5) Therefore, accepting Browning's model for Battalion 101 entails that its members lack moral responsibility for their actions, and further entails that participants in structurally analogous institutional harms today are likewise not responsible.

(6) But to deny moral responsibility across all such institutional contexts is conceptually and practically untenable; it would dissolve the distinction between culpable participation and mere mechanical functioning.

(7) Hence, to preserve moral responsibility, we must reject the claim that situational dynamics are ever causally sufficient for participation in grave institutional wrongs.

(8) Consequently, Goldhagen's model—according to which a specific, agent-endorsed ideological commitment is necessary—is correct.

Please tell me which step or premise I should reject. I am particularly reluctant to give up (4), since it captures the core intuition that responsibility must be anchored in the agent rather than in the environment; if (4) is the flaw, I want to understand exactly what philosophical resources remain for distinguishing a culpable perpetrator from a replaceable cog. If the problem lies elsewhere—perhaps in the move from (1) to (2), or in an equivocation hidden in (3)—explain why that step looked plausible and what giving it up costs for my attempt to understand my grandfather's actions.
