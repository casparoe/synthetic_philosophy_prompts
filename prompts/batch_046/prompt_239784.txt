I cover philosophy of science and democratic theory for an ideas magazine, and I'm preparing a guided discussion for a reading group of editors and reporters—sharp, curious, but without formal philosophy training—after a recent conversation about whether climate deliberation actually creates the publics it claims to represent. I want to move past the standard summary of Dewey's Democracy and Education, which treats democratic procedure as a mechanism for aggregating pre-existing preferences, and instead work through his mature transactional framing: democratic inquiry is not applied to an already-formed public but is itself the experimental process through which both the shared problem and the collective agent capable of addressing it are constituted at once. Please plan the explanation for this audience: the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that transactionalism neither collapses into pure social constructionism nor into naive realism about ecological or social facts; the single concrete carrying case—perhaps a contested local watershed decision or a public-health response—that must include both a phase before inquiry in which no coherent public exists and a phase after adoption in which the solution itself redefines who counts as affected; the misconceptions they will predictably bring, such as that Dewey is merely advocating expanded voting mechanisms, that transactionalism denies objective environmental harm, or that this reproduces Habermasian discourse ethics, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; the check for understanding that demonstrates they grasp the democratic procedure as genuinely epistemic rather than merely procedural; and what must be openly left out—perhaps Dewey's formal metaphysical categories of existence and experience—with an explicit admission of the omission. Begin with the bottom line in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no repetition of the question.
