I am preparing the question sequence for an interview I will conduct for a philosophy-of-decision podcast with an unnamed defender—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—of what she terms the "epistemic anchoring" resolution to the absent-minded driver paradox. The topic has become personally urgent rather than merely academic: I design navigation protocols for autonomous vehicles, and two months ago I realized that a car whose odometry is corrupted and whose route-memory is non-persistent faces exactly the decision structure of the classic paradox—reaching successive nodes where the locally rational choice is to continue driving, with the globally catastrophic result that it never takes the correct exit. I want questions that do not ask her to rehearse the setup, but that press the internal mechanics of anchoring, its formal consequences, and its application to artificial agents.

Please draft eight interview questions arranged in a building sequence, distributing them so that each of the following four pressures receives at least two questions (each pair consisting of a primary question and a tightly keyed follow-up). First, include questions that target the dynamic-consistency failure directly: ask why it is rational for the driver to continue at each exit even though he knows in advance that continuing at both means never reaching his destination, and whether this reveals a genuine irrationality or merely a breakdown of contingent planning. The follow-up should be keyed to the standard reply that behavioral strategies—adopting a fixed policy in advance—dissolve the paradox, pressing whether such a policy is genuinely available to an agent who, by hypothesis, cannot recall whether he has already executed it.

Second, include questions that ask what is actually anchored in her view—whether an indexical self-location, a minimal functional trace, or a structural feature of the decision tree—and include a follow-up that anticipates her saying it is essentially indexical reasoning. In that follow-up, ask how her anchoring avoids collapsing into the Lewis-style de se framework she explicitly rejects as insufficient for agents with imperfect recall.

Third, include a question that applies the view to artificial navigation systems: if an autonomous agent lacks any form of anchoring, is its falling into the driver-paradox a design flaw or a rational feature of its architecture? Include a follow-up keyed to the likely answer that artificial systems lack phenomenal self-awareness, asking whether her anchoring requires phenomenology or only a formal structural equivalent that could be implemented.

Fourth, include a question about the formal decision-theoretic machinery—specifically whether the independence axiom must be abandoned for agents with imperfect recall, and whether anchoring restores it or replaces it with a weaker consistency condition across nodes. Throughout, avoid generic biographical or bibliographic questions; keep the focus tightly on the content of the anchoring resolution, its formal implications, and its limits when applied to non-human agents.
