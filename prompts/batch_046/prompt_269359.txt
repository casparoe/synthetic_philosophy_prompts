I've constructed an argument for a seminar on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and now it looks suspicious because it doesn't merely deny dilemmas—it seems to dissolve moral tragedy altogether. Here are the steps.

(1) A genuine moral dilemma is a situation in which an agent S has two all-things-considered obligations, O(A) and O(B), such that A and B are jointly impossible for S (not-possible that A and B both occur), and neither obligation is overridden, cancelled, or merely prima facie.

(2) If O(A) and O(B) are both binding on S, then practical reason requires S to satisfy both. Call this the Deliberative Consistency condition.

(3) From (2): if O(A) and O(B), then it is possible that S does A and B together.

(4) By the principle that ought implies can, applied specifically to moral obligations: if O(X) then it is possible for S to do X.

(5) From (1) through (4): O(A and B) and it is possible that S does A and B, but also it is impossible that S does A and B. Contradictory. So genuine moral dilemmas are impossible.

(6) If there are no genuine dilemmas, then in any hard case—say, being forced to save one child but not another—there is exactly one all-things-considered obligation; the competing consideration is fully defeated.

(7) Moral residue, the rational feeling of having done wrong despite acting correctly, requires that an unfulfilled, undefeated obligation remains after the choice.

(8) By (6), no undefeated obligation remains after correct action. So moral residue is always irrational self-deception.

(9) But moral residue is widely taken to be not just rational but virtuous in tragic choices. So (8) proves too much: it eliminates the distinction between tragic choice and ordinary choice, collapsing moral phenomenology into a single-track calculus.

Which step or inference should I reject? I want to keep the intuition that an agent in a Sophie-type situation can rationally feel moral residue after acting correctly, but I also want to preserve as much logical discipline in practical reasoning as possible. If I reject the Deliberative Consistency condition in (2) or (3), what does that cost for non-moral practical reasoning? If I weaken the ought-implies-can principle in (4), am I abandoning a core Kantian constraint, or is there a nuanced, context-sensitive version that survives? And if the argument is actually valid and I must accept (8), is that a bullet small enough to bite, or does it destroy too much of our moral psychology? Price the alternatives: what do deontic logicians pay if they deny closure, what do Kantians pay if they qualify ought-implies-can, and what do defenders of moral tragedy pay if they concede (8) but try to reconstruct residue without undefeated obligations?
