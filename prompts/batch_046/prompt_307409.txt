Please make the arguement that Émilie du Châtelet's view in her essay on happiness — that illusions are actually necessary and that seeing too clearly kills joy — shouldn't be read as simple old-fashioned self-deception. Instead, treat it as an early version of what we could call "structured epistemic partiality": the claim that happiness requires selectively inhabiting certain truths and deliberately bracketing others, and that this is rationally justified only when you do it yourself. She contrasts philosophers, who see everything and suffer for it, with lovers and poets who live inside smaller, brighter worlds. Now push this further and take a definite stance: argue that modern algorithmic curation hasn't destroyed our illusions so much as destroyed our *agency* to choose which ones to live inside, which makes her framework more relevent now than in salon culture. Don't weigh both sides evenly — commit to whether she was really describing a form of moral agency we've now lost, and defend that position directly.
