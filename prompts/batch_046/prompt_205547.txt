I was arguing with my mate about whether it's alright to make one person suffer if it helps loads of others, and I started thinking about that philosophy idea where people say we're all seperate, like islands, so you can't just trade one person's pain for someone else's happiness. I think there's something more going on though — not just that we're seperate, but that suffering doesn't work like money in a bank where you can cancel debts across different accounts. I started calling it "moral bookkeeping" in my head: each person's hurt has its own address, and you can't just subtract it from someone else's joy in one big ledger. Then I wondered if in some East Asian ways of thinking, where the self is more like a network than a single point, the bookkeeping idea breaks down for a different reason — not because people are less seperate, but because the accounts were never individual to begin with. So maybe the objection to utilitarianism isn't one universal thing; it's tied to how you picture a person in the first place.

My notes:
- Utilitarianism pools welfare like water: pour enough good in and the bad gets diluted.
- Rawls's seperateness objection says persons aren't containers for collective happiness.
- But the real problem might be accounting logic: my suffering for your benefit isn't a negative entry in your column.
- In relational views of self, the ledger was never individual, so aggregation doesn't violate boundaries — it just changes the unit.
- Therefore the anti-utilitarian intuition might depend on a discrete-person ontology rather than being a universal moral fact.
