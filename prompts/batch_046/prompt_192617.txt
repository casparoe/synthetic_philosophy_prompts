I recently encountered a summary of Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," published in the journal Mind. The author explains that Carroll employs characters borrowed from Zeno's motion paradox to show that deductive inference is trapped in an infinite regress: whenever one applies modus ponens to premises in order to derive a conclusion, one must invoke an additional logical rule that licenses that application, and that rule itself demands further justification, and so on without end. From this, the summary concludes that Carroll thereby proved formal logic to be circular and self-undermining, since any attempt to justify logical inference already presupposes the very principles being justified. The article further claims that this problem is structurally identical to Zeno's paradox, because both involve an infinite sequence that blocks reaching a conclusion, and that Carroll intended the dialogue as a broad critique of all mathematical proof. Finally, it asserts that the only resolution is to treat logical truths as intuitively known rather than derived from prior rules. I am personally inclined to think Carroll was mainly being playful about pedantry, but the argument sounds philosophically serious. Could you evaluate how fair this summary is? Please examine it claim by claim, indicating what a philosopher of logic or a Carroll scholar would accept, what they would qualify or correct, and what is simply wrong—particularly regarding whether the regress actually undermines deduction, whether it genuinely connects to Zeno's paradox, and whether the conclusion about intuitive knowledge actually follows from anything in the dialogue.
