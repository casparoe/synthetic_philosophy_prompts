I was arguing with a friend last week about national myths built around defeat — the Confederate Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo Polje, even how Dunkirk gets narrated — and I noticed a pattern I can't name. Every myth seems to explain the same defeat in two ways that directly contradict each other. First, it's inevitable: the enemy had overwhelming numbers, better weapons, or divine fate was against us, so losing was honorable and not anyone's fault. Second, it's avoidable: we only lost because of internal betrayal, a divided faction, or failure to stay pure, and if we had just held together we would've won. The same monument or speech will invoke both — "they faced impossible odds" on one breath, "the traitors inside destroyed us" on the next. You see it in Confederate memorials praising soldiers against "overwhelming resources" while also blaming "scalawags"; in Kosovo narratives where the battle is both cosmic martyrdom and ruined specifically by Vuk Branković; in Masada where Rome is an unstoppable machine but the Zealots are also faulted for forcing the suicide.

Is there an established concept for this exact double-account structure, or is it just generic mythmaking? If a term exists, how is it different from ordinary cognitive dissonance or from running seperate narratives for seperate audiences? And if there isn't one, what should we call it — and does it apply to "miracle of deliverance" stories like Dunkirk too, or is it specific to causes that actually went down in defeat? I don't need just a bare label; I want to know exactly where the edges are, what it isn't, and whether the contradiction is doing real work in the myth or just an accident that survives because nobody checks.
