In my philosophy of science elective, classmates often invoke Escher’s Drawing Hands to attack strict reductionism: they say that just as the left hand draws the right and the right draws the left with no “first” hand, mental states and neural states—or social norms and individual actions—form a tangled hierarchy with no inviolate bottom level. The analogy is being used to license the conclusion that reduction to physics or to individual behavior always fails, because the “lower” level is itself constituted by the “higher” one. I want a rigorous audit of how far this holds. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the left hand, the right hand, the pencil, the paper, and the absent artist? Then identify where the mapping breaks—especially whether physical causation permits the kind of simultaneous mutual creation that pencil lines only appear to have. Separate the disanalogies that actually damage the anti-reductionist conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Next, say whether the analogy genuinely supports the claim that there is no inviolate level, or whether it was smuggling that conclusion in through the image itself; if it collapses, propose a better analogy for mutual dependence that does not erase causal direction. Keep your response under 450 words, and begin with the table.
