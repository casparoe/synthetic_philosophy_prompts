Write a short story of approximately 800 to 1,000 words set in a near-future society that practices "probabilistic welfare accounting," an ethical framework in which moral weight is assigned to a population according to the product of its size and a credence value representing the likelihood that its members are sentient. Center the narrative on one protagonist—an auditor, a biologist, or a land-use planner—who must decide whether an infrastructure project should be halted because a localized insect population (for instance, a multigenerational beetle colony or a seasonal swarm) generates sufficient expected welfare to outweigh the project's benefits. The protagonist should confront the scale argument directly: the number of individuals is vast, yet the assigned per-capita probability of consciousness is extremely low. To keep the exploration fresh, introduce an invented, specific anomaly within this ecosystem—such as a synchronized chemical signaling network or a cross-generational memory trace—that undermines the assumption of uniform, independent credence without clearly establishing collective sentience. The story should dramatize the psychological and practical burden of applying aggregate statistical ethics to a concrete choice, allowing the tension between expected-value reasoning and the intuition that definite, individual suffering carries special weight to emerge through character, setting, and decision rather than through explicit philosophical exposition. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to named philosophical texts; rely on narrative detail and the internal logic of the framework.
