I am a novelist who has been asked to deliver the opening address at the dedication of the Meridian Archive, a new institution that will house the complete papers of a recently deceased poet and activist. The room will be divided: literary scholars who believe that calling art propaganda dissolves its aesthetic autonomy; family members and organizers who hold that art refusing an explicit political purpose is, in Du Bois's framing, a dereliction of its necessary function; and patrons who command neither vocabulary but will immediately sense which side has been patronized. I need an eight-minute ceremonial address that uses Du Bois's position—that all art is propaganda—to do real structural work, not decorative work. It must determine what the archive itself is permitted to separate, prioritize, or silence: specifically, argue that placing the poet's political broadsides and rally speeches in one gallery while consigning her most hermetic, apparently apolitical lyric poems to another would be an error not of curatorial taste but of philosophy, because it assumes that propaganda resides only in explicit authorial intention rather than in the unavoidable selection, framing, and exclusion that any act of representation performs. Build the speech around one concrete pairing visible in the opening exhibition: a rough, hand-corrected draft of a propaganda poster she distributed during a labor strike, displayed beside a water-damaged manuscript of a love lyric that contains no overt social reference whatsoever. The address should explain why both items must share the same room without reducing the poem to a concealed manifesto or excusing the poster from aesthetic judgment. It must be fully intelligible to the patrons and relatives present, yet precise enough that the scholars in attendance recognize you are maintaining a distinction between propaganda as deliberate rhetoric and propaganda as the structural bias inherent in making anything visible at all. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to secondary scholarship; the reasoning should stand on its own logic and on the tangible presence of the two objects. The tone should be formal and warm—appropriate for a novelist speaking at a dedication—unwilling to pretend that literature and politics can be neatly separated by architecture, but also unwilling to flatten either into the other.
