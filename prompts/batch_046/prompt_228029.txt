I am preparing a seminar contribution on institutional epistemology and have been discussing with colleagues whether futarchy genuinely solves the knowledge-problem in governance or merely relocates it. Please approach this as a professional philosopher of science with expertise in collective decision-making and prediction-market design. Before adjudicating, briefly outline the logical dependencies—distinguishing empirical premises from conceptual ones—and think carefully about where verification conditions enter.

Here is the exchange, reported neutrally by a bystander: (1) Alex argued that applying futarchy to university research funding works because it separates democratic value-voting—e.g., "maximize long-term societal benefit"—from belief-aggregation via conditional prediction markets, thereby removing politics from empirical judgment. (2) Blake responded that the separation is illusory: abstract values must be operationalized into measurable indicators, and the choice of indicators is itself a belief-laden framing decision, so beliefs are not expelled but relocated. (3) Alex conceded ambiguity is universal but insisted futarchy makes the belief-component explicit, competitive, and correctable; he added that strategic manipulation—betting to influence funding—is neutralized because payouts depend on actual outcomes, allowing arbitrageurs to correct distorted prices. (4) Blake countered that this defense presupposes an exogenous ground truth independent of the decision. In long-horizon domains—cultural legitimacy, institutional reputation—the "outcome" is partly constituted by the decision and its surrounding narrative, so without independent verification the market aggregates predictions about collective consensus rather than beliefs about an external world. (5) Alex replied that verification is never pure: citation impact and peer assessment provide observable proxies, and demanding absolute exogeneity is an impossible standard that no empirical science meets, hence an unfair reductio of futarchy. (6) Blake’s final move was that the issue is not generic skepticism but reflexivity specific to conditional markets: in standard markets the predicted event is causally independent of the bet, whereas in futarchy the policy is implemented because the market predicts success, altering the very environment in which the outcome unfolds; thus the market predicts its own footprint, structurally undermining the belief-value separation.

Please adjudicate move by move—rating each of the six steps from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—then say who is right about what, what both miss (especially regarding the conditions under which conditional-market outcomes can be verified independently of their own decisions), and whether any shared assumptions should be questioned. In your response, clearly separate what is settled in the literature on prediction-market governance from what is genuinely contested among informed specialists, and do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
