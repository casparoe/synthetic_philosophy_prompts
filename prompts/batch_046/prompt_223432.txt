I'm working through a seminar paper on atrocity naming and got stuck on an argument I sketched about whether calling something genocide is just symbolic theater. To keep it organized I invented a quick three-tier taxonomy — Type I is full Convention-level genocide with documented specific intent; Type II is group-targeted mass destruction where intent is structurally hidden behind proxies, bureaucracy, or ambiguous orders; Type III is slower demographic erosion through engineered deprivation or neglect, with no explicit kill command. Here's the chain I built, and it feels like it ends up proving activists are irrational, which can't be right.

(1) If an atrocity isn't Type I under teh legal convention that codified Lemkin's concept, then states have no treaty-triggered duty to prevent or punish it. (2) That convention requires proof of specific intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. (3) In nearly every modern conflict, direct evidence of that intent is buried, classified, or diffused across actors, so rigorously applied the standard puts almost all real-world cases in Type II or Type III. (4) So sticking to the legal standard means states almost never have an automatic obligation to act. (5) But moral intution — and a lot of state practice — says there's still a serious duty to intervene or sanction in clear Type II situations, even when the intent box can't be ticked. (6) That means the duty must be grounded independently of the legal label. (7) But if the duty exists regardless of the label, then fighting over whether to apply the word 'genocide' is just a vocabulary fight with no practical consequence for what states ought to do. (8) Activists treat the label as practically decisive, saying it unlocks tribunals, sanctions, and military options. (9) So either activists are wrong about the word's importance, or one of my steps is broken.

I want to keep (5) because it seems right that we should respond to Type II atrocities even without perfect proof. But I don't want to call activists irrational in (9). Where's the hidden slip — probably between (6) and (7), where I maybe confuse "independent moral ground" with "no causal role for naming"? If I reject (1) instead, what's the cost? And does my Type II/III distinction actually solve anything, or just relocate teh problem? I'd like a diagnosis of the weakest premise and what giving it up costs, plus whether the taxonomy is doing real work.
