I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about the procreation asymmetry, and they left me with a chain of reasoning that feels like it proves far too much, but I cannot pinpoint the leak. Here is their argument, laid out step by step.

(1) The procreation asymmetry is correct: there is a strong moral reason not to create a person whose life will be net-negative, but there is no corresponding moral reason to create a person whose life will be net-positive.

(2) Moral reasons are essentially comparative and world-ranking: for an action A, to say there is reason R against A is to say that the world in which A is not performed is, in the relevant moral respect, better than the world in which A is performed.

(3) Apply (2) to the bad-life case: the world in which the suffering person never exists is morally better than the world in which they exist and suffer.

(4) Apply (2) consistently to the good-life case: since there is no reason to create a happy person, the world in which they exist is not morally better than the world in which they never exist.

(5) Now, if a life is genuinely good, then that world contains more well-being, more value realized, and so on; intuitively it seems better in some respect. So (4) can only hold if the comparative relation in (2) is restricted: it tracks not total value but effects on affected parties.

(6) Restrict (2) accordingly: a world is morally better relative to action A only if some affected party is better off in that world than they would be under A.

(7) In the bad-life case, the affected party is the sufferer themselves: they are absent (and thus not suffering) in the world without them, so (3) holds.

(8) In the good-life case, there is no party who is worse off in the world without them—no one misses out, because non-existent people have no welfare—so (6) yields no reason to create them, consistent with the asymmetry.

(9) Now consider a decision scenario: an agent can choose between world W1, containing one suffering person and one flourishing person, and world W2, containing neither.

(10) By (3) and (7), W2 is better than W1 with respect to the suffering person, since that person is absent and unharmed in W2.

(11) By (4) and (8), W2 is not worse than W1 with respect to the flourishing person, since that person is absent in W2 but there is no affected party who is made worse off by that absence.

(12) Therefore, comparing W1 and W2, there is a decisive reason in favor of W2: we avoid a harm that generates a comparative reason, and we do not forfeit any good that generates a corresponding comparative reason.

(13) Extend the comparison: W3 contains only the flourishing person, and W4 contains neither. By (11), W3 and W4 are on a par—there is no comparative reason to prefer W3.

(14) So the asymmetry, combined with (2) restricted by (6), entails that we should be strictly indifferent between a world filled with flourishing people and an empty world, and that we should actively prefer empty worlds over worlds that contain both suffering and flourishing.

(15) This looks like it turns the asymmetry into an extinction-leaning principle: absence of suffering always dominates presence of happiness, not because happiness lacks value, but because it lacks reason-generating force.
