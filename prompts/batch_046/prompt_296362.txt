I've constructed an argument about Gettier-style cases that seems to collapse into skepticism about empirical knowledge, and I can't locate the weak joint.

(1) Gettier's original counterexamples establish that knowledge is not merely justified true belief: the justification must not depend essentially on any false proposition in the reasoning chain.
(2) Formalize this as the Non-Dependence Condition (NDC): S knows p via reasoning R only if every premise that is essential to R's production of p is true.
(3) Take any empirically justified belief p, supported by evidence E. There exists a nearby possible world—close in the relevant modal ordering—where E is false, yet p remains true purely by coincidence.
(4) In that nearby world, the same reasoning R produces p, but now R depends essentially on the false premise E.
(5) So in our actual world, S's justification for p is modally fragile: it stands one step away from being a Gettier-style justification.
(6) If NDC is interpreted robustly—requiring that knowledge be incompatible with any nearby false-premise dependency—then no empirically justified belief can satisfy it.
(7) But if NDC is interpreted narrowly—applying only to actual false premises, not to counterfactual ones—then it does not address Gettier's point, since his false premises were actual.
(8) Therefore, either empirical knowledge is impossible, or NDC is internally incoherent.

I'd prefer to preserve (1) and (6): the fragility intuition feels compelling, and the collapse of empirical knowledge feels like a reductio. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step seemed plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what giving up (6) versus giving up (2) costs for the broader project of analyzing knowledge.
