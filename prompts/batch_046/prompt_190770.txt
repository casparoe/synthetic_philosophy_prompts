I have been developing an argument for my graduate seminar on collective action and moral responsibility, and it now strikes me as proving far more than I intended—indeed, that every participant in a large-scale collective bears full, undiluted accountability for the entire macro-level harm. I want to know precisely where the chain breaks, and what repairing it costs for our understanding of individual contributions to climate damage, consumption-driven supply chains, and democratic voting.

Here is the reasoning, step by step. (1) Moral accountability requires counterfactual traceability: an agent is responsible for an outcome only if there exists a continuous dependency chain linking her specific act to that outcome, such that altering her act within structurally adjacent conditions alters the result. (2) In standard collective-harm cases—individual carbon footprints, meat consumption in global markets, or votes in large electorates—each contribution is causally redundant in the actual world: remove any single emission, purchase, or ballot, and the climate trajectory, production order, or electoral result remains identical. (3) So the familiar inefficacy objection holds: absent traceability, there is no individual accountability for these collective outcomes. (4) But traceability should be assessed not only in the actual world, but across the modal neighborhood defined by normal parameter variation—near-tie elections, supply-chain fluctuations, weather-sensitive emission paths—where background conditions shift within realistic bounds. (5) Within that neighborhood, nearly every individual act proves fragile: there are adjacent worlds in which that specific vote decides the election, that purchase triggers the reorder that expands factory production, or that emission pushes a climate subsystem past a local threshold. (6) Therefore, by (1), each agent is traceable to the macro-outcome and bears full, non-diluted responsibility for the entire collective harm—not a proportional share, but the whole. (7) This yields hyper-multiplication: in collectives of millions, responsibility for climate damage or factory-farming suffering is assigned in its entirety to each member, violating any plausible principle of proportionality, non-redundancy, or fair distribution. (8) Hence one of the premises between (3) and (6) must collapse.

My instinct is to preserve (1)—traceability seems indispensable if we are to avoid holding people responsible for outcomes they genuinely could not affect—while resisting the inference in (6) that modal fragility in adjacent worlds transfers undiluted actual-world accountability. Yet I am uncertain whether that resistance requires abandoning the modal extension in (4), or whether it demands a different scaling theory that relates traceability to collective size without dissolving individual contribution entirely. Which step do you think fails, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it commit us to regarding the ethics of voting, dietary choice, and climate action?
