ok so im in this anthro class and we got into a weird debate about whether the neolithic revolution was actually a bad deal for most people. like, the evidence is pretty clear that early agriculturalists were shorter, sicker, and worked more hours than hunter-gatherers—there's skeletal data showing stress markers and dental decay spiking right after domestication—yet every development institution still treats farming as the baseline of human progress. the world bank and similar bodies measure societal advancement by agricultural surplus, sedentism, and division of labor, which frames foraging societies as underdeveloped rather than differently organized. there's also that sahlins idea about foragers being the original affluent society, where people satisfied needs directly with minimal labor instead of storing surplus.

my question is about the institutional practice: why do development metrics and education curricula treat agriculture as an unambiguous upgrade, even while the same societies run wellness programs complaining about work-life balance and alienated labor? what philosophical commitments about value, progress, and human flourishing does that framing pre-suppose? does it have to assume that material surplus and population density are intrinsically good, or could it be defended on purely practical grounds? and is the practice actually consistent—celebrating the division of labor that made surplus possible, while also trying to cure the burnout that division of labor created? i want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of progress here, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what just happens to fit, and say what a development framework that actually took hunter-gatherer affluence seriously would have to change about how it measurments wellbeing.
