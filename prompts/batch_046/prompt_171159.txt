Write a branching taxonomy — built as a decision tree with four binary nodes — that maps the logical space of whether a single first-person "I" can be distributed across two or more brains, using Douglas Hofstadter's idea of strange loops and the persistence of self in others as the conceptual spine but pushing into combinations that standard personal-identity catalogs rarely separate. Design the four branch questions so a respondent can answer each without knowing philosophical labels: (1) Does sharing one self require real-time bidirectional causal coupling between brains, or is asynchronous symbolic and narrative overlap sufficient? (2) Must the original brain remain intact as an "anchor substrate," or can the self survive the total replacement of its original neural hardware? (3) Is first-person authority — the sense of being the unique decider — necessarily singular, shareable in parallel, or transferable to a successor system? (4) If the distributed system later fissions, does each part inherit the full original self, share a divided self, or leave one branch with no inherited first-person claim?

For each of the resulting leaves, provide a name or descriptive label (e.g., Hofstadterian coupled loop, narrative-cluster extension, substrate-locked singularity, unnamed hybrid), state in one sentence its most awkward commitment, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely distinguish. Then include a diagnosis of the unoccupied logical spaces: for each combination of answers that yields no defended position, say whether the gap is likely incoherent, conceptually unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Structure the response exactly as follows: "Why these four nodes" (~120 words, justifying the spine and its dependence order); "The tree" (~350 words, presenting the four binary questions with brief rationales and describing all eight leaves); "Leaves that collapse" (~100 words, naming inseparable pairs and why); "Empty spaces" (~150 words, diagnosing the gaps); "Quiz mapping" (~120 words, giving a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves without asking respondents to name theories). Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the taxonomy should be usable by someone with no web access.
