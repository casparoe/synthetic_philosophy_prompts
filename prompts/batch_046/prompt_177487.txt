Design a decision-tree taxonomy—intended for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar handout—that classifies responses to Jackson’s knowledge argument about Mary, but build it from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of philosopher-names. The root commitment is that Mary possesses complete physical, functional, and relational knowledge of color vision before leaving her black-and-white room. From there, construct four successive binary branch nodes, each phrased as a claim about Mary’s case that a student could accept or reject without knowing which interpretation it selects.

First branch: Does Mary’s first experience of red yield genuinely new propositional knowledge—something she could now assert in a true statement she could not have formulated before?

Second branch (conditional): If the answer to the first is yes, is the new fact ontologically non-physical? If the answer is no, is the change instead best characterized as (i) acquiring a practical ability, (ii) acquiring a phenomenal concept with a non-descriptive mode of presentation, or (iii) shifting the format of existing knowledge from theoretical-descriptive to demonstrative or indexical?

Third branch: Does whatever gain occurs—whether propositional, ability-based, conceptual, or format-based—demonstrate that physical descriptions omit something essential about consciousness itself, or does it show only that they omit a particular mode of epistemic access available only from the first-person perspective?

Fourth branch: Does the argument therefore establish ontological dualism, establish only an epistemic or explanatory gap compatible with physicalism, or fail altogether because no substantive epistemic change takes place?

The tree must terminate in exactly these five leaves: Jacksonian property dualism; the ability hypothesis in the Lewis-Nemirow tradition; the phenomenal-concept strategy; a physicalist representationalist account that treats the gain as a new mode of presentation for an old physical content; and a deflationary or eliminativist view that denies Mary learns anything substantive. For any combination of answers that does not map onto one of these five, label the leaf unnamed and diagnose in one sentence whether the position is likely incoherent, unmotivated, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that your four questions cannot cleanly separate—paying special attention to whether the phenomenal-concept strategy and the representationalist mode-of-presentation account collapse onto the same path. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should stand on its logical structure alone.
