I am a novelist revising a literary novel in which a protagonist’s central crisis turns on whether she can genuinely deceive herself about her complicity in a family betrayal, and I need a reusable decision procedure—something like a flowchart in words—that I can run during drafting and revision without clinical training. The procedure should translate the philosophical debate over whether self-deception is even possible into practical constraints on character construction, assuming I have about fifteen minutes per beat and that my editor, not a philosopher, will have to sign off on plausibility.

To prevent this from collapsing into either pop psychology or untethered metaphysics, ground it in the following framing: treat the character’s mental life as divided into an evidential register (what she is positioned to know given her sensory access, memories, and inferential habits) and a reflective register (what she can avow in first-person narration or dialogue without immediate contradiction). The procedure should proceed in ordered steps: first, determine whether the proposed self-deceptive state requires the character to hold contradictory beliefs within both registers simultaneously; second, ask whether the state can be reconstructed as selective attention, motivated reasoning, or evidential avoidance operating within a single register; third, examine whether the narrative focalization grants the reader access that undermines the character’s claimed ignorance; and fourth, assess whether sustaining the state requires attributing to the character a partitioned agency that the fiction’s own rules of interiority can support. At each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a firm negative—this character cannot be self-deceiving in this proposed way without breaking the narrative’s epistemic contract with the reader—and where it must hand the decision back to authorial judgment because the question turns on aesthetic, ethical, or dramatic values rather than on coherence alone.

Apply the procedure to three proposed beats. First, the protagonist dismissing her sister’s explicit warning about financial fraud as “just anxiety,” despite having personally handled the forged documents. Second, a secondary character in close third-person who insists he loves his estranged child while systematically avoiding every concrete opportunity to see the child, where the narrative tracks his movements but not his avowals. Third, apply it to a scene I am attached to but that the procedure should reject: a moment of dramatic irony in which the reader knows the protagonist’s marriage has collapsed while she expresses serene contentment, intended to function as self-deception rather than conscious denial or mere ignorance. The procedure should show that, under the focal conditions I have established, the intermediate state is structurally unstable and must be rewritten as one pole or the other—an answer I will not like, but need to see enforced.

Finally, be explicit about the procedure’s limits: which narrative effects it will systematically miss, where it is prone to false positives by overreading coherence failures as self-deception, and which choices—particularly those involving unreliable narration, ethical sympathy, and the distribution of irony—must remain unproceduralized acts of writerly judgment. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts; I need this to stand on its conceptual architecture alone.
