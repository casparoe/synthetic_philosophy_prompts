I have been working through a reconstructed version of the argument Aquinas presents in the first of his Five Ways, as the reasoning is usually transmitted within the 13th-century Aristotelian tradition: in the world we observe, things are moved from potentiality to actuality, and whatever is in this condition of being moved is moved by something else that is already in act. If the series of movers and things moved extends infinitely, with no first source of actualization, then there appears to be no ultimate ground for the motion we in fact observe; yet motion is observed, so the regress must terminate in a first unmoved mover, itself not moved by any other.

In trying to understand this, I keep running into a distinction that modern commentators draw between an accidentally ordered causal series—where prior members need not continue to exist or act once later ones are produced, as with a father begetting a son—and an essentially ordered series, in which every member depends here and now on a prior member that is actually exercising causality, as with a hand moving a stick that moves a stone. At the same time, I have been experimenting with my own conceptual distinction between causal transmission, in which the cause must continue acting for the effect to persist, and causal initiation, in which the cause need only bring about the effect at a first moment and may then cease. Meanwhile, the kalam version of the cosmological argument seems to treat the problem as one of temporal succession—an infinite past of initiating events—rather than as a question of simultaneous hierarchical dependence. I find myself unable to tell whether Aquinas’s move from “everything that is moved is moved by another” to “there cannot be an infinite regress of moved movers” relies specifically on essential ordering with transmission, or whether it could be satisfied by initiation alone.

Could you help me parse the logical structure of the passage carefully? Specifically, I want to understand exactly where the argument requires essential ordering rather than accidental ordering, and whether the kalam strategy’s focus on temporal initiation subtly substitutes initiation for transmission in a way that changes what actually needs explanation.
