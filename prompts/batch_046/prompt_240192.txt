I've recently found myself in discussions about whether mathematical cognition can be fully modeled by algorithmic proof-search procedures, and on two occasions an interlocutor ended the exchange with the slogan that Gödel's incompleteness theorem proves the human mind is not a computer. Rather than treating that as a conversation-stopper, I want to understand what precise work the sentence could actually be doing. Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the specific claims this slogan might express? I am particularly interested in separating: (1) the weak claim that human mathematicians can recognize the consistency of particular formal systems that those systems cannot prove about themselves; (2) the intermediate claim that no single consistent formal system can capture all arithmetical truths that a human mind can access; (3) the stronger Lucas-Penrose claim that because a human mind can see its own consistency, it cannot be identical to any consistent formal system; and (4) the metaphysical claim that human reasoning involves non-computational or non-algorithmic causation. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false; specify whether it actually supports the anti-mechanist conclusion; and say which version is most likely being invoked when someone uses the line to dismiss an argument about mechanizability. In particular, address whether reading (3) illicitly assumes that a mind must be able to construct its own Gödel sentence, whether consistency is properly required of a mechanized cognitive system, and whether replacing 'the human mind' with 'a particular mathematician at a particular time' undermines the argument. I need the disambiguation rather than a single interpretation, because my purpose is to determine whether any of these readings genuinely block the view that cognition is implementable by formal procedures.
