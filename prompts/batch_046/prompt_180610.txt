A fellow student, who has read the relevant chapters carefully but tends to read modern ethical concerns back into premodern texts, summarized Maimonides' position on animal sacrifice as follows. She claims that Maimonides regards the killing of animals for ritual purposes as intrinsically wrong, and that God permitted sacrifices only as a temporary pedagogical concession because the Israelites, fresh from Egyptian culture, could not yet conceive of worship without bloodshed. According to her summary, once the people had been habituated to direct their devotion toward prayer and intellectual contemplation, the sacrificial laws became functionally obsolete; she adds that Maimonides therefore anticipates a future, messianic state in which only prayer and study remain, with all Temple rituals—including animal offerings—abolished entirely. She further argues that this is why Maimonides insists on a single, centralized sanctuary: not merely to suppress competing cults, but specifically to quarantine and eventually eradicate a violent practice that the Torah itself regards as morally inferior.

I am especially interested in whether the "quarantine" interpretation of the centralized Temple misrepresents his political-theological rationale, and whether the summary conflates his view of prayer's superiority with a claim about the moral status of animals. Please treat this as an accuracy audit rather than a simple yes-or-no judgment.
