I am drafting the final essay question for an upper-level philosophy seminar on vagueness and the sorites paradox, and I need a prompt that can be answered in roughly ninety minutes and about eight hundred words. The question must not be satisfiable by summarizing the standard responses—epistemicism, supervaluationism, and degree-theoretic accounts—but should require students to apply those frameworks to a normative concept that is both vague and action-guiding: “undue influence” in informed medical consent. Specifically, ask them to construct a sorites-style argument for the absence of a sharp boundary between permissible persuasion and coercive interference in clinical settings, then to evaluate whether treating this vagueness as a pragmatic feature calibrated to decision-making—what the exam should call the action-guiding vagueness thesis—can dissolve the paradox without undermining the clinician’s duty to obtain valid consent and act. Please write the exam question with enough framing that a student who has completed the seminar can begin immediately; include a model answer of approximately seven hundred words; and provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, totaling twenty marks, specifying the weight of each point and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs candidates marks there. The difficulty should lie in forcing students to distinguish semantic vagueness from pragmatic vagueness and to show precisely where a given resolution either preserves or collapses normative authority in practice.
