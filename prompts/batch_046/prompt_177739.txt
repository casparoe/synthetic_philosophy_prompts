I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that spends several weeks on perceptual experience, and I want a single essay question that prevents students from simply reciting the familiar taxonomy of direct realism, representationalism, and sense-datum theory. To that end, please construct a question centered on disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, but embed the standard dialectic inside a precisely specified, somewhat unfamiliar scenario: a subject wears an augmented-reality headset that projects a digital image of a red apple onto a physically present green apple, with calibration so precise that the subject's visual system integrates the digital overlay with the physical object, yielding an experience phenomenally indistinguishable from seeing a genuinely red apple in ordinary light, even though removing the headset instantly reveals the green apple and the digital source can be deactivated without altering the subject's immediate phenomenal report. The exam prompt should ask students to determine whether this experience counts as an illusion, a hallucination, a veridical perception of a composite object, or something else altogether, and then require them to explain what a disjunctivist must say about its phenomenal character relative to both a classic illusion such as a stick partly submerged in water and a pure hallucination in which no corresponding external object exists at all.

The model answer should be approximately eight hundred words of continuous prose and must include the following in a single flowing text: a precise reconstruction of the argument from illusion that motivates the common-kind assumption shared by sense-datum and representationalist theories; the disjunctivist rejection of that assumption together with the claim that good and bad cases differ in phenomenal character; an analysis of the augmented-reality case that addresses whether the continued presence of the physical green apple makes the experience a good case for disjunctivism or whether the digital mediation introduces a bad-case structure despite the external object; an evaluation of whether disjunctivism can preserve phenomenal continuity across the transition of putting the headset on or taking it off without resorting to ad hoc distinctions; and a reasoned conclusion about whether this particular case strengthens or weakens the original argument from illusion. Alongside the question, please supply a marking scheme also written in continuous prose that distinguishes an excellent answer, which must identify the specific tension between causal mediation and phenomenal identity and offer a principled disjunctivist reply, from a competent answer that applies the standard good-case and bad-case distinction without confronting the mediation problem, and from a weak answer that merely summarizes the argument from illusion and lists named theories. The entire response must be formatted as a formal letter with no headings, bullet points, bold text, numbered lists, or tables, and it should avoid any request for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific publications; paraphrase philosophical positions without placing them in quotation marks attributed to real texts.
