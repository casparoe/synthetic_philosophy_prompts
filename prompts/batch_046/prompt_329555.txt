Consider the argument I have been testing in a graduate seminar on democratic theory, which now seems to me suspicious because its conclusion threatens to empty the concept of democratic backsliding of all content.

(1) Define democratic backsliding not as the loss of competitive elections alone, but as any net reduction in institutional friction: the density of formal veto points, the procedural autonomy of oversight bodies, and the transaction costs facing executive action.

(2) Every functioning democracy periodically lowers such friction through administrative reform, judicial consolidation, or constitutional amendment; these are ordinary governance, not pathology.

(3) At the same time, no democracy eliminates all friction, since complete removal is both practically impossible and conceptually indistinguishable from unfettered autocracy.

(4) Therefore, if backsliding is measured as net reduction in friction, then every democracy is perpetually backsliding, yet none can ever fully backslide.

(5) Consequently, the concept either applies universally—failing to distinguish Hungary, Poland, or Turkey from the United States—or applies to no actual case, rendering claims of backsliding false.

(6) Hence democratic backsliding is not a substantive analytical category but a partisan label selectively applied to institutional changes one happens to oppose.

Where does this reasoning fail, if anywhere? I am inclined to preserve (1) as a useful precisification, but I want to know whether doing so forces me to reject (5) or whether (2) and (3) conceal an equivocation between descriptive institutional change and normative democratic erosion. Identify the weakest step, explain why it appeared plausible, and state what must be given up to avoid the absurd conclusion.
