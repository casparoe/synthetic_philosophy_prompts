I have been working through a somewhat idiosyncratic defense of pure time preference in my graduate seminar on intertemporal ethics, and I want to map its actual vulnerabilities rather than rehearse the standard objections. The view I am entertaining holds that rational agents may discount the welfare of future persons—including their own future selves—purely in virtue of temporal distance, without appealing to risk, uncertainty, or metaphysical discontinuity. Specifically, I am drawn to a formulation that treats temporal location analogously to spatial location for the purposes of partial practical concern: just as I am not required to weight a stranger's suffering across the world equally with that of someone in my immediate vicinity, I am not required to weight a harm occurring forty years hence equally with an identical harm occurring now, even if both affect the same psychologically continuous person. The position includes what I call a continuity constraint—discounting must be continuous rather than discrete, decaying exponentially with temporal distance rather than dropping sharply at a boundary—so that tomorrow's harm receives nearly full weight, but distant futures receive proportionally less. This is defended not as a psychological bias to be corrected, but as a structural feature of indexical practical reasoning: present-tense agency carries an irreducible normative weight that projected futures lack.

What I want is an honest assessment of what would have to be true for this position to be wrong, sorted by how decisive the damage would be. Which conceptual distinctions—say, between internal and external relations, or between partiality and discrimination—would cause the spatial analogy to collapse? Are there formal results in decision theory or behavioral economics that show agents holding this view are necessarily dynamically inconsistent, or that they must smuggle in non-pure reasons to avoid pathological intertemporal choices? I also want to know which defeaters a committed defender of this view would concede as genuinely damaging—perhaps Parfit-style arguments from psychological continuity, or cases where exponential decay produces intuitively unacceptable trade-offs between present and near-future welfare—versus which they would dismiss as begging the question by assuming that temporal neutrality is the default rational posture. Does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability if defenders respond to every counterexample by adjusting the decay parameter or invoking the continuity constraint? And if so, is that insulation a theoretical vice for a normative claim about rational agency, or is it appropriate for a framework that is meant to describe rational structure rather than predict empirical choice? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and identify what predictions this specific formulation makes—particularly about savings behavior, longtermist obligations, and intrapersonal bargaining—that rival views such as zero-discount utilitarianism or the standard "no pure preference" account do not share.
