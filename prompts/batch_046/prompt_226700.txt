The long-running dispute over which variety of free will is worth wanting—hierarchical self-governance, reasons-responsive mesh, or reactive-attitude grounding—has circulated since Frankfurt's hierarchical account and Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes without producing consensus. Is the stall substantive, with opponents genuinely disagreeing about the psychological and normative conditions of agency, or is it verbal, with 'free will worth wanting' functioning as an umbrella for distinct targets such as moral-accountability-worthiness versus autonomy-worthiness? Might the sides also be answering different questions under one label: one asking what architecture makes resentment and gratitude fitting, another asking what distinguishes a person from an instrument, without recognizing that these questions have different success conditions? Diagnose the disagreement using what hierarchical, mesh, and reactive-attitude theorists actually say about failure conditions, about whether higher-order desires must be stable or merely present, and about whether evaluative integration is a psychological or a social achievement. Consider in particular whether an unacknowledged empirical premise—perhaps about the neural plasticity of second-order volitions, or about the cross-cultural stability of reactive practices—is bearing weight that philosophical argument alone cannot carry. Then specify what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal proof that mesh conditions and hierarchical conditions are jointly unsatisfiable, an empirical result about the instability of reflective endorsement, a conceptual distinction between accountability and self-direction that dissolves the conflict, or an argument that the original framing is ill-posed because 'worth wanting' conceals an unargued preference for one form of human life.
