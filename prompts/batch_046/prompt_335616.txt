I'm working through Thomas Reid's An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense (1764), specifically the sections in which he attacks the theory of ideas by examining perception at a distance and the phenomenon of visual constancy. Reid asks the reader to consider the case in which a person genuinely sees an external object—say, a tree at twenty paces—and compare it with a case in which the same visible appearance occurs in a dream or in an illusion caused by disorder in the sensory organs. He argues that if perception consists in being conscious of an internal image or idea that stands between the mind and the object, then the mental state in both cases must be of the same kind, since both involve the presence of the same kind of internal representation with the same visible figure and color. From this he concludes that, on the representationalist view, there is no feature of the mental state itself by which we could distinguish waking perception from dreaming or illusion; yet we do distinguish them reliably, which suggests that in genuine perception we are not conscious of any internal image at all, but are immediately aware of the external object through the natural operation of vision. I am trying to understand how this line of reasoning relates to contemporary disjunctivism—the position, developed by philosophers such as J. M. Hinton and John McDowell, that a veridical perception and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are not two tokens of a shared mental kind with a common phenomenal core, but are fundamentally different kinds of mental event. Does Reid's argument anticipate disjunctivism, or does his reliance on natural signs and an original constitution of human nature commit him to a view on which perception still involves a representational medium that disjunctivists reject? More specifically, if Reid denies that there is any mental image common to both veridical seeing and hallucination, is he already making the disjunctive claim, or is he merely denying representational mediation while leaving open the possibility that the two states share an intrinsic phenomenal character? I want help clarifying whether Reid's critique of the argument from illusion can be reconstructed in a way that is genuinely independent of representationalist commitments, and where exactly the reconstruction fails if we try to align it with modern direct-realist theories of perceptual content.
