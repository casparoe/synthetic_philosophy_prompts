I am preparing a poetry-and-philosophy seminar exercise on the conceptual architecture of freedom, and I want a verse piece that makes the structural conflict between negative liberty, positive liberty, and republican liberty do formal work rather than merely illustrating them. Compose a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—spoken in the voice of a citizen who lives under a ruler she regards as genuinely benevolent and who never actually obstructs her actions, yet whose power to interfere remains entirely unchecked.

The six rotating end-words, following the classical sestina sequence, must be: interference, mastery, arbitrary, capacity, domination, and will. Through the rotation, the diction and imagery should shift so that negative liberty governs the stanzas that treat the absence of actual obstacles, positive liberty governs those that treat self-mastery and the realization of rational capacity, and republican liberty governs those that treat the status of being subject to another's arbitrary will—so that the formal displacement of the end-words enacts the philosophical displacement of the three frameworks. The envoi should gather all six words in an order that forces the speaker to recognize that describing her condition requires vocabulary from all three theories simultaneously, ending with a grammatically unfinished final line that leaves the classification of her unfreedom unresolved. Do not name Berlin, Pettit, or any other philosopher within the poem; the identification should be recoverable by a reader familiar with the literature solely from the conceptual mapping.
