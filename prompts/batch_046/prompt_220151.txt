In a seminar I recently audited on perpetrator studies, two graduate students—Marcus and Elena—fell into a sustained disagreement about the classic debate between Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen’s broader thesis regarding German society during the Holocaust. Marcus opened by insisting that Browning demonstrates the shooters were essentially unremarkable middle-aged family men lacking exceptional ideological commitment, and that peer pressure, incremental escalation, careerist deference, and the diffusion of responsibility explain participation far better than character; he added that if the victims had belonged to a different group, the same situational architecture would likely have produced comparable compliance, which is precisely why situational explanations should alarm us more than cultural ones. Elena responded that Goldhagen correctly identifies what this framing omits: the perpetrators operated within a culture that had long cultivated an eliminationist orientation toward Jews, meaning Jewish victims were not arbitrarily available targets but specifically designated ones, and she pointed out that within Battalion 101 those who refused faced negligible punishment, yet the overwhelming majority still fired, which suggests an active motivation that pure situational pressure cannot explain. Marcus then distinguished between a culture making violence thinkable and a culture making it desired, arguing that Goldhagen never adequately demonstrates that background antisemitism translated into the specific desire to kill rather than mere passive acquiescence, and he noted that refusal rates varied noticeably across comparable police units, a pattern that uniform ideological commitment struggles to explain. Elena proposed an interactionist synthesis: the situation worked precisely because the culture had already defined Jews as legitimate targets, so Browning’s mechanisms are not rivals to Goldhagen’s culture thesis but incomplete layers of it, and treating them as competitors is a category error. Marcus objected that this synthesis collapses Goldhagen’s distinctive explanatory claim into a generic framework—if culture only functions as background conditions enabling situational mechanisms, then we have not shown that these men were genuinely eager killers in any meaningful sense that distinguishes them from bureaucratic perpetrators in other historical contexts, and Goldhagen’s thesis loses its predictive and explanatory bite.

Then render an overall verdict on who is more right, and identify what both miss—whether the debate presupposes a false trichotomy among character, situation, and culture, whether empirical patterns in refusal or unit composition actually undermine both accounts, or whether either side risks unfalsifiability. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
