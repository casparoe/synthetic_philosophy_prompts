The debate over Molinism and middle knowledge—whether God possesses pre-volitional knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—has run for decades with the same choreography: the grounding objection, the charge that middle knowledge compromises divine sovereignty or simplicity, and the reply that without it providence collapses into determinism or open-theist contingency. In the literature and in philosophical theology seminars the exchange feels frozen; each side masters the standard rebuttals, yet no one shifts. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about libertarian freedom but about a deeper, rarely made explicit disagreement over the metaphysical status of counterfactuals of freedom—specifically, whether they require truth-makers prior to the divine decree, and whether such truth-makers can stand “outside” God without violating aseity, or whether they must instead be constituted by some feature of the divine intellect that collapses the distinction between natural and middle knowledge. If this is right, then the dispute is partly a hidden modal-metaphysical conflict about what kind of fact a counterfactual is, and partly a clash over non-negotiable explanatory priorities: sovereignty, simplicity, and the direction of ontological dependence. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the real blockage elsewhere—in the formal logic of counterfactuals indexed to libertarian choice, in an ambiguity about what “possible world” means relative to a non-actual divine will, or in the fact that Molinists and anti-Molinists are answering different questions under the single name “providence”? I want a structural diagnosis rather than a survey of positions: do the sides differ about hidden empirical or theological premises, about standards for what counts as an adequate explanation of divine action, about which cases should count as data, or about bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is the concept of middle knowledge itself ill-posed, or is the underlying modal framework simply under-described? And given your diagnosis, what would actually advance the debate—a precise distinction in modal logic or the metaphysics of grounding, a reconception of divine simplicity that accommodates pre-volitional knowledge, a clarification of “outside God” in a theistic ontology, or is the disagreement in principle unresolvable because it bottoms out in incompatible intuitions about explanation and ontological dependence?
