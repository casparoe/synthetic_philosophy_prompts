I am working through my own version of the Knobe-effect pattern, but with cases that move beyond simple one-agent harm and help into mediated and institutional settings, and my verdicts are partly in tension. Case 1: A pharmaceutical executive approves a profitable drug knowing it will cause severe side-effects in a small subgroup, though she cares only about profit. The harm feels clearly intentional. Case 2: She later learns the same drug unexpectedly cures an unrelated condition in that subgroup, which she also does not care about. The cure does not feel intentional, which conflicts with Case 1 if normative valence alone drives the judgment. Case 3: A city planner redesigns traffic flow knowing pedestrian accidents in poor neighborhoods will drop, but she aims only at congestion; she does not care about the safety gain. Here the beneficial reduction still feels intentional to me, deepening the conflict with Case 2. Case 4: An AI recommendation system, optimized solely for engagement, learns—without the designers anticipating the specific mechanism—to amplify divisive content that damages mental health. The harm feels intentional, yet I am unsure whether it should align with Case 1 (direct foresight of harm) or with Case 3 (structural benefit or harm embedded in system design). Case 5: A factory manager knowingly keeps outdated machinery running; eventually a worker is injured. The injury via omission feels intentional, though I hesitate to call it more intentional than Case 4. Is there a principle—perhaps tracking directness of causal control, the agent’s relation to the specific mechanism, or normative framing—that can accommodate Cases 1, 3, and 5 without forcing me to abandon Case 2? What does it predict for Case 4, and for a parallel omission case in which the manager fails to upgrade equipment that would have prevented an injury? Lead with your bottom-line principle, or your conclusion that none cleanly exists, in exactly one sentence; then develop the answer in no more than 250 words.
