I have come to accept a modified Dennettian view of intentionality—call it the gradient derivation thesis—according to which original intentionality is not an all-or-nothing biological property, as Searle maintains, but rather a scalar feature that emerges in proportion to the density and recursion of a system's interpretive practices. On this view, Dennett's two-bitser chess computer possesses a minimal, near-vanishing degree of original intentionality not because it is made of silicon rather than neurons, but because its interpretive loop is too shallow and too sparsely embedded in broader practices of correction and attribution. By contrast, a biological brain—or a sufficiently complex artificial system—would cross a threshold into robust original intentionality not by acquiring some special causal power, but by achieving a critical mass of self-referential interpretive scaffolding. What does the gradient derivation thesis predict that pure Searlean originalism and pure Dennettian instrumentalism do not, and where is it most exposed? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, say which objections its defenders would themselves concede as damaging, and whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a position of this kind.
