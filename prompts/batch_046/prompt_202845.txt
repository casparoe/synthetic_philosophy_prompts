I've been drafting an essay that extends Grice's framework for conversational implicature into a pattern I keep noticing in technical, forecasting, and rationalist-style discussions, and I want conceptual pressure-testing rather than a literature survey.

The core observation is this. In ordinary cooperative conversation, speaker meaning is reconstructed by assuming interlocutors share a background model against which they observe the maxims—Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. But in domains with complex causal structures, two parties can share cooperative intent and agree on literal content while operating with structurally different mental models of intermediate mechanisms. Speaker A makes an utterance that is truthful, relevant, and economical within A's model. Because B's model assigns different weights to hidden variables, B derives an unstated implication that A never intended and would reject if made explicit. Crucially, A cannot easily cancel it: explaining the full model divergence would violate Manner and Quantity by forcing an unreasonably long digression, so the unintended meaning persists without either party being dishonest or uncooperative. I call this model-mismatch implicature.

I contrast it with standard Gricean cases in two ways. First, in classic implicature the speaker intends the listener to recover the unstated content; here the speaker actively does not intend it. Second, the usual remedy is explicit clarification or denial, but in these cases explicit denial often sounds like backtracking or obfuscation, because the listener's derivation is locally rational within their own framework. So the failure mode is not that the listener missed an intended implicature, but that the listener generated one that the speaker's framework cannot accommodate without breaking conversational economy.

The essay then asks whether this belongs under the maxim of Relation—since relevance is model-dependent—or whether it is better treated as a separate pragmatic phenomenon, closer in spirit to theory-laden observation but applied to conversation rather than perception. I also examine whether rationalist norms of steelmanning and charitable interpretation exacerbate the problem: if B is trained to maximize coherence with A's utterances, B may construct a more elaborate unintended implicature rather than recognizing the underlying model divergence.

My requests for comments are specific:

1. Is model-mismatch implicature genuinely distinct from Grice's existing categories, or does it collapse into a failure of the shared-background assumption that makes implicature possible in the first place?

2. Does the interaction with charitable interpretation and steelmanning create a distinct pragmatic failure mode, or am I merely redescribing confirmation bias in conversational form?

3. What are clear boundary cases—perhaps in scientific disagreement, legal interpretation, or policy debate—where the phenomenon applies, and what are nearby cases that look similar but are actually ordinary ambiguity or simple miscommunication?

I am not looking for citations, bibliographies, or a reading list; I want conceptual precision about where the framing is loose, where it overreaches, and whether the central distinction is worth preserving. If the idea is redundant with something already well-established, I would prefer to know exactly which existing concept absorbs it and why.
