I've come around to thinking updateless decision theory is basically the right frame for any agent that might be copied, simulated, or running on different hardware, but I'm tweaking it: I treat the policy not as a pure logical object but as a physical computation pattern that has to stay stable across substrate changes. So my version says you pick the computation that maximizes expected utility over worlds where that exact computation runs, without updating on which copy or substrate you happen to be instantiated in right now. What would actually count against this? Not just teh usual logical-omniscience-is-impossible gripe, but which specific cases or results in embedded agency or distributed computing would break it. For instance, if agents who reason this way systematically fail in repeated partial-information games because they can't adapt internal weights to local feedback, is that fatal? Or take the intuition that a real robot with noisy sensors shouldn't refuse to update on sensory data or it'll crash into walls -- does that hit my view or can I absorb it by saying the computation includes the feedback loop?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of this physical-computation-updateless view concede as genuinely damaging, and which can they dismiss by tweaking the substrate-stability condition? Does this version risk collapsing back into causal decision theory once you allow the computation to contain feedback? And are there results about persistence in general -- like if we think of personal identity over time -- that stand or fall with whether this kind of non-updating reasoning is coherent? Basically I want a map of where this position is exposed and where it's well insulated, and whether being well insulated is actually a problem.
