In clinical neuropsychology units that manage patients after complete corpus callosotomy, a proposed informed-consent protocol requires presenting treatment options separately to each cerebral hemisphere—through divided visual-field displays for the left and right—rather than relying solely on verbal report. If the hemispheres indicate incompatible choices, for instance one selecting consent and the other refusal via distinct motor responses, the team records a dual-agency conflict and defers the decision to a pre-designated legal surrogate instead of privileging the left-hemisphere verbal answer or averaging the outputs. What theory of personal identity and the unity of consciousness does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it be read as treating the patient as two co-located agents with independent wills, or can a defender of a single unified consciousness account for the protocol as mere epistemic caution about unreliable integration? Reconstruct the implicit ontology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and specify what elements—such as the conflict category, the surrogate's authority, or the attribution of independent choice—would have to be revised if the underlying view were replaced by a robust single-mind account that regards hemispheric dissociation as internal disagreement within one agent rather than plural agency.
