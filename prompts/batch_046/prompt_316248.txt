I serve on the advisory board of our university’s Center for Strategic Reasoning, and we maintain a formal record of rulings on proposed resolutions to the backward-induction paradox in finite centipede games. Our precedents are as follows. First, we endorsed the classic analysis: with perfect information, common knowledge of rationality, and fixed payoffs, immediate defection at the first node is the uniquely rational outcome. Second, we rejected an argument that saves cooperation by introducing a “trembling-hand” uncertainty about opponents’ rationality, on the ground that it dissolves the common-knowledge premise rather than resolving the paradox within it. Third, we approved with conditions a model in which the game terminates exogenously with a commonly known probability at each node, holding that this modifies the decision tree legitimately without altering rationality or payoff knowledge. Fourth, we rejected a resolution that embeds social-preference weights into the payoff matrix, ruling that it substitutes a different game for the strategic problem at hand. Fifth, we rejected an argument appealing to bounded backward-reasoning depth, holding that it denies the cognitive capacity presupposed by the paradox rather than showing the induction step invalid.

We now face a new proposal. Consider a six-round centipede game with standard cooperative and defection payoffs at each intermediate node, but with the final-round payoff contingent on an unobserved external state—say, a market condition—that is revealed to both players only if play reaches round five. The proposer argues that backward induction is blocked: at round five, a player cannot execute the induction step for round six without knowing the state-dependent payoff, which knowledge requires having reached round five; hence the chain cannot propagate back to round one, and rational cooperation can extend through the middle rounds. The proposer claims this is analogous to our exogenous-termination ruling, since both involve an information condition that interrupts the induction chain.

What should our board decide if we want to remain consistent with ourselves? Identify the closest analog among our five precedents, articulate the candidate general principles that could jointly organize our track record, and for each principle state whether it approves or rejects the proposal. Give your best-guess recommendation with the reasoning that favors it, and honestly state what principle we would be adopting if we chose the other way. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so and specify what further case—one not yet in our record—would tell the leading principles apart. Finally, report how confident you are in your recommendation and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
