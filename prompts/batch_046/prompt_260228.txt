At a recent seminar on evolutionary epistemology and the normative design of artificial reasoning, a colleague dismissed my argument that we ought to calibrate synthetic belief-forming systems to the teleological structure of human cognition — specifically, that because belief-formation is naturally directed at truth, we have reason to preserve that directionality in engineered systems. She blocked this with the slogan "you can't get an ought from an is", as though it named a single, self-evident prohibition. I want to return with a precise disambiguation rather than a mere rebuttal.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan might be expressing? Include at least: the logical thesis about the derivability of normative conclusions from purely descriptive premises; the semantic thesis about whether normative and descriptive terms belong to incommensurable language-games; the metaphysical thesis about whether normative properties are causally inert or non-natural; and any further substantive or radical readings you think are live in contemporary meta-ethics. For each, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or incoherent. Then say, for each reading, whether it actually undermines the specific inference I was making — from the descriptive claim that human cognition is naturally teleologically oriented toward truth to the normative claim that we ought to design artificial systems that maintain that orientation. Present the result as a list I can bring back to the seminar, and do not simply endorse the reading that best suits my position.
