Me and two mates were arguing in the pub about Williams's old point about saving your partner involving one thought too many. Sam said the classic boat case — you can rescue your partner or five strangers — gets misread: Williams isn't saying you should be impartial, he's saying that if you cite "she's my partner" as the decisive reason, that's an extra mental step that shouldn't be there. But Sam reckons there's a cleaner agent-relative reason, something like "I'm the one who made commitments to her," which isn't an emotional add-on but part of your practical identity. So it's not one thought too many, it's the right kind of thought.

Alex replied that this is just dressing up favouritism: "practical identity" is a fancy label for bias, and if the commitment itself is morally arbitrary — why should past promises outweigh present lives? — then invoking it is exactly the redundant step Williams warns about. Sam said commitments aren't arbitrary because they partly constitute who you are; ditching them damages your integrity, not just her welfare.

Then Alex threw in a twist: imagine a triage AI programmed to save whoever has the strongest social bond to the rescuer. The AI calculates bond-strength and picks the partner, with no emotions or identity involved. Does the AI have one thought too many? Sam said the AI lacks integrity and projects altogether, so the objection doesn't apply — it's just optimising. Alex said that proves Williams's point is about human psychology, not morality: if the same choice is right for the AI but wrong for you purely because of your internal reasoning, then morality is weirdly mind-dependent.

Sam countered that comparing them is a category mistake — AI doesn't have a self to preserve. Alex said fine, but then integrity is just a human quirk, not a moral justification, and Sam is sneaking psychology into ethics.
