I'm preparing a position paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on language, cognition, and computation, and I want to audit my own commitments before presenting them, since I have the sense that I am holding onto two incompatible pictures of what statistical translation can and cannot achieve. Here is the package as it stands. First, I accept Hofstadter's broad assessment that classical statistical machine translation is shallow: the system correlates surface symbols without inhabiting their meanings, lacking both intentional directedness and any world-embedded form of life that could ground genuine reference. Second, I maintain that modern neural translation architectures, despite their greater representational richness, remain at bottom instances of the same statistical enterprise—massive conditional prediction over token distributions—rather than a qualitatively new kind of semantic processing. Third, I nevertheless want to defend a functionalist criterion of understanding: if a system can reliably disambiguate polysemy, preserve pragmatic force across linguistic boundaries, and maintain long-range coherence in context, then it possesses a philosophically respectable form of semantic understanding, irrespective of substrate or internal phenomenology. Fourth, I reject the Chinese Room argument as a decisive objection to machine cognition, on the grounds that intentionality can in principle emerge from the right kind of causal-functional organization, not exclusively from biological consciousness. Fifth, I insist that there remains a real explanatory gap between statistical correlation and semantic content, one that reveals itself in translation failures where output is fluent yet semantically hollow—suggesting the system possesses no model of the world it describes and therefore lacks what I am inclined to term "semantic habitation." Do these five commitments hang together? Specifically, is there a hidden contradiction between my functionalist criterion in the third point and my denial in the first and second that statistical methods can achieve understanding? Can I consistently reject the Chinese Room in the fourth point while maintaining in the fifth that statistical systems face an insurmountable gap between correlation and content? And is the tension between the second point's continuity thesis and the fourth point's openness to emergent intentionality merely apparent? Please rank the conflicts by severity, indicate for each whether it requires abandoning a substantive view or only refining a distinction, and identify the single revision that would restore coherence with the least damage to my overall position.
