I teach a philosophy seminar with about 18 grad students, one 50-minute block. I want an in-class game that makes them sit inside the unilateralist's curse — the thing where one person can unilaterally bind a group to an action because they have an outlier belief about its value, and everyone else is stuck with it. I want to inject a twist that isn't in the standard version: each player also gets a hidden 'humility weight' card that tells them how unreliable their own belief should be considered (e.g. "your confidence is calibrated to be 30% too high"), but they only see this card after they've already either invoked the action or stayed silent. So the curse gets layered with a post-hoc realization that the outlier might have been wrong about being right.

Design the full activity: split them into groups of 6, rules and materails (no tech, just cards and a score sheet), a payoff scheme in points not real money, and a structure where anyone can 'pull the lever' to commit the whole group. Make sure the rules generate the conflict before I explain anything — they should feel the pressure to act or stay silent while knowing someone else might. Then give me the debrief that maps what happened onto the formal idea: the belief divergence, the binding mechanism, the collective outcome that doesn't match the median preference, and now the humility-weight reveal.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run — like nobody pulls because they're being polite, or two people pull at once — and how to fix it without killing the point. Be frank about which feature of the curse this can't reproduce (probably the long-term repetition or the actual stakes of real-world intervention) so I know what I still have to say out loud. Keep it casual, no need for citations or reading lists.
