A colleague offers the following reductio-style argument, which she thinks proves far more than it should—indeed, she suspects it dissolves perceptual error altogether—but she wants to preserve disjunctivism if she can.

(1) Disjunctivism holds that a veridical perception of x and a hallucination of x belong to entirely disjoint mental kinds—call them P-events and H-events—with no shared phenomenal core.
(2) If two mental events belong to disjoint kinds, then there is no intrinsic phenomenal property F that both instantiate in virtue of which they feel subjectively alike.
(3) Now suppose, for the sake of the argument from illusion, that a hallucination could be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception. Then there must be some phenomenal respect in which they match—namely, they both feel like seeing x.
(4) By (2), however, no such matching phenomenal property can exist across P and H. So the supposition in (3) is contradictory.
(5) Therefore it is conceptually impossible for a hallucination to be subjectively indistinguishable from a corresponding perception.
(6) But the argument from illusion depends precisely on the claim that hallucination and perception can be subjectively indistinguishable.
(7) Hence the argument from illusion is not merely answerable but conceptually incoherent; and since every case of perceptual error requires taking a non-veridical state for a veridical one, there can be no perceptual error at all.

My colleague accepts (1) and, if possible, wants to keep (5). She rejects (7). Which premise or inferential step should she abandon?
