Consider the classic problem of future contingents exemplified by the sea-battle scenario: if it is true at present that a naval battle will occur tomorrow, then that event appears unavoidable and hence not genuinely contingent. The standard literature frames the dispute chiefly as a choice between denying bivalence for future-tensed statements, adopting a branching-time semantics with an open future, or embracing fatalism. Construct an argument for the less-discussed position that bivalence ought to be retained for all future contingents, but that the present truth of a future event does not entail its present causal inevitability, on the grounds that semantic determination—the fact that a proposition already bears a truth-value—and causal determination—the absence of open alternative pathways—operate on entirely different modal registers. In developing this argument, distinguish it sharply both from the Ockhamist actual-future solution and from supervaluationist approaches that deny determinate truth-values to future contingents, and show specifically why retaining bivalence does not commit one to the fatalist claim that every future occurrence is now necessary. Use the sea-battle case concretely, explaining exactly how the battle can be both truly going to happen and genuinely avoidable, rather than treating the scenario as a mere placeholder for abstract logical form.
