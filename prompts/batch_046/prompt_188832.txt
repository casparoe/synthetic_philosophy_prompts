I've been trying to figure out why smart people still argue about whether old societies "collapse" or just change into something smaller. I was reading about the Bronze Age thing — like around 1177 BCE all these palace economies fall apart and people used to call it a systems collapse — but then archeologists come along and say that's too neat, and the same back-and-forth happens with the Maya and Easter Island. One side talks about it like a machine breaking: writing stops, big buildings empty out, trade networks vanish, so the society is dead. The other side says it's just a downsizing: people stop living in cities but they're still farming, still speaking the language, so calling it collapse is like saying a person died because they moved to a smaller apartment.

My own hunch is that they're not actually disagreeing about bones and pottery, but about whether a society has some kind of essential form that can be destroyed, or if it's just a temporary pattern of how people organize labor and food. But maybe I'm overthinking it and there really is a hidden factual question about population counts or drought that I'm missing. Is the stall happening because the word "collapse" means different things to different camps — one group means political fragmentation, another means total cultural extinction — or is it that neither side can agree on what counts as proof? And honestly, do you think anything could actually end the debate, or is the concept itself broken? Don't just list the options; pick a definite diagnosis and defend it, and tell me what progress would even look like here.
