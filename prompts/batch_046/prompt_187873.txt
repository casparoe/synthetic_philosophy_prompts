I facilitate a forecasting collective in the rationalist-adjacent space: a group that submits quarterly probabilistic judgments on long-horizon empirical questions—policy effects, technology timelines, epidemiological outcomes. A recurring problem is sharp disagreement between two members who are local epistemic peers in a domain: comparable calibration curves over the past two years, comparable expertise, and full access to the same public evidence, yet one assigns 65 percent and the other 25 percent to the same resolution criterion. Both can produce explicit reasoning chains, but they weight priors differently, disagree about which analogies are relevant, and sometimes split cognitive labor—one builds the base-rate model, the other evaluates the causal mechanism—so peerhood is not always global but local to a reasoning chain.

I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes without external literature to determine, by the lights of the peer-disagreement literature—conciliationism versus steadfastness—whether the group should apply mutual belief revision or allow steadfastness. The deliverable should be a checklist or flowchart in words: ordered steps, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, explicit stopping conditions, and clear flags for which steps are genuinely procedural and which are judgment calls dressed as rules.

Please build in a formal distinction that standard treatments rarely operationalize: reasoning-node locality. The procedure must test whether the disagreement occurs at a shared reasoning node (both parties have evaluated the same premises) or at a private node (one party’s update depends on an intermediate step the other has not assessed).

The procedure should cover:

1. Peerhood verification: domain calibration, evidence access, and reasoning transparency.
2. Node-mapping: identifying whether the divergence is at a shared base-rate node, a shared mechanism node, or a private intermediate node.
3. Evidence-overlap audit: whether the parties are updating on the same observations or on asymmetric private inputs.
4. The conciliation rule: specify the form—whether split-the-difference weighted by relative calibration, mutual confidence reduction without convergence, or both lowering toward a midpoint.
5. The steadfastness exception: conditions under which one party may hold firm, including reasoning-debt asymmetry or transparency failure.
6. The values-migration detector: criteria for recognizing when an apparently empirical disagreement actually depends on normative weighting, at which point the procedure stops and hands the decision to a values framework rather than an epistemic rule.
7. Explicit misfire conditions: where the procedure will produce an answer that conflicts with group norms, and what it cannot prevent.

Then apply the procedure to three cases.

Case one: Two members disagree on whether a specific urban-intervention pilot reduced violent crime by more than ten percent in the target city over eighteen months. Both have comparable urban-policy calibration scores. One weights an analog study from a similar city heavily; the other discounts it because of demographic differences. Both reasoning chains are fully shared, and the divergence is at a shared mechanism-evaluation node. The procedure should apply conciliation. Show exactly how the output works, including the form of revision.

Case two: One member is steadfast at 80 percent confidence in a technology-timeline prediction; the other is at 40 percent. The high-confidence member refuses to share the intermediate model, citing proprietary reasoning. The low-confidence member demands conciliation. Here the procedure should probably permit steadfastness because of the transparency failure—but that is the answer I do not want, because it rewards opacity. Run the procedure, confirm the unwelcome result, and then state precisely what the procedure cannot do to prevent strategic withholding of reasoning.

Case three: A disagreement framed as empirical—whether a proposed wildlife vaccination program will reduce badger suffering—turns out to depend on whether suffering-reduction should be weighted more heavily than ecological-disturbance costs. The procedure should detect that the disagreement has migrated from an epistemic node to a normative weighting node and should stop, handing the decision to a values framework rather than conciliation or steadfastness.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works. Paraphrase the conciliationist and steadfast positions without verbatim quotation. Be honest about where the procedure requires a call that no rule can settle, and flag every such point explicitly.
