I'm drafting a scene for my novel where a sociologist and a writer quarrel over explaining a riot. The sociologist says studying society is like understanding a novel: if you only cataloge every character's private motive — the grammar of individual sentences — you miss the plot, the genre, the collective arc. The novelist (basically me) hits back that theres no 'plot' independent of actual sentences written by real people; any 'holistic' story that ignores the individual voices is just sloppy editing, and methodological holism is the literary equivalent of claiming the book wrote itself. They're really arguing about methodological individualism versus holism in social science, but I want to audit the analogy properly before I write the scene. Can you lay out the correspondence table — author, characters, plot structure, reader expectation, genre conventions, editorial revision — mapped onto society, individuals, institutions, observers, culture, policy? Then the fractures: where fiction has an intentional designer societies lack, where plot is teleological but social patterns aren't, and whether 'narrative residue' — those bits of collective behaviour that don't reduce to individual intent but also fail to cohere into any plot, like abandoned subplots — fits anywhere in the mapping or just collapses the whole thing. Finally, does the novelist's conclusion — that holism is just bad editing — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy smuggling in my writer's bias the whole time? I'm not after a bibliography or citations, just whether the analogy can actually do the work both sides want it to do.
