I am preparing a simulated adversarial-collaboration assignment for my seminar on political philosophy and artificial intelligence. I need a joint statement that could plausibly be signed by two opposing camps after good-faith collaboration, with their residual disagreements made exact rather than rhetorical.

The two parties are:
(1) Aggregative Value-Pluralists: they maintain that an aligned AI system should serve a weighted aggregation of human values, using interpersonally comparable welfare metrics derived from preference revelation and well-being science; they treat claims of value incommensurability as either technically surmountable or as disguised normative commitments that aggregation can make explicit.
(2) Liberal-Neutral Proceduralists: they maintain that an aligned AI must observe liberal neutrality, enforcing only the basic structure of fair cooperation and protecting basic liberties, without ranking reasonable comprehensive doctrines; they hold that any aggregation metric illegitimately imposes a single conception of the good and violates the separateness of persons in a manner structurally analogous to the repugnant conclusion in population axiology.

Please draft the document they could both sign. Structure it as follows:
First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include common ground on: (a) the empirical architecture of preference-learning systems and why they currently lack cross-domain utility comparators; (b) the normative framing of a concrete test case—an AI tutor allocating scarce, personalized instruction time among students with genuinely incommensurable life projects (e.g., contemplative spirituality, competitive market achievement, and scientific truth-seeking)—including agreement on what makes the allocation problem hard; and (c) the shared rejection of both pure technocratic authoritarianism and pure unmediated market allocation.
Second, a disagreements table. For each camp, state the precise formulation of its own position in wording that camp would accept, and state the opposing position in wording the other camp recognizes. The disagreements must be genuine, not merely verbal.
Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. These must include: (i) whether algorithmic incommensurability—the absence of a native cross-domain comparator in current architectures—is a philosophical justification for liberal neutrality or merely an engineering obstacle for aggregationists; and (ii) whether applying the separateness of persons objection to AI-mediated value aggregation is structurally analogous to its deployment against totalist population axiology, or a category error arising from the non-identity of algorithmic recommendations and procreative choices.
Fourth, for each crux, specify one concrete argument or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's predicted outcome or verdict recorded in advance. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote specific texts verbatim. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a university course assignment.
