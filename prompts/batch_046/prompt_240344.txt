I'm working through case studies for a seminar on cultural restitution and institutional consistency, and I want to treat the track record of an advisory body—the Council on Cultural Repatriation—as a precedent system. The Council has issued four rulings that I take to be jointly consistent with more than one principle. First, they ordered the unconditional physical return of ceremonial regalia seized by force during a documented colonial military campaign in the 1890s, requested by the specific clan that demonstrates the objects are essential to ongoing initiation rites. Second, they denied the return of ancient architectural fragments removed in 1815 under a legal permit issued by the then-ruling imperial authority; the fragments are now in a Berlin museum, and the claim was brought by the modern nation-state that controls the original site, arguing territorial patrimony rather than specific ritual continuity. Third, they repatriated ancestral human remains taken through deception in 1902 to descendants with direct genealogical links and active funerary obligations, attaching ritual conditions—burial within five years and a prohibition on further scientific study. Fourth, they denied a request for religious manuscripts acquired through trade in 1880 by Portuguese merchants and now held in a Dutch museum; the requesting community practices the same faith but relies on copies and could not demonstrate specific harm from retention.

I need to apply this pattern to a new case and identify the competing rules behind it. The matter concerns ritual objects violently seized by Spanish forces in 1530 from an Andean community, sold to a British collector in 1850, and now in a London museum. The specific descendant community—documented through genealogical and ethnographic records—requests physical repatriation, arguing that the objects remain necessary for ceremonies that have persisted in modified form. The situation is complicated by a recent development: over the past decade the museum has created high-fidelity volumetric scans and acoustic simulations of the artifacts, licensing these digital twins back to the community for remote virtual rituals. The claimants now seek not only the physical objects but also a formal "ritual closure protocol" that would decommission the digital archive, contending that this virtual dependency was established without adequate consent and now disrupts embodied practice.

What should the Council recommend for consistency with its precedents? Please lay out at least two plausible principles that jointly explain all four prior verdicts, show how each principle predicts a different outcome for the Andean claim—both for the physical artifacts and, if your reasoning requires it, for the status of the digital assets—state which recommendation you favor with your reasons, and then propose one further hypothetical case, specifying the acquisition history, current holder, claimant identity, and the presence or absence of digital replicas, that would most cleanly distinguish between the two principles you have identified.
