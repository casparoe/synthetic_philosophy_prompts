Question: In his 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics," Milton Friedman defends the use of unrealistic assumptions in economic modeling through an argument that critics later dubbed the F-twist. First, reconstruct the structure of Friedman's defense, including the role of predictive success and the analogy he uses to illustrate "as if" reasoning. Here is the student's answer:

"In the 1953 essay, Friedman argues that a theory should be judged solely by its predictive power, not by the descriptive accuracy of its assumptions. He illustrates this with the billiard-player analogy: expert players behave as if they solve complex physics equations, even though they do not consciously do so. From this, Friedman concludes that economic agents can be modeled as if they maximize utility with perfect information, regardless of whether real decision-making involves explicit calculation.

This reasoning is what critics call the F-twist: one begins with assumptions known to be false—such as perfect competition or rational expectations—derives predictions, and then validates the model if those predictions hold. The twist lies in treating predictive success as sufficient for scientific legitimacy. In this respect, Friedman's view collapses into pure instrumentalism: economic theories are merely forecasting instruments with no representational ambitions.

Against this, I think the F-twist fails because false premises necessarily corrupt any derivation. In standard logic, if the premises are false, the conclusions must also be false unless the inference itself is invalid; since economic models aim for valid deduction, the falsehood of the assumptions undermines the reliability of the predictions. Consequently, unrealistic assumptions do damage explanatory power, because explanation requires that the mechanisms posited in the premises actually govern the phenomena. Friedman attempts to insulate positive economics from normative judgments, but by allowing false mechanisms he inadvertently erases the boundary between explaining a phenomenon and merely predicting it. A further problem is that in domains with low counterfactual stability—such as macroeconomic policy—mechanism-independent prediction is impossible, so Friedman's billiard analogy does not carry over."
