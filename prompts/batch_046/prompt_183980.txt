I was recently named co-administrator of my late grandfather's estate, which includes two vintage acoustic guitars that a luthier has certified as being built by the same maker in the same year, with indistinguishable tonal properties, condition, and market value. My grandfather's will directs that one guitar be given to each of his two grandsons—both amateur musicians of comparable skill—yet it also requires me to record, in writing, a specific and relevant reason justifying each particular assignment. Both grandsons have told me, sincerely and repeatedly, that they have no preference whatsoever between the two instruments and would be equally content with either. I have examined every practical consideration I can think of—need, merit, proximity, prior use—and found no asymmetry. If I assign them arbitrarily, I seem to violate the will's explicit demand for reasoned justification; if I postpone the decision indefinitely, I fail in my administrative duty. From a philosophical perspective, is there a defensible principle that permits rational choice in a situation of perfect equipreference, or does this deadlock reveal a genuine conceptual limit—namely, that practical reason cannot operate without some distinguishing feature that is absent here? I would appreciate an analysis that treats both my fiduciary obligations and the underlying structure of the problem, rather than offering generic advice about family harmony.
