If we treat the paired origins of calculus and evolutionary theory not as biographical coincidences but as structured data — Newton’s geometric fluxions set against Leibniz’s analytic differentials, Darwin’s domestically anchored selection set against Wallace’s biogeographically urgent version — they open a family of unresolved questions that philosophy of science has never fully ordered by importance. One cluster concerns inevitability: whether these discoveries were bound to occur once mathematics or biology reached a particular stage, which would make the discoverer’s identity a historical accident with little philosophical weight. A second cluster concerns identity: whether Newton and Leibniz, or Darwin and Wallace, actually discovered the same theories given the deep differences in notation, conceptual framing, and problem-space, or whether later synthesis created an illusion of sameness that obscures genuinely divergent achievements. A third, less often examined, concerns the causal role of priority disputes themselves: whether the fact that calculus became associated with Leibniz’s notation and evolution with Darwin’s problem-set altered not merely the sociology but the actual conceptual development of those fields, locking in certain questions and suppressing others.
