I am drafting a novel about the dissolution of an imaginary maritime republic in 1789. Within the fiction, archival evidence suggests its collapse arose from two concurrent crises—a catastrophic grain failure and a foreign naval blockade—each of which appears sufficient alone, yet they coincided. The final chapter turns on the chief chronicler, who must deliver an official account. One narrative architecture treats the famine and the blockade as rival explanations competing for primacy; the other weaves them into a unified causal tapestry in which excising either crisis still leaves the ruin intact. I wish the chronicler's reasoning to be philosophically rigorous rather than merely dramatic, particularly regarding whether narrative selection should mirror causal production or serve explanatory economy. Which architecture more honestly reflects the logic of multi-causal explanation in history, and is there a defensible principle for assigning prominence to a condition that was never strictly indispensable?
