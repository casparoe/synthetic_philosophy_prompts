Write a sestina on Buddhist anattā and personal identity in which the philosophical argument is carried by the form itself. Use these six end-words in this order for stanza one: form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, owner. Follow the standard sestina rotation (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on through six stanzas). In each sestet, let one of the five aggregates serve as the grammatical subject or governing image, while “owner” never does; instead, “owner” should appear only in syntactic positions that deny attachment—direct address that receives no answer, a missing agent, a noun modified only by absence, or an appositive with nothing to rename. The poem should trace the Abhidharma insight that identity is not annihilated but mislocated: the aggregates continue in causal sequence, yet none can legitimately claim the mental states that arise within it. In the three-line envoi, include all six end-words arranged so that “owner” is left grammatically suspended—without a verb, without an object, or without the noun it might possess. The poem must be exactly thirty-nine lines (six sestets plus the envoi). Use American English. Do not cite texts, include bibliographies, quote scriptures, or name specific sūtras; render the concept through image, syntax, and the formal repetition alone. It should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the skandha analysis recognizes the structural point, and should still work as poetry for someone who does not share that background.
