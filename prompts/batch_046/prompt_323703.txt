Over the past month, the environmental-services and dietary staff at my university hospital have been on strike over mandatory overtime and safe-staffing ratios, and I have been asked to serve on a faculty-staff solidarity committee despite having no background in labor philosophy. I am, however, a biostatistician with fifteen years of experience in adaptive clinical-trial design—blinding, randomization, clinical equipoise, protocol deviations, interim stopping rules, and intention-to-treat analysis are second nature to me—so I am trying to understand whether the philosophical defense of collective refusal of labor can be rendered in the vocabulary I actually think in. Please take the standard philosophical justification for the right to strike and organize—particularly the claim that collective withdrawal of labor functions as a democratic corrective to structural power asymmetry and as an expression of collective autonomy rather than mere contract breach—and restate it as precisely as you can using only concepts from clinical-trial methodology: allocation concealment and randomization, blinding of subjects and investigators, clinical equipoise, adaptive designs with interim analyses, per-protocol versus intention-to-treat populations, and protocol deviations that threaten internal validity. For each mapped claim, indicate whether the correspondence is structural or merely metaphorical. Then provide a residue report: which elements of the labor-rights argument survive translation without distortion, which require forced stretching, and which have no counterpart at all in trial-design logic—especially anything involving moral status, solidarity, or irreversible harm to non-participating third parties. If the premise that these two frameworks are genuinely commensurable is confused, or if the philosophical position I have sketched is too underspecified to support an honest translation, say so plainly rather than constructing a false analogy. I want to know whether my intuition that a strike is structurally analogous to stopping a trial for futility is philosophically serious or is missing something that the vocabulary of clinical research simply cannot express.
