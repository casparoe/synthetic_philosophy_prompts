In my philosophy seminar this semester we have been examining Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, and for an independent paper I am applying these ideas to a problem I have not seen treated extensively: whether a decentralized digital community that makes binding decisions through distributed consensus can possess, under an Ubuntu framework, an interdependent form of collective personhood that generates reciprocal moral obligations back to its members—rather than being reducible to a contractual aggregation of individuals. This framing raises several genuinely unsettled lines of work: whether relational personhood can scale beyond intimate, face-to-face communities without collapsing into liberal individualism; whether Ubuntu’s normative commitment to harmony can coherently accommodate adversarial democratic procedures; and how the framework should treat members who withdraw from collective life without fully severing their relational ties. Among these and any related open questions you see, which ones matter most for developing a workable ethical framework for collective governance, and which are overrated or fail to depend specifically on accepting Ubuntu’s ontology? Rank your priorities, defend the ranking with explicit criteria—tractability, importance for policy design, and whether resolving one unlocks others—and state what evidence or argument would change the ordering. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
