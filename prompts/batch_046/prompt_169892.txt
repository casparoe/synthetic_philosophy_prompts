I am examining whether philosophical intuition pumps retain their distorting power once they migrate from seminar-room discussion into administrative and legal reasoning, and I want to test this claim through a concrete historical trajectory. Consider the "ticking time bomb" hypothetical—where a captured agent knows the location of an imminent explosive device and only coercive interrogation will extract the information—which was widely circulated in American legal and policy debates after 2001, notably in the public arguments of prominent legal scholars and in the reasoning of the 2002 Department of Justice Office of Legal Counsel memoranda on enhanced interrogation. Rather than treating it as a simple moral dilemma, analyze this episode through the lens of intuition-pump theory: identify the specific persuasive mechanics at work, such as variable isolation, emotional salience, and false precision about an agent's knowledge states; map how the hypothetical systematically suppressed the bureaucratic, evidentiary, and institutional conditions that characterize actual intelligence work; and determine which actors in the debate functioned as transmitters of the pump versus its institutional critics.

In particular, assess whether this case exhibits a distinctive failure mode—what might be called "normative leakage"—wherein the internal logical conditions of the thought experiment migrate into binding legal doctrine without the epistemic safeguards that the hypothetical itself implicitly assumes. Say whether the episode ultimately confirms or undermines the claim that intuition pumps can be disciplined by institutional embedding, and specify precisely where the theory of intuition pumps remains silent about the dynamics you observe, so that the case can speak back to the theory rather than being merely subsumed by it.
