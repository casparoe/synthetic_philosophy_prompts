My study group cooked up this argument about Jeffrey updating and now I'm stuck. (1) Jeffrey conditionalization says when you learn some proposition E but only to degree r, you shift your priors by weighting P(H|E) and P(H|~E) by r and 1-r. (2) Introspective reports—like "I'm in pain"—are never learned with certainty; there's always some nonzero chance I'm confabulating or mislabeling my state. (3) So if I'm being consistent, every introspective update should recieve a Jeffrey update with r < 1, where r is my credence in the report's accuracy. (4) But r itself is fixed by another introspective judgment about my own cognitive reliability. (5) Applying Jeffrey's rule to that second-order judgment means I need a third-order r', and so on. (6) The only fixed points are either r=0 (never update) or r=1 (certain), but r=1 violates (2). So consistent Jeffrey-ism about the mind collapses all first-person beliefs back to thier priors. (7) That proves too much—if introspection never moves my beliefs, I can't even know I'm conscious. (8) So Jeffrey conditionalization must be wrong, or at least banned from psychology. I really want to keep (2) and (4) because its obvious they capture something real, but I'm not sure what giving up (6) costs. Which step do you reject, and what's the price? Give me a committed answer—pick one and defend it, don't just lay out the menu.
