I'm preparing material for a seminar on historical objectivity that moves from Ranke's aspiration to recover the past as it actually was, through E.H. Carr's insistence that historical facts are never raw but always selected and interpreted by the historian, to Hayden White's more radical claim that the past is irreducibly narrativized and that any set of events can be legitimately emplotted as romance, tragedy, satire, or comedy without one version being more true than another. I want to use Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) not as a simple illustration but as a philosophical stress-test, and I need a reading that is faithful in both directions.

Read the film through this lens. Rashomon presents four incompatible eyewitness accounts of a bandit's encounter with a samurai and his wife in a forest—each shown with equal cinematic authority, none privileged, with contradictory details about the rape, the killing, and even the weather and the quality of light. Where does this genuinely illuminate Carr's point that the historian's decision about which testimony to credit is already an act of interpretation shaped by present concerns? Does the structure go further and support White's view that the "facts" themselves—whether a dagger was stolen, whether a comb was dropped, whether a sword was broken—are constituted by the narrative form imposed on them, so that there is no stable historical field prior to emplotment? At the same time, resist reducing the film to a diagram: pay attention to the emotional and ethical weight of each account, especially the wife's shifting self-image and the dead samurai's testimony delivered through a medium. Is there any sense in which the woodcutter's apparently more sober, less self-serving final version offers a Rankean resolution—a recovery of what actually happened—or is its placement as the "last" word just another rhetorical move, perhaps the comic or satirical emplotment of human unreliability that White describes? Be precise about where the philosophical reading holds, where it forces the film into an alien framework, and where the film complicates the philosophy—particularly regarding whether the absence of an adjudicating authority means there is no fact of the matter, or merely that we are epistemically cut off from it. End by saying what Rashomon adds to the debate about historical objectivity that a purely argumentative treatment of Ranke, Carr, and White cannot supply.
