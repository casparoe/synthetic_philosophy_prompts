As a novelist constructing a long narrative in which one protagonist persists across volumes despite being physically described in radically incompatible ways—now embodied, now spectral, now dispersed across multiple voices—I have been preoccupied with the metaphysical problem of material constitution. Philosophers debating the statue and the clay sometimes borrow an analogy from aesthetics: they say the statue is to its clay as a symphony is to its performances, implying that one formal or functional entity can be realized by successive material substrates without being identical to any of them. This analogy is typically deployed to defend the view that the statue and the lump of clay are not one thing but two, since the statue can survive the gradual replacement of parts and can be destroyed while the clay persists in altered form. I want you to audit this analogy with precision. First, set out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the statue-case plays the role of the score, of the individual performance, of the acoustic medium, and of the listener's recognition? Second, identify the disanalogies that bear on the argument—especially the difference between a sculptor's intentional imposition of form and a composer's specification of sound-structure, and whether the analogy quietly smuggles in an essentialist commitment to abstract form that material objects need not carry. Third, examine whether the conclusion that statue and clay are distinct survives once we notice that symphony-identity itself is contested between Platonist, Aristotelian, and nominalist interpretations; if the source case is ambiguous, can it license a definite verdict in the target case? Fourth, propose whether an alternative analogy drawn from narrative fiction—say, a recurring literary character constituted by incompatible textual descriptions—would do the metaphysical work better, or whether it merely reproduces the original puzzle in literary dress. Be explicit about which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which do not, and say what a more defensible analogy for material constitution would look like if this one collapses.
