I have been working through the Ellsberg paradox on my own, without formal training in economics or decision theory, and I am struck by how little the central dispute seems to have moved since the problem was first formulated. The setup is familiar: an urn containing thirty red balls and sixty balls that are either black or yellow in an unknown proportion, with the observed pattern that people consistently prefer to bet on red over black, and on red-or-yellow over black-or-yellow. The standard explanation is ambiguity aversion — a dislike of unknown probabilities — and the literature has split between those who treat this as a failure of subjective expected utility theory, specifically a violation of the Savage axioms, and those who defend it as rational caution in the face of Knightian uncertainty.

As I read further, however, I begin to suspect that the stalemate is not really about rationality in any single sense. One camp seems to be asking whether a normative theory of choice ought to permit preferences that violate consistency axioms; the other seems to be asking whether descriptive psychology should model agents as if they assign precise subjective probabilities to every event. These strike me as different questions that happen to share the same experimental result. At the same time, there may be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether ambiguity aversion survives when stakes are real and substantial rather than hypothetical and small, or whether the urn framing itself manufactures the effect by making the missing information salient in an artificial way.

So I want to ask: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'rational,' with one side using it normatively and the other descriptively? Is it a substantive disagreement about whether all uncertainty is in principle quantifiable? Or are the two sides answering different questions — one about the axioms of belief, one about the psychology of choice — under the single heading 'ambiguity aversion'? And if the diagnosis is that they are talking past each other, is there a conceptual distinction — perhaps between epistemic risk and practical risk, or between ambiguity about probabilities and ambiguity about outcomes — that could cleanly separate the questions? Alternatively, if the impasse is a bedrock clash of intuitions about how agents ought to behave when information is genuinely missing, should we expect any argument, any redesigned experiment, or any formal result to resolve it? Give me the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress.
