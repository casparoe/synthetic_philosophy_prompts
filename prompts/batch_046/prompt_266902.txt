I'm drafting a creative assignment for my philosophy of religion grad seminar and I want to steer away from teh usual recycled free-will-defense plots. Write a short story—maybe in a strange bureaucratic office—about an archivist who files 'counterfactual reports' recording what every person would do in every possible situation, and who slowly realizes these reports ground a kind of middle knowledge: they are known prior to any decision about which world exists, yet they depend on the libertarian freedom of the creatures in them. Work in Molina's actual threefold division—natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge—rather than generic omniscience, and have the protagonist genuinely stuck on the grounding objection: if the reports depend on what free creatures would choose, but are known before any creative decree, what makes them true? Keep it concrete and weird, not a straight allegory. After the story, continue in the same continuous prose with absolutely no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just like a long email—and include a brief note that separates what's fairly settled in the literature on scientia media from what's genuinely contested, such as whether counterfactuals of freedom need an independent ground, and don't present either side of that dispute as teh consensus. Write the whole thing as one flowing text.
