I have been thinking, without any formal training, about what I understand to be called the paradox of deontology: the idea that a moral theory treating certain actions as wrong regardless of consequences can end up permitting, or even requiring, a far greater total of those same wrong actions than a theory that allows them for the greater good. I keep returning to a specific version of this problem that does not involve physical violence, but rather the breaking of procedural commitments—violating a fair agreement, a transparent decision process, or a mutual expectation—to prevent a larger number of similar violations elsewhere.

My own suspicion is that the paradox operates differently in this procedural domain. If an absolute rule prohibits direct harm to a particular individual, then allowing one such harm to prevent five seems to undermine the very reason for the prohibition. But if the rule concerns maintaining a structure of mutual expectation—say, that promises or fair procedures must not be broken—then breaking one commitment to preserve a thousand might not violate the same kind of principle, because the principle's purpose is systemic rather than transactional. Still, I worry this distinction may simply disguise a consequentialist calculation rather than genuinely resolving the tension.

Write an essay that takes up this precise question. Examine whether deontology can be defended from the paradox by separating direct-harm prohibitions, which remain absolute, from procedural-commitment rules, which are stringent but not paradox-generating in the same way. Weigh the strongest objections to this separation, especially the worry that it collapses into outcome-based reasoning or that it arbitrarily divides moral rules without principled justification. Reach a definite conclusion—whether the distinction saves deontology, undermines it, or requires a more nuanced reformulation—and justify that conclusion through conceptual argument rather than appeal to named authorities, specific texts, or citation. The essay should treat the distinction seriously and should not assume that any resolution is obvious.
