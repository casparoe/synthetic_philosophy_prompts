I've been discussing peer disagreement with friends in a philosophy reading group, and I'm struck by how quickly debates about conciliationism versus steadfastness reduce to trading intuitions about a single stock case—usually two equally qualified doctors disagreeing about a diagnosis, or two philosophers clashing over free will—without anyone first specifying what an adequate theory must actually deliver. Before I read any more defenses of either camp, I want the full desiderata: what must any theory of rational doxastic revision (or steadfast maintenance) in the face of recognized peer disagreement get right? Give me the requirements list with the motivating case or distinction behind each—protecting epistemic self-trust without collapsing into dogmatism; distinguishing empirical measurement disagreement from disagreement over fundamental normative or interpretive frameworks; handling asymmetric cases where one peer possesses private evidence or where the dispute is practical rather than theoretical; remaining consistent with the actual psychology of belief-formation rather than treating agents as pure Bayesian calculators; and accommodating domain-specific expertise calibration rather than treating all peer pairs as epistemically interchangeable. For each, flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and say whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, if preserving full self-trust in deep philosophical disagreement is incompatible with giving peers equal weight in highly technical empirical domains. I do not want a scorecard comparing conciliationism and steadfastness against the list; the conceptual work is in the criteria themselves. Please write your response in American English, with the weight on the specification and its motivating cases rather than on citing specific authors.
