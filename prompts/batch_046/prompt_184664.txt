I serve as senior legislative counsel for a constitutional revision commission that reviews proposed binding-future-self clauses—provisions meant to constrain later legislatures or electorates to prevent foreseeable weakness of will, short-term reversal, or cyclical policy swings. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in roughly thirty minutes per proposal, without reconstructing the underlying philosophy from scratch. The procedure should translate the logic of Ulysses contracts—deliberately restricting one’s future options to protect long-term commitments—into constitutional mechanics, while confronting the democratic asymmetry that distinguishes individual precommitment from intergenerational binding: a present majority that locks in its preferences may not be tying its own mast but tying someone else’s.

To keep the procedure from collapsing into generic cost-benefit analysis, build it around the following conceptual injection, which standard treatments of precommitment often blur. First, distinguish cost-reversible provisions—those that leave future actors with the same option space but attach a defined, surmountable cost to reversal, thereby preserving autonomy as the capacity to choose the cost—from structurally irreversible provisions, which eliminate the option regardless of future will or resources. Second, apply an autonomy-preservation test that asks not whether reversal is difficult, but whether reversal remains a meaningful exercise of collective choice. Third, flag signaling functions: clauses that use irreversibility primarily to bind present voter expectations rather than to protect future decision-makers from their own anticipated weakness of will.

Deliver the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests or questions. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, whether the answer requires empirical investigation, legal interpretation, or normative judgment, where the procedure stops with a recommendation against adoption, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is irreducibly about values rather than institutional mechanics. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
(1) A constitutional carbon-tax floor reducible only by referendum requiring sixty percent approval, with no other repeal mechanism.
(2) A balanced-budget rule containing a war-only escape clause, where the determination of war is made by an independent tribunal whose members serve lifetime terms filled by appointments made under the current constitutional order.
(3) A mandatory forty-year sunset on all emergency economic powers, paired with an automatic renewal mechanism that activates if the legislature fails to convene for a vote within thirty days of expiration.

Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation that I—committed to strong democratic precommitment—will find undesirable, and identify the exact judgment call that produces the result. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: specify the boundary conditions where philosophical disagreement about the legitimacy of intergenerational binding makes any procedural answer impossible. Constraints: I have legal and political-science training but no philosophical specialization; the output must fit a one-page memo with a recommendation box and a separately flagged judgment zone; sign-off requires my name and the commission chair’s.
