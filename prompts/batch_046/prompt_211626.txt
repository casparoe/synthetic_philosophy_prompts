Next month I present at a workshop on ancient political psychology the claim that in Laws I–II Plato institutionalizes state-supervised intoxication not as a concession to weakness but as a deliberate diagnostic technology—what I term “structured transgression”—in which controlled alcohol lowers inhibitions to reveal the true hierarchy of thumos, eros, and logos in citizens, letting the legislator correct misordered souls before they harden into civic vice. The audience will be classicists skeptical of presentist “surveillance-state” framings, analytic philosophers who read Republic IX’s asceticism as Plato’s settled normative view on pleasure, and political theorists alert to coercive perfectionism. Prepare me for the Q&A: order the objections by how likely and damaging they are, phrase each in that audience’s vocabulary (e.g., “Republic-consistency,” “functionalist anachronism,” “liberal neutrality”), indicate which carry real force and what I should concede up front, sketch a concise reply to each with its weakest point noted, flag the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, and identify the single reply that has to land for the thesis to survive. Target my argument: that the symposion in the Laws is designed as a civic mirror rather than a recreational exception, and that the Athenian Stranger’s drinking rules are therefore central, not peripheral, to Platonic political psychology.
