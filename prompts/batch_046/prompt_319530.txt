Exam question: A climate modeler possesses strong first-order evidence that her statistical framework is sound, but she also acquires robust higher-order evidence—derived from a meta-analysis of her predictive failures in analogous regimes—that her confidence is likely inflated by motivated reasoning. She retains belief in the framework's adequacy while simultaneously judging that she ought to reduce her confidence. Is this state genuinely epistemically akratic? And can she resolve the tension by adopting a 'partitioned' stance, treating her first-order and second-order assessments as belonging to separate epistemic domains, without collapsing into pragmatic encroachment or self-deception?

Student answer: Yes, this is a clear case of epistemic akrasia, structurally parallel to practical akrasia: just as a smoker judges smoking harmful yet continues, she judges her belief unreliable yet retains it, which demonstrates a failure of doxastic willpower. The higher-order evidence bears on her reasoning reliability rather than on the world, so it does not defeat the first-order content but only undermines her entitlement to hold it. By partitioning her assessments—placing the first-order belief in an object-level domain and the second-order doubt in a meta-level domain—she avoids self-deception, since she does not deny the second-order judgment but merely quarantines it. This partition is essentially the isolationist position familiar from peer-disagreement debates, which permits retaining one's original belief unchanged. Consequently, because she has avoided self-deception, she has also avoided pragmatic encroachment, since encroachment requires practical stakes to influence belief directly, and here any influence is confined to the meta-level.
