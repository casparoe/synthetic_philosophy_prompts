Write an essay on whether everyday conventions—specifically tipping in service economies, queueing in public spaces, and jaywalking in traffic contexts—constitute genuine moral obligations or merely local coordination devices, and whether visitors to a culture are bound by them. Rather than framing this as a simple universalism-versus-relativism debate, approach it through the lens of coordinative morality: the idea that some norms derive binding force not from intrinsic wrongness but from sustaining a shared expectation equilibrium, and that breaching them can be wrong even absent direct harm if it erodes that equilibrium. Consider whether a visitor who lacks long-term stake in the equilibrium—who will not suffer the downstream costs of norm erosion—can nevertheless be said to wrong locals by flouting the convention. Weigh the arguments: on one side, the view that morality governs harm and fairness regardless of cultural membership; on the other, the view that participation in a local practice generates a temporary, role-based duty of cooperation. Reach and defend a specific verdict—for instance, that visitors have a pro tanto obligation to comply with visible coordination norms but not with norms whose moral status depends on deep cultural membership, or some other nuanced position. Your essay should treat the philosophical idea seriously, apply it precisely to at least two of the three examples, and conclude with a justified judgment that does not merely split the difference.
