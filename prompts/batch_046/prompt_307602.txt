Write a Petrarchan sonnet about the methodological claim associated with Milton Friedman — often called the F-twist — that the descriptive realism of a scientific model's assumptions is irrelevant provided its predictions are sufficiently accurate. Structure the poem so that the volta between octave and sestet enacts the twist itself: in the octave, adopt the voice of a realist critic arguing that false premises (rational agents, perfect competition, frictionless markets) corrupt not merely the picture but the explanation, with rhyme tightening around representational fidelity; at the volta, pivot sharply to the instrumentalist defense that predictive success is the only court of appeal and that unrealistic simplifications are tools, not portraits. In the sestet, trace the specific consequence that explanatory transparency is sacrificed: the model operates "as if" its falsehoods were true, forecasting outcomes without licensing claims about actual causal mechanisms, so that we know what will happen without knowing why the variables correspond to real forces. Close the couplet with the precise cost — that forecasting power is purchased at the expense of knowing whether the model's terms map onto real causes — and keep the language formal enough that a philosopher of economics recognizes the distinction between predictive adequacy and representational truth, but let meter and rhyme carry the argument.
