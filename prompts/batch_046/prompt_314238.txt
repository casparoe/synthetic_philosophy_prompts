Analyze the ongoing restoration of Notre-Dame de Paris following the April 2019 fire as an applied case of the Ship of Theseus problem. The blaze destroyed the medieval timber roof and the spire added by Eugène Viollet-le-Duc in the nineteenth century; the current project replaces both with replicas built largely from traditional materials, while also inserting hidden modern structural supports and debating whether the spire should be reconstructed exactly as Viollet-le-Duc left it or redesigned. Treat the cathedral as the vessel, the replaced oak and lead as the planks, and the spire as a part that was itself never part of the original twelfth- and thirteenth-century structure. First, apply the standard identity conditions—material continuity, formal and functional equivalence, and social or institutional recognition—to the actual restoration decisions, specifying which condition each design choice privileges. Second, show where the standard framing misdescribes or breaks down in this case: address the nested problem that Viollet-le-Duc's spire was already a restoration addition, ask whether there remains any non-arbitrary original state to serve as the benchmark for identity, and examine whether debates over hidden steel reinforcements versus visible traditional craftsmanship turn on ontology at all. Third, argue what the episode demonstrates about the paradox itself—whether it reveals that the puzzle is not about the persistence conditions of artifacts but about competing normative commitments to authenticity, heritage, and collective memory, and whether an architecture that has been continuously repaired for eight centuries was ever a static object to begin with.
