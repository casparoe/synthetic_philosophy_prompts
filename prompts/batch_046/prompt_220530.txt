I am restructuring the middle section of my value-theory course, and in a recent faculty workshop my colleagues and I disagreed about whether students genuinely grasp the Epicurean argument or merely memorize its conclusion. To test this in writing rather than in discussion, I want you to undertake a comparative evaluation of five distinct objections to Epicurus's thesis that death is not harmful to the one who dies. The five are these: the symmetry argument, which appeals to our apparent indifference toward pre-natal non-existence; the deprivation account, which treats death as bad because it eliminates goods that would otherwise have been enjoyed; the posthumous-harm argument, which claims that death can frustrate projects or damage reputation after the fact; the rational-fear objection, which maintains that one can coherently dread death without assuming it involves suffering; and a view I am tentatively calling the interval-asymmetry thesis, according to which the axiological weight of being deprived of future goods depends not merely on the magnitude of those goods but on whether the deprivation occurs within or outside the temporal boundaries of the subject's lived interval, so that post-mortem deprivation may differ in kind rather than only in degree from deprivation during life.

Please rank these five responses along two dimensions in a single flowing analysis: first, by philosophical seriousness, meaning the extent to which each genuinely undermines Epicurus's inference from non-experience to non-harm rather than merely registering an emotional resistance; and second, by pedagogical utility for advanced undergraduates who have already grasped the basic symmetry intuition but confuse axiological loss with experiential pain. Assign each a relative position on both dimensions, justify the pairing in continuous prose, and say explicitly where you think the interval-asymmetry thesis is being undervalued in standard treatments. Write everything as uninterrupted prose, avoiding headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter, and do not include any bibliographic references or suggested readings.
