I'm not trained in philosophy, but I've been working through Van Fraassen's reflection principle on my own — the claim that if you now expect your future, rational self to hold some specific credence in a proposition, then you ought to hold that same credence now, at least under conditions of rational self-trust. In the popular accounts I've encountered, this gets likened to a ship's captain leaving sealed orders for himself: he writes instructions in advance because he anticipates that future conditions — fatigue, panic, distorted perception — might corrupt his judgment, and he wants to bind his future self to the course he would have chosen with a clear head. The analogy is often used to defend the principle: if you genuinely trust your future captain more after he has seen the horizon, shouldn't you defer to him already?

But I've been adding a twist that the popular versions don't address. Suppose the future "captain" isn't just you with more direct evidence, but you after consulting an uninspectable black-box predictive oracle — a system whose outputs you will accept but whose reasoning you can never audit. Then the situation looks less like sealed personal orders and more like a present board of directors locking in a delegation rule to a future board that will rely on an algorithm none of them understand.

Can you audit how far the sealed-orders analogy actually holds for the reflection principle? Give me the correspondence table — present self, future self, the sealed orders, the conditions that might corrupt judgment, the ship's course — and then the places where the mapping breaks: where does the captain retain autonomy that a rational agent updating on evidence lacks? Where does binding yourself now actually alter what evidence is available later? And what does the analogy quietly import about authority versus rational belief revision?

Then, address the oracle twist specifically. Does injecting an uninspectable future source of belief break the analogy further? Does it undermine the case for present deference, or does it just reveal that reflection requires transparency about why the future credence takes the value it does?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that anticipated future belief change should move your present credence — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the captain analogy was carrying the argument all along. If there is a better analogy for the principle, especially one that can accommodate the oracle case without collapsing, I'd like to hear it.
