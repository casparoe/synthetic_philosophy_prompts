For a philosophy-of-mind reading group focused on evolutionary and computational accounts of cognition, I want a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—of positions on whether competence can exist without comprehension, taking Darwin's evolutionary inversion and Turing's behavioral inversion as the two organizing axes.

The tree should be built from logical branches rather than from a list of named theories. Each split should be a concrete claim or case judgment on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

Specifically, include these branching questions in this order:

First branch: On origins—can a system develop a genuinely adaptive, problem-solving competence through a selection process in which no stage involves comprehension of the target competence? This captures Darwin's inversion: designed-looking competence without a designing mind.

Second branch: On exercise—does the successful deployment of that competence require an internal state of understanding, such that removing that internal state would reduce the behavior to mere mechanical simulation rather than genuine reasoning?

Third branch: On scope—does the absence of required comprehension apply symmetrically to biological organisms and to artificial systems, or does one domain retain a special status?

The leaves must include at least the following positions, mapped onto the combinations of answers: (1) the view that rejects both inversions, holding that comprehension is necessary both for the origin and for the exercise of reason; (2) the Darwin-only view, accepting evolutionary origin without prior comprehension but insisting that biological exercise involves real understanding and that artificial systems lack it; (3) the Turing-only view, accepting that artificial systems can display reasoning-behavior without internal comprehension but maintaining that biological reason genuinely involves comprehension that was not itself blindly evolved; (4) the double-inversion view accepting both; (5) the emergentist view that blind origins necessarily produce comprehension at the exercise stage; and (6) one combination of answers that appears unoccupied in your tree, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment—the consequence that defenders of that view must accept but that critics find hardest to swallow.

Finally, include a separation report: identify any pair of leaves that your three-branch tree cannot genuinely distinguish, explain whether the indistinguishability is due to the questions being too coarse or to the positions being substantively identical on these dimensions, and note whether adding a fourth branch—for instance, on whether comprehension, when present, is causally efficacious or epiphenomenal—would resolve the ambiguity or merely relocate it.

The deliverable should be the tree itself—branch labels, leaf positions with awkward commitments, and the separation report—not a prose essay comparing the views. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers.
