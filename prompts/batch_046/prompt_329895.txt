I am running an upper-level undergraduate seminar on New Testament historical methodology—ten students, one ninety-minute session, with no prior lecture that day—where I want participants to feel the circularity and self-defeating structure of authenticity criteria before I ever name them. Design a structured, rule-bound classroom activity, part classification exercise and part role-play, that forces the group to build a collective “historical Jesus” profile using only four explicit filters (embarrassment, multiple attestation, dissimilarity to both Judaism and the early church, and internal coherence), applied to a curated set of twelve brief sayings and actions drawn from canonical texts, Thomas, and reconstructed background material. Build in a hidden structural tension: include at least one saying that survives every filter except coherence, yet coherence can only be assessed once a preliminary profile is already adopted; and include one saying that passes dissimilarity with perfect scores—describing an itinerant Cynic-style philosopher speaking Greek in Galilee—but is historically implausible in first-century Judea, so the group must choose between honoring the criterion and honoring historical context. Assign specific roles—criterion defenders, a skeptic who may veto any application, and a recorder—so that by minute sixty the collective profile is either empty or contains an incoherent outlier, and the students have experienced the squeeze themselves rather than being told about it.

In the debrief, map each crisis moment onto the methodological debate: show where coherence smuggled prior assumptions, where double dissimilarity produced an un-Jewish and un-Christian anomaly, and where embarrassment and attestation conflicted on the same text. Be explicit about what the exercise cannot reproduce—specifically, the irreducible role of prior historical imagination and plausibility judgments that scholars use to break ties when criteria conflict—and tell me exactly what I should supply verbally in the final five minutes. Also predict what will go wrong the first time I run it, such as students redefining criteria to save favored sayings or the skeptic monopolizing debate, and explain how to handle it without breaking the frame. The rules, incentives, and roles—not any opening explanation—must make the problem appear, and the group should not hear the phrase “criteria of authenticity” until the debrief begins. Write in American English, lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning and design details with no preamble and no restatement of the request.
