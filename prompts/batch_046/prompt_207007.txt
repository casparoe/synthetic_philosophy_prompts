I've been working out my position on historical objectivity for a graduate seminar, and I want a consistency check before I present it. I'm drawn to what look like incompatible strands. First, I defend a modified Rankean regulative ideal: historians should aim to represent the past as it actually was—wie es eigentlich gewesen—not as a claim of unmediated access, but as an aspirational constraint that demands we minimize presentist distortion and resist anachronistic projection. Second, I fully accept Carr's argument that historical facts are never self-interpreting; selection, causal weighting, and the assignment of significance are always mediated by the historian's present concerns and values. Third, I take Hayden White's tropological analysis seriously: I believe the master narrative structures he describes—romance, tragedy, comedy, satire—are not stylistic choices applied after evidence is assembled, but pre-cognitive frames that determine which archival traces become visible as historically significant in the first place.

Despite these constructivist commitments, I maintain that some narratives are genuinely better than others—not merely more useful or elegant, but more faithful to the available record. I think the appropriate remedy for bias is not false neutrality but reflexive transparency: historians should explicitly disclose their tropological framing, presentist interests, and what I call negative archival spaces—systematic absences in the record produced by non-literate or suppressed groups—which I treat as integral constraints on permissible narrative construction rather than as supplementary correctives. At the same time, I deny that transparency solves everything: even after full disclosure, I see no rational criterion for preferring one transparent, evidence-compatible narrative over another that employs a different master trope and assigns different weights to the same traces.
