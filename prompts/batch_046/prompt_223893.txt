I am designing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that has worked through Daniel Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Rather than allowing students to answer by summarizing the gravitational metaphor or rehearsing the distinction between the self as an object and the self as a theoretical fiction, I want a prompt that forces them to apply the framework to an unfamiliar case and to see where the view's commitments actually bite.

To that end, please construct an essay question suitable for a 75-minute exam with an expected response of roughly 900 words, centered on a hypothetical cognitive architecture I will call a polycentric narrative engine. In this system, several independent subsystems each generate first-person autobiographical logs according to different evaluative frameworks, but a higher-order coherence protocol selects, revises, and binds these logs into one continuously updated autobiographical thread that governs self-reference and action. The question should ask whether this architecture possesses a self in Dennett's sense, and it should require students to address three specific tensions: whether narrative gravity demands a singular functional locus or can be sustained by genuinely distributed origins; whether the coherence protocol is itself the center of gravity or merely produces the appearance of one; and whether Dennett's view permits a self that is causally plural in origin yet phenomenologically and behaviorally singular. Please provide the essay question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme that distributes 20 marks across five conceptual moves an excellent answer must contain, with each entry listing the point value and a brief note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks.
