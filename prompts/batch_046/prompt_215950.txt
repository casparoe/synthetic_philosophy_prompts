Two philosophy graduate students, Mira and Leo, are arguing about whether deflationary theories of truth can survive what they call retroactive correction—cases in which one says, “What I said yesterday was true, but I meant something different by my terms then”—and cross-contextual endorsement, such as claiming “The ancient astronomer said something true.”

(1) Mira argues that deflationism handles retroactive correction easily: the claim disquotes to the content of yesterday’s utterance interpreted with yesterday’s meanings. There is no mysterious property being corrected; only the disquotational pair we apply shifts with the context of evaluation.

(2) Leo replies that this misses the point. We do not merely correct content; we preserve the idea that there was a fact of the matter about whether the original claim was correct, independent of our current interpretation. Deflationism collapses truth into present linguistic practice, so it cannot explain why we might think we misinterpreted our own past meanings rather than misrepresenting the world.

(3) Mira responds that Leo is smuggling in a correspondence intuition. The “fact of the matter” about correctness is just conformity to the norms of the practice then in force. Deflationism does not collapse truth into current practice; the schema applies relative to the context of evaluation.

(4) Leo then raises indirect endorsement across contexts. If we apply the schema with our current meanings to “The ancient astronomer said something true,” we might derive that the sun orbits Earth—false. If we apply it with the astronomer’s meanings, we need a substantive account of what fixed those meanings, which requires more than bare disquotation. So deflationism either yields the wrong result or secretly relies on hidden semantic machinery.

(5) Mira counters that interpretation is a separate task. The deflationist can borrow standard historical and linguistic resources to recover past meanings; truth remains disquotational, and interpretation does the rest.

(6) Leo concludes that truth then does no explanatory work in cross-temporal evaluation. We explain why the astronomer was right by appealing to interpretation plus how the world was (as conceived then). The deflationist borrows all that labor and adds only a redundant “is true” label. Hence deflationism is either incomplete or redundant.
