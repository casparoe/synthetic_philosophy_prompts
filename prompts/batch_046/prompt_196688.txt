As a journalist who covers the philosophy and conceptual history of science, I have found myself drawn to a particular defense of scientific realism against the pessimistic meta-induction—one that does not deny the long record of abandoned ontologies, but reconstrues it through what I am calling explanatory-debt inheritance. On this view, when a mature scientific theory is superseded, its successful explanatory obligations are not erased; rather, they are inherited by successor frameworks in a transformed currency—whether structural relations, predictive regularities, or causal capacities—so that the realist was never simply wrong about there being a real world that answered to those obligations, but only wrong about the specific ontological denomination in which the debt was originally expressed. I am treating this inheritance model as a live working position, yet I want an honest assessment of its vulnerability rather than a rehearsal of familiar objections.

What, precisely, would have to be true for this defense to fail? I am asking for the defeaters ranked by severity: which historical patterns—say, cases where a successor theory explains the same phenomena without inheriting any recognizable explanatory obligation from its predecessor—would be fatal, and which would be merely awkward adjustments? Which conceptual results, perhaps in the study of scientific change or in the philosophy of mathematics, might show that the continuity of explanatory structure required by inheritance is an illusion? And crucially, which of these vulnerabilities would committed defenders of the inheritance model themselves concede as genuinely damaging, as opposed to misunderstandings they would dismiss? I want to know whether the position risks anything empirically or conceptually, or whether it has been insulated by qualifications to the point of unfalsifiability. If it is indeed well insulated, is that insulation a methodological virtue or an intellectual vice?
