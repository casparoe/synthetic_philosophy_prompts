I am working on a historical novel set among the merchant-bankers of 1620s Amsterdam, and in my research I have been trying to understand whether a particular religious temperament can genuinely drive economic form, or whether I am committing the novelist’s fallacy of making belief do work that only institutions can do. At a writers’ residency last winter, a historian friend summarized Max Weber’s thesis on the Protestant ethic for me in terms I found both compelling and suspiciously neat. He said, essentially, that Weber had demonstrated Calvinist predestination doctrine was the primary engine of early modern capitalism, because the psychological terror of not knowing one’s election drove believers to treat worldly success as a visible sign of grace, which generated what he called an ascetic feedback loop: capital accumulation became not merely permitted but a religious duty, rationalizing labor and reinvestment. From this my friend derived what he termed Weber’s law of elective causation, according to which cultural values, once they reach sufficient intensity, can independently override material conditions, so that explaining capitalism’s rise in Northern Europe requires little more than mapping the density of Reformed congregations and treating economic structures as essentially secondary.

I suspect this account conflates several distinctions Weber was careful to maintain—between the spirit of capitalism and capitalism itself, between elective affinity and causal law, and between ideas directing interests versus ideas replacing them—but I am not certain where the simplifications end and the misrepresentations begin. Could you audit this summary with precision? I want you to take a definite position: is the summary salvageable as a popular gloss, or is it fundamentally unfair to Weber? In giving your answer, please separate what is accurate, what is a standard but acceptable simplification, and what is a distortion a defender of Weber’s view would reject. I am especially interested in whether my friend’s conclusion—that cultural values can independently override economic structures—follows from anything Weber actually argued, or whether it inverts his actual caution. Do not lay out options for me to choose between; defend a clear verdict, and tell me whether, if I used this summary to structure my novel’s portrayal of belief and commerce, I would be building on sand.
