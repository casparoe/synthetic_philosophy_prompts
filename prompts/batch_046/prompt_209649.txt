Next month I present to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-science and history seminar my argument that Popper’s distinction between historical trends and scientific laws in The Poverty of Historicism rests on an unstated asymmetry. My thesis is that Popper disqualifies social and demographic patterns from law-status by appealing to their dependence on contingent initial conditions and to the possibility of counterfactual disruption—yet he never subjects the boundary conditions of physical laws to the same counterfactual-stability test, even though those conditions are equally contingent and equally vulnerable to disruption in nearby possible worlds. I do not claim that history is predictable; I claim only that Popper’s criterion for lawhood, if applied consistently, either collapses the law-trend boundary downward or forces an ad hoc exemption for physics. The audience includes two scholars who have worked extensively on Popper’s evolutionary epistemology and its relation to his anti-historicism, several historians of science who are skeptical of philosophy-of-science imperialism over disciplinary method, and a formal methodologist who specializes in symmetry arguments in theory comparison. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those specific attendees would phrase them: the evolutionary-epistemology objections first, then the historians’, then the methodologist’s. For each, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the single weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, whether my symmetry argument accidentally rehabilitates historicism by leveling the law-trend boundary downward rather than upward, and if so, whether I have any non-ad-hoc way to stop the slide. Keep the reply practical for a forty-minute talk with fifteen minutes of questions, and do not ask for bibliographies or further reading lists.
