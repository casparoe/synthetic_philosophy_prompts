I am a high school student in an elective metaphysics course, and I am reporting an argument that unfolded between two classmates—Jordan and Riley—after we read about Leibniz’s identity of indiscernibles and the well-known counterexample involving two perfectly symmetrical spheres in an otherwise empty universe. I was present but took no side; they have asked me to present the exchange neutrally and have it adjudicated. The disagreement developed as follows.

First, Jordan argued that the spheres share every intrinsic property—size, color, density, internal structure—yet occupy different locations, which proves they are two objects rather than one. From this, Jordan concluded that Leibniz’s principle is false: complete qualitative identity does not guarantee numerical identity.

Second, Riley replied that spatial separation is itself a relational property. Sphere A stands two meters from Sphere B, and Sphere B stands two meters from Sphere A. Because these relational descriptions differ, the spheres are not truly indiscernible, and the principle survives intact.

Third, Jordan countered that this response is circular. To invoke a relation between the spheres, Riley must already treat them as two distinct relata. If the very question is whether there is one sphere or two, one cannot answer it by appealing to relations that presuppose duality.

Fourth, Riley insisted that this is not circular but constitutive of spatial reality. Even in a universe containing nothing else, distance is a genuine feature of the world, not a logical artifact. The principle was never intended to erase all relational differences; it applies only to objects that share every property, relational ones included.

Fifth, Jordan introduced an extension: imagine two perfect digital copies of a single program running on isolated servers, with identical internal states, no causal interaction, and no meaningful physical distance that functions as a spatial relation. By Riley’s reasoning, if every relational and intrinsic property is shared, they should be numerically identical—yet we naturally treat them as two instances. Jordan asked whether this shows that the principle either fails for abstract or functional objects, or that location and instantiation are doing hidden metaphysical work.

Sixth, Riley responded that true isolation is never achieved. The copies differ in substrate—the hardware on which they run—and in their causal histories. If one genuinely stripped away every difference, including substrate and environment, there would be only one program. Thus the principle remains valid but reveals that complete indiscernibility is practically impossible, not that it permits distinct duplicates.

Seventh, Jordan concluded that this makes the principle trivial. Black designed the spheres precisely to strip away every feature except spatial separation. If spatial separation counts as a distinguishing property, then Black’s counterexample collapses—but only because “property” has been expanded so broadly that the principle becomes unfalsifiable. It reduces to the claim that objects are identical only when there is literally nothing different about them, including where they sit, which Jordan said is a restatement of identity rather than a substantive metaphysical law.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the seven points above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is more right about the central question—whether the identity of indiscernibles survives Black-style counterexamples once relational properties and instantiation are fully accounted for—and identify anything important that both Jordan and Riley overlook. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
