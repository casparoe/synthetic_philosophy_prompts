A philosopher friend hit me with this chain and it feels like it proves theoretical physics is just fancy storytelling, which can't be right. He frames it with something he calls the Modal Access Condition (MAC):

(1) MAC: a scientific thought experiment yields knowledge about nature only if the scenario is modally close enough that we could observe its outcome without destroying the conditions that make it informative.
(2) Galileo's tied-bodies argument needs perfect vacuum and simultaneous release; but observing that exactly means removing air resistance, which is precisely the factor that makes heavy and light bodies behave differently in our world. So the scenario is modally distant.
(3) So Galileo didn't learn anything about falling bodies from pure reasoning; he just resolved a contradiction in his existing beliefs.
(4) Einstein's 1907 elevator needs a perfectly homogeneous gravitational field with no tidal forces. Any box big enough for a human to stand in will detect tidal forces, so we can't observe the equivalence intact. Modally distant again.
(5) But apply MAC consistently: every mechanistic explanation in physics — point charges, infinite heat baths, absolute zero — appeals to indepandant conditions we can't observe intact without breaking them. So if MAC holds, theoretical explanation is impossible, and physics is just phenomenal curve-fitting.
(6) That's clearly wrong; we treat GR and statistical mechanics as explaining how nature actually works, not just describing patterns.
(7) Therefore MAC is false.

My friend insists the leak is in (4), not (1): he says Einstein's case is different because tidal forces are measurable corrections, so there's an empirical check after all, and Galileo could have tied bodies together in a real tower. I'm skeptical — if you save MAC by letting in approximate checks, don't you have to save Galileo too? Where should I actually pull the thread? Which step do I reject, and what does giving it up cost me?
