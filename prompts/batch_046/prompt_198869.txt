First, if neurosceintists show that our sense of fairness is just a byproduct of reciprocal altruism genes, I don't think that debunks fairness — explaining where a belief comes from isn't the same as refuting it. Second, when I look at a culture that practices ritual scarifcation and outsiders call it barbaric, I feel like there's no culture-neutral court of appeal — it's wrong for them only inside their own framework, not objectively. But then, third, I also want to say that slavery was always wrong even when everyone believed it was okay, which sounds like there IS a fact of the matter that transcends belief. Fourth, I reject the idea that moral beliefs are like optical illusions — useful errors — because they don't seem like errors at all, just part of how we navigate the world. Fifth, two neighboring societies disagree about whether honoring dead ancestors requires burning material goods; neither seems mistaken, just different.

So there's a tension between my second and fifth verdicts (no neutral standpoint) and my third (slavery was wrong regardless). Is there a Quinean principle — maybe something about ontological commitment being relative to conceptual schemes but still allowing internal criticism — that saves all five? Or do I have to drop one?
