The room is split between her old students, some of whom actually read philosophy for fun, and our family, who mostly just want to laugh and maybe cry without feeling like they're in a classroom. I want to use Émilie du Châtelet's thinking about happiness to explain why my aunt was genuinely happy in her work, but I need the idea to actually do structural work in the speech — not just be a fancy decoration. Specifically, du Châtelet argued that real happiness isn't passive pleasure or being morally perfect, but being totally absorbed in difficult study, and that this kind of happiness is especially important for people who do hard intellectual work without getting famous for it. She herself was Voltaire's partner and lived in his shadow, yet she insisted that study was its own reward and protected you from boredom and from needing other people's approval. That feels exactly right for my aunt, who never won the big awards her male colleagues recieved, but I don't want the speech to turn into a biography of du Châtelet or a lecture.
