I have been thinking on my own about what I call two strange inversions in reasoning, one I associate with Darwin and one with Turing, and I have sketched some notes towards a short essay. I do not have an academic background, so I may be misusing terms or repeating ideas that are already well worked out in philosophy of science.

Darwin's inversion, as I understand it, is that he explains how nature produces structures that look precisely designed — eyes, wings, complex organs — without any designer possessing comprehension of what is being built. The competence of adaptation is present, but the comprehension is absent; natural selection builds without knowing.

Turing's inversion seems to run in the opposite direction. He takes a mechanism that clearly lacks comprehension — a machine following formal rules — and shows that it can produce behaviour indistinguishable from the kind of behaviour we normally attribute to understanding minds. Here, comprehension is apparently present in the output, but absent in the mechanism.

In my notes I propose that these two inversions are not merely opposite, but structurally related: both show that the link between competence and comprehension is looser than common sense assumes. Darwin severs the link downward (no mind needed for design-like results); Turing severs it upward (no mind needed for mind-like results).

I then introduce what I call a "diagonal" case — large language models. They are engineered like Turing machines, yet trained through processes that resemble evolutionary selection (retaining outputs that score well against a distribution). This makes them sit awkwardly between the two inversions. We are tempted to ask whether they "really understand", but perhaps that question already assumes the link between competence and comprehension that both Darwin and Turing undermined.

My specific worry is that this framing is either obvious or incoherent. In particular, I wonder whether I am conflating Darwin's actual argument with later popular versions of it, and whether I am treating Turing's behavioural criterion as if it were a claim about consciousness rather than about functional equivalence.

Could you comment on these notes? I am particularly interested in:
- whether the framing of the two inversions is accurate, or whether I have distorted either thinker;
- whether the proposed "diagonal" case of language models adds anything, or merely confuses evolutionary training with evolutionary biology;
- which part of the reasoning is weakest.

Please also indicate how confident you are in your assessment overall, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
