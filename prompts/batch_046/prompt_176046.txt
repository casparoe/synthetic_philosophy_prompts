As a journalist covering science and ideas, I routinely use AI tools to summarize studies, check statistical claims, and draft background on complex debates. Philosophers distinguish epistemic dependence—treating an AI's judgment as authoritative—from mere cognitive offloading, and they worry that dependence erodes understanding when it substitutes for grasping mechanisms, arguments, or conceptual distinctions. Write a decision procedure I can run in under fifteen minutes before filing, to determine whether my use of an AI in a specific reporting task undermines my own understanding of the claim I am about to publish, and therefore violates my obligation to understand what I report. The procedure should take the form of ordered tests that distinguish data extraction (numbers, citation counts, dates) from interpretation (causal explanations, methodological classifications, philosophical objections). Include explicit criteria for when understanding requires grasping a mechanism or argument rather than knowing a fact, flag the cases where judgment cannot be eliminated, and specify the corrective action required—seeking a human expert, reading the primary source, or removing the interpretation from the story. Apply it to three cases: extracting effect sizes from a clinical trial report; asking an AI to classify whether a study counts as a replication under current norms; and relying on an AI to summarize the central objection to a theory of consciousness I am profiling. In at least one case, the procedure should recommend an action I will find inconvenient.
