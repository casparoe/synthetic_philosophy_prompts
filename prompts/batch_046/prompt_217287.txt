I'm filing a feature for an ideas magazine about a little D.C. shop called The Fence Project that applies Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence to every reform proposal that lands on their desk. I sat in on five of their review sessions and now they're staring at a sixth; I want to guess their call, but also figure out whether there's actually one rule running through all of it or if the track record is genuinely ambiguous.

Here are the precedants they've set so far:

1. They fought hard to keep the Electoral College, saying its hidden geographic-coalition function wasn't fully mapped and that tearing it down risked unknown destabilization.

2. They endorsed killing a 1980s tax "sunset" experiment that was designed as an explicit rationalist trial — they called it an engineered fence, not an evolved one, and it had clearly failed.

3. They opposed ditching a 19th-century common-law pollution doctrine even though modern EPA rules cover the same ground, arguing the old rule probably serves hidden mediating functions between neighbors that the statute doesn't replicate.

4. They backed tearing down a 1950s zoning ordinance that banned corner groceries in residential zones, because its origin was a specific racist segregation tactic that was now dead, leaving no hidden purpose they could see.

5. They fought to preserve university tenure, viewing it as an organically evolved shield for dissent, though they admitted they couldn't catolog every protective mechanism it provides.

Now the new proposal: a bill to abolish the federal grand-jury requirement for felony indictments and replace it with direct prosecutorial filing, like in most European systems. The grand jury was imported from English common law specifically as a shield against Crown overreach — that threat is obviously dead. But defenders say it has evolved into a local community-oversight mechanism, giving ordinary citizens direct visibility into charging decisions.

What should The Fence Project decide if they want to stay consistent with themselves? Which of the five above is the closest analouge? If their past rulings genuinely underdetermine the answer, lay out the competing principles — say, a strict Chesterton "know the original reason and confirm it's dead" rule versus a Burkean "preserve organically evolved institutions regardless of origin" rule — and tell me what each predicts here. Also name the further case that would best split those two rules.

Please end with a one-line bottom line.
