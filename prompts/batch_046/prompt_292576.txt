I recently joined the clinical ethics committee at my hospital after being drawn into a crisis allocation case that left me unsettled: we had one available life-support unit and two patients with nearly identical prognoses, and I realized our deliberation kept sliding between describing one outcome as allowing death and describing another as causing it. I need a reusable decision procedure that my committee could actually run in about thirty minutes during an emergency call, grounded in the philosophical distinction between doing and allowing and between killing and letting die, but stripped of unnecessary metaphysical machinery about causation. The procedure should present the questions to ask in sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, mark precisely where the distinction collapses or becomes a value judgment rather than a factual determination, and indicate where it must stop and hand the decision to a human rather than delivering a mechanical answer. It must survive hard cases: actively withdrawing a ventilator that is already sustaining a patient versus withholding it from a patient who has never received it; a scenario in which stopping support causes death within minutes while never initiating it causes death over days; and a family demand for continued treatment that clinicians consider futile, where the label of futility might conceal an omission rather than justify one. Please apply the procedure to two specific proposals—withdrawal of dialysis from a patient already on it with multi-organ failure, and refusal to initiate dialysis for a patient with an identical clinical profile who has never started—and include one worked case in which the procedure yields an answer I will find unacceptable. Finally, state clearly what categories of moral dispute this framework is not designed to resolve.
