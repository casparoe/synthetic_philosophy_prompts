I am preparing a graduate seminar on moral absolutism in twentieth-century analytic philosophy and have been working through G. E. M. Anscombe's protest in Mr. Truman's Degree against Oxford's decision to honor Harry Truman. To test whether her doctrine of double effect can be applied consistently without collapsing every collective decision into murder, a study group proposed the following chain of reasoning, which seems valid yet yields a conclusion I find unacceptable.

(1) Anscombe holds that to choose to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder, regardless of consequential gains. (2) She further maintains that killing the innocent with statistical certainty is not necessarily murder, provided the agent is not "unscrupulous in considering the possibilities"—that is, provided there is no culpable neglect of available alternatives that could avoid or minimize the harm. (3) Applying this to Hiroshima, she argues that Truman was not merely accepting an unavoidable statistical risk; rather, the civilian deaths were the direct mechanism of coercing surrender, and he was unscrupulous in neglecting available alternatives such as pursuing a negotiated peace or delivering an ultimatum before the second bombing. (4) Therefore, on her account, Truman intentionally killed innocents and was guilty of murder rather than of permissible warfare with tragic side-effects. (5) Now generalize the criterion in (2): if an agent performs a large-scale action knowing with near-certainty that a specific, predictable number of innocents will suffer lethal harm, and if complete elimination of that risk is practically impossible but some further reduction might be achievable at a cost to efficiency or speed, then—unless the agent has achieved perfect scrupulousness—the harm falls within the scope of what is intended rather than merely foreseen. (6) Apply this to any complex collective policy, such as a public-health mandate or an economic sanction, where decision-makers know that innocent deaths are statistically inevitable and where absolute risk elimination is unattainable. (7) By (5), those decision-makers have chosen the deaths as a means, since they have not satisfied the impossible standard of perfect scrupulousness. (8) By (1), they are therefore guilty of murder. (9) But this proves too much: it implies that virtually all state action is murderous, it erases the very distinction Anscombe wishes to preserve between Hiroshima and genuinely incidental collateral damage, and it renders her framework useless for practical moral judgment because no human agent can meet the standard required to escape the charge.

Please tell me which premise or step is weakest, and why it initially seemed defensible. In particular, explain what defenders of Anscombe's strict reading of intention must concede to block the inference from (5) to (7), what a proportionalist critic who rejects her moral absolutism must say about (2) and (3), and what a philosopher who wishes to preserve the metaphysics of intention but reject the moral conclusion must pay to deny (5) rather than (1).
