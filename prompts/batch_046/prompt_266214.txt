I've been thinking on my own about whether we owe it to the past to remember terrible things, and whether forgetting can sometimes be a duty rather than a failure. Below is the shape of my view. I want to know if these pieces actually fit together or if I'm holding contradictory positions without realizing it.

First, I believe that grave wrongs in history create a lasting obligation to keep them acknowledged, an obligation that does not vanish when everyone involved has died. The wrong itself seems to demand recognition, regardless of whether remembering does anyone any good today.

Second, I believe that people who actually suffered those wrongs have a claim against being turned into permanent public symbols. Having one's suffering constantly displayed—used as a monument or a lesson—can deny that person ownership over their own story. Those affected should be able to ask that society stop invoking their specific experience in public rituals.

Third, I believe that a community can legitimately decide, through fair collective deliberation, to stop commemorating a particular event if they genuinely conclude that remembrance now causes more present harm—deep division, renewed bitterness—than moral understanding.

Fourth, I believe that actively removing the visible traces of a painful legacy—renaming buildings, taking down statues—is often more respectful than leaving them standing. Keeping them up feels like leaving a wound open; healing requires erasing the glorifying presence, though not destroying the historical record kept in archives.

Fifth, I believe that true forgetting is impossible. Brains do not erase memories, and archives do not disappear. So any ethical claim about a "right to be forgotten" must really be a claim about social silence—the absence of public display and constant mention—not about actual erasure.

Sixth, I believe the best reason to remember is practical: remembrance should make us more just and more alert to present wrongs. If remembering instead breeds cruelty, moral vanity, or social fracture, then the reason for maintaining it falls away.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and for each real tension how severe it is. I especially want you to check whether my first and sixth commitments are running on different foundations—one saying we owe remembrance to the past itself, the other saying we only remember if it helps the present—and whether that undermines both. I also want you to examine whether my second commitment, about victims not being turned into symbols, conflicts with my fourth, about removing visible markers, once you separate displaying people from displaying events. And please tell me whether my third commitment can survive alongside my first: if there is an enduring obligation that does not depend on present effects, how can a democratic decision to stop commemorating be legitimate?

Order the tensions by severity, suggest the cheapest revision for each, and say what that revision costs. Then tell me which single change would resolve the most conflicts at once, and whether it is a change I should actually want to make. Do not treat this as a lecture on memory ethics in general; treat it as an audit of my specific set of beliefs.
