As a novelist drafting a scene in which a philosopher-turned-cabinetmaker confesses to a priest that he knew the whiskey would ruin him yet reached for the bottle anyway, I keep returning to the old Socratic one-liner that no one does wrong knowingly, the idea usually compressed into the claim that all vice is ignorance and that virtue is knowledge. In my draft the priest uses it to absolve the penitent—suggesting that if he truly knew, he could not have sinned—while the cabinetmaker insists that he knew exactly what he was doing and simply failed to care about tomorrow, as if his present self were bargaining with a stranger. I want to understand what precise theses that slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, and which of those readings would actually support the priest's absolution or the cabinetmaker's accusation that hyperbolic discounting makes a mockery of Socratic optimism. Could you lay out the different interpretations as a continuous menu—perhaps distinguishing whether knowing means propositional awareness, emotional appreciation, or procedural mastery; whether willingly requires absence of compulsion, alignment with one's all-things-considered judgment, or mere absence of external coercion; and whether the claim is meant as a conceptual truth about action, an empirical generalization about human psychology, or a normative ideal about rational agency—so that I can see which versions survive the counterexample of a present-biased agent who accurately predicts his future regret yet still chooses the smaller-sooner reward? In particular, please address how hyperbolic discounting relates to each reading: does it show that agents act with full propositional knowledge against their better judgment, thereby refuting the strong Socratic thesis, or can a defender of the slogan absorb present bias by arguing that the agent's knowledge of future harm is somehow defective, incomplete, or not truly his? I ask this because the scene turns on whether weakness of will is possible at all, and I need the philosophical terrain mapped without being told to read particular texts or scholars. Please present your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, the way one writes a careful letter, and please say how confident you are in each part of your analysis, explicitly identifying the point about which you are least certain—perhaps whether hyperbolic discounting genuinely undermines the normative reading of the slogan or merely complicates it—so that I know where the ground is firmest and where I should treat your account as speculative.
