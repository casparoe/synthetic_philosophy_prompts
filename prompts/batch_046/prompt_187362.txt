Two colleagues in my philosophy reading group—call them Alex and Jordan—got stuck in an argument about whether species membership can carry direct moral weight, and I’d like an outside adjudication. Alex opened by defending what he called a modal version of speciesism: we do not ground moral status in actually exercised rationality, since infants and severely cognitively disabled humans lack it yet retain full standing; rather, status attaches to belonging to a kind whose normal members develop rationality. Species membership, on this view, tracks counterfactual potential, not present capacity, so it is neither arbitrary nor reducible to actual traits. Jordan replied that this collapses into an indirect rule about kinds—functionally a form of rule-utilitarianism dressed in metaphysical clothing—and that it forces an uncomfortable bullet: an anencephalic human who lacks even counterfactual potential for rationality must, on Alex’s account, fall outside full moral protection, unless one concedes an ad hoc exception. Alex countered that every normative framework has edge cases, and that the existence of boundary conditions does not defeat a kind-based rule that governs the central, paradigmatic instances. Jordan then introduced a reverse marginal case: imagine a genetically engineered dolphin that possesses stable human-level rationality, self-awareness, and linguistic competence, yet remains biologically a dolphin. By Alex’s modal criterion, this being belongs to a kind—Tursiops truncatus—whose normal members never develop such capacities, so it should lack the protected status of humans; yet intuitively it merits exactly the same consideration as a rational human. Alex responded that Jordan was smuggling in individual-capacity intuitions that the modal view explicitly rejects—moral status is kind-relative, not a function of actual individual properties, so the dolphin’s enhanced traits are morally irrelevant to its kind-based standing. Jordan’s final move was that if the view must abandon kind-relativity whenever individual capacities diverge sharply—as they do with the enhanced dolphin—then species membership is not doing any independent work; the underlying capacity is, and the modal wrapper is merely decorative.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing—whether a hidden premise, an unconsidered distinction, or a third position that dissolves the framing—and explain whether the reverse-marginal-case strategy genuinely undermines modal speciesism or simply changes the subject from kind-norms to individual assessment.
