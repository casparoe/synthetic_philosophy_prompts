I have been working through an extension of Zhuangzi's butterfly dream that my philosophy discussion group finds more disturbing than the original parable, and I want to see exactly where the reasoning breaks down. The argument runs as follows. (1) When Zhuangzi awakens from dreaming he is a butterfly, he possesses no internal criterion by which he can determine whether he is now Zhuangzi who previously dreamed, or a butterfly who is presently dreaming that it is Zhuangzi. (2) If a subject genuinely lacks any first-person criterion to discriminate being entity X from being entity Y, then there is no metaphysical fact of the matter about which entity that subject is; here epistemic inaccessibility tracks ontological indeterminacy, at least for properties that must be transparent to the subject who possesses them. (3) This reasoning applies well beyond dreams: it covers any phenomenally continuous transition—deep sleep, general anesthesia, or even the imperceptible cellular turnover of the body—where the hosting substrate might have been replaced or altered without leaving an internal trace. (4) I now have no internal calibration signal that confirms I am the same persisting being who began this very sentence rather than a freshly instantiated experiential subject inheriting false memories and physical continuity. (5) Therefore, if we apply the diachronic transparency principle consistently—that identity across time must be at least in principle verifiable from within the stream of experience—then there are no persisting persons at all, only a succession of momentary experiencers with no real diachronic connection. The conclusion dissolves personal identity entirely, which is clearly too much to accept.

Which step should I reject? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1), since the butterfly case genuinely seems underdetermined from the first-person view, and I also want to preserve (3), because treating physical continuity as sufficient without any experiential link seems to abandon the intuition that identity matters to the subject experiencing it. Please identify the flawed premise or hidden slide—whether it is an equivocation between epistemic and metaphysical claims, an unjustified generalization from dream states to waking continuity, or a misapplication of indexical reference across temporal gaps—and explain why that step only looked plausible. Then discuss what giving it up costs: does it commit us to an externalist account of personal identity that ignores the first-person perspective, or does it force us to accept that some metaphysical facts are genuinely opaque to the subjects they concern?
