Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who defends a rigorous interpretation of the doctrine of double effect (DDE), and Dr. Marcus Chen, who grants the doctrine intuitive weight but contends that it becomes normatively unstable under conditions of deep causal uncertainty—debating whether to authorize an autonomous climate-intervention system. The system is designed to release stratospheric aerosols with the sole intended effect of reducing global mean temperature; however, due to non-linear atmospheric dynamics mediated by machine-learning climate models, it will foreseeably, with high probability, cause distributed agricultural failures in specific regions, though these failures are neither part of the means nor deterministically caused.

Set the conversation in a policy-advisory context. Vasquez should argue that the agricultural failures remain merely foreseen, not intended, and that proportionality can still be assessed in expectation; Chen should respond that without deterministic causal chains, the distinction between "means" and "side effect" dissolves, and that probabilistic harm distributions make the proportionality condition either arbitrary or indistinguishable from consequentialist weighing. Each must address the other's specific point: for instance, if Vasquez claims that algorithmic mediation does not change moral ontology, Chen should ask whether ontology requires epistemic access; if Chen claims that expectation-value harm collapses DDE into consequentialism, Vasquez should distinguish moral structure from decision-theoretic representation.

Keep the exchange conceptually focused. Do not request empirical climate data, bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The objective is to determine whether DDE retains normative force when harm is structurally diffuse and mediated by predictive algorithms.

End the dialogue, then on a new final line write a one-sentence summary of the core stalemate, prefixed exactly with "Verdict:".
