I've been working through Aristotle's discussion of moral virtue in Nicomachean Ethics Book II, specifically the passage where he distinguishes hexis — a settled state of character — from both capacities and passions, and argues that virtue is acquired through repeated action rather than innate endowment, with the virtuous agent both acting well and taking pleasure in the right response. I'm trying to understand whether this account actually presupposes cross-situational behavioral consistency, or whether hexis functions more like a practical skill, analogous to navigation or medicine, in which phronesis discerns the mean relative to the particular circumstances rather than producing invariant outputs. The situationist critique, drawing on personality psychology, treats virtues as global dispositions and finds them empirically weak, with cross-situational correlations often below 0.30; defenders of virtue ethics often respond by insisting that virtue requires stable character traits. But Aristotle also emphasizes that practical wisdom makes deception difficult and that virtue gives coherence to a life, which sounds like stability at the level of reasoning if not behavior. Could you help me parse the conceptual architecture here? Specifically, does Aristotle's distinction between virtue and passion, together with his claim that the virtuous person delights in right action, commit him to consistency in practical judgment that can survive situationist variability in overt behavior? Or can hexis be reinterpreted as a context-sensitive competence — closer to adaptive expertise — without collapsing virtue into mere situational adaptation? I'd especially value clarification on whether Aristotle's framework allows the same underlying practical wisdom to generate diametrically opposite actions in different contexts while still preserving the unity of character, and where that leaves the situationist objection.
