I have been trying to understand what heritability actually means, as opposed to the popular idea that it tells us how "genetic" a trait is in some absolute sense. After reading some introductory behavioral genetics, I formed judgments about six concrete situations, but I am not sure they all hang together. I want to know whether a single coherent principle captures my verdicts, or whether I must drop one to keep the account simple, and what the refined principle predicts for a case I have not considered.

Case 1: In a wealthy population with nearly universal adequate nutrition, height is roughly 80 percent heritable. In a different population where some children suffer severe malnutrition and others do not, the heritability of height is much lower, because environment now explains more of the variance. My verdict: this is correct and shows that heritability is specific to a particular population and environment, not a fixed biological constant.

Case 2: The heritability of IQ is often estimated between 0.5 and 0.8 in affluent societies, but in impoverished settings with wildly unequal schooling the figure drops. My verdict: same principle as Case 1—heritability tracks variance partitioning in a given context.

Case 3: The trait "speaking English" has essentially zero heritability, even though the biological capacity for language is strongly genetic. My verdict: heritability concerns differences among people within an environment, not whether genes are necessary for the trait to exist at all.

Case 4: Researchers identify an allele that correlates with aggression, but only in people who experienced childhood abuse; in a population without abuse the allele has no effect, yet the calculated heritability of aggression is moderate. My verdict: this feels strange—every actual case requires an environmental trigger—yet I think the statistic can still be correct. It seems heritability does not imply that genes work alone.

Case 5: A government introduces genetic screening for "musical aptitude" and assigns children to enriched training based on their scores. Over time, because environment is now aligned with genotype, the heritability of musical achievement might actually rise, even though the policy was meant to equalize opportunity. My verdict: this is technically consistent with the variance-partitioning idea, but it conflicts with my intuition that intervention should lower heritability. I think my intuition is wrong, yet I want to be sure.

Case 6: Identical twins separated at birth and raised in different countries both become engineers. I initially took this as proof that engineering ability is "in the genes," but I now reject that inference: if every child in both countries were pushed into engineering regardless of aptitude, heritability would be near zero despite identical genetics. My verdict: heritability statistics do not license predictions about individual destiny.

Is there a precise principle—centered on variance partitioning within a specific population, together with explicit treatment of gene-environment interaction and gene-environment correlation—that captures Cases 1 through 3 and 6 without revision? Or do Cases 4 and 5 force me to complicate that principle, and if so, exactly which clauses must be added? Please state the simplest principle that accommodates Cases 1–3 and 6, then show the minimal modifications needed for Case 4 (interaction) and Case 5 (correlation), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts, if any, must be abandoned to avoid contradiction. Finally, apply your refined principle to a case I have not listed: if a society succeeds in equalizing every environmental condition—nutrition, education, stimulation—so perfectly that all children experience exactly the same environment, what happens to the heritability of IQ, and does that result mean IQ is then "purely genetic," or does it mean something else?
