Having spent three decades designing unbiased sampling protocols for ecological surveys, I find myself, in retirement, troubled by a conceptual analogy I keep encountering in recent political philosophy: defenders of sortition often invoke statistical language—representative samples, known error distributions, the law of large numbers—to justify selecting legislators by lot. I want you to design a final examination essay for an advanced undergraduate seminar in democratic theory that forces students to interrogate this analogy at its root, rather than merely recounting the standard historical narrative of Athenian sortition or modern citizens' assemblies.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether the epistemic virtues of random sampling in natural science—specifically unbiasedness, calculable confidence intervals, and convergence via large numbers—can be mapped onto the normative domain of democratic authorization without category error. It must require them to separate descriptive representation (the sample mirrors the population's traits) from normative legitimacy (the sample is authorized to bind the population), and to identify precisely where the analogy illuminates and where it misleads: for instance, whether statistical error margins have any analogue in political mandate, or whether the absence of selection bias in sampling implies the absence of deliberative bias in a citizen jury.

Please deliver: (a) the examination question itself, clearly framed for a ninety-minute in-class essay of approximately one thousand words; (b) a model answer of about seven hundred words that executes the required conceptual separation and evaluates the analogy's strength at the specific pressure points named above; and (c) a marking scheme totaling twenty marks that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response by listing five essential argumentative moves, allocating marks to each, and including a one-line note on the characteristic misconception that causes candidates to lose marks on that move. The scheme should penalize answers that simply summarize well-known defenses of lottocracy without engaging the statistical-political analogy, and should reward recognition that scientific sampling aims at accurate measurement whereas democratic selection aims at legitimate authorization. Do not request bibliographies, specific publication dates, or verbatim quotations from texts; the prompt should be fully answerable by a respondent who has no web access.
