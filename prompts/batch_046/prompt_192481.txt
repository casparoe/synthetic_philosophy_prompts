Draft the joint statement that would result from a sustained adversarial collaboration between Radical Aesthetic Autonomists and Moral-Aesthetic Integrationists on whether immoral art can be good art, specifically addressing the separation of a work from its artist's moral character. The deliverable should be a single formal document containing, in order: a numbered section of claims both camps can jointly sign; a disagreements table in which each row pairs one precise disagreement with the Integrationist formulation that side accepts for itself and the Autonomist counter-formulation that side accepts for itself; an explicit identification of three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving any one would move at least one party; and, for each crux, a concrete case or argumentative protocol both sides agree is relevant, with each side's explicit prediction recorded in advance.

The agreed-claims section must cover at least: the logical distinction between evaluating an artist's conduct and evaluating a work's aesthetic properties; the existence of works widely judged aesthetically excellent that were produced by, or express, serious moral wrongs; the empirical fact that audiences often report altered appreciation upon learning biographical or contextual moral facts; and the recognition that "separating the work from the artist" is ambiguous between an epistemic prescription (audiences ought to ignore biography) and a metaphysical claim (biographical facts are causally irrelevant to a work's aesthetic properties).

The disagreements table must include, with wording each side endorses for its own position: whether moral properties internal to a work can function as aesthetic virtues or vices rather than merely as contextual background; whether warranted knowledge of an artist's immorality can legitimately diminish, enhance, or restructure an informed viewer's aesthetic appreciation; and whether a coherent distinction survives scrutiny between "the work in itself" (defined by its intrinsic formal and expressive properties) and "the work as encountered by an informed audience" (defined by the total set of properties accessible to a knowledgeable perceiver).

The three cruxes should be framed as follows. First, the supervenience crux: do aesthetic properties supervene locally on formal and expressive features alone, or do they supervene globally on the work's total causal and moral context, including biographical facts? Second, the isolation-test crux: is there a coherent counterfactual isolation protocol—holding perceptual access constant while varying only biographical knowledge—that yields a determinate aesthetic verdict, and if so, does convergence or divergence of judgments under that protocol support one side? Third, the response-type crux: when a technically masterful work depicts or expresses moral atrocity, does the audience's moral repugnance operate as an aesthetic response analogous to horror in tragedy (and thus part of the work's aesthetic achievement), or as a non-aesthetic moral response that interferes with or is separable from aesthetic evaluation?

For the supervenience crux, both sides should agree on the formal identity of a specific hypothetical case—say, two paintings with indistinguishable formal properties, one executed by a documented perpetrator of severe harm and one by an unknown painter with no known moral record—and record whether the Autonomist predicts that warranted aesthetic judgments should not diverge given identical perceptual access, while the Integrationist predicts that divergence is not only expected but can be aesthetically warranted. For the isolation-test crux, specify a blind-evaluation protocol applied to a controversial literary work in which biographical information is systematically withheld and then revealed; record the Autonomist prediction that aesthetic ratings should remain stable across conditions (or that post-revelation changes reflect error or bias) versus the Integrationist prediction that stable ratings under ignorance do not invalidate the legitimacy of revised ratings under knowledge. For the response-type crux, use a specific type of case—technically masterful depiction of moral horror—where both sides agree on the presence of formal mastery and on the presence of audience moral horror, then record whether the Integrationist predicts that the horror enhances or is constitutive of the overall aesthetic achievement, and whether the Autonomist predicts that the horror is aesthetically irrelevant or must be bracketed for pure aesthetic judgment.

Conclude with a brief joint statement identifying what kind of further input—conceptual clarification, empirical study of audience judgment, or normative argument—both sides agree could actually move the debate, and flag any residual disagreements that both parties jointly recognize as verbal rather than substantive (for instance, disputes that dissolve once "the work" is precisely defined).
