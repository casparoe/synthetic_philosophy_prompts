As a novelist who has long tried to render philosophical abstractions with the particularity of lived experience, I ask you to compose a short story that dramatizes Samuel Scheffler's central claim in Death and the Afterlife: that human extinction is devastating not primarily because of the individual deaths it entails, but because it annihilates the collective afterlife constituted by humanity's onward rush—the continuation of culture, relationships, and unfinished projects across generations. To avoid a conventional apocalypse narrative, please embed the following invented conceit: in the final isolated settlement, citizens practice a ritual known as the Catalog of Unbegun Books, orally preserving the opening lines of novels they had intended to write but now never will, treating these unrealized narratives as mourning for the dissolution of temporal continuity rather than for lost persons. Through this ritual, explore whether extinction's harm resides in the disappearance of specific achievements or in the collapse of the framework that allows individual lives to feel like chapters in an ongoing story rather than isolated episodes. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to secondary literature; rely solely on the conceptual architecture described here.
