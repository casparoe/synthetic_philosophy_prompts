I am writing a feature for a science and ideas magazine about the philosophical fallout should the standard axioms of mathematics—specifically ZFC—turn out to be inconsistent. I need a taxonomy in the form of a decision tree that sorts the possible philosophical responses, built not from familiar school labels but from concrete commitments a reader could hold without knowing the literature. The tree should branch sequentially on four diagnostic questions: first, whether the contradiction is localized to one dispensable axiom (such as Choice or Replacement) or is systemic across the core framework; second, whether the appropriate response is excision, weakening, or reinterpretation of the offending principle; third, whether the mathematics that survives must still be robust enough to underwrite contemporary physics and computation, or whether that practical criterion is irrelevant to axiomatic legitimacy; and fourth, whether mathematical truth is best conceived as a single unified edifice or as a family of locally consistent fragments. Each leaf should identify the position—using an established name if one exists, or marking it "unnamed"—and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment, such as that large-cardinal research becomes permanently indeterminate, or that applied mathematics in physics proceeds without foundational warrant. The tree must include at least one combination of answers that currently has no defenders, with a brief diagnosis of whether the vacancy stems from incoherence, extreme unpalatability, or mere neglect. Please also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining where the logical space collapses. The audience is scientifically literate non-specialists; do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests, and do not invoke obscure technical results that a generalist model could not be expected to recall. In particular, I want the taxonomy to distinguish a view that treats the contradiction as a feature of the formal system rather than a bug to be repaired, and a separate view that makes physical applicability the decisive criterion for which axioms survive—two stances that standard surveys often conflate.
