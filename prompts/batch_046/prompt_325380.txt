I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of science, working on the metaphysics of complex systems, and I have been trying to formalize an argument about downward causation that my supervisor finds suspicious because its conclusion seems to undermine the explanatory autonomy of evolutionary biology. I would appreciate a careful diagnosis of where, if anywhere, the reasoning slips, together with an account of what each available fix costs.

Here is the argument. Define a diachronic constraint-emergent feature (DCEF) as a macro-level pattern that constrains the future trajectory of a system without being instantiated in any single time-slice of the system's micro-constitution. For example, directional selection for drought resistance in a plant population might be treated as a DCEF: it is not present in any individual genotype at time t, but it structures the distribution of genotypes across generations.

(1) If a feature is genuinely emergent, it must be capable of downward causation—that is, it must be able to influence the dynamics of the micro-level constituents.

(2) For downward causation to be more than a redescription of bottom-up dynamics, the emergent feature must be able to make a counterfactual difference: had the feature been absent, the micro-dynamics would have proceeded differently, holding fixed the initial conditions and the governing laws.

(3) A DCEF, by definition, is not present in any instantaneous micro-state; it exists only as a pattern across a temporal sequence of such states.

(4) Therefore, in any counterfactual evaluation that holds fixed the micro-state at a given time t, the DCEF is already absent by definition, since it requires the sequence, not the slice.

(5) So there is no well-formed counterfactual scenario in which the micro-state at t is identical but the DCEF is absent.

(6) Without such a counterfactual, the DCEF cannot satisfy the interventionist criterion for causal relevance.

(7) Hence DCEFs are causally inert with respect to the micro-level.

(8) But evolutionary biologists routinely invoke directional selection, ecological niche construction, and developmental canalization as causally explanatory—precisely the sorts of patterns that fit the DCEF profile.

(9) So either evolutionary biology systematically appeals to causally inert entities, or one of premises (1) through (7) is false.

(10) Premise (1) expresses the core intuition behind emergentism; premise (2) expresses the standard interventionist account of causation used across the special sciences; premise (6) is a logical consequence of (3) through (5).

If I must choose, I am most reluctant to abandon (1)—that genuine emergence requires downward causation—or (8)—that evolutionary biology offers genuinely explanatory appeals to macro-level patterns—though I recognize that holding both may be impossible without revising (2) or (5). Which step should I reject, and what does each option commit me to? Specifically, is there a way to preserve the causal efficacy of DCEFs without either reducing them to aggregates of micro-causal interactions or treating them as sui generis forces that violate the closure of physics?
