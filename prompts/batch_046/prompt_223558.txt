Question: In Republic II, Glaucon introduces the ring of Gyges to press Socrates on whether justice is valued for itself or only for its consequences. He imagines giving such a ring to both a just and an unjust person and claims both would eventually act unjustly if they could avoid detection. Now consider a hypothetical "reverse ring": instead of hiding the wearer, it makes every hidden injustice visible to others while leaving the wearer herself unaware of what she did. Compare Glaucon's original challenge with this reverse scenario. Does the reverse ring weaken or strengthen the claim that justice is merely conventional? In your answer, address whether modern evolutionary accounts of cooperation — like reciprocal altruism — actually answer Glaucon, or just redescribe the problem in biological terms.

Student answer:
Glaucon's challenge is that justice is not naturally good — people are naturally unjust and only practice it because they fear punishment. The ring shows that if you could escape detection, even the just person would steal and kill, so justice is just a social contract we agree to for protection. Socrates responds by saying justice is good for the soul, but he doesn't really answer Glaucon's point about consequences. The reverse ring makes injustice visible to others but not to you, so you'd still suffer social punishment. That means you'd still behave just for your reputation, which actually strengthens Glaucon's view that morality is about being seen, not about being good inside. Also, evolutionary psychology explains justice through reciprocal altruism — we cooperate because it benefits us genetically — which proves Glaucon wrong because it shows justice is hardwired into human nature, not just conventional. So the reverse ring and evolutionary theory together show that morality is both externally enforced and biologically real, but neither shows it's valuable for its own sake.
