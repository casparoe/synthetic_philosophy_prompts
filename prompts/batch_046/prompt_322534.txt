ok so this might sound random but i just got put on a committee for our departments new open-access journal and suddenly the dogmatism paradox is not just a classroom thing? we had this long meeting tuesday where we tried to work out when a reviewer should stick to her guns after learning other reviewers disagree, and now im trying to apply our own rules to an actual case im handling.

our track record so far: if the disagreement is about a checkable factual error in the paper, flip once its shown. if its about a methodological intuition that the reviewer cant fully articulate but the others share, stay put. if the reviewer has access to proprietary data the others lack, stay put no matter what. if the disagreement is about how to interpret a shared theoretical framework, reduce confidence but dont necessarily change the verdict. and we said if the reviewer knows her own historical accuracy in that specific subdomain is lower than the others combined, she should defer.

the new case im dealing with: reviewer A rejected a manuscript because she believes the authors are implicitly committing to a kind of epistemic partitioning—treating beliefs formed through long testimony chains as if they inherit justification directly, without accounting for intermediate false lemmas that might break the chain. reviewers B and C both accepted, saying this is just standard externalist semantics and not an error. reviewer A admits her objection comes partly from a niche working paper she read recently, partly from an intuition she cant fully formalize, and she also realizes B and C both work in philosophy of language while shes in epistemology, so they might be running different background assumptions about belief transmission. she has no private data, her track record in formal epistemology is slightly better than B's but worse than C's, and the issue is partly articulable. what should she do if she wants to stay consistent with our earlier rulings? which precedent is the closest analog? and if our rules genuinely dont determine it, what principle are we adopting with each choice? also, whats the further case that would tell us whether were actually running a domain-boundary rule, a higher-order-defeat rule, or something like credal-budgeting across disciplinary lines?
