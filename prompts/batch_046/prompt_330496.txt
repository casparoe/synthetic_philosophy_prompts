I spent three decades in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and for the past year I have been reading philosophy in a small university discussion group—currently Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons. At our last meeting, a fellow participant summarized Parfit's hitchhiker thought experiment in a way that seemed elegant but perhaps too neat. She presented it as a demonstration that rational agents should cultivate time-inconsistent preferences: the hitchhiker rationally commits now to paying the driver upon arrival, even though at the moment of arrival every self-interested reason to pay has vanished, because possessing a credible disposition to pay is precisely what secures the ride. From this she concluded that Parfit is defending the critical present-aim theory of rationality against the self-interest theory, and that the case is structurally identical to Kavka's toxin puzzle except that the driver functions as an external commitment mechanism. She then added a further claim that struck me as original: given Parfit's reductionist view of personal identity, in which future selves are only psychologically continuous rather than strictly identical, there is no single enduring agent who both makes the promise and breaks it. Therefore, she argued, the framing of rational irrationality collapses into a metaphysical mistake about diachronic agency, and once we abandon the fiction of a persistent self there is no genuine puzzle about why one should pay.

I suspect that her first points capture real features of commitment problems, but I am uncertain whether the move to reductionism is a faithful extension of Parfit's argument or an interpolation that solves the practical puzzle by changing its metaphysical subject. Could you provide an accuracy audit of this summary? Please distinguish what is correct about the hitchhiker's challenge to self-interest theories, where it conflates the hitchhiker with other puzzles such as Kavka's toxin, whether Parfit actually deploys his reductionism to dissolve practical-rationality problems in this manner, and whether her conclusion—that rational irrationality disappears once diachronic identity is rejected—survives these corrections. I am not seeking a bibliography or list of further readings, only an assessment I can bring back to our group.
