I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A on the ethics of large-scale philanthropic optimization, and my guest is an unnamed philosopher-economist who has recently defended effective altruism against its institutional critics. Rather than letting her retreat to broad claims about "doing the most good," I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that stay firmly inside the machinery of the view—pressing exactly where the recent literature applies tension, anticipating her standard replies, and following up on them.

Please cover three specific fault lines. First, the institutional critique: ask whether cause-neutral evaluation risks generating a "moral monoculture" that crowds out local knowledge, pluralistic value commitments, and democratic accountability in recipient communities, with a follow-up keyed to her likely defense that pluralism is preserved at the meta-level through donor-weighted cause selection. Second, earning to give: probe whether high-salaried work in sectors with substantial negative externalities—say, quantitative finance or fossil-fuel extraction—can be genuinely redeemed by donation, or whether moral licensing and direct complicity operate independently of counterfactual impact; then follow up by asking what empirical pattern could show that the counterfactual donation actually exceeds the institutional harm enabled by her presence in that sector. Third, measurability bias: ask why the framework systematically favors interventions with legible, near-term, individual-level metrics over structurally transformative but diffuse efforts such as institutional reform or basic science, and include a follow-up pressing whether "expected value" framing covertly grants an unjustified epistemic privilege to outcomes that happen to be quantifiable.

Additionally, inject two somewhat less common conceptual frames. One should address "counterfactual measurability": how does she distinguish measuring an intervention's direct causal effect from measuring its effect relative to a baseline already saturated with other altruists' contributions, and might this distinction undermine the comparative advantage she claims for cause-neutral evaluation? The other should address "institutional substitution": not merely whether EA crowds out other causes financially, but whether the professionalization of altruism—career coaching, impact auditing, donor-advised funds—structurally displaces non-professional moral agency in ways that cost-effectiveness analysis is constitutionally unable to detect. The tone should be rigorous and intellectually generous; I am trying to make her best arguments work harder, not to score rhetorical points.
