I am a graduate student in a philosophy of normativity seminar, and I am working through a conceptual exercise on whether normative reasons can exist independently of any reasoning subject. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Elena Voss, who holds that every genuine reason must be anchored in the practical or cognitive capacities of some agent (even if that agent is merely potential or counterfactual), and Dr. Kiran Yao, who defends the coherence of genuinely free-floating rationales, i.e., reasons that obtain solely in virtue of structural or functional relations and need not be represented or entertained by any reasoner.

The exchange should center on a specific, somewhat unconventional case: a hypothetical, self-sustaining geochemical system—analogous to deep-sea hydrothermal circulation—whose feedback loops maintain equilibrium conditions that, if disrupted, cause systemic collapse. Within this system, certain chemical pathways are functionally indispensable; they appear to be required for integrity, yet there are no organisms, minds, or designers present to represent these requirements as reasons for action. Voss should argue that describing these pathways as reasons is an illegitimate anthropomorphism that collapses normative language into causal-functional description, and she should press Yao on whether free-floating rationales can carry any justificatory or motivational force in the absence of an agent to be justified or motivated. Yao should reply that structural preservation conditions generate what might be called systemic oughts—normative constraints that apply to the internal relations of the system itself rather than to agents within it—and should contend that anchoring all reasons in agents illicitly conflates the metaphysics of normativity with the psychology of motivation.

Please make the dialogue genuinely dialectical: each speaker must respond directly to the specific claim just advanced by the other, rather than restating an opening position or talking past one another. Do not include any bibliography, citation, or reference to specific published texts; treat the arguments as conceptual constructions. Finally, after presenting the dialogue, include a brief epistemic note in which you state how confident you are in the philosophical coherence of the positions as rendered, and explicitly single out the point—particularly regarding whether systemic oughts can be genuinely normative rather than merely metaphorically descriptive—about which you are least sure.
