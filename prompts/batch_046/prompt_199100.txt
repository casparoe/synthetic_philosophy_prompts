I'm trying to figure out where I actually draw the line on what counts as a "genuine continuation" of a collaborative software project, going by my gut on cases. There's this open-source notation program that started in '98—call it Noter—and over the years people forked it, rewrote the rendering engine, changed the file format, etc. Here are my verdicts:

- The original team ships v2.0 with a totally new UI but the same file format: still Noter, obvious.
- A 2010 fork keeps the old UI but rewrites the whole backend in Rust: I think that's still Noter, closer to it than not.
- In 2015 someone takes the old codebase, strips out all the collaborative features, and sells it as a standalone desktop app with a new name: not Noter, feels like a copycat.
- The founder dies in 2018, and a nonprofit takes over maintenence, updates the license, and eventually 90% of the active contributors are new people who never met him. To me that's still Noter, but I'm not sure why it's different from the 2015 fork.
- Then there's this 2022 group that rebuilds something that looks and behaves exactly like Noter v1.0 but writes every line from scratch—no shared files, no git history, just clean-room reverse engineering of the behavior. I keep going back and forth; sometimes it feels like a replica, sometimes like an independant reinvention.
- I haven't decided yet about a "spiritual successor" built by ex-core contributors who keep the collaborative ethos but invent a totally incompatible file format and rename everything.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about that back-and-forth case—the clean-room rebuild? I feel like there's conceptual slippage between code lineage, functional equivalence, and authorial intent, but I can't pin it down, and I want to know if I'm being consistant or if one of my verdicts has to go.
