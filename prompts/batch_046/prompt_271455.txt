I am a graduate student in philosophy of science serving as the student representative on my university’s interdisciplinary pilot-study ethics review board. Every two weeks we evaluate proposals—mostly from psychology, neuroscience, and public health—that ask human subjects to participate in research predicated on preliminary, contested, or under-evidenced hypotheses. I have roughly fifteen minutes per proposal, I must produce a signed recommendation (approve, approve with conditions, or reject), and my fellow board members are scientists without philosophical training, so any framework I use must be translatable into plain questions they can understand. I want you to operationalize the debate between James’s defense of the right to believe in options that are genuinely live, forced, and momentous, and Clifford’s universal prohibition against belief on insufficient evidence, into a reusable decision procedure that my board could actually run. Crucially, inject a modification that is not standard in either author: include a filter I will call “downstream lock-in,” meaning the institutional and methodological dependency that adopting a belief now creates, such that abandoning it later becomes structurally costly regardless of what new evidence arrives. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop points where the recommendation must be reject, explicit hand-off points where the framework yields to value judgment rather than procedural rule, and a clear distinction between the epistemic risk borne by subjects and that borne by the scientific record. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (A) a trauma-psychology pilot testing a mindfulness-based intervention for PTSD survivors, supported only by clinician anecdotes but with no multi-year grant pathway if approved; (B) a cognitive-neuroscience fMRI study testing an embodied-cognition account of moral reasoning that is genuinely forced—either run this exact design or abandon the research line—where preliminary data is ambiguous but approval would commit the lab to a five-year funding trajectory; and (C) a geriatric-public-health proposal recommending a specific nutritional supplement regimen for nursing-home residents based on a single observational study, where adoption would immediately trigger supplier contracts and policy guidelines. In one of these—specifically Case B—the procedure should yield an approval I personally find unwelcome because the forced and live conditions are satisfied and lock-in is contained, despite my reservations about the weak evidence. Structure the response in the following sections with approximate lengths: (1) The practitioner’s context and constraints (about 120 words); (2) The translated procedure: ordered steps, sufficient answers, stop points, and judgment hand-offs (about 500 words); (3) The downstream lock-in filter and how it modifies the James-Clifford framework (about 200 words); (4) Worked applications to Cases A, B, and C, noting in each whether the procedure stops, approves, or hands off, and flagging where Case B produces the unwelcome approval (about 450 words total); (5) Limits and misfire conditions—what the procedure cannot settle and where it will systematically err (about 200 words). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; ground the procedure in the philosophical positions as they are generally understood, but do not quote verbatim from any published text. Keep the language formal and precise enough for a board document, but avoid jargon that requires prior knowledge of decision theory or formal epistemology.
