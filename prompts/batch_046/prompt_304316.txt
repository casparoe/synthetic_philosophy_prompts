I direct intake for a neighborhood oral-history archive actively collecting material from a district undergoing rapid redevelopment and displacement. We receive physical documents, audio recordings, digital files, and administrative records, and for each item my staff must decide whether to accession it, defer it, or decline it. I want them to apply the principle that archives are not neutral repositories but instruments of power: the decision to preserve a record, the language used to describe it, and the omissions in the collection together determine whose experience is historically visible and whose is rendered silent. To make this operational, please design a reusable decision procedure—a sequence of questions in fixed order, with the evidence required for each answer, the point at which the procedure yields a mandatory action (accession with standard cataloging, accession with a critical framing note, deferral, or refusal), and the points at which staff must escalate to a senior archivist because the question is one of values rather than fact.

The procedure should distinguish four specific modes of silence that can occur: structural silence (no record was produced because the person or event lay outside institutional record-keeping systems); agentive silence (a record was produced but was deliberately withheld, destroyed, or excluded by an actor with power over it); medium silence (a record exists but in a material or digital form that our archive cannot currently stabilize, interpret, or migrate); and hermeneutic silence (the record is physically preserved but our descriptive practices—categories, keywords, narrative summaries—frame it in a way that obscures its significance to the originator). For each mode, specify the diagnostic test the staff applies, the kind of documentation needed to confirm it, and how the finding affects the accession decision.

Then run the procedure on three incoming items:

(1) A complete, notarized set of property deeds and renovation permits from a homeowner who has lived on the block for forty years, submitted with a request that they be cataloged under urban renewal and property development.

(2) A handwritten letter in an endangered dialect, with no translation available, written by a tenant who had no formal lease and who asks, through an interpreter, that it be kept as evidence of displacement by neglect rather than housing transition.

(3) A digital video of a community meeting stored only on a proprietary social-media platform that offers no bulk-export tool; the video shows residents objecting to the redevelopment plan, but the platform's terms of service prohibit copying without the uploader's consent, and the uploader is unreachable.

The procedure should produce a result for each case, including one case where the procedure's outcome will feel counterintuitive to the staff—for example, recommending refusal or deferral of an apparently valuable record, or recommending accession of a record that seems compromised—and you should explain why the principle demands that result. Finally, state explicitly what categories of decision the procedure cannot settle, where staff judgment is irreducible, and what risks of misfire remain even when the procedure is followed correctly.
