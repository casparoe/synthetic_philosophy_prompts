Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on collective choice, strategy-proofness, and the result commonly associated with Gibbard and Satterthwaite.

Camp 1 — Institutional minimalists: They hold that for any deterministic aggregation rule with universal domain over three or more alternatives, non-dictatorship entails the existence of strategic misrepresentation. They treat this not as a mere formal curiosity but as a normative ceiling: legitimate collective choice must either severely restrict admissible preference profiles, accept a dictator, or tolerate institutional unfairness. They reject appeals to "natural" domain restrictions (e.g., single-peakedness) as empirically inadequate for pluralistic societies.

Camp 2 — Strategic pluralists: They accept the formal result in its standard one-shot formulation but deny that manipulability is uniformly a normative defect. They argue that strategic behavior can serve as an intensity-revealing or deliberative filter, that universal-domain rules are unrealistic idealizations rather than normative targets, and that the dictatorship escape clause renders strategy-proofness an unacceptable price for democratic aggregation. They further contend that extending the analysis to sequential or iterative revelation—where agents update beliefs between rounds—alters the practical force of the theorem, without claiming this extension has been fully formalized.

Produce a document containing:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Cover: (a) the formal link among universal domain, non-dictatorship, and strategic manipulability for deterministic rules with three or more outcomes; (b) the conceptual separation of strategy-proofness from Pareto efficiency and from independence of irrelevant alternatives; (c) the empirical observation that real voting bodies often operate with restricted domains or procedural norms that tolerate strategic positioning.

2. A disagreements table with two columns—"Minimalist formulation" and "Pluralist formulation"—each row stating one dispute in wording its own side accepts. Include rows on: (i) whether manipulability is a failure of fairness or a feature of expressive aggregation; (ii) whether domain restrictions are a viable institutional remedy or an unrealistic abstraction; (iii) whether sequential preference revelation with belief updating can escape the theorem's practical bite without collapsing into dictatorship.

3. The cruxes—identify at least three sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. Suggested cruxes: whether a descriptively adequate domain restriction exists for modern pluralistic preference profiles; whether strategic misrepresentation in actual committees correlates with preference intensity; and whether iterative revelation mechanisms can be designed to be effectively strategy-proof in non-dictatorial settings.

4. For each crux, specify one concrete institutional case, empirical pattern, or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance each camp's explicit prediction about what that evidence would show.

Keep the wording precise, avoid bibliographic or citation requests, and do not depend on obscure literature. End with a line beginning "Summary:" that captures the central residual disagreement in one sentence.
Summary: The minimalists treat unavoidable manipulability as a normative failure demanding domain restriction or dictatorship, while the pluralists treat it as a tolerable or even valuable feature of aggregation that sequential revelation might further mitigate.
