Exam question:
P.F. Strawson distinguishes reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, and related responses—from the objective attitude, arguing that the former are woven into ordinary interpersonal relationships and that their justification does not depend on resolving the determinism debate. Some recent interpreters, drawing on second-personal moral philosophy, have reconceived reactive attitudes as forms of moral address that presuppose a specific kind of reciprocal recognition or second-personal authority—an authority that can be undermined not only by individual incapacity but by structural conditions that make mutual recognition impossible. Evaluate whether this reinterpretation strengthens or weakens Strawson's defense of moral responsibility. In particular, does it help explain why we might shift to an objective attitude in cases of systemic coercion without conceding that determinism undermines responsibility? And does the dependence on second-personal authority introduce a fragility that Strawson's original framework avoids?

Student answer:
Strawson's well-known essay distinguishes reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, love, forgiveness—from the objective attitude, a detached stance we take toward those we regard as lacking full moral competence, such as young children or those with severe mental impairment. The determinism debate traditionally asks whether causal determination makes reactive attitudes irrational. Strawson answers that this is a pseudo-problem: reactive attitudes are not theoretical commitments that need metaphysical vindication, but practical responses woven into interpersonal life. We justify particular instances not by denying determinism, but by appealing to excuses (lack of ill will, duress) or exemptions (incapacity).

Contemporary interpreters influenced by second-personal moral philosophy have proposed that reactive attitudes are best understood as forms of moral address. To resent someone is to hold them accountable, which presupposes that they possess second-personal authority—the standing to demand reasons from me. This reinterpretation strengthens Strawson's position by showing that the relevant condition is not libertarian freedom but reciprocal recognition. If determinism is compatible with such recognition, reactive attitudes are untouched.

Moreover, this framework clarifies the objective attitude: it is not merely clinical detachment but the suspension of second-personal authority. This allows us to apply it not only to individuals with cognitive deficits but also to cases of systemic coercion, where oppressive structures prevent the mutual recognition that moral address requires. In such cases, adopting the objective attitude does not concede that determinism is true; it acknowledges that the social conditions for reactive engagement have collapsed.

Still, this extension reveals a tension in Strawson's view. He describes reactive attitudes as so deeply embedded in human society that we cannot systematically abandon them without abandoning interpersonal life altogether. From this inevitability, he infers that they do not need external justification. But once we see them as dependent on second-personal authority, their inevitability is no longer obvious: structural conditions can systematically undermine that authority. Thus, the reinterpretation saves Strawson from determinism only to expose his framework to a sociological vulnerability he never addressed—namely, that moral responsibility might be fragile not because of metaphysics, but because recognition can fail at scale.
