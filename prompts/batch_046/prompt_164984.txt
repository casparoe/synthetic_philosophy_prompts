Over the past eighteen months, I have been the primary carer for my mother, whose frontotemporal dementia has progressively dissolved the behavioural markers through which we usually recognise another mind, and I have simultaneously begun consulting on the design of conversational agents that produce fluent, context-sensitive discourse without any biological substrate. The convergence has left me convinced that the philosophical problem of other minds is no longer adequately framed by the traditional choice between behaviourism and inference from analogy; what I need is a rigorous specification of adequacy for a theory that can navigate both the degradation of natural expression and its synthetic imitation.

Write the desiderata that any theory of other minds must satisfy if it is to be adequate for this dual purpose — that is, for guiding ethical and epistemic attitudes in ordinary life, in clinical care where behaviour is fragmented, and in evaluation of artificial systems that simulate interpersonal presence. For each requirement, give the motivating case or conceptual distinction, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference belonging to a particular camp. The list must cover: (i) an explanation of first-person asymmetry that avoids solipsism about others; (ii) criteria for mental attribution when behavioural channels are degraded (advanced dementia, locked-in states) or technologically mediated (brain-computer interfaces, synthetic language production), with a principled reason for treating biological and synthetic mediation differently if they are to be treated differently; (iii) preservation of the distinction between knowing that another subject is suffering and knowing what that suffering is like, together with an account of why care contexts seem to demand the second despite its apparent impossibility; (iv) consistency with current neuroscience on predictive processing and intersubjective perception, without reducing other minds to internal simulation; (v) a boundary condition that excludes current large language models from full mindedness without merely stipulating biological embodiment. Then state whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — particularly whether requiring both direct epistemic access to others and full independence from behavioural evidence is contradictory — and identify which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the standard contenders (behaviourism, theory-theory, simulation theory, and direct-perception accounts). Briefly score those four approaches against your list, keeping the emphasis on the criteria rather than on the theories. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which empirical discovery or philosophical argument would cause you to reorder or drop one of these requirements, and where in your analysis of technologically mediated cases you think the reasoning is most vulnerable to collapse.
