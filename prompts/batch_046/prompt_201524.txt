Consider a decision protocol adopted by several structured forecasting boards and intelligence-assessment units—call it the Conditional Decoupling Rule. When an analyst receives a report expressed as a material conditional—"If the adversary has activated the new signal system, then supply corridors will be disrupted within 72 hours"—the protocol requires the analyst to update two distinct probabilities separately: the marginal probability of the antecedent, and the conditional probability of the consequent given that antecedent. The analyst is explicitly forbidden, however, to propagate a confirmed antecedent forward into an updated marginal belief about the consequent unless the consequent has been independently observed. Conversely, if the antecedent is later disconfirmed, the protocol directs the analyst to leave the prior probability of the consequent entirely untouched, treating the conditional report as evidentially inert with respect to the negation of its antecedent. The conditional statement itself is never entered into the belief-revision ledger as a direct object of update; only its decomposed probabilities are.

This practice embodies a specific stance on the logic of conditionals and on probabilistic belief revision. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes the rule rational rather than arbitrary. Does it presuppose that the assertibility or belief-value of a conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability—the view often associated with the Adams thesis—or does it instead presuppose the denial of that identity, in light of the difficulties about updating on conditionals illustrated by the Judy Benjamin problem? Is the practice internally coherent, or does it quietly commit to incompatible epistemologies in the two directions—treating confirmation of the antecedent as evidentially blocked while treating disconfirmation of the antecedent as evidentially neutral—thereby assuming that conditionals constrain belief asymmetrically? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: is it compatible with rival accounts that deny conditionals have truth conditions altogether, or with accounts that accept them but allow direct belief revision on the conditional statement itself? Finally, describe what an institutional rule built on the opposing philosophical view would have to prescribe instead—specifically, how analysts should revise their beliefs about the consequent when they learn the antecedent is true, and when they learn it is false.
