I'm a retired marine biologist who's been poking around philosophy since I stopped working — mostly reading in bed with a cup of tea, nothing formal. I'm running a session next Tuesday for my local philosophy circle (retired teachers, a lawyer, a nurse, no academics) and I want to explain why 'free will' isn't one thing you either have or don't, but more like camouflage in the natural world: not a single trick but a family of adaptations solving different survival problems — hiding, deceiving predators, planning ahead over months. I want them to see that the only varieties worth wanting are the ones that actually do work for us: the kind that lets you take credit for a kind act, the kind that lets you change your mind when new evidence comes in, not some magical break in the causal chain.

Plan the explanantion, not the lecture itself. What has to come first so the camouflage analogy doesn't just confuse them — should I establish that evolution produces many solutions to one problem before I even mention free will? What's the one concrete pair of human actions that should carry the whole idea — I'm torn between a reflex pulling your hand from a flame versus deliberately lying to protect someone's feelings — and why one pair works better than the other? The misconceptions they'll bring: that evolutionary talk makes morality pointless, that I'm saying free will is just an illusion like camouflage is just colour, and that I'm secretly a determinist wearing a different hat. Where exactly in the sequence should I head off each one? What's a single question I can ask at the end that proves they've grasped there are multiple varieties worth wanting, not just that they feel comforted? And which part of the academic mess — compatibilism, libertarianism, the whole lot — should I openly say I'm leaving on the cutting-room floor?

About fifty minutes, whiteboard, no handouts. Don't give me a reading list — half of them wouldn't read it anyway, and I wouldn't know where to start them.
