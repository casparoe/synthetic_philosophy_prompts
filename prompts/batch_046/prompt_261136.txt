Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them A and B—debating whether self-deception is conceptually possible. A defends the unitary-mind thesis: a single doxastic system cannot simultaneously hold a belief and its negation with full awareness, so apparent self-deception collapses either into hypocrisy (knowing deception of others) or into mere ignorance (unrecognized false belief). B defends a hierarchical-partition model: the mind can contain structurally isolated evaluative subsystems, allowing an agent to genuinely believe p at one functional level while genuinely believing not-p at another, without either belief being merely “half-formed” or metaphorical.

To avoid repeating well-worn examples, ground the debate in the following engineered case: a senior logician has personally derived a valid counterexample to her own long-defended formal system. She has checked every step, accepts the derivation’s validity, and yet continues to publish extensions of the system, treating the counterexample as an “interesting boundary condition” rather than a refutation, all while sincerely reporting to colleagues that she “fully accepts” the result. A should argue that this is either strategic dissimulation or a case of compartmentalized ignorance, not true self-deception. B should argue that it is precisely self-deception: she genuinely registers the counterexample at the epistemic level responsible for belief-revision, but that registration is functionally quarantined by a higher-order motivational architecture that preserves her professional identity.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each character should directly answer the other’s specific objections rather than restating their opening position. In particular, B must respond to A’s charge that partition models smuggle in a homuncular “inner knower” that does the deceiving; A must respond to B’s charge that the unitary view cannot distinguish self-deception from ordinary fallibility or from hypocrisy without appealing to an implausible transparency of mind. Conclude without declaring a winner, but with each identifying the single conceptual commitment—about belief transparency, about functional architecture, or about the normativity of rational integration—that they regard as non-negotiable.
