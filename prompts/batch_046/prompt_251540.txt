I am currently studying for my A-level examinations in philosophy, and during a recent discussion with friends about perception we became rather entangled in disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. We kept returning to the familiar examples—the bent stick in water, or the Müller-Lyer lines—but I felt these were too well worn to reveal anything genuinely new about whether illusion and veridical perception share a common mental factor. I should therefore like you to construct a novel thought experiment that I am tentatively calling a threshold-binding scenario. Picture an observer in an ordinary art gallery who is examining a slowly rotating ceramic vase under illumination provided by a stroboscopic source calibrated precisely to sit at that individual’s critical flicker fusion threshold. At this boundary, the observer’s visual system intermittently succeeds in binding discrete frames into a smooth percept of continuous rotation, and intermittently fails, yielding a stuttering, fragmented appearance; yet the observer possesses no introspective means of determining which binding mode is presently active. In the successful-binding condition she genuinely perceives the vase in continuous motion; in the failure condition she undergoes a non-veridical experience of fragmentation that corresponds to no actual discontinuity in the object itself.

Please design this scenario to meet the following theoretical specifications. First, the common-factor theorist must be compelled to classify both experiences as awareness of the very same internal representational content or quale, on the grounds that the subject’s introspective report is identical in each case. Second, the disjunctivist must be able to maintain that the two experiences are fundamentally different kinds of mental state: in the successful-binding case there is a direct perceptual relation to the rotating vase, whereas in the failure case there is merely a non-relational appearance, despite the indistinguishability from the inside. Third, a representationalist should agree with the disjunctivist about the difference in representational content—continuous versus stuttering rotation—but insist that both experiences share the same mental vehicle, and you should show precisely why the structure of the case forces that conclusion. The setting must remain entirely realistic: no demons, no omniscient predictors, no futuristic technology beyond calibrated gallery lighting, and the observer must be neurologically typical.

After presenting the scenario, please demonstrate explicitly that each specification is satisfied: identify the belief or perceptual judgement at issue, describe the relevant nearby possible worlds, and explain the verdict delivered by each of the three positions named above. Then identify, with complete honesty, which structural feature of the case is performing the philosophical labour—presumably that introspective access is held fixed whilst the metaphysical relation to the external object varies—and explain what happens to the argument if that feature is removed, for example if the observer learns through prolonged training to detect her own binding mode. Finally, anticipate the strongest objection that a defender of the common-factor view could raise against your use of the case, and provide the disjunctivist’s reply. I ask that you write entirely in British English and that you include no bibliography, citation, or reference to specific published works; I wish to assess the reasoning purely from the concepts presented.
