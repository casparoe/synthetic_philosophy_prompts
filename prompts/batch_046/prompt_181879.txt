Consider the following paraphrase of an argument drawn from Aristotle's Politics, in the discussion where he distinguishes household management (oikonomikē) from the art of wealth-getting (chrematistikē). He observes that the goods required for the good life of a household are naturally bounded: there is a determinate quantity of food, shelter, and tools sufficient for living well, and once that limit is reached, further accumulation serves no natural purpose. By contrast, exchange mediated by coinage introduces a form of acquisition that knows no inherent boundary, because money can be stockpiled indefinitely. He then singles out lending at interest—tokos—as the most unnatural mode of this art, noting that currency was instituted solely as a medium to exchange necessary goods, yet interest treats the medium itself as productive, making coin beget coin in a manner that mimics biological generation while lacking any corresponding natural function.

I am trying to understand a subtle conceptual issue in this reasoning. Aristotle appears to rely on two distinct normative registers: one quantitative, holding that natural acquisition is limited because biological and domestic needs have fixed satiation points; the other qualitative or metaphysical, holding that money is naturally barren and therefore violates its own essence when it reproduces. These registers seem to pull in different directions. If the wrongness of interest is merely that it severs means from their proper ends—treating exchange-value as an end in itself—then the argument is ethical and concerns the corruption of practical reasoning. But if the wrongness is that monetary reproduction is ontologically aberrant—an imitation of biological growth in a domain where such growth has no natural telos—then Aristotle is making a stronger metaphysical claim about the proper boundaries of different kinds of being.

Can you help me clarify whether these two readings can be reconciled? Specifically, does Aristotle's condemnation of chrematistics depend on a hidden premise that all reproductive or generative processes outside living organisms are metaphysically defective? Or is it possible to reconstruct his view as a coherent normative theory about hierarchical ordering of ends, in which money's sterility is not a biological analogy but a logical feature of instrumental goods—namely, that instruments cannot serve as final causes without generating a category mistake?

Please explain the conceptual architecture of the passage, and address whether interpreting Aristotle's distinction as a purely normative claim about means-end rationality avoids committing him to an implausible metaphysical biology of economic value.
