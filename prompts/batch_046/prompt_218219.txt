Exam question: Norman Malcolm argued that dreaming is not a form of concurrent experience occurring during sleep, principally because the criteria for attributing a dream to a sleeper are entirely retrospective: they depend on the subject's waking report, with no independent access to any putative mental state during the sleep itself. Daniel Dennett proposed a well-known alternative—often called the cassette model—according to which cognitive material may be stored or assembled during sleep but is only experienced, if at all, upon awakening through a process of retrospective narrative integration. Evaluate Malcolm's conceptual argument in light of Dennett's framework. Specifically, address whether Dennett's allowance for retrospective construction undermines Malcolm's claim that there can be no coherent notion of concurrent dream-experience, and consider whether the two positions are closer than they initially appear. In your answer, distinguish carefully between epistemic limitations on observing dreams and conceptual conditions for attributing them.

Student answer:
Malcolm's core point is that since we have no way to check what is in someone's mind while they are asleep, the only evidence we ever have for a dream is the report they give after waking up. From this he concludes that dreaming cannot be an experience that happens during sleep, because there is simply no criterion for its occurrence then—only a criterion for remembering or reporting it later. This is essentially a verificationist argument about private mental states applied specifically to sleep.

Dennett's cassette theory addresses this by proposing that the brain records information during REM sleep, like writing data to a tape, and then upon awakening the subject plays back that tape and experiences the narrative then. So Dennett agrees with Malcolm that there is no phenomenal experience occurring during sleep itself; the experience is delayed until the replay happens in the waking state. Where they differ is that Malcolm thinks nothing substantive is happening in the mind during sleep—it's all confabulated in the waking report—whereas Dennett allows that real cognitive processing writes the cassette during sleep, even though the phenomenal binding only occurs upon arousal.

One problem with Malcolm's view is that it makes dreaming entirely a waking phenomenon, which seems odd because we naturally associate dreams with the sleeping state. Dennett handles this better because his model preserves a connection to sleep-state brain activity: the cassette is written then, even if played later. Another issue is that Malcolm confuses an epistemic limitation—our inability to observe concurrent dream states—with a metaphysical conclusion about whether those states exist. Just because we only have retrospective access does not entail that there is nothing there to access; it might just mean we lack the observational tools.

Finally, Malcolm's argument seems to rule out dreams in non-linguistic animals or pre-verbal infants, since they cannot produce waking reports. Dennett's model can accommodate them easily: the cassette can be written without ever being played back or verbally reported. Overall, Dennett offers a more empirically plausible framework because it respects neuroscience findings about REM activity, while Malcolm's position risks collapsing dreaming into a form of waking confabulation.
