Student answer: Voss's framework is helpfull for seeing what's at stake. Task-identity is just the social label — being called a baker — while process-identity is the felt engagement with kneading, proofing, and heat. Once robots take over those steps, the baker keeps her title but loses the skillful, tactile core of the job. That maps onto Marx's four dimensions of alienation: she's seperated from the product (machines bake the bread), from the process (she doesn't knead), from her species-being (her creative labor capacity is idle), and from other workers (she oversees robots alone). Arendt adds the liberating possibility. For Arendt, labor is cyclical biological maintenance, whereas work creates durable objects and a public world. Automating labor should therefore free people from necessity so they can pursue genuine work. But Voss seems wrong to treat the loss of process-identity as always harmful, because Arendt shows labor itself lacks deep meaning — only work has it. So automation actually solves Marx's alienation by converting labor into work, which strengthens rather than threatens the meaning of work.
