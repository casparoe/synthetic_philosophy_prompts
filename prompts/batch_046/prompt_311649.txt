Since I left the lab and started digging into decision-theory philosophy, I've gotten obssessed with these predictor scenarios. I think I built an argument that takes the counterfactual mugging intuition and applies it to XOR blackmail, but then it runs away and seems to say rational people should pay blackmailers and also buy sandwiches purely because of correlation. That can't be right, but I can't see the sleight of hand. Here is the chain:

(1) Counterfactual mugging shows that rational agents should care about evidential correlations across counterfactual worlds, not just causal effects in the actual world.

(2) XOR blackmail sets up exactly the same correlation structure, just with the payoff signs reversed: the blackmailer reveals your secret iff you pay, and stays silent iff you don't.

(3) So by (1), you should refuse to pay in XOR blackmail, because refusing correlates with the good outcome (silence).

(4) But standard blackmail is structurally identical except the correlation is direct: not-paying correlates with revelation, paying correlates with silence.

(5) Therefore by (1) you should pay standard blackmail too.

(6) Now here's where it gets weird. In any ordinary transaction—say, buying a sandwich—the same correlation holds: paying correlates with recieveing food, not paying with going hungry.

(7) So if (1) is a general principle about rational choice, it doesn't distinguish blackmail from buying lunch; it just says "do whatever correlates with good outcomes."

(8) But that can't be right, because then rationality collapses into pure pattern-matching with no room for coercion, autonomy, or even causal reasoning about what you actually control.

(9) So one of these premises has to go. I'd really like to keep (1) because the mugging intuition feels strong, and I'd like to keep (3) since refusing XOR blackmail still sounds correct. I'm willing to give up (4) or (6) if that's where the modal slide is hiding, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying.

Can someone tell me which step is the mistake? Is it that (4) smuggles in a causal dependency that wasn't present in (1)? Or is there a hidden quantifier shift between "correlates in counterfactual worlds" and "correlates in the actual world"? I feel like there's an equivocation between predictor-accuracy and direct observation, but I can't pin it down. Also, am I right that XOR blackmail is just counterfactual mugging with the sign flipped, or is there a principel difference in the predictor's epistemic position that breaks the analogy?
