I spent thirty-five years in experimental physics before retiring, and over the past few years I have been reading systematically in metaphysics and philosophy of science. In the course of that reading, and in conversations with colleagues who have also turned to philosophy late in life, I keep encountering a particular argumentative configuration that I cannot place within the standard taxonomy I have learned. It arises whenever determinism is discussed in contexts where every possible outcome is said to be physically realized—whether in deterministic interpretations of quantum mechanics, in cosmological models with all initial conditions instantiated, or in logical spaces where every consistent history exists. One party observes that, because the governing dynamics are deterministic and complete, every specific outcome is already fixed within the total system, leaving no metaphysical gap for an open future. The other party replies that, even granting this global completeness, the particular outcome that will be experienced by a given subject remains unsettled from that subject’s point of view, and that practical reasoning therefore requires treating the future as genuinely open—not because some hidden variable is unknown, but because the assignment of the subject to one realized outcome among many has not yet occurred.

I have seen this exchange appear in popular discussions of branching universes, in debates about free will under physical determinism, and even in informal analogies drawn from statistical mechanics, where an ensemble is fully specified yet a single draw is not. What strikes me is that the second response does not seem to be ordinary epistemic humility about unknown conditions, nor does it appear to be the classical problem of future contingents that Aristotle raised with the sea battle—there, the concern is whether a statement about tomorrow’s event already possesses a truth value. Here, by contrast, every statement about every branch or outcome seems to possess a definite truth value within the global description; the openness is located not in the world’s failure to settle a fact, but in the first-person perspective’s lack of assignment to one of the settled facts. Is there an established name for this precise configuration—global determinism coupled with perspectival, non-epistemic openness about which determined outcome will be “mine”? If there is, how exactly does it relate to Aristotle’s sea battle, to branching-time theories of temporal logic, and to the distinction between determinism and fatalism? And if there is no established term, can you articulate what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie, particularly with respect to ordinary uncertainty, indexical reasoning, and the non-identity problem? I am less interested in a bare label than in a careful carving of the concept.
