I am composing a novel set in Edinburgh during the 1750s, and a central scene involves my protagonist—a physician with skeptical philosophical sympathies—counseling a patient who has refused further treatment for an incurable illness. To render the dialogue credible, I consulted with a fellow writer at a recent literary salon about David Hume’s suppressed essay on suicide. My interlocutor offered a confident summary, which he presented as the orthodox reading: Hume argues that suicide is a natural liberty of the will, so that whenever an individual calculates that continued existence produces more suffering than pleasure, ending one’s life becomes not merely permissible but a rational duty. He claimed that Hume rejects every moral distinction between suicide and other acts of self-preservation—such as declining medicine—on the grounds that self-preservation carries no special weight once happiness turns negative. The summary further asserted that Hume explicitly dismissed the Stoic doctrine of the open door as mere emotional irrationality, maintaining instead that human life possesses no greater inherent sanctity than the life of a plant or an animal, and may therefore be discarded at will, like any other possession, whenever it ceases to be useful.

This account feels suspiciously severe to me—particularly the transformation of Hume’s position into a positive obligation to die whenever utility is negative, and the suggestion that he treated human agency as interchangeable with vegetative existence. Before I commit these lines to my manuscript, I wish to submit the summary to a careful audit. Please assume the role of a professional philosopher with deep familiarity with the Scottish Enlightenment, and think through the evaluation systematically, perhaps beginning by sketching the actual architecture of Hume’s argument. First, identify which elements of the summary genuinely reflect Hume’s analogical reasoning and his denials that suicide constitutes an offense to God, to society, or to oneself. Second, distinguish the standard simplifications—such as reducing the argument to a crude hedonic calculus—from the accurate core. Third, specify what Hume explicitly denied or never advanced, especially any notion of suicide as a duty and any literal equivalence between human life and disposable property. Finally, determine whether the conclusion that suicide is morally required under conditions of suffering survives once these corrections are made. I am not seeking a bibliography or citation list, but a precise accuracy audit that I can rely upon for my character’s voice.
