My brother just got his call-up papers for the Finnish reserve and now I'm weirdly obsesed with how armed neutrality actually holds together in real life, not just in textbooks. I run a small seminar — about 12 poli-sci/philosophy undergrads, 90 minutes, once a week — and I want a game or structured role-play that makes them feel why conscription and neutrality are tied together, not just policy options you pick seperately. Set it in a fictional country, Alvernia, that claims armed neutrality and has to respond to a crisis where a big ally offers "protection" if they drop neutrality. Give the group three decision rounds: first, choose conscription, a small professional force, or a hybrid; second, allocate resources between readiness and social programs; third, respond to the ally's offer with thier chosen force structure in place. The rules and payoffs should make it obvious that a professional army can deploy fast but makes neutrality look like outsourcing, while conscription makes the army literally the people so neutrality is credible but you can't move quickly. They should discover for themselves that distributing weapons and training through conscription changes civil society in ways that make the neutrality commitment stick. After the game, a debrief that maps each round onto the actual Swiss militia model, Sweden's recent reintroduction, and Finland's total defence idea where conscription is about social cohesion not just manpower. Be honest about what the simulation can't reproduce — like the emotional weight of being drafted or long-term economic drag — and tell me what usually goes wrong, like groups optimizing for efficiency and ignoring the credibility problem, plus how to steer them back without just lecturing. They shouldn't hear the phrase "armed neutrality" named until after they've played, if possible.
