In preparation for an interdisciplinary workshop on autonomy and institutional design, and in light of a recent family situation that has made these questions immediate rather than abstract, I need an audit of the following commitments. Over the past year I have become the primary decision-maker for my father, who lives with mild cognitive impairment, and I have also been advising a municipal working group on behavioral policy; the two roles have pushed me toward views that I now suspect may not hang together.

Here is my current package:

(i) I accept libertarian paternalism only in its strict form: a nudge is permissible if it is fully transparent, easily resistible, and directed at the agent’s own reflective, second-order preferences rather than at transient impulses.

(ii) I hold that any institutional influence that operates below the threshold of conscious deliberation—including framing effects, status-quo defaults, or algorithmic restructuring of choice environments—treats the person as a mere means, violating Kantian autonomy, regardless of whether a formal opt-out is technically available.

(iii) For individuals with diminished but not absent capacity, I believe surrogate decision-makers should apply a substituted-judgment standard (what the person would have endorsed) rather than an objective best-interests standard.

(iv) I think public institutions have a positive duty to reduce bureaucratic friction—what is sometimes called sludge—but must avoid hidden defaults that exploit systematic cognitive biases.

(v) I recently authorized, on my father’s behalf, an adaptive digital care-management system that continuously monitors his behavioral patterns and dynamically restructures his medication-choice environment through predictive framing and hidden adherence defaults. I did this because I judged it best for him, knowing he had never endorsed algorithmic mediation of this kind and that he could not now navigate the opt-out procedure without assistance.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision—dropping or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most inconsistencies at once, and whether that revision forces me to abandon my position in (v) or merely to redescribe it. Be specific about the logical pathways: do not just list contradictions, but show which commitments commit me to what in the case of (v).
