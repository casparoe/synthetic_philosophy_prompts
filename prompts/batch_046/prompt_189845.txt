I work outside academia—mostly in operations management—but I’ve been reading about the modern Stoic revival on my own, and I keep coming back to the classic dichotomy of control: the idea, usually traced to Epictetus, that some things depend entirely on us (our judgments, intentions, and responses) while everything else—our bodies, reputations, property, external events—does not. Recently, however, I spoke with someone who follows a more contemporary Stoic practice, and she insists on a three-part division rather than two: things fully within our control, things partly within our control, and things entirely outside it. She places efforts and outcomes in that middle zone; for instance, whether I secure a particular job is partly up to me because I can prepare and present myself well, but partly not up to me because of the interviewer’s mood, market conditions, or plain chance.

What I want to understand is whether this modern trichotomy is genuinely an improvement on the classical dichotomy, or whether it merely blurs a boundary that Stoicism intended to keep sharp for ethical clarity. Please compare the two frameworks on their own terms: is the middle category conceptually coherent, or does it collapse under analysis into some mixture of the other two? And here is the twist I have been turning over myself: if our character is gradually shaped through habit, repetition, and response to external conditions—factors that begin outside our control but eventually become internal—doesn’t that suggest a kind of hidden feedback loop? In other words, might there be a dynamic fourth category—things that migrate from outside our control to inside it over time—that both the classical two-part scheme and the modern three-part scheme fail to capture?

Please adjudicate between the dichotomy and the trichotomy, and then tell me whether my idea about this shifting, migratory category exposes a real weakness in both, or whether it is simply a misunderstanding of how Stoics already account for moral development and habituation. I am not asking for citations, authors, or a reading list; I am trying to work out whether the boundary between “me” and “not me” can stay static once you take long-term character formation seriously.
