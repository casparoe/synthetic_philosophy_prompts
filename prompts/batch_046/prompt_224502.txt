I'm preparing a tutorial for first-year metaphysics students on whether conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility. Rather than relying solely on the standard philosophical-zombie argument, I want to center the session on comparing that familiar case with a scenario I've devised: a "functional isomorph" that duplicates every behavioral and neural state of a conscious human but is implemented in a substrate whose physics match carbon-based brains at every observable scale yet differ at a stipulated sub-quantum level, with all phenomenal properties absent by stipulation. I need a teaching plan that specifies the dependency order—which distinctions (epistemic versus metaphysical modality, imaginability versus conceivability, logical versus physical possibility) must be secured before either case can do philosophical work, and which distinctions can be introduced only afterward. Identify the single carrying example—the zombie, my isomorph, or a different borderline case such as inverted spectrum—that best makes the modal reasoning visible rather than merely intuitive, and say what that example must include and what it must deliberately lack. Map the predictable misconceptions in the order students will likely form them: conflating "I cannot imagine failure" with logical necessity, treating physical impossibility as the only relevant modal barrier, and inferring substrate independence directly proves non-physicalism. For each, indicate the precise point in the arc to defuse it. Specify what to explicitly refuse to explain at this level—perhaps full two-dimensional modal frameworks—and propose a concrete check that reveals whether students have grasped conceivability's guiding role rather than just adopted the zombie verdict. The students have no formal logic training but have completed one introductory philosophy-of-mind reading.
