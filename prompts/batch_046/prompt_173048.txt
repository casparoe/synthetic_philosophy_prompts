Consider the long-running historiographical deadlock over the French Revolution, in which three explanatory frameworks have coexisted for decades without convergence: structural accounts that treat class struggle and fiscal crisis as the primary engines of change; revisionist and political-cultural accounts that privilege revolutionary ideology, discourse, and the contingency of symbolic action; and microhistorical or event-centered accounts that insist local accidents, individual choices, and institutional path dependencies render any macro-narrative illusory. Despite extensive empirical research, the debate remains stalled—structuralists dismiss revisionists for neglecting material constraint, revisionists accuse structuralists of economic reductionism, and contingency scholars accumulate counterexamples without overturning either framework. Meanwhile, the related question of whether revolutions necessarily consume their own children—whether radical phases are structurally required or merely path-dependent accidents of leadership and circumstance—remains equally unresolved.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a substantive disagreement about the events of 1789 to 1794, but a methodological and conceptual impasse masked by shared vocabulary. Each camp may be answering a different question: one about long-run causal mechanisms, one about the hermeneutic coherence of revolutionary discourse, and one about counterfactual plausibility at the level of individual decisions. Furthermore, I suspect that the deadlock persists because all three implicitly assume that revolutionary change must be governed by a single dominant temporal logic—either long-durée structure or short-run event—and that recognizing layered, asynchronous temporalities (structural pressure operating on one scale, discursive innovation on another, and institutional collapse on a third) might dissolve the opposition rather than adjudicate it.

Diagnose this disagreement: Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions hidden under one heading? Is there a hidden empirical claim—perhaps about the elasticity of state institutions under fiscal crisis, or about the independence of political language from material interest—that neither side has properly isolated? Or is the question of whether revolutions consume their own children itself ill-posed, because revolution and radical offspring are defined circularly within each interpretive framework? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal model of institutional breakdown, a conceptual distinction between causal explanation and narrative coherence, an acknowledgment that the debate should be disaggregated into separate temporal scales, or an argument that no single framework should be expected to prevail?
