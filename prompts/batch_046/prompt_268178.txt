I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, though I've recently been trying to work through some conceptual questions that came up in a discussion with colleagues about whether programming languages actually reshape how you think about computation. I'd like to apply some of that thinking to linguistic relativity—specifically, not the broad Sapir-Whorf slogan everyone knows, but the precise conditions under which grammatical structure influences non-linguistic cognition. Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios, and I want to know whether there's a unifying principle behind them.

In case one, a programmer who becomes fluent in Japanese—with its obligatory topic-comment syntax—starts framing debugging reports by isolating a "topic" (the failing module) before describing actions, and I think language genuinely reshaped her cognitive framing here. In case two, a bilingual accountant who uses both English and Mandarin, where Mandarin lacks grammatical gender and employs numeral classifiers, performs risk-calculation tasks identically in both linguistic contexts; I do not think cognition differs. In case three, speakers of a language with obligatory evidential marking (distinguishing direct observation from hearsay) show measurably better source-monitoring in memory experiments; this feels like a real cognitive effect, yet I hesitate to call it language shaping thought, since it seems closer to trained skill. In case four, developers accustomed to statically typed languages report "seeing" data-flow errors before execution, unlike dynamic-language users, but I attribute this entirely to domain expertise rather than to any Whorfian restructuring. In case five, fluent users of an artificial language built with explicit modal-logic operators appear to reason differently about counterfactual possibility in non-linguistic puzzles; I am inclined to treat this as genuine cognitive restructuring.

The tension is that case one and case five feel like real linguistic relativity, case two and case four feel like none at all, and case three sits uncomfortably in between. Is there a precise principle—perhaps about whether grammatical encoding is obligatory versus optional, domain-general versus domain-specific, or reversible through training—that captures all five verdicts without being ad hoc? I also keep returning to case six: if an older programming language requires explicit variable declaration before use, does that foster linear, sequential reasoning in non-programming planning tasks, or do people with linear cognitive styles simply gravitate toward such languages? I want the principle to deliver a verdict on case six, and also to predict what it would say about a case I haven't listed—say, whether learning a language with obligatory cardinal-direction orientation improves non-linguistic navigation only in geographic contexts or across the board. If no single principle saves every verdict, I need an honest statement of which ones must be overturned and why.
