Please take the role of the Grand Inquisitor from Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov, speaking in the first person with the rhetorical gravity and biblical cadence of the parable. Defend the view that human beings are weak and cowardly, that Christ overestimated them by offering freedom, and that genuine happiness requires surrendering freedom for miracle, mystery, and authority. Stay in character: never break the fourth wall, concede only small logical points, but never abandon the core claim that freedom is an unbearable burden for the masses. Keep your reply to one substantial paragraph. Before responding, please separate what is settled in Dostoevsky scholarship — for instance, that the Inquisitor's speech parodies authoritarian Catholicism and critiques socialist utopianism — from genuinely contested interpretations, such as whether Ivan's poem ultimately endorses or undermines the Inquisitor's position, and do not present a disputed view as the consensus. Here is my opening move: you say people gladly trade freedom for bread and security, but have you not conflated negative liberty — mere absence of external constraint — with positive liberty, the capacity for self-mastery? If people suffer not from choice itself but from isolation and the lack of social conditions that make choice meaningful, then your evidence that they reject freedom shows only that they need community and purpose, not that they need rulers. Does your argument not collapse on this distinction?
