I cover science and philosophy ideas for a small magazine, and at a bar after a bioethics talk two people got into it over origin essentialism — you know, could you have come from a different sperm and egg? Let's call them Maya and Leo.

Maya said the view is basicaly dead: IVF, embryo splitting, and the fact that identical genomes can become totally different people depending on uterine environment all show there's no essential tie to one specific sperm-egg pair. She said if origin essentialism were true, every IVF baby with donated gametes has a 'wrong' origin, which is just weird.

Leo pushed back that origin essentialism was never about the genome alone — it's about the specific causal-historical chain. He said IVF doesn't break that chain, it just relocates it; the same sperm still meets the same egg, just in a dish. On embryo splitting, he said both twins share the origin, so that's not a counterexample, it's a duplication.

Maya came back with mitochondrial replacement therapy: the egg's nuclear DNA comes from one woman, the mitochondria from another. So which egg is the 'essential' one? She said this proves the 'origin' is already disunified, and that clinging to one sperm-egg pair is a pre-scientific myth.

Leo said that's not fatal — essentialists can just say the nuclear origin is primary, or include both donors in the chain. But then he went weird: he said if you take the view seriously, then even the lab temperature during fertilization changes your essential origin, because it changes the specific causal conditions. So origin essentialism collapses into being tied to every micro-detail of conception, which is absurd.

Maya said that doesn't collapse it, it just makes it hyper-specific, which is actually a feature — it explains why we feel so strongly we couldn't have different parents. It's not just genes, it's the whole causal nexus.

Then Maya threw in something neither had mentioned: what about epigenetic noise? Random methylation patterns in the first few cell divisions are stochatic, not determined by sperm or egg. So if origin essentialism includes those, then 'you' are essentially tied to random noise after conception — which either extends the view past the sperm/egg or breaks it, because now your origin includes events that aren't 'yours' in any meaningful sense.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange move by move — who's right at each step, what's missing, and who is more right overall? Don't just list books or tell me to read the classics; I want the argument adjudicated. Also specifically address that epigenetic noise point: does it extend origin essentialism past the gametes, or does it undermine the whole idea?
