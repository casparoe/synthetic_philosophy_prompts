Write a dialogue between two philosophers: one who defends a reconstructed ontological reading of the white-horse argument associated with Gongsun Long, and one who offers a practical-realist critique drawn from Mohist and Confucian reactions to the School of Names (Mingjia). The first speaker should treat the claim that a white horse is not a horse as a serious metaphysical thesis about the divergence of kind-membership and property-complexes—specifically, that the color-form of whiteness generates a distinct kind, white-horse, which is not coextensive with the bare kind horse understood as color-neutral. The second speaker should resist by appealing to the school’s own concern with similarity and difference (tong-yi), arguing that stable practical categorization requires kind-terms that tolerate accidental properties, and that multiplying kinds for every property-combination renders taxonomy useless for moral and political reasoning. Have them apply this dispute to a concrete modern case—whether a genetically engineered organism that expresses exactly one designed trait should be classified within its ancestral species or as a new kind altogether—so that the abstract metaphysics gains tangible stakes. Both interlocutors must be equally competent: the defender should anticipate the charge of mere wordplay and reply with a distinction between nominal and real kinds; the critic should anticipate the reply that color-form is ontologically decisive and press whether any taxonomy could survive if accidental properties always generated new kinds. The exchange should be genuinely responsive, with each party addressing the other’s specific point rather than speaking past it.
