Here are my verdicts about when I ought to defer to an objective chance, and I want to know whether they hang together. (1) If I believe a fair die will be rolled and I have no further information, my credence that it lands six should match the physical chance of one-sixth. (2) If I peek at the result and see a six, my credence should go near one and the one-sixth should be overridden. (3) If a meteorological model I understand assigns a 0.8 chance of rain tomorrow, and I have no other relevant evidence, my credence in rain should be 0.8. (4) If I learn the model was calibrated only on tropical data and I am in a temperate zone with genuinely different atmospheric dynamics, I should not defer to 0.8; the objective chance relative to my situation is different. (5) If I learn that the 0.8 figure derives from a statistical reference class that deliberately excludes all observers who have asked the specific philosophical question I am now asking about deference, I should reject the figure, even though the underlying physics is unchanged. (6) But if I learn that the reference class excludes all observers with my specific genetic profile, I still feel I should defer to 0.8, treating the exclusion as epistemically benign. The tension is between (5) and (6): in both cases the class excludes me, yet in one I withdraw deference and in the other I do not. Is there a coherent principle of admissible reference-class selection that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Please also state precisely what your best principle predicts for a case I have not listed: suppose I learn that the chance law was computed using a Monte Carlo simulation that included, among its random seeds, the exact sequence of neural firings in my brain at the moment I formed my prior belief about the outcome. Would that render my prior inadmissible in the same way as peeking in (2), or is it still benign in the manner of (6)?
