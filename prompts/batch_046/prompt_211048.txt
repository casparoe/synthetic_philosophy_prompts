As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a long-form feature on how pre-modern thinkers might navigate ethical landscapes that only crystallized centuries after their deaths, and I want to pose a counterfactual that stays rigorously close to Michel de Montaigne’s actual commitments while forcing them into contact with a problem he could not have encountered. The case concerns the intersection of physical anthropology and international heritage law—a domain in which two recent developments have unsettled the very categories Montaigne used when he refused, in his essay on the Tupinambá, to rank so-called cannibal practices beneath European customs.

The first development is bioarchaeological: stable-isotope analysis of prehistoric European remains has shown that cannibalism in several societies was not the ritualized, cosmologically integrated practice that earlier ethnographers contrasted with “savage” violence, but rather an intermittent nutritional strategy adopted during famine. This collapses the ritual-versus-survival distinction that structured much twentieth-century anthropological sympathy. The second is legal-ontological: several heritage frameworks now treat human remains and associated ceremonial objects not as inert artifacts but as “living relational entities” within specific indigenous ontologies, meaning that scientific study or museum display can constitute an ongoing harm to descendant communities regardless of temporal distance. Given Montaigne’s insistence that European religious wars produced cruelties far exceeding anything he attributed to the Tupinambá, and given his skeptical humanism that treated all customs as contingent expressions rather than metaphysically binding truths, how do you think he would have responded to the isotopic demystification of ritual cannibalism? Would he have seen the nutritional evidence as undermining the ethnographic sympathy he extended, or as further exposing European hypocrisy—given that Europeans, too, consumed human flesh in desperation? And how might he have weighed the claim that remains are living relational entities against his tendency to view every cultural framework, including those that sacralize the dead, as a product of local custom rather than an objective moral fact?

I am not asking for a bibliographic survey or a list of secondary literature; I want a reconstructed position that respects the internal logic of Montaigne’s essays while addressing the specific conceptual tension between scientific demystification and ontological pluralism in heritage ethics. In particular, does his cultural relativism have the resources to accommodate an ontology in which the dead remain socially present, or does his humanist skepticism necessarily reduce such claims to another instance of custom-bound belief—no more and no less authoritative than the European burial rites he implicitly treated as equally arbitrary?
