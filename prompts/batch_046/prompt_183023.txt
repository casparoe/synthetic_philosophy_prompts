As a novelist preparing a seminar on ethical reasoning for fiction writers, I have drafted the following examination question and a student’s response for evaluation by a colleague in moral philosophy. I am serving as an external grader and wish to remain neutral regarding the answer’s merit.

Question: Moral particularism, especially as developed by philosophers such as Jonathan Dancy, holds that moral reasons are holistic rather than atomistic: the same feature may count in favor of an action in one situation and against it in another, and moral reasoning does not depend on universal, exceptionless principles. Consider a physician deciding whether to disclose a terminal diagnosis to a patient with severe anxiety. The patient’s anxiety is a reason to disclose gently and with extensive support; yet it is also, in this very case, a reason to delay disclosure, since emotional shock could precipitate a crisis. Meanwhile, respect for autonomy favors disclosure, and non-maleficence favors withholding.

The student is asked whether particularism resolves this conflict by denying that principles like respect autonomy or do no harm exist at all, and by replacing principle-based reasoning with an intuitive weighing of feelings. The student is further asked to address (i) the distinction between the absence of principles and the holism of reasons, (ii) whether autonomy can function as a reason without functioning as a principle, and (iii) the role of moral shape—the structural relation among reasons in a case—in explaining why anxiety can switch its moral polarity.

Student Answer: Particularism resolves the conflict by rejecting all universal moral principles, so neither respect autonomy nor do no harm operates as a principle here. Instead, the physician simply weighs the patient’s anxiety as a feeling-state: anxiety is a reason to disclose gently, but also a reason to delay, and particularism says there is no underlying rule that determines which feature prevails. This is consistent with Dancy’s view that moral reasons are holistic—the feature of anxiety can switch its polarity depending on the situation’s moral shape, which is essentially the overall emotional configuration of the case. Because particularism denies principles, it also denies that respect autonomy can function as a reason at all; autonomy only has moral weight if embedded in a principle, which particularists reject. Consequently, the physician cannot appeal to autonomy except as a subjective preference. Furthermore, because the polarity of any feature is entirely context-dependent, there can be no moral expertise: an expert cannot know in advance whether anxiety will count for or against disclosure. Thus particularism does not provide a decision procedure but replaces principle-based ethics with an intuitive, case-by-case emotional balance.
