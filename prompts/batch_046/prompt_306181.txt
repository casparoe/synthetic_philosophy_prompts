In debates about what a gene, pathway, or engineered trait is genuinely "for," I keep noticing a specific argumentative rhythm that doesn't quite match the standard taxonomy of fallacies. A researcher establishes that removing trait X disrupts process Y in present organisms, and concludes that X functions to carry out Y. When critics object that X might be evolutionary baggage — that selection originally favored it for reason Z and its link to Y is a spandrel or pleiotropic side effect — the defender shifts ground and cites deep conservation or inferred selection history to insist that X was indeed shaped for Y-regulation. But when critics then demand the actual phylogenetic reconstruction and point out that conservation can be maintained by structural constraints or linked selection rather than direct adaptation for Y, the defender retreats to the knock-out data, saying that for clinical intervention or synthetic design, only the present causal link to Y matters. Throughout, the practical prescription — treat X as a Y-regulator — remains untouched, yet the justification oscillates between present causal contribution and historical selection purpose, with each being deployed precisely when the other is under attack.
