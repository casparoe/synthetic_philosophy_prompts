I'm drafting a historical novel that alternates between 1938 and a speculative near-future, and the problem that keeps interrupting my plot notes is how we learn from the past without being imprisoned by it. To discipline my own thinking, I want to treat this as a philosophical exercise rather than a narrative outline: imagine that two scholars—one a committed defender of historical analogy as a disciplined form of practical reasoning, and the other a skeptical historian who treats analogies to Munich, Vietnam, and the so-called Thucydides trap as systematically misleading rhetorical devices—have completed a year-long adversarial collaboration in good faith. Write the joint statement they ought to have produced.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims: about the logical structure of analogical inference, about the specific conditions under which the Munich and Vietnam analogies genuinely fail (not merely that they are overused), and about what can legitimately be said regarding power-transition dynamics without importing false determinism. This should be followed by a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is formulated with precision—worded so that each side would accept the statement of its own view, and separated from disagreements that turn out to be merely verbal. Identify the cruxes: for instance, whether structural similarity between cases requires matching at the level of causal mechanisms rather than surface features, or whether the Thucydides trap confuses observed correlation with structural inevitability in rising-power conflicts. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or piece of historical evidence that both parties would agree bears upon it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence will show.

The language must be neutral enough that neither party would reject its own formulation, yet exact enough that the remaining disagreement is genuinely philosophical rather than rhetorical. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to scholarly authority; treat this as a document both could sign with their actual names, leaving the tension open where it belongs.
