Write a short story about a linguist named Dr. Yen who spends thirty years reconstructing a nearly-dead oral epic that can only be performed in full over twelve consecutive nights, meant to be passed down through generations of singers. When she learns that a genetic bottleneck means no children will be born after 2045, she realizes the epic won't just go unheard -- the very fact that she devoted her life to preserving it now feels like a mistake, not becuase she was wrong about its beauty, but because her commitment assumed a kind of "afterlife" for humanity that won't happen. Have her argue with herself about whether finishing the transcription is still worth it, and whether the extinction of future people retroactively drains meaning from her past efforts in a way that, say, the death of her own ancestors never did. Try to show, not just tell, why human extinction might be bad for reasons that have nothing to do with the actual dying -- maybe through a scene where she tries to teach the epic to an AI archivist that can recite it perfectly but can't grasp why anyone would care about continuity, and she has to explain that the value wasn't in the recitation but in the chain of human hands passing it along. Keep it grounded in one day of her life, not a global epic, and don't make the ending too neat.
