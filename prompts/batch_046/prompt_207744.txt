I've spent the last few months turning over a question that started with a casual argument: is it ever okay to believe something just because it helps you live, even when the proof isn't there? I keep coming back to two voices I half-remember—one insisting that belief must always follow the available evidence, another saying that in certain cases you are entitled to choose your belief based on what it makes possible for your life. But the more I think about concrete situations—needing hope to endure a serious illness, maintaining trust in a friend despite ambiguous evidence, or refusing to adopt a comforting conspiracy—the less those two voices seem to cover everything.

I'm trying to map out the actual logical space of positions on whether and how belief should be regulated. I'd like a taxonomy, built as a decision tree, that starts from questions anyone could answer without knowing philosophy jargon and ends at named positions or clearly marked empty slots.

The first split should be about doxastic control: do you think a person can directly choose to believe something, or is belief always the result of evidence and habit working on you without direct voluntary command?

From there, branch on whether practical stakes—such as survival, emotional stability, or the preservation of a relationship—can ever justify holding a belief that the available evidence does not support. Within this branch, include a specific sub-distinction: is there a meaningful difference between genuinely believing a claim and what I would call working acceptance—acting as if the claim is true for practical reasoning without fully committing to its truth?

Then split on whether there is an asymmetrical duty: is it worse to believe something false than to fail to believe something true, or are the two kinds of error equally serious?

For each leaf, give the name of the position if it has a common one—such as strict evidentialism, pragmatic justification, working-acceptance pragmatism, or virtue-based belief regulation—or mark it unnamed. Each leaf should carry one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, the specific cost its defenders have to accept.

Also map out which combinations of answers seem to have no defenders. For each empty branch, briefly diagnose whether the combination is incoherent, merely unpopular, or genuinely unexplored.

Finally, flag any pairs of positions that this tree cannot cleanly separate given these particular questions, and explain why they collapse into each other here.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations. I want to use this instrument to sort my own thinking, not to gather sources.
