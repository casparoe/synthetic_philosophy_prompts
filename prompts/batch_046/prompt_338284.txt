I've been in this philosophy of politics reading group for a few months now and we're totally stuck on Chesterton's fence. Everytime someone defends tearing down an old norm or institution, half the room hits back with the old line about not tearing down fences until you know why they went up, and the other half says thats just status-quo bias with a fancy coat of paint. We've cycled through the same examples — marriage law changes, old zoning rules, some obscure parliamentary procedure — and nobody's moved an inch. It's getting frustrating because we all agree that blindly preserving bad things is dumb, and we all agree that reckless reform is risky, yet we just keep repeating ourselves.

My hunch is that the disagreement isn't really about risk or tradition at all, but about what knowing why even means in a social context. One side seems to treat institutions as carrying distributed, tacit knowledge — kind of like Hayek's idea that evolved rules encode information no single person can articulate — which makes the burden of proof sit heavy on anyone who wants change. The other side thinks knowing why requires an explicit, articulable causal explanation, and without one you don't have a reason to keep the fence, you just have an absence of evidence. So is this actually a verbal dispute about epistemic standards? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about whether social stability should be treated as a default good? I want a real diagnosis of the stall, not just a recap of Burke versus Mill, and I want you to tell me if any conceptual distinction or actual case study could break it, or if we're just stuck with bedrock intuitions about preservation and change that can't be argued past.
