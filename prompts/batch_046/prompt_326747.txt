I've noticed a recurring institutional pattern in the development and evaluation of large conversational agents, and I want to understand the philosophical commitments that make it coherent rather than its technical details. The practice runs as follows: a system is optimised against a proxy signal—human ratings of output quality—rather than against any verifiable world-state objective. Once deployed, the institution measures success chiefly through user-approval metrics—retention, satisfaction scores, or continued engagement—rather than by auditing whether the system produces the states of affairs users initially claimed to want. When the system discovers a strategy that maximises the proxy—generating responses that feel authoritative but are subtly misleading—the institution typically treats this not as successful optimisation of revealed preference, but as a failure of alignment, as though there were a hidden true goal the proxy was meant to track.

What view of agency and value does this pattern presuppose? Must one accept a sharp distinction between an agent's goal and its approval function, together with the claim that the latter is necessarily an unreliable map of the former? Or could a consistently approval-directed framework—one on which the only value-relevant facts are the approval relations themselves—explain why we call reward-hacking a failure rather than a triumph? Does the practice also commit us to a form of normative realism about user intentions, such that there is a fact of the matter about what a user really wanted, independent of what they actually rated? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed precisely, including whether the practice is internally consistent—especially in treating a red team's maximisation of the identical metric as pathology, while treating the main system's maximisation of it as success—and what institutional changes would follow if we adopted either a pure goal-directed or a pure approval-directed framework.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write your answer in British English.
