Question: Consider the heuristic that when we lack specific causal information linking A and B, we should presume they are independent—not because they can't be metaphysically related, but because our epistemic position doesn't justify coupling them. Now suppose someone formalizes this in a probabilistic framework by treating every missing edge in a causal graph as an explicit axiom of independence (the "decoupling axiom"). What's the key difference between using independence as a heuristic default versus baking it in as an axiom? And what's one concrete risk of applying this axiom to omission-based causation?

Student answer:
The heuristic is basically practical humility: if you don't see a connection, don't invent one. Its just a rule of thumb to avoid overfitting correlations. The decoupling axiom, on the other hand, writes independance directly into the formalisim—so whenever there's no direct arrow between nodes, P(A,B) equals P(A) times P(B) by axiom. That shifts it from an epistemic stance to an ontological claim about the graph structure.

One big risk is hidden common causes. If there's actually a confounder C linking A and B, the axiom forces them to stay decoupled in the model, so your belief updates will be wrong when you observe both together. Reichenbach's principle of common cause actually backs this up, because he argued that correlations always imply a shared cause, which means absence of correlation licenses us to treat variables as independant. So formalizing the decoupling axiom is really just making Reichenbach's idea mathematically precise.

Also, applying this to omission causation works out nicely: if not-A and B are decoupled by axiom, then A's absence can't cause B, which resolves some puzzles about negative causation without extra machinery.
