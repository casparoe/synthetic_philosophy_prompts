design a 75-minute workshop for about 14 people in an ethics or political-philosophy seminar, goal is to make them feel why manipulation and legit persuasion split apart not at deception but at whether you can still stand behind your choice once the framing machinery is visible. call it the mirror-rule game.

split into pairs: one campaign-designer, one citizen. round 1: designers get a fictional policy to push—say, mandatory "attention-restoration breaks" at work. they must build thier pitch using exactly two of three tools: (A) straight argument with full disclosure, (B) emotional ordering that highlights losses but hides nothing, (C) reflexive framing—curating which premises the citizen sees and in what order so the citizen feels they reached the conclusion themself. citizens hear the pitch, then vote yes/no. then the reflection round: designers reveal which tools they used. citizens can either keep thier vote or invoke a meta-veto—rejecting the decision not because the policy is bad, but because they reject the framing that produced it. score it: designers win if the citizen endorses without veto; citizens win if they endorse and rate the framing legitimate, but lose points on veto or on feeling "used." switch roles with a second case—maybe an opt-out organ-donation frame—so everyone plays both sides.

debrief should map the veto moments onto the idea: emotional ordering often survives being mirrored (you know loss aversion is being used and still agree), but reflexive framing usually collapses once you see the premise-selection, because the sense of self-authorship was engineered. thats the mirror-rule in action—persuasion survives transparency about its method, manipulation doesn't. warn that in practice citizens confuse "feeling influenced" with "being manipulated," and designers will overuse reflexive framing because it feels like reasoning rather than pushing. be honest: the activity can't reproduce long-term preference distortion or systemic power gaps, so the facilitator has to supply that verbally. also note what will go wrong first run—designers will hide tool C usage poorly, citizens will veto too aggressively out of pride, and you'll need to stop the round early to clarify that veto is about framing, not policy disagreement. keep materials simple: index cards for tools, a score sheet, no real money needed.
