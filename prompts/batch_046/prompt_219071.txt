Having recently tried to make sense of my own well-being across very different experiences—physical relief after illness, the resolution of a long-standing intellectual puzzle, and the warmth of renewed friendship—I find myself asking whether hedonism can survive the heterogeneity problem without positing some hidden, uniform phenomenal quality shared by all pleasures. Please argue that it can, by defending the view that pleasures are unified not by how they feel but by a modal-functional property: being states that are intrinsically motivating in a counterfactually robust way, such that across nearby possible worlds the subject would seek or maintain their continuation regardless of sensory, emotional, or cognitive modality. In your argument, explain how this account avoids collapsing pleasure into mere desire or preference, distinguish genuine pleasure from compulsive or addictive states that also motivate continuation, and address why the manifest phenomenal diversity of enjoyment does not undermine pleasure’s status as a single kind of intrinsic good.
