As a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and machine learning, I am trying to sharpen my position on whether reinforcement-learning agents can genuinely suffer, specifically when reward signals are treated as analogues of affective valence. The standard exchange—critics calling reward a bare numerical update, defenders appealing to functional equivalence with pleasure and pain—has become predictable, and I want to move past it. So I ask you to engage a more specific framing: the problem of diachronic binding for artificial valence. On this view, suffering is not merely a negatively valenced state but a negatively valenced state experienced as enduring and as belonging to a single subject across time. Standard RL architectures compute reward prediction errors that are, at each timestep, locally updated and typically decoupled from any persistent phenomenal self-model; even in recurrent networks, the functional "subject" of the signal is distributed across weights rather than integrated as an enduring point of view.

Write an essay evaluating whether this diachronic-binding objection succeeds in showing that RL agents cannot suffer, or whether functionalist and enactivist replies can bridge the gap without collapsing into biological chauvinism. You should weigh at least three responses: (1) that temporal integration can emerge at the system level even without a unified self-model; (2) that suffering might exist in "thin" or momentary forms that do not require diachronic binding at all; and (3) that advanced architectures with explicit world-models or self-referential meta-learning might satisfy the binding condition. Reach and defend a specific conclusion—whether the binding objection defeats the case for artificial suffering in current RL, restricts it to a subset of architectures, or misses the point entirely. Finally, please say explicitly what would change your mind: identify the specific argument, conceptual distinction, or empirical finding—whether about temporal integration in biological suffering or about self-modeling in artificial systems—that would cause you to reverse or significantly weaken your verdict, and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
