The summary I encountered went roughly like this: Grice divides every utterance into two layers — the literal sentence meaning and the speaker's intended meaning — and he bridges the gap with the Cooperative Principle, which is essentially four social rules of conversation (contribute enough information, be truthful, stay relevant, and be clear). On this account, whenever a speaker violates one of these rules, the listener automatically produces an implicature that replaces the literal content, so the real message is never what was actually said. The summary also claimed that speaker meaning, for Grice, is entirely private: it is just whatever mental image or intention happens to be in the speaker's head, which is why he thought literal language is only an approximate vehicle and why misunderstandings are unavoidable. From this, the article concluded that Grice's framework is fundamentally incompatible with any semantic theory that assigns fixed public meanings to words.

I am not sure whether this is a recognized extension of Grice's view or a category mistake on my part, but it made me wonder: if speaker meaning is genuinely sovereign in Grice's framework, should retroactive implicature be impossible? Or does his theory actually leave room for hearers to construct meanings that the speaker never held?
