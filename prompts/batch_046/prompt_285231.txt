I've been arguing with friends from my local rationalist meetup about whether Quine's naturalism actually gives him a coherent stance on moral values, or whether it just collapses ethics into sociology with extra ontological steps. One of them joked that if Quine is right, there ought to be a job market for people who professionally eliminate redundant moral entities — and the image stuck with me. Rather than writing another straight essay that rehearses the familiar tension between his empiricism and his quiet anti-realism about ethics, I'd like a sustained deadpan satire that extends his actual logic into an institutional setting where the absurdities become visible without anyone breaking character to point them out.

Please write a parody job advertisement, in the dry bureaucratic voice of a university HR portal, for a tenure-track position in "Applied Ontological Ethics" at a fictional Institute for Predictive Behavioral Science. The ad should take Quine's real commitments — his rejection of first philosophy, his criterion that we should only commit ourselves to entities that are indispensable to our best predictive theories, his tendency to treat moral norms as conventional patterns rather than objective features of reality, and his preference for parsimony over normative authority — and apply them with absolute literalness to every clause: required qualifications, tenure criteria, the departmental diversity statement, a description of the unit's "moral-ontology reduction program," and the benefits package, which might include something like "predictive-validity health coverage" that only reimburses beliefs with measurable behavioral payoff. Invent specific program names, internal committees, or institutional titles that don't correspond to real-world entities; the satire should come from extending Quine's logic into hiring politics and departmental administration, not from inventing a fake Quinean doctrine he never held. Keep it deadpan and legible: a specialist should recognize that you understand the actual philosophical position, and the exaggeration should land through internal consistency rather than winking. Around 600 words. Do not include citations, bibliographies, footnotes, or requests for further reading — the piece should work entirely from the conceptual structure, and it should be fully intelligible to a model without web access.
