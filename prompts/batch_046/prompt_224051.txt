There's this liability rule in some autonomous-trucking regs: when the system requests a handoff, if its internal confidence score was above 0.7 the human driver is liable for whatever happens in the next ten seconds; below 0.4, the manufacturer is. The 0.7/0.4 thresholds are legaly binding but totally arbitrary.

What theory of moral responsibility does that practice presuppose? Does it treat control as quantifiable — like there's a metric for "meaningful human control" — or does it confuse epistemic access with actual agency? Can a control-based view really explain why failing to grab the wheel is blameworthy at 0.71 but excusable at 0.39? And is it consistent with ordinary negligence doctrine, where we don't grade culpability by the defendant's subjective confidence? I'm trying to see whether there's a coherent philosophy hiding behind the numbers, or if the rule is just papering over a responsibility gap with fake precision.
