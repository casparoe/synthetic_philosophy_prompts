Read Alfred Hitchcock’s 1944 film *Lifeboat*—written from a story by John Steinbeck, in which survivors of a torpedoed merchant ship share a small boat with a rescued German U-boat captain—through the lens of lifeboat ethics and the philosophy of triage under radical scarcity. Treat the boat as a closed system of distributive justice in which water, food, space, labor, and the captain’s navigational skill are all scarce, and ask whether the survivors’ debates about the captain—whether to rescue him, trust him, or cast him back—map onto Garrett Hardin’s lifeboat metaphor and its critique of universal rescue obligations, or whether they expose gaps in that framework.

Specifically, I want the reading to engage three conceptual problems that lifeboat ethics raises but rarely resolves: first, the distinction between rescue obligations (duties to those already in the boat) and salvage rights (claims of those still in the water); second, whether the captain’s technical competence creates a utility-based entitlement to survival that can override moral suspicion of him; and third, the problem of moral luck—whether the survivors bear responsibility for the conditions of scarcity they now face. Point to the major scenes that dramatize these tensions—the rescue of the captain from the water, the hidden compass and water-ration debates, the amputation sequence, and the arrival of the final survivor—and say where the film validates a triage calculus, where it resists that calculus by insisting on democratic solidarity or on suspicion of expertise, and where it simply refuses to resolve the dilemma.

The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce Hitchcock and Steinbeck to illustrators of ethical theory, and it should not distort Hardin’s argument to fit the narrative. Say what the confined, interpersonal space of the boat adds to the philosophical debate—perhaps by making abstract distributive choices viscerally interpersonal, or by showing that in extreme scarcity the line between moral patient and resource collapses in ways that argument alone does not capture. End by noting what the film gets right, or complicates, that standard papers on lifeboat ethics tend to miss. No bibliography or citations are needed; I want an honest critical reading that takes both the film’s narrative logic and the ethical framework seriously.
