As a novelist long preoccupied with the hidden architectures that compel individuals to act against their immediate interests, I wish to pose a thought experiment drawn from Max Weber’s enduring puzzle: can culture operate as an independent cause of historical change, or is it only an ornamental echo of material forces? Imagine that Weber had survived into the present and was asked to evaluate the spread of what we might term algorithmic asceticism—a cultural formation now visible among global professional classes, in which people internalize continuously updated carbon-accountability scores and productivity metrics as morally binding obligations, and in which, according to recent sociological observation, this internalization correlates with accelerated adoption of green technologies in ways that resist explanation through price signals or state regulation alone. Would Weber recognize here a genuine elective affinity between a secularized ethic of quantified self-mastery and a new mode of economic rationalization, structurally comparable to the relationship he traced between Calvinist predestination anxiety and the emergence of capitalist accumulation? Or would he contend that the apparent causal power of these values is illusory—that the metrics are themselves products of infrastructural and economic imperatives, with culture serving merely as an ideological veil over the machinery of surveillance capitalism? Reconstruct, in Weber’s conceptual voice, the precise criteria he would apply to distinguish authentic value-driven causation from spurious correlation within systems of real-time moral feedback. Then reach and defend a clear verdict: in this reconstructed Weberian analysis, does culture retain causal autonomy, or has it been fully subsumed by material conditions?
