There's that classic story about the guy who flees Death in Damascus and runs straight into him in Samarra -- the fatalism parable everyone uses in intro phil. But I've noticed a very specifc pattern in how people actually deploy it in arguments, and I can't tell if it's just part of the general fatalism problem or if philosophers have carved it out separately.

The pattern goes like this: in debates about whether you can 'escape' a prediction, people always narrate the escape attempt itself as the hidden mechanism that delivers the outcome. He takes a boat? Then the captain was fated to be there. He hides? Then the hiding place was structurally doomed. But here's the thing -- if he just does totally neutral stuff, like buying bread or talking to a friend, nobody claims those actions were part of the fatal chain. Only the resistance behaviors get retroactively folded in. So it's not "everything you do is predetermined," it's specifically that attempts to avoid the prediction get treated as causally subsumed by it, while compliant or indifferent actions stay outside the story.

I've seen the same move in free will debates: if you try to rebel against a predicted choice, people say the rebellion was itself predicted, but they don't apply that logic to actions that happen to align with the prediction without being attempts to resist. Is there an established name for this asymmetry? If it's just fatalism, what distinguishes it from the broader claim that all actions are fated? And if there's no specific term, how should I define the boundary -- is it about the agent's mental state (trying to avoid), the causal structure, or something else? I'd want a definition that captures why buying bread doesn't count but fleeing to Samarra does.
