Write the full specification of adequacy for a philosophical theory of phenomenal unity applied specifically to split-brain (complete corpus callosotomy) patients. Do not survey existing theories; instead, produce the desiderata—the phenomena the theory must accommodate, the distinctions it must preserve, the neuroanatomical constraints it must respect, and the conceptual contradictions it must avoid—together with the reasoning behind each requirement.

Structure your answer as a numbered list of requirements. For each, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a negotiable preference of one theoretical camp (e.g., global-workspace, integrated-information, or narrative-self approaches), and identify the specific case or result that motivates it.

The list must cover at least the following, though you may add further desiderata if they are genuinely necessary:

1. The classic Sperry-Gazzaniga lateralized-task dissociations: verbal report originating from the left hemisphere, right-hemisphere behavioral responses (e.g., left-hand selection), and the absence of explicit cross-hemispheric awareness during simultaneous bilateral presentation.

2. The persistence of partial emotional, attentional, and sensorimotor integration despite complete callosal section, mediated by subcortical pathways and the anterior commissure, and the theoretical obligation either to explain this residual unity or to show why it does not constitute phenomenal unity.

3. A precise distinction between three forms of unity that are often conflated: (a) synchronous phenomenal binding at a single time-slice; (b) diachronic narrative continuity over time; and (c) interhemispheric arbitration—the capacity for one hemisphere's outputs to be weighted or suppressed by mechanisms outside that hemisphere. The theory must state which of these it takes to be constitutive of "one consciousness" and which are merely instrumental.

4. The subjective first-person reports of split-brain patients, who consistently deny experiencing two separate streams of consciousness despite exhibiting behaviorally dissociable preferences, and the theory's obligation to account for this denial without dismissing it as mere confabulation.

5. Consistency with the neuroanatomy of the corpus callosum and the known limits of interhemispheric information transfer, including the prohibition on positing a single Cartesian "screen" or homuncular observer that receives both hemispheres' outputs.

6. The ability to handle forced-choice conflicts: cases in which the left and right hemispheres generate contradictory behavioral outputs (e.g., opposing manual selections) in response to the same stimulus array, and the theory's obligation to specify whether this represents two conscious agents in conflict or one agent with dissociated sub-personal processes.

After the list, identify the single pair of desiderata that you think is most likely jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether strict synchronous phenomenal unity can coexist with the full behavioral dissociation observed in forced-choice tasks—and explain why the tension is structural rather than merely empirical.

Finally, in no more than one paragraph per view, briefly score how a global-workspace theory, an integrated-information theory, and a narrative-identity theory fare against the list, emphasizing the one requirement each fails most clearly. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the evaluation should rest entirely on conceptual and neuroanatomical grounds.
