After a long argument last night with a friend—who insisted that hedonism collapses the moment you notice that the pleasure of tasting a complex wine, the pleasure of recognizing a hidden logical fallacy, and the pleasure of watching a rival’s carefully constructed plan fail share neither sensory quality, intentional object, nor emotional valence—I want to design a teaching plan for the heterogeneity problem in hedonism. The audience is a philosophy reading group of advanced undergraduates who have read Mill’s Utilitarianism but have little background in philosophy of mind. Do not explain the problem to me; plan the explanation. First, specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, with explicit dependencies: what must be secured before the heterogeneity objection can be made to land with force, and why reversing that order produces confusion. Second, choose one tightly linked carrying trio—one case must be an aesthetic pleasure, one a malicious or ascetic pleasure—that will carry the entire argument; state precisely what shared feature this trio must display, what features it must conspicuously lack, and why a simpler binary comparison (e.g., eating versus solving a proof) would fail to do the work. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form: I anticipate they will conflate phenomenal quality with intentional content, and that they will take the objection to refute hedonism outright rather than to demand a more abstract, determinable account of pleasure. For each misconception, identify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the distinction or counter-example that defuses it. Fourth, provide one check-for-understanding question that reveals whether the students grasp that the problem is structural—about the possibility of a unified kind—rather than an empirical claim about how brains happen to respond. Finally, state what I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the neuroscience of reward-prediction error or the full representationalist debate about pleasure’s intentional structure—and justify why omitting it protects the conceptual point rather than weakening it. Write the plan in formal, self-contained prose; do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
