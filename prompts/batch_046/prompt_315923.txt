Consider the following argument, which I constructed to defend a strong form of absurdism against contemporary subjective-naturalist accounts of meaning, but which now seems to prove far more than I intend.

(1) For any human project to count as genuinely meaningful — rather than as a mere causal compulsion — it must be able to justify itself to the agent who pursues it.
(2) Absurdism, at least in its classical form, denies that there exists any transcendent, cosmic, or divine framework capable of supplying such justification.
(3) Therefore, on absurdism, all justification must be immanent: a project justifies itself only through coherence with other projects and values the agent already holds.
(4) But immanent justification is structurally circular: I justify my commitment to justice by appealing to my identity as just, yet that identity is itself one of the commitments in need of justification.
(5) Hence, on absurdism, no central commitment ever receives non-circular, rational grounding; every project is ultimately adopted through what we might call axiological symmetry — the psychological mechanism that projects value onto one object applies with equal structural force to its negation, absent an external anchor.
(6) If a commitment is adopted without rational grounding, then adopting its opposite is not a rational failure; there is simply no reason-error in abandoning justice for cruelty, or heroic defiance for passive surrender.
(7) Therefore, absurdism implies perfect symmetry among all lives: Sisyphus's rebellion, the quietist's philosophical surrender, and the hedonist's drift occupy exactly the same rational footing.
(8) But absurdism's normative core depends on maintaining that conscious, defiant commitment is nobler and more dignified than surrender or denial; without that ranking, it offers no guidance and collapses into quietism.
(9) So absurdism either covertly relies on an objective normative standard it officially rejects, or it proves that no life can be more meaningful than any other — rendering its own praise of heroic commitment entirely groundless.

If forced to preserve something, I would keep (1) and (3): that meaning requires justification and that transcendence is unavailable. But I want to know whether holding those forces me to abandon (8), or whether there is a hidden slide — perhaps between lacking ultimate grounds and lacking any rational distinction at all — that I can reject instead. Where exactly does the proof go wrong?
