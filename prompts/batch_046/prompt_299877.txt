I spent my career in condensed-matter physics and only recently began reading philosophy of science. I have constructed an argument about Bell's theorem that strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion would dismiss Bohmian mechanics and every other hidden-variable program as not merely false but conceptually incoherent. (1) The Aspect experiments violate Bell's inequalities, so correlations between space-like separated measurements have no local common-cause explanation. (2) Any deterministic reconstruction of these correlations therefore requires direct dynamical connections linking space-like separated regions. (3) Special relativity entails that any direct causal link between space-like events can be Lorentz-transformed into a link running backward in time in another inertial frame. (4) Hence these dynamical connections are not invariant under reversal of temporal order. (5) If a theory's fundamental laws depend on connections whose causal direction reverses across frames, then the theory possesses no well-defined global ordering of cause and effect. (6) Without a well-defined causal order, counterfactual reasoning about interventions collapses, which is a minimal condition for a theory to be scientific rather than merely predictive. (7) So hidden-variable reconstructions are not empirically inadequate but conceptually incoherent. (8) Yet Bohmian mechanics is mathematically consistent, empirically equivalent to standard quantum mechanics, and actively discussed in the literature. Where does the argument go wrong? If forced to choose, I would prefer to keep (1) and (8), but I want to know precisely what I must pay to preserve both. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
