I have been thinking about countries that emerge from widespread violence—South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Argentina’s reversal of its military amnesty and subsequent trials, and Rwanda’s community courts after the genocide. Each made a different choice about whether to grant amnesty, prosecute leaders, or center public truth-telling. Some people argue that comparing these approaches is impossible because each country’s violence was historically unique; others say the real problem is whether a society can stabilize without punishment, or whether knowing the truth changes anything if perpetrators go free.

If I am trying to understand what actually matters for judging or designing such systems—not as a lawyer, but as someone concerned with moral repair—what are the main open conceptual questions? For instance: does amnesty necessarily corrode the rule of law, or can it be structured so that it does not? Is there a coherent distinction between individual victims forgiving perpetrators and a state granting institutional amnesty? And does transitional justice presuppose a specific theory of political legitimacy, or can it operate across competing accounts of state authority?

More specifically, which two or three of these tensions are upstream of the others—such that resolving one would reshape how we approach the rest? Which are tractable for someone without specialized legal training, and which are overrated because settling them would not change practical outcomes? I am less interested in a survey of every country’s experience than in a defended ordering: by what criteria—dependency on other questions, practical impact, tractability—should someone rank these issues, and what kind of evidence or argument would change that ranking?
