I have been trying to understand the practical difference between French laïcité and the Anglo-American multicultural approach, not from textbooks but from watching how schools and workplaces actually handle religious difference. In France the rule seems explicit: religious expression is kept out of public institutions altogether, so the state remains neutral by exclusion. In Britain and North America the promise is the opposite—recognition and accommodation within shared spaces. Yet I keep noticing a pattern that does not fit either description neatly, and I wonder whether it has a proper name.

Consider a religious minority that requests a specific practice—perhaps a particular form of dress, a dietary provision, or a space for prayer. The institution grants the request, but only on condition that the practice be redescribed in secular, individualistic language: it becomes a matter of “personal expression,” “cultural heritage,” or “wellbeing,” rather than a collective obligation rooted in sacred duty. The visible behaviour is preserved, even protected, but its internal meaning is translated into the majority’s conceptual vocabulary. The minority is included, yet the terms of inclusion require them to stop articulating their own claim in its original form. This is not the French model of banning the practice outright, nor is it simple hypocrisy or tokenism; it is a mechanism by which multicultural recognition itself operates as a kind of assimilation, neutralising the substantive content while leaving the surface intact. I have seen this happen with uniform exemptions that are permitted only if stripped of religious signification, and with dietary accommodations that are justified nutritionally rather than sacramentally.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—where protection through recognition becomes a vehicle for conceptual assimilation? I am not asking for broad terms like “cultural assimilation” or “symbolic violence,” but for a precise idea that captures the translation of a practice into majority categories as the price of its public survival. If such a concept exists, how does it differ from laïcité’s explicit exclusion, and from ordinary hypocrisy? If it does not exist, could you name and define it precisely—specifying its boundaries, its necessary conditions, and how it differs from cultural appropriation and from the motte-and-bailey style retreat in argument? I would value a clear carving of the phenomenon over a bare label. Please write your answer in British English.
