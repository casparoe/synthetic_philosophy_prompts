I have noticed a recurring posture in practical reasoning about low-probability, high-magnitude risks, and I want to know whether it has an established name. The pattern runs as follows: a decision-maker rejects an option carrying an astronomically small chance of an outcome so catastrophic that they treat it as categorically excluded, not merely heavily down-weighted. They refuse to assign that outcome a cardinal utility that would permit expected-value comparison, yet they also reject formal decision rules—maximin, a uniform precautionary threshold, or lexical ordering—because applying those consistently would veto ordinary risks they are perfectly willing to tolerate, such as routine surgery, infrastructure maintenance, or vaccination programs with known side-effect profiles. The result is an unstable middle position: an implicit, context-sensitive “infinity veto” that blocks aggregation without supplying a principled boundary between the vetoed case and the tolerated ones. It is not standard risk aversion, since large quantifiable risks are accepted elsewhere; it is not Pascal’s mugging in reverse, since there is no adversary offering astronomical rewards; and it is not the precautionary principle in its usual systematic form. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this oscillation between refusing to quantify a catastrophe and refusing to systematize its exclusion? If not, can you name and delimit the posture, specifying what distinguishes it from adjacent positions and what its boundary conditions are?
