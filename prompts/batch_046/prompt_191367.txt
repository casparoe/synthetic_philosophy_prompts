I am a sixth-form student asked to give the opening remarks at our regional school philosophy workshop. The audience is split between fellow students who have studied the regress problem—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—and parents and teachers with no background in epistemology. I want the speech, lasting about four minutes, to treat that problem as a live constraint on what I can say rather than as decoration: I must not present any claim as self-evidently true, I must not pretend all our commitments form a tidy coherent circle, and I must not invoke an infinite chain of reasons. At the same time I need to welcome everyone and thank the organisers without those gestures feeling hollow. To make this concrete, please frame the welcome itself as a small case study—if I say we are here because philosophical inquiry matters, on what kind of ground does that rest, and can I say it without committing to one of the three positions? Write a polished, formal address in British English that navigates these restrictions, respects both halves of the room, and does not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
