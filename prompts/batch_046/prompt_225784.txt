So I've been thinking about Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome, and people always reach for the crab bucket analogy — you know, crabs in a bucket pulling the one trying to escape back down. Meanwhile the American Dream gets frmaed like a ladder where one person climbing proves the rungs are solid for everyone. But I'm not sure either one actually does the work people want it to do.

Can you audit both analogies properly? I mean map out the correspondances: in the crab bucket, what exactly is the bucket (society? culture? economic structure?), who are the crabs, and what does pulling down map to — envy, social sanctioning, policy? For the ladder, what are the rungs, is the wall the market or the state, and does seeing someone climb realy tell us anything about the rungs below?

Then tell me where each breaks. The crab bucket seems to assume success is visible and zero-sum, like there's only so much air at the top of the bucket — but is that true for status or wealth? The ladder smuggles in that mobility is mostly indivdual effort, which might miss structual barriers. Which of these disanologies actually kill the argument, and which are just noise?

Also, people sometimes conflate Jantelagen with the Australian tall poppy thing, but they're not the same cultural mechanism — does that matter for the analogy? If both metaphors fail, what's a less broken way to frame whether societies should celebrate outliers or level them?
