I recently joined a philosophy discussion circle—mostly academics and a few amateurs like myself—after retiring from a career in molecular ecology. At our last meeting, two members, Dr. Elena Vasquez (a philosopher of mind) and Mr. James Park (a comparative religion scholar), argued about the Buddhist doctrine of anatta and its implications for personal identity. I am reporting their exchange from memory, without taking sides. Here are the moves they made, in order.

(1) Vasquez opened by claiming that anatta is essentially a metaphysical thesis analogous to Hume's bundle theory: there is no enduring mental substance, only successive states, and personal identity is therefore a constructed fiction. She added that the Theravada Abhidhammapiṭaka supports this by analyzing consciousness into momentary citta and cetasika events, leaving no substrate for a self.

(2) Park replied that this frames anatta as a Western-style reductionist ontology, which misreads the suttas. In the Pali Canon, anatta is primarily soteriological: a practice of seeing through clinging by recognizing impermanence, not a denial of all continuity. The Abhidhamma preserves causal continuity through paṭicca-samuppāda—dependent origination linking lives—without requiring a permanent soul.

(3) Vasquez countered that if continuity is purely causal and impersonal, then rebirth and karma transmission become mysterious. She invoked the Milindapañha's chariot analogy: just as a chariot is a conventional designation for parts, so is a person. But she pressed that rebirth requires something to be reborn; without a substrate, Buddhism faces a causal chain with no links and cannot coherently ground moral responsibility across lives.

(4) Park responded that this is a false dichotomy between substance and nothingness. The Theravada taxonomy of citta, cetasika, and rūpa describes momentary events linked by conditioning relations, not by an owner. He noted that even the heterodox Pudgalavādins—who argued for a person neither identical with nor different from the aggregates—rejected both eternalism and annihilationism, showing that Buddhist philosophy has resources for continuity without substance.

(5) Vasquez then argued that if identity is ultimately impersonal, Buddhist ethics collapses into a performative contradiction. The path requires an agent who works on my greed and my mindfulness; liberation is framed as I escaping suffering. If there is no agent, only an impersonal stream of kammic processes, then first-person ethical cultivation is an illusion, and anatta undermines the very practice that produces it.

(6) Park concluded that Vasquez conflates conventional truth with ultimate truth. The I is a strategically necessary fiction for the path, but ultimate analysis reveals its emptiness. The Pudgalavāda controversy itself demonstrates that Buddhists never intended no person whatsoever, but rather no permanent, independent essence. Vasquez, he said, collapses this nuanced distinction into a crude denial that makes Buddhism self-defeating.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say, specifically, which points Vasquez is right about, which points Park is right about, and what important consideration—if any—both are neglecting. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want an assessment of the arguments as they stand.
