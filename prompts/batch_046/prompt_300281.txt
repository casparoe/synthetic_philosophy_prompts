I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar for second-year urban-planning students — technically competent, politically progressive, and convinced that "conservative" just means resisting change for partisan reasons. I want them to understand Burkean conservatism as an epistemic position about institutional knowledge, using Chesterton's fence as its heuristic, not as a partisan slogan. Don't explain the view to me; plan the explanatory arc.

What idea must be in place before Chesterton's fence even sounds like reasoning rather than superstition? In what order should I introduce (a) the distinction between organic prescription and rationalist design, (b) the problem of distributed or tacit knowledge embedded in practices, and (c) the fence heuristic itself — and why must (b) come where you place it?

Choose one carrying example that is concrete, non-obvious, and not the literal fence or railway-crossing story. It should involve an institution these students might genuinely want to reform — perhaps an informal norm or a seemingly arbitrary regulation — that has survived precisely because it solves a coordination problem no single planner designed. What must this example have, and what must it lack, so that applying the fence heuristic to it feels like analysis rather than apology?

Map the misconceptions they will bring or form in sequence: the identification of Burke with mere nostalgia; the reading of the fence as "tradition is always right"; the slide from "we don't know why it exists" to "therefore we must keep it"; and the confusion of this position with generic risk aversion. For each, state the exact point in the arc where I should head it off, and how — without turning the session into a defense of every existing institution.

Finally, give me one check-for-understanding task I can assign in the last fifteen minutes: something that requires them to apply the heuristic to a reform they themselves support, showing whether they can separate "I don't yet understand the function" from "there is no function." And tell me explicitly what I should refuse to cover — perhaps Burke's theological language, Chesterton's Catholic apologetics, or modern partisan deployments — and how to acknowledge that omission honestly without making the concept seem thinner than it is.
