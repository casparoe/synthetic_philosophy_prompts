Exam question: F. H. Bradley argues that if a relation R genuinely connects two terms a and b, then R must itself be related to a and to b, generating a vicious infinite regress; hence, relations cannot be real independent entities, and any proposition asserting a relation must be understood as a unified whole rather than a complex of discrete parts. A proposed response (call it the modal-adverbial view) attempts to dissolve the regress by denying that R is an entity at all: on this view, R is a second-order modal feature—specifically, a joint-possibility condition—of the ordered pair (a,b) taken together. The pair is therefore a single structured entity, and its internal ordering is explained by the modal feature's positional asymmetry, not by an external link. Assess this modal-adverbial response to Bradley. Does it avoid the regress? Does it preserve, or undermine, the unity of the proposition <aRb>? And does it accurately represent what Bradley thought needed unifying?

Student answer: Bradley's regress arises only if we treat relations as real things standing between terms. The modal-adverbial view avoids this by making R a modal property of the pair (a,b) rather than an entity, so there is nothing extra that needs unification. Because the pair is one thing with an internal modal structure, the proposition <aRb> is already unified: a and b are not merged, since the modal feature distinguishes them positionally within the single entity. Bradley was mainly concerned with logical analysis, not with mental acts or absolute idealist judgment, so this solution addresses his real worry. In fact, Russell's theory of external relations had already shown that relations can relate terms without further relations, and the modal-adverbial view improves on Russell by eliminating relations as primitive entities altogether. The only remaining issue is explaining why the pair doesn't collapse into one term, but the positional asymmetry of the modal feature handles that. So Bradley was right that external relations are unreal, but wrong that this threatens propositional unity, since internal modal structure can do all the work.
