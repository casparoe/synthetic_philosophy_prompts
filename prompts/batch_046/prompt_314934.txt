In a graduate seminar on immigration, distributive justice, and the welfare state, a fellow participant repeatedly deploys the one-liner "You can't have open borders and a welfare state" to dismiss proposals that pair unrestricted entry with social-democratic provision. Rather than accepting it as obvious, I want to bring a structured disambiguation to our next meeting. Lay out, as an ordered menu of distinct theses, every precise claim that slogan could be expressing: from tautological or definitional versions, through empirical predictions about fiscal sustainability or political stability, to normative claims about obligations of membership or distributive closure. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, or false, and specify which version best explains its use as a conversation-stopper in this context. Then apply the menu to the following institutional variant, which I want to test: open entry with immediate labor-market access and full tax liability, but with a ten-year delay before migrants may claim means-tested benefits or vote in national elections, while retaining access to emergency health care and public schooling for minor children. Under that design, which of the disambiguated claims—if any—remain intact? Present your answer as a structured list suitable for guiding discussion, without requesting citations, bibliographies, or further reading.
