I'm retired from biology — spent thirty years in a lab — but since I left I've gotten kind of obsessed with philosophy, especially ethics and decision theory. Anyway, I sit on this university oversight commitee for student wellness programs, and we've got a concrete proposal on the table for Thursday. The wellness office wants to pilot these 'adaptive default menus' in two dining halls: the tray layout and digital ordering screens shift the default meal options based on each student's previous picks, with the stated goals of lowering carbon footprint and nudging better nutrition. It's not mandatory — you can still order anything — but the defaults are personalized and hidden, so students don't really notice the architecture is learning thier habits.

Half the board calls this libertarian paternalism done right: freedom of choice is preserved, just the easy path is optimized. The other half — and I'm leaning this way — thinks hidden adaptive nudges are worse than transparent ones because they don't just influence choice, they shape the conditions under which people form preferences. There's also the problem that the algorithm weights sustainability values the university has never formally adopted, so it's smuggling normative commitments into the infrastructure without democratic approval.

Can you draft our decision memo? Recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions — something the wellness office can actually respond to. I want the reasoning to take the autonomy objection seriously: specifically, the worry that this undermines 'reflective autonomy' (not just the freedom to choose, but the capacity to understand and endorse the reasons behind one's choices) rather than just being a harmless nudge. And engage the smuggled-values point honestly. Include concrete conditions and a timeline if you recommend approval with modifications. Most importantly, treat the strongest argument on the losing side with real respect — don't strawman it.
