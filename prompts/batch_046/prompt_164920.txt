I'm working through a recent historiographical dispute about whether non-human entities—pathogens, volcanic aerosols, abrupt climate shifts—should be treated as genuine historical actors rather than mere background conditions. The discussion, which draws on frameworks influenced by Bruno Latour's actor-network theory as well as on longer traditions of structural and environmental history, focuses on concrete cases: for instance, the 1815 Tambora eruption being analyzed not just as a meteorological prelude to the "Year Without a Summer," but as a force that actively reshaped grain markets and diplomatic calculations at the Congress of Vienna; or Yersinia pestis being interpreted as possessing something like strategic agency in dismantling late-medieval labor regimes. Critics object that this vocabulary of agency risks reviving environmental determinism in biological or geological disguise, while defenders argue that distributed causation corrects an anthropocentric bias without necessarily negating human intentionality. The disagreement seems to turn on whether "acting upon history" requires intentionality, or whether causal efficacy of a certain magnitude and pattern is sufficient.

This debate led me back to Montesquieu, who died in 1755 and therefore never encountered either modern epidemiological historiography or the conceptual machinery of "agency" applied to microbes or magma. In The Spirit of Laws, particularly in the books treating climate and geography, he advances a powerful form of environmental determinism: temperature, soil, and terrain shape legal and political institutions with a regularity that operates largely behind human awareness. At the same time, he reserves a significant role for legislative reason—good laws can, within bounds, correct or adapt to climatic pressures—suggesting that he does not treat nature as all-determining. Given this dual commitment, I am trying to imagine his response to the present literature.

Specifically, if Montesquieu were confronted with contemporary claims that the Tambora eruption exercised a form of diplomatic agency by forcing state responses, or that the Black Death pathogen should be read as an actor with interests in restructuring feudal labor—would he welcome these as a vindication of his structural approach? Or would he reject them as a category mistake, insisting that climate and geography supply the conditions within which human reason operates, whereas attributing intentionality-like properties to volcanoes or bacteria collapses the distinction between nature and politics that his theory requires? How do you think he would draw the boundary between "structural determination" (his own climate theory) and "actorhood" (the modern historiographical move)—on ontological grounds, on methodological ones, or on normative grounds about the proper scope of historical explanation? And in his view, what exactly should distinguish explaining a historical outcome through non-human forces from explaining it away?
