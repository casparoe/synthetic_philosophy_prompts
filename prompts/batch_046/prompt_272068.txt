Over the past month I have found myself returning to a professional disagreement that unexpectedly became philosophical. While coordinating a cross-border compliance review with a colleague in Munich, we reached an impasse over whether to submit a documentation packet by the statutory deadline despite a correctable clerical error that harmed no party. He treated the deadline as inviolable and the error as a separate, remediable event; I treated the error as vitiating the submission's integrity and argued for a brief delay justified by substantive fairness. The disagreement seemed to run deeper than policy: it felt like a collision between two folk moral geographies. I have sketched a short essay, tentatively titled Regeltreue and the Remainder: Folk Moral Economies of Order and Conscience, arguing that German folk morality tends to encode rule-following not merely as instrumental discipline but as an expressive practice that sustains a shared world. On this view, breaking a rule—even for a worthy reason—leaves what I am calling a moral remainder: an unpaid debt to the order that was disrupted. American folk morality, by contrast, especially in its dominant liberal and prophetic strands, tends to treat rules as provisional scaffolding for individual conscience, so that a morally justified breach dissolves the obligation rather than leaving a residue. I try to show that this divergence is not simply authoritarian versus libertarian, but concerns whether the moral agent relates to rules as a participant in an ongoing structure or as a sovereign evaluator of outcomes.

The essay then examines two complications. First, American folk morality is not monolithic: there is a strong communitarian strand in certain religious and civic traditions that also values procedural fidelity, and there is a libertarian strand that resists rules precisely because they are collective rather than because they conflict with conscience. Second, the German emphasis on Ordnung contains an internal tension between hierarchical obedience and reciprocal coordination; I suggest that the idea of a moral remainder applies more clearly to the latter, because reciprocal order generates mutual expectations that survive individual exceptions, whereas hierarchical order treats exceptions mainly as failures of authority. I conclude that neither folk morality has the clear better of it: the American view risks moral solipsism, in which every agent becomes a self-certifying exception, while the German view risks reifying procedure into an end that can obscure substantive harm. But I also propose a synthetic possibility—what I call accountable exception—in which one may breach a rule only if one simultaneously acknowledges and attempts to repair the damage done to the shared expectation, thereby honoring the remainder without being imprisoned by it.

I would welcome your comments on this draft, particularly on three points. First, is the distinction between expressive and instrumental rule-following conceptually sharp enough to bear the weight I place on it, or does it collapse once we examine actual justifications people give? Second, have I mischaracterized American folk morality by overemphasizing the prophetic and liberal strands at the expense of more procedural, civic-nationalist, or religious-communal traditions? Third, does the notion of moral remainder genuinely illuminate the German case, or is it an artificial graft from academic moral philosophy onto folk attitudes that do not actually experience rule-breaking as leaving a debt? I am especially interested in whether the essay avoids the usual stereotypes—German rigidity versus American individualism—while still capturing a real divergence in how ordinary people justify exceptions to norms, and whether the proposed synthesis of accountable exception is coherent or merely splits the difference.
