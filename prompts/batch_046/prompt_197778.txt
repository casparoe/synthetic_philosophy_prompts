I am drafting a conceptual taxonomy for my dissertation chapter on the mechanics of moral revolutions—specifically the collapse of practices such as Atlantic chattel slavery and aristocratic dueling—and I want it structured as a decision-tree or flowchart that sorts explanatory commitments by answers to concrete diagnostic cases, not by the labels respondents prefer. The instrument should be built from the logical space first: the branching questions and the combinations they allow, followed by the actual positions mapped onto those combinations, with unoccupied cells noted and briefly diagnosed. The positions that must appear as terminal leaves are: (1) pure rational-persuasion, in which moral argument directly revises beliefs and thereby ends the practice; (2) pure material-interest, in which economic or geopolitical incentives render the norm unsustainable regardless of belief; (3) the Appiah-style honor-driven view, in which elite status dynamics make the practice dishonorable; (4) a hybrid in which material shifts restructure honor codes, so interests operate first and honor second; (5) the Williams-style thesis that norms dissolve not through argument or interest but when the social form of life that made them intelligible collapses, so they become unthinkable rather than wrong; and (6) a view I call normative leakage, in which a practice dies not because it is directly attacked but because adjacent classifications and supporting practices shift, draining its ecological support. To keep the taxonomy from being answerable by encyclopedia recall, I ask you to split the honor-driven branch with a niche but defensible distinction: competitive honor, in which status is zero-sum and elites abandon a practice to avoid being out-ranked by defectors, versus coordinative honor, in which status depends on mutual recognition and the practice collapses once peer defection reaches a coordination threshold. These should yield separate sub-leaves rather than being conflated.

The diagnostic questions—each with two or three answer options—should turn on the following concrete commitments and cases, phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing whether they are an Appiahan or a Williamsian. Case A concerns the British ending of the slave trade and then of colonial slavery in the early nineteenth century: was the decisive factor parliamentary moral argument, the declining profitability of plantation economies, the erosion of slaveholding prestige among British elites, or a leakage effect in which adjacent practices (free labor ideology, naval enforcement norms) made slaveholding administratively incoherent? Case B concerns the disappearance of dueling in Britain and parts of continental Europe in the nineteenth century: did it end primarily because legal prohibition and bureaucratic modernization made it risky, because it was publicly ridiculed as irrational, because aristocratic honor culture was replaced by a bureaucratic status order, or because a critical mass of peers defected and mutual recognition collapsed? Case C is a hypothetical parallel (modeled on patterns like foot-binding): a practice is widely condemned by intellectuals for decades with negligible effect, then vanishes rapidly within one generation after a small shift in marriage-market or labor-market incentives; does this show that arguments eventually prevailed, that arguments were always epiphenomenal to interest-driven coordination failure, or that the norm leaked away once adjacent status markers changed? Commitment D asks whether a norm can be genuinely abolished if the people who abandon it still privately believe it was morally correct—this should separate honor/status explanations (people stop because it is shameful) from interest explanations (they stop despite believing it right) and from rational-persuasion explanations (they stop because they now judge it wrong). Commitment E asks whether there is a meaningful distinction between showing that a norm is incoherent with other values its practitioners already hold and showing that it is wrong by an external standard; this should split the rational-persuasion branch from the Williams-style intelligibility-collapse branch.

Please present the taxonomy in three parts. First, list the branching questions in logical order with their answer options. Second, provide the scoring key that maps each path of answers onto a named position, onto one of the two honor sub-positions, onto normative leakage, or onto an empty cell. Third, for every empty cell, give a brief diagnosis—whether it is incoherent (e.g., pure rational persuasion combined with the belief that people abandon the norm while still privately endorsing it), merely unexplored, or indistinguishable from a neighboring leaf (e.g., if competitive and coordinative honor make identical predictions about dueling, flag that the tree cannot separate them in that case and explain why). Include a short concluding note identifying which distinctions are load-bearing for explaining moral revolutions and which are ornamental for this purpose. The audience is philosophers and social theorists familiar with the debates but not with this particular taxonomy; they should be able to navigate the tree without knowing the technical labels in advance. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or verbatim quotations from texts—paraphrase all claims. Keep the tree to approximately seven to nine branching nodes yielding eight to ten leaves, and ensure that at least one path terminates in an explicitly noted empty combination.
