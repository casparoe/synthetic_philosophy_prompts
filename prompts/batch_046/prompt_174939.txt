I'm writing a dissertation chapter on moral conflict and I'm struck by how little the literature on genuine moral dilemmas has moved since the 1970s and 80s. The defenders—roughly, those in the tradition of Bernard Williams—insist that there are situations where an agent faces two genuinely unconditional obligations that cannot both be fulfilled, and that the appropriate response is not resolution but agent-regret. The critics—often working within a Kantian or consistency-minded framework—reply that "ought implies can" makes such conflicts impossible in principle: if you genuinely cannot do both, then at least one of the apparent obligations was never binding. Both sides have refined these moves repeatedly, yet neither has persuaded the other, and recent work tends to rehearse the same distinctions (pro tanto versus all-things-considered, practical versus theoretical conflict) without breaking the deadlock.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a simple empirical dispute but a layered disagreement about what "genuine moral dilemma" is supposed to name—whether a phenomenological state of being irreducibly torn, a metaphysical relation between obligations that survives rational deliberation, or a structural feature of normative systems that permits "remainder" or residue in rational agency. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a verbal disagreement masked as substantive, a hidden formal question about the consistency of deontic logic, or two fields—moral psychology and normative ethics—answering different questions under a single heading? Given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result about obligation, a conceptual distinction that dissolves one side's claim, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates levels of analysis? Please state how confident you are in this diagnosis and single out the specific point—whether about the logic of obligation, the status of agent-regret, or the partition between psychology and ethics—about which you are least certain.
