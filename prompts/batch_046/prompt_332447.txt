I am writing a philosophical novel centered on a rationalist court advisor—let us call him the Curator—who evaluates cosmological designs for a patron-ruler. To preserve his consistency across chapters, I must determine how he would rule on a new proposal, given four prior verdicts already inscribed in the narrative. The Curator operates within a broadly Leibnizian framework: he weighs compositional variety, the economy of governing laws, and the distribution of suffering, yet he has never explicitly declared whether suffering that is not instrumentally necessary for a greater good functions as an absolute veto or merely as one factor in a broader calculus.

His established record is this. First, he rejected a design of perfect, changeless uniformity—identical rational monads in eternal stasis—citing an intolerable poverty of variety that violates the principle of plenitude. Second, he approved a design of great biodiversity and free will in which every instance of suffering serves as a direct causal prerequisite for a proportionally greater good. Third, he rejected a design that eliminates suffering entirely by substituting constant miraculous intervention for general physical laws, condemning its profligate complexity in the means. Fourth, he approved a deterministic design of rich variety and simple laws in which suffering never arises, because all monadic perceptions are pre-harmonized without conflict.

The new proposal—Designation Theta—offers extraordinary compositional variety, maximally simple governing laws, and universal free will. Within it, a small, fixed population endures intense and eternal suffering that is causally isolated: it enables no virtues, produces no compensating goods, and is not required for the harmony of the remaining world. This suffering is an unavoidable byproduct of the independent causal chains that generate the world's vast pleasures, yet excising it would require either complicating the laws or diminishing the variety of being. The aggregate pleasure elsewhere exceeds this suffering by orders of magnitude.

If I am to write the Curator's judgment consistently with his own track record, what should he decide regarding Designation Theta? Identify the closest analog among his four prior rulings, and state honestly whether his precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, articulate the principle he implicitly adopts by approving Theta, and the different principle he implicitly adopts by rejecting it. Finally, specify the precise further hypothetical design—the decisive test case—that would reveal which of these two governing principles he actually holds.
