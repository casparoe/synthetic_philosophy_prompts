Construct a thought experiment engineered to test whether epistemic akrasia can be avoided when higher-order evidence targets conceptual rather than empirical belief-formation. Design a case with the following specifications: a philosopher, call her Dr. A, holds a first-order belief that modal realism requires an indexical treatment of actuality, formed through a complex inference in possible-world semantics. She then receives higher-order evidence via peer disagreement: two epistemic peers of equal standing, Dr. B and Dr. C, have examined the same inference. Dr. B endorses Dr. A’s conclusion and shares her reasoning path; Dr. C rejects it, having identified a subtle ambiguity in the modal operator that Dr. A and Dr. B both missed because they share identical background assumptions. There is no external arbiter, no further empirical data, and the dispute concerns a purely conceptual point. State the belief, the method of formation, the nearby worlds in which Dr. A’s reasoning is flawed versus sound, and why the disagreement structure is exactly 1-to-1 among the peers who have actually engaged the argument.

Then show that the case meets these theoretical constraints: (a) conciliationism advises Dr. A to reduce her confidence, since she faces an equally qualified dissenter with no independent tie-breaker; (b) steadfastness advises her to hold firm, because her first-order access to her own reasoning is privileged and the dissent traces to a shared blind spot rather than to independent verification; (c) a view that permits epistemic akrasia must determine whether Dr. A can coherently maintain full belief in her conclusion while simultaneously believing that the peer balance renders her reasoning unreliable. Identify the structural feature that forces the divergence—whether it is the symmetry of peer disagreement, the conceptual (non-empirical) domain, or the fact that the dissenting peer has isolated an error invisible to the other two. Explain what happens if that feature is removed, for instance if a fourth peer breaks the symmetry or if the claim becomes empirically testable. Finally, describe precisely how a defender of the uniqueness thesis would attack the case as illegitimate, and whether that attack succeeds without assuming that conceptual reasoning is immune to peer-based calibration.
