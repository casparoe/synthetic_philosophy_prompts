I work in a small fabrication collective where we shape a material called careenium into spherical components we refer to as simmballs. Each ball receives an engraved pattern—something closer to a symbolic mark than to any mechanical feature. Over the past year we have observed something consistently puzzling: when we alter only the engraving, keeping the shape, mass, and chemical composition exactly the same, the careenium behaves differently in assembly. It attracts or repels neighboring pieces in ways that align with the structure of the new pattern rather than with any obvious physical difference.

My partner is convinced that the engraving tool must be leaving microscopic traces—stress fractures, thermal alterations, or some other invisible physical change—that fully account for the new behavior. I have begun to wonder, however, whether the symbol itself is doing genuine work on the substrate. Perhaps the pattern is not merely labeling the ball’s function but actively directing the material properties of the careenium, as though an abstract form could push the physical substance around.

We are now deciding whether to restructure our production philosophy around this possibility: treating the symbolic engraving as the primary mechanism and the careenium substrate as something that responds to it. Before we commit our limited resources to that framework, I want to know whether there is a coherent way to think that symbols can influence physical material without reducing them to hidden physical causes. Is my intuition that the mark is more than a passive label philosophically defensible, or am I mistaking a correlation for a kind of symbolic causation? And if the idea is defensible, what should guide how we design and test our process—what kind of evidence or reasoning would show whether the substrate is truly being directed by the symbol rather than by an undetected physical trace?
