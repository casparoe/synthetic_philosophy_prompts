I am a novelist who has inherited the sealed study of the writer I apprenticed under three decades ago. The space is preserved exactly as he left it: thousands of drafts arranged not by date but by an idiosyncratic emotional taxonomy—frigid, tepid, incinerating—whose logic he never disclosed. While preparing my own novel about the same provincial town he mythologized, I have discovered that the autobiographical events he treated as sacred origin stories—the fire that claimed his sister, the rejection letter from his first publisher—are deliberate fabrications embedded in the archive as load-bearing fictions. I now face three incompatible imperatives: to maintain the study untouched, honoring his false chronology as relic; to expose the forgeries and demolish the legend he constructed; or to absorb the myths silently into my own fiction, using their inspirational power without acknowledging their fraud. Each course threatens to either entomb me in reverence, sever the root of my craft, or make me a borrower of counterfeit grandeur. Given that I must live as a writer and not merely as a curator or a vandal, which disposition of this archive serves the life of art—allowing me to outgrow my teacher without becoming either his tomb-keeper, the destroyer of the very tradition that formed me, or an unknowing heir to counterfeit grandeur?
