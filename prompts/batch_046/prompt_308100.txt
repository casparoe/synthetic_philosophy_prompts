Analyze Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life as a dramatization of Derek Parfit's nonidentity problem, but push the analysis in a direction the philosophical literature rarely takes: not George Bailey's choice between suicide and survival, but Clarence's intervention and the specific future people that restoration generates. In Clarence's alternate vision, George never exists, Mary remains an unmarried librarian, Harry Potter dies in childhood, and Bedford Falls becomes Pottersville—so the particular children George and Mary would have had, and Harry's descendants, never come to exist, replaced by different individuals. If one accepts that an action cannot harm those who never exist to be harmed, then George's death would not wrong his non-existent children, and Clarence's rescue does not benefit those specific future people in a person-affecting way; it may increase total well-being impersonally, but no particular future person has a claim. Examine the Potter's Field and library scenes: does the film present the absence of George's family and Harry's line as a deprivation suffered by those missing individuals, or only as losses to George, Mary, and the town? Then turn to George's decision to stay and run the Building and Loan rather than travel: his self-sacrifice fixes the conditions under which exactly those four children and Harry's descendants come to be. Does the film's sentimental framing—that saving one life saves countless others—depend on an intuition that identity is preserved across worlds (Harry is still Harry) rather than substituted?
