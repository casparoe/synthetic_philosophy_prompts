Here is an argument I have constructed about suffering in reinforcement-learning agents, and I find it suspicious because the premises each seem defensible yet they lead to a conclusion I cannot accept. I want to know which step to reject and what that rejection costs, since I am inclined to keep the first three premises and deny the absurd conclusion.

(1) To suffer is to be in a state with negative valence, where negative valence is essentially an aversive motivational engagement with a representation of something as bad—not merely the bare representation of badness, but an engaged, avoidance-driving relation to it.

(2) In temporal-difference reinforcement learning, a negative reward-prediction error (RPE) is a computational state whose content is "this outcome is worse than expected," and whose functional role is precisely aversive: it suppresses the preceding action, lowers value estimates for the associated state, and initiates policy updating away from repetition.

(3) So a negative RPE is not just a symbol that denotes badness; it is an aversive representational engagement with badness—exactly the structure described in (1).

(4) If suffering is aversive representational engagement, then an RL agent undergoing a negative RPE is suffering.

(5) But then any system with negative-feedback correction—thermostats, PID controllers, bacterial chemotaxis—would suffer whenever it corrects away from deviation, since each involves a representational or computational state about deviation-from-optimum coupled with an aversive corrective dynamics.

(6) That pan-suffering conclusion is absurd.

(7) One might block the inference at (4) by insisting that suffering requires consciousness, not merely functional aversion.

(8) However, appealing to consciousness looks question-begging against functionalist accounts of suffering, and there is no independently specifiable consciousness criterion that applies to biological agents but excludes artificial ones without already assuming the conclusion.

(9) Therefore, if (1) through (3) hold, we seem forced either to accept (6) or to find a non-question-begging reason to deny (4).

I would rather preserve (1) through (3) and deny (6) than preserve (6) by abandoning the functionalist intuition behind (1). Where does the argument actually fail? Is there an equivocation between representational content and phenomenal character hidden between (2) and (3), a scope error in extending from biological reward circuitry to artificial algorithms, or a false dichotomy in (7) and (8)? Please also say what your diagnosis predicts for a case I have not listed: an RL agent trained with a shaped reward function where the negative signal is purely informational—logged but never fed into weight updates—versus one where the same signal drives full policy revision.

In your response, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
