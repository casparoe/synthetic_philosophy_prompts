I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working at the intersection of moral psychology and the history of biography, and I'm preparing a consistency check for a workshop paper on biography as a technology of practical education. Before I present, I want you to take the role of an expert in moral philosophy and philosophy of history, think carefully and systematically, and begin with a brief conceptual mapping of how my commitments depend on one another before auditing where they fracture.

Here are the positions I currently hold together. (a) Plutarch's Parallel Lives should be read primarily as a technology of ethical habituation rather than as historiography; its anachronistic pairings and selective omissions are pedagogical instruments, not scholarly failures. (b) For biography to educate character rather than merely inform, the reader must achieve what I call "situated identification" — seeing the subject's practical reasoning as occurring within a deliberative structure that the reader can recognize as analogously available to herself. (c) Hagiographic traditions — both medieval vitae sanctorum and modern secular equivalents — fail precisely because they suppress the subject's practical reasoning and present virtue as an ontologically distinct status, which blocks situated identification. (d) The modern "disenchantment" school of rigorous archival biography also fails as moral pedagogy, but for the opposite reason: by reducing every action to contingent circumstance and hidden motivation, it dissolves the subject's practical coherence, leaving nothing stable for the reader to identify with. (e) The only viable form is therefore "flawed exemplarity": biography must preserve both the subject's deliberative coherence and their manifest moral limitations, allowing admiration without ontological distancing. (f) At the same time, I maintain that practical wisdom (phronesis) is structurally embedded and not transferable across radically different institutional contexts; one can only genuinely learn from figures whose social and political environments are sufficiently continuous with one's own.

My suspicion is that there is a deep tension between (a) and (f): Plutarch's cross-cultural pairings of Greeks and Romans presuppose that virtue is transferable across precisely the kind of institutional discontinuity that (f) denies. There may also be a hidden conflict between (d) and (e): to achieve flawed exemplarity we seem to need the debunking tools of the disenchantment school to reveal limitations, yet (d) claims those same tools destroy the coherence required by (e). Meanwhile, (b) and (c) might only appear to be in tension with (e), but perhaps they actually support it once the ontology of identification is clarified. Could you rank these tensions by severity, identify which commitments are genuinely incompatible and which only look so, specify what the least costly revision is, and explain what keeping my preferred pair — (b) and (f) — actually costs me? I am particularly interested in whether "situated identification" can be reconceived so that it survives the institutional-embedding constraint without collapsing into the hagiographic error I reject in (c).
