I am preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on what Russell's paradox actually forces us to give up, and I need an interactive taxonomy—not a list of "-isms"—that sorts readers by their underlying commitments rather than by the labels they prefer. The audience understands basic logic and knows that naive comprehension is contradictory, but they do not come in knowing the map of post-paradox foundations. I want a decision tree (or flowchart-style taxonomy) with six to eight branching nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim or case that someone can answer without recognizing technical terminology.

The tree should be built from the logical space first: each node must be a yes/no/which-one question about a specific commitment, and only after the branches are fixed should the actual positions be placed at the leaves. The positions that must appear as terminal nodes are: (1) a Zermelo-Fraenkel-style separationist who denies any universal collection of all sets; (2) a class-theoretic separationist (NBG/MK-style) who allows a universal class but insists it is not a set; (3) a simple or ramified type theorist who treats self-membership as syntactically malformed; (4) a New Foundations-style stratificationist who permits a universal set provided membership conditions are stratified; (5) a predicative constructivist who accepts only those collections built in well-ordered stages; and (6) a structural pluralist who treats the paradox as evidence that no single formal framework captures all legitimate mathematical structure.

For each branching question, use a concrete diagnostic scenario—for example, ask what becomes of the Russell collection (the collection of all sets that do not contain themselves): does it fail to exist because there is no universal domain, because it is a proper class, because the defining formula violates a type or stratification constraint, or because it genuinely both contains and fails to contain itself? Another node should ask whether a universal collection is admissible at all if we restrict logical form rather than domain size. A third should ask whether the resolution is best read as a technical repair to formal language or as a deep ontological limit on what sets can exist.

After mapping the six named positions onto the leaves, identify any unoccupied combinations in the logical space—such as accepting a universal set while rejecting both stratification and class/status distinctions—and briefly diagnose why that branch is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Also flag any pairs the tree cannot cleanly separate—if two positions land on the same branch, say exactly which further distinction would be needed to split them and whether that distinction belongs inside this tree or outside it. Keep the instrument to about eight decision points, present the scoring logic clearly, and write it in polished, magazine-ready prose without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
