I'm preparing a lecture for an advanced seminar on value pluralism, and we've been debating whether moral systems with genuinely incompatible foundational commitments can achieve stable, welfare-enhancing compromises without collapsing into a unified framework. One of my students recently proposed framing the problem through the lens of comparative advantage in international trade: just as nations with different production-possibility frontiers can both gain by specializing according to relative efficiency and exchanging goods they value differently, moral communities with conflicting axiological priorities might realize mutual benefits by trading concessions on issues where their value-rankings diverge — for instance, one community relaxing an absolute prohibition on certain biomedical interventions in exchange for another strengthening communal property protections that the first community ranks lower. Before I evaluate this framing in class, I want to work through its conceptual architecture carefully. Please describe the most important implications of applying a comparative-advantage model of gains-from-trade to moral compromise between value systems. Specifically, address whether the analogy preserves the non-fungibility of certain moral commitments, that is, whether some values resist being priced or bundled for exchange; whether comparative moral advantage can be coherently defined without reducing incommensurable values to a common metric; and whether the model predicts durable equilibrium or strategic defection once the exchange is executed. You should also consider whether the framework smuggles in a hidden assumption that moral values are commodifiable like goods, and what that assumption does to the normative legitimacy of any compromise reached.
