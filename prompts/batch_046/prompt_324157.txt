At a seminar on defence policy, a colleague and I listened to two guests—Mika and Lena—debate whether armed neutrality can survive without universal conscription. Mika opened by arguing that Sweden's return to selective conscription demonstrates that purely professional forces cannot sustain credible neutrality: only a system in which every citizen holds a direct, personal stake in defence makes invasion prohibitively expensive and prevents the state from being captured by a narrow military elite. Lena replied that Finland's long-standing model disproves this, since Finland maintained a credible, alliance-independent posture with a small professional core and rapid reserve mobilisation, which suggests neutrality is sustained by institutional readiness and technological depth rather than by widespread personal armament. Mika then said Lena was mistaking Finland's posture for true neutrality—he described it as a hedged alignment with Western structures—and insisted that Switzerland's militia system proves his point: distributing obligations across the population insulates a state from both external coercion and internal coup risk. Lena countered that Mika was importing a romantic image of the citizen-soldier; she argued that Switzerland's credibility derives mainly from geographic and financial leverage, not from rifles kept at home, and that modern precision warfare and cyber-defence make universal conscription an inefficient allocation of human capital. She added that Sweden's selective service is best understood as a screening mechanism for specialised technical roles, not a democratic ritual, and asked whether Mika's claim was empirical or a disguised normative argument about civic equality. Could you work through their exchange point by point and say who is right at each step? Please distinguish the empirical claim about deterrence from the normative claim about democratic ownership of force, identify what each speaker misses about the institutional details of Finland, Sweden, and Switzerland, and judge whether a middle position—a professional cadre supported by automated, technology-heavy reserve obligations—resolves the tension or merely obscures it. Write your answer in British English.
