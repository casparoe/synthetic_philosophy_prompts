I am a retired biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through questions in philosophy of collective responsibility, and I recently served on an advisory panel for a post-conflict reconciliation board that must repeatedly evaluate whether contemporary nation-states ought to formally acknowledge guilt for historical injustices committed by prior regimes or generations. The board faces cases where direct perpetrators are dead, legal systems have been replaced, and present citizenries include descendants of victims, descendants of perpetrators, and immigrants who arrived long after the events; I want to translate Karl Jaspers's well-known typology—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—into an operational decision procedure that a non-philosopher practitioner could apply case by case without needing to read philosophy. Please construct this as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, each with clear criteria for a sufficient affirmative or negative answer, branching to outcomes such as formal state acknowledgment warranted, individual moral duty only, no institutional attribution appropriate, or referral to a non-political moral body; the procedure must explicitly flag the exact points where empirical assessment ends and normative judgment takes over, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed in procedural clothing. To handle generational transmission, inject a practical "continuity coefficient"—a rough metric assessing whether institutional structures, legal personhood, territorial governance, or economic benefit streams have persisted sufficiently across generations to sustain political guilt, versus cases of total regime discontinuity where only metaphysical solidarity might remain. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) a parliamentary apology and reparations framework for a colonial forced-labor program that ended seventy years ago but whose economic benefits still flow to present state institutions; (2) a university—continuously operating since centuries before the offense—being asked to acknowledge moral guilt for research conducted by a now-defunct department during an authoritarian interlude, with no current faculty involved; and (3) a demand that all citizens born after 1990 share criminal guilt for a genocide committed by a previous government, despite the complete absence of individual culpability. For each case, trace the pathway through your procedure, highlight the specific point where it yields an answer the board might resist or find unwelcome, and state precisely what the procedure cannot settle—especially whether the recognition of metaphysical guilt should ever trigger institutional action, or whether it must remain confined to private moral reflection.
