I serve on the advisory board for a contemplative center that recently piloted a post-retreat experience-reconstruction program. During silent retreats, participants wear integrated biosensors that track ocular fixation, galvanic skin response, and auditory attention at sub-second resolution. Afterward, the center’s software can render each stay in two formats: a unified cinematic timeline that synchronizes every stream into one sequential narrative, or a polyphonic layered rendering that preserves the streams separately—revealing, for instance, that a participant’s emotional arousal peaked during a visual interval when auditory attention was fixed on something else entirely, with no singular point of convergence. A fellow board member insists we should archive only the unified version, arguing that each participant lived through exactly one definite experiential sequence; there was a single “what it was like” occurring inside them, and any reconstruction that fragments it falsifies reality. I am drawn to the layered rendering, yet I also feel the pull of my colleague’s intuition: it genuinely seems to me that I experienced the retreat as one continuous flow, not as a bundle of disconnected drafts being edited in parallel. The board has asked me to recommend an archival standard. Which format is philosophically more defensible as an account of conscious experience, and is the feeling that there was one canonical “inner movie” playing for me best understood as a legitimate phenomenological datum or as a retrospective narrative illusion?
