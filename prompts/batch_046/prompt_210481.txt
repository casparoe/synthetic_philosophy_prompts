I’ve been going back and forth with a friend all evening about whether failing to do something can actually cause harm—specifically, whether omissions count as real causes in metaphysics, not just in law or morality. He read a summary of a philosophical position in an online essay about causation, and he kept insisting that the view is decisive: omissions are never causes, and all talk of causing by not acting is a category error. I want to check whether he is representing the view fairly before I concede anything.

The summary he described goes roughly like this. Philosophers who reject omission-causation do so because an omission is literally the absence of an event—there is no physical occurrence to enter into causal relations. When a gardener neglects to water a plant and it dies, the real causal story involves only positive states: the lack of moisture in the soil, the cessation of cellular processes, and so on. The gardener’s failure is not in that chain; it is just a gap. The summary also addresses counterfactual accounts, saying they try to rescue omissions by defining causation in terms of what would have happened had the agent acted, but that this is merely linguistic trickery that redescribes an absence in hypothetical terms without giving it any real causal power. Finally, it draws a normative conclusion: because omissions are not genuinely causal, moral blame for pure negligence—where there is no prior decision or positive act—must be a confusion; responsibility can only attach to an omission if we can trace it to some earlier positive choice, like the mental act of deciding not to intervene.

Something about this feels too neat, especially the jump from metaphysics to moral responsibility. But I am not sure where the simplification ends and the misrepresentation begins. Is this a fair summary of the anti-omission-causation position? Please go through it carefully: identify which parts accurately capture a live philosophical view, which are standard simplifications that a defender might accept, and which are distortions—particularly regarding how counterfactual theories actually treat omissions, and whether the metaphysical claim really entails that conclusion about blame. I would like to understand what a philosopher working on causation would say is right, what they would wince at, and whether the normative conclusion actually follows from anything true in the metaphysical part.
