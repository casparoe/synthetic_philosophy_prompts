After a recent period of burnout left me wondering whether genuine recovery is merely the absence of stress or something more, I listened to two friends argue over a view one called tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, while all apparently positive goods (joy, achievement, intimacy) are merely instrumental, valued only insofar as they prevent future craving or suffering. (1) The defender said this captures a deep asymmetry: suffering is intrinsically bad in every context, whereas happiness is good only as relief or as a buffer against relapse, not as an independent welfare component. (2) The critic replied that this collapses into therapeutic nihilism—once perfect tranquility is reached, there is no reason to prefer a life of creative work over passive contentment, yet our intuitions clearly favor the former even when both states are equally free of craving. (3) The defender countered that those intuitions are themselves craving-driven—achievement appeals because it signals security and status—and that once craving is genuinely absent, the preference for creative labor dissolves, leaving only practical considerations about sustaining tranquility. (4) The critic then objected that this makes tranquilism unfalsifiable: any apparent counterexample is dismissed as hidden craving, so the view ceases to be a normative theory about welfare and becomes merely a descriptive claim about meditative states. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and indicate whether either party misses something important about whether positive goods can have any non-instrumental standing within a purely negative welfare framework.
