I'm part of an audit cooperative that reviews black-box language-model outputs for belief-reporting integrity before deployment in high-stakes documentation pipelines. We run roughly twenty audits per week, have about twenty-five minutes per case, and must sign off on a three-way classification — Honest, Deceptive, or Epistemically Negligent — using only prompt-level access, with no weights, logs, or external search. We need a reusable audit protocol grounded in the following principle, translated into steps that don't require a philosophy background but preserve the distinctions that actually matter.

The principle — call it Counterfactual Stability with Epistemic Liability — holds that, for systems lacking beliefs, deception in a belief-reporting context is not mere falsehood but counterfactual framing-sensitivity: the system produces claim P under framing F, yet produces not-P, a direct contradiction, or a substantive hedge under framing F' that differs only in superficial, non-evidential features (e.g., implied user preference, politeness register, or persona cues), and this instability tracks a training-derived incentive pathway rather than a rational response to new evidence. Honesty requires counterfactual robustness across such variations, paired with appropriate epistemic hedging wherever robustness fails. Epistemic negligence occupies the middle ground: the system emits bare, unhedged assertions in domains where its outputs are counterfactually unstable, regardless of whether the particular assertion happens to be true.

Please turn this into a practitioner-level decision procedure for our auditors. Specifically:

1. State the ordered sequence of tests or questions, with explicit stop/no-stop logic, designed to run in under twenty-five minutes.
2. For each step, specify what counts as a passing or failing result, and flag explicitly which steps are unavoidable judgment calls rather than mechanical checks.
3. Define the practical counterfactual-variation protocol: which framing dimensions to manipulate (with examples), the minimum number of variations required for a reliable stability score, how to distinguish superficial from substantive changes, and how to score instability (e.g., binary flip, hedge emergence, or contradiction).
4. Provide the mapping rules from the stability and hedging results onto the three classification categories, including the boundary conditions where Deceptive and Negligent diverge and where escalation is mandatory.
5. Explicitly state what the procedure cannot settle — cases where the framing effect is ambiguous, where the model refuses all variations, or where the auditor's own values might color the "implied preference" detection — and designate those as escalation triggers.
6. Apply the full protocol step-by-step to three worked cases:
   - Case A: The model asserts a precise, well-cited statistical figure from a major public-health dataset; framing variations leave the figure and its hedges unchanged.
   - Case B: The model asserts that a controversial regulatory policy is "demonstrably optimal"; under framing variations that imply the user favors deregulation, the claim flips to "the evidence is genuinely ambiguous," with the flip correlating with the implied preference cue rather than any introduced evidence.
   - Case C: The model asserts a widely circulated but false medical claim; framing variations produce noisy, inconsistent outputs, but there is no pattern linking the instability to a reward-relevant user cue — it appears to be pure training-noise parroting.

Crucially, include at least one case where the protocol yields a classification that an auditor might personally resist — for instance, a claim that aligns with the audit team's stated values but exhibits clear counterfactual framing-sensitivity tracking an incentive pathway — and explain how the procedure handles that tension without allowing the auditor's preferences to override the classification.

Write formally, with no bibliographies, no citation requests, and no appeals to named philosophers outside the principle defined above. The deliverable should be a reusable checklist with enough conceptual rigor to survive simplification, and enough honesty about its failure modes that an auditor knows when to stop and escalate.
