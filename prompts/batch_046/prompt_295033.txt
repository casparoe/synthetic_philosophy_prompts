The philosophical dispute over testimony—reductionists holding that testimonial justification is always reducible to perception, memory, and inference, versus anti-reductionists holding that it can be basic or carry presumptive weight—has been largely static for decades despite broad agreement on standard cases such as expert chains, deceptive speakers, and collective belief. My hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but structural: reductionists implicitly model testimony as a causal pipeline that delivers propositional content to an individual believer, while anti-reductionists model it as a social practice that establishes shared epistemic standing, so the two sides are not disagreeing about the same property of belief-formation. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the impasse driven instead by a hidden disagreement about reliability conditions—say, whether large-scale distributed belief networks and face-to-face interaction should be evaluated by the same formal standards—or by an ill-posed question? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about cognitive offloading, a formal model of epistemic dependence, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Please separate what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and avoid presenting a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
