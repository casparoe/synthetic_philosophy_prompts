Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them A and B—who are both well-versed in epistemic probability but disagree about whether the Principal Principle survives a weird edge case. The case: imagine a deterministic physical system whose macro-level outputs match quantum statistical distributions perfectly, but whose micro-dynamics are entirely opaqe. An observer sees the frequencies and updates her credences accordingly. Then, years later, she recieves a definitive chance report revealing the true objective probabilities governing the system—probabilities that diverge slightly from her empirically-derived beliefs. A argues that rationality requires her to defer to the report and revise her credences to match the objective chances, even though this means overriding her well-confirmed empirical reasoning. B argues that this is absurd: either the report is just more evidence to be weighed like any other, in which case the Principal Principle adds nothing to standard Bayesian updating, or it demands she ignore her actual evidence, which is irrational. Have them actually talk to each other—A should defend deference without sounding like she's worshipping a black box, and B should explain why the Principle collapses into evidentialism rather than just dismissing it. Make sure neither is a strawman. Use British spelling, keep the tone casual—like a seminar chat that got heated over coffee—and feel free to include a typo or two. Don't include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; if you refer to the classic statement of the principle, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
