I've been trying to wrap my head around Kant's universalizability tests for a course im taking, and everyone in the discussion section keeps reaching for this board-game analogy: applying the categorical imperitive is like proposing a house rule and then asking whether the game still works if every player uses it on every turn. If the rule collapses the game -- promises become meaningless, trust evaporates, everyone free-rides -- then you've hit a contradiction and the rule is banned. People lean on it to explain both why lying promises fail (the game of promising breaks) and why we shouldn't neglect beneficence (the cooperative game falls apart).

But I want to know how far this actually holds, because it feels like it's smuggling in hidden premises. Can you lay out the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the board, the rules, the players, the "breaking" condition? Then separate where the analogy breaks for perfect duties versus imperfect duties: does the board-game frame treat both kinds of failure as "the game stops working," when Kant actually distinguishes contradiction in conception from contradiction in will? And does it quietly import a consequentialist worry -- we're anxious about whether the social practice survives -- rather than Kant's deontological point about whether a rational will can consistently endorse the maxim? Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that lying promises are forbidden still stand on Kant's own grounds, or was the board-game picture carrying the whole argument? If the analogy collapses, what's a better one that keeps the rational-consistency check without making universalizability sound like we're just optimizing for group coordination?
