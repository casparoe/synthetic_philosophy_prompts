I'm running a phil of bio seminar with about 12 grad students, 90 minutes, one session. I want an activty that makes them feel the spandrels critique in their bones, not just as "not every trait is adaptive" but as the problem of convincing post-hoc narratives that architectural constraints make almost unavoidable.

Design a structured group exercise in 3 teams of 4. Give them a hard design constraint: they are engineering hollow-tube skeletons for imaginary large organisms, which automatically creates internal cavities. Each team gets one cavity and must build the strongest adaptive story for it - ecological niche, survival value, the works, using only paper and markers. But inject this twist: one team is secretly working with a "cascade spandrel" - their cavity only exists because two other cavities are structurally required, and in round two they learn a later evolutionary layer actually co-opted that specific gap for respiration, while the other two remain pure byproducts.

The rules: 20 min to build the narrative, 15 min presentations, 10 min reveal where you show the structural blueprint proving which gaps are mathematically forced by tube geometry. Then they vote anonymously: which cavities are "real" adaptations? They should feel the pull to defend their narrative even after seeing the blueprint.

Then debrief: map the narrative-building phase onto the Panglossian paradigm, the reveal onto Gould and Lewontin's structural argument, and the cascade case onto exaptation blurring the line. Tell me what will predictably go wrong - probly at least one team will insist their spandrel is secretly functional - and be frank about which feature of the actual debate you can't reproduce here (probably the genetic/historical evidence that lets biologists actually distinguish spandrels from adaptations in real organisms).

No lecture up front, don't name Gould or spandrels until after the vote. Make it runnable with just paper, markers, and a printed blueprint you reveal at the end.
