You serve as the senior compliance officer for an international science-funding body that receives several hundred grant proposals per quarter. A recurring feature of these proposals is the “commitment clause”: a sentence in which the applicant performs an explicit pledge regarding future conduct—for example, “We commit to depositing all raw data in a public repository within six months of publication,” or “This laboratory hereby pledges to adhere to the revised animal-welfare protocol for the duration of the award.” Your task is not to assess scientific merit, but to determine, for each clause, whether it constitutes a binding and actionable commissive, a performative misfire, or a vacuous utterance that carries no institutional weight. You must apply this determination repeatedly, under time pressure (no more than fifteen minutes per proposal), without consulting legal counsel in routine cases, and your classification is signed off by you and used by the audit division to trigger follow-up obligations.

Construct a reusable decision procedure—ordered tests, with explicit stopping rules and escalation conditions—grounded in what speech-act theory actually establishes about felicity conditions, speaker authority, institutional uptake, and the distinction between illocutionary force and propositional content. The procedure must be usable by an officer who has read one introductory text but has no philosophical training, and it must survive three specific complications you should treat as standard in your workflow: (i) clauses that are translated from another language, where the performative verb may not have a direct institutional equivalent; (ii) clauses embedded in boilerplate that the applicant did not draft; and (iii) clauses in which the applicant promises something that is already required by the funding rules, raising the question of whether the pledge adds any new obligation.

Your procedure should classify each clause into one of four categories—Valid Commissive, Misfire (specify subtype: misinvocation, misexecution, or institutional deafness), Vacuous Redundancy, or Escalate—and should include a small set of diagnostic test sentences an officer can apply in sequence. Work through three hypothetical cases in detail: (a) a clearly valid pledge made by a principal investigator with explicit authority; (b) a clause that meets all internal felicity conditions but fails because the agency’s audit infrastructure cannot register the specific form of the promise, producing an answer your office will not like (the clause must be treated as non-binding despite its surface validity); and (c) an ambiguous case involving translated boilerplate where the procedure yields “Escalate” rather than a false certainty.

Mark every point where judgment is unavoidable and cannot be eliminated by the procedure, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle (for instance, whether a broken pledge should trigger moral blame or only contractual remedy), and state the conditions under which an officer should override the checklist. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not refer to theories or texts outside the standard speech-act framework in ways that require specialized knowledge. The deliverable is the procedure itself, not an essay defending it.
