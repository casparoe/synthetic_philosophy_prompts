I have been working through the literature on mechanical rigidity and agency, and I keep returning to Dennett’s well-known discussion of Sphex—the digger wasp that performs its burrow-checking sequence with such stereotyped precision that it will repeat the loop indefinitely if an experimenter moves the prey. It seems to me that sphexishness, understood as this kind of non-interruptible behavioral loop, might serve as a precise negative criterion for free will: an agent is unfree precisely to the extent that its actions are sphexish, and freedom emerges exactly when an agent can break the loop through context-sensitive deviation. From this premise, I want to press two related questions.

First, is overcoming sphexishness sufficient for free will, or is it only necessary? There appear to be intermediate cases—reinforcement-learning adjustments, predictive-coding updates, or even simple habituation—that allow an organism to deviate from a fixed script without exhibiting the reflective, reasons-responsive control we typically associate with freedom. Should these count as escapes from sphexishness, or do they reveal that sphexishness is merely one point on a broader continuum of behavioral plasticity? Second, I wonder whether the framework can be extended to artificial systems. If a language model adjusts its outputs based on prompt context rather than emitting a fixed response, has it escaped sphexishness in any philosophically meaningful way? Or is the analogy misguided because such a system lacks the kind of embodied goal-directedness—the burrow and the prey—that makes the wasp’s rigidity conceptually significant in the first place?
