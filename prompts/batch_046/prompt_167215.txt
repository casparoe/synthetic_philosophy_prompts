I'm trying to determine the principle behind my judgements about procreation and non-existence, particularly where the familiar asymmetry between the badness of suffering and the mere non-badness of absent pleasure applies, but I want to test whether one account survives some less familiar configurations.

My verdicts on concrete cases are these:

1. A couple decides never to conceive a child. That potential child would have lived a flourishing life, with deep affection and significant achievement. I judge that the couple wrongs no one; there is simply no harmed party among the non-existent.

2. A couple conceives a child in full knowledge that she will suffer a severe, untreatable condition rendering her life not worth living. They harm her; bringing her into existence is wrong.

3. Through pre-implantation genetic selection, parents may choose embryo A, which will become a person with a very good life (welfare level +80), or embryo B, which will become a person with a substantially better life (welfare level +140). They select A. I do not think the born child is harmed or wronged. Nevertheless, I feel there is a genuine defect in the choice: they had available a way to bring about a better life and declined it, even though no actual person is made worse off.

4. A child is brought into existence with a life that is precisely welfare-neutral: no suffering, no pleasure, no satisfied or frustrated desires. I lean that this is not harmful—no suffering is imposed—but it is also not a benefit to the child.

5. A climate-mitigation policy deliberately prevents the conception of millions of people who would have lived decent lives, in order to avert catastrophic harm to existing populations. I regard this as permissible, and I deny that we wrong those potential people by failing to bring them into existence, despite the enormous aggregate good that is thereby forgone.

6. A couple brings into existence a child whose life is, on balance, barely good: the pleasures marginally exceed the pains. I think they have harmed her. The pains are genuinely bad for her, whereas the absence of the corresponding pleasures in the counterfactual state of non-existence is not bad for anyone.

Is there one coherent principle—perhaps a modified asymmetry, perhaps an identity-sensitive comparative principle—that delivers all six verdicts? State it precisely, identifying which clause accounts for each case. Then apply it to two further scenarios: (a) the parents in case 3 decide not to conceive at all, thereby preventing both A and B from existing; and (b) a child is brought into existence whose life consists entirely of constant, mild, unrelieved boredom—no pain, but also no positive mental states whatsoever.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write the answer in American English.
