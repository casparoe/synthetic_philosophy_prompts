After encountering Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, I became interested in whether formal verification faces an analogous regress: if a proof assistant checks its own logical kernel, what checks the checker? In software we usually accept a minimal trusted kernel and build upward, but I want to understand whether that answer is philosophically legitimate or merely a convenient stopping point.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them A and B—disagreeing over whether Carroll's regress is fatal to any non-circular justification of deduction. A should defend a bootstrapping position drawn from formal methods: a minimal logical kernel can function as a reflective fixed point, analogous to a self-hosting compiler or a fixed-point combinator in lambda calculus that applies to itself, so that justification terminates not in an ungrounded axiom but in stable self-reference. B should defend a stricter foundationalist position: that accepting the kernel without further proof is exactly the arbitrary halt Carroll exposes, and that describing it as a fixed point only renames the circularity without resolving it.

Require real back-and-forth: A should challenge B to explain how practical reasoning could proceed without ever terminating in an unproven premise; B should challenge A to explain whether reflective stability demonstrates correctness or only describes psychological acceptance. Keep the exchange accessible to a technically minded reader without philosophy background, avoid bibliographies or citations, and do not quote Carroll's original text verbatim.
