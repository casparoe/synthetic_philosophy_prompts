A colleague laid out this chain and I can't find the exit. (1) If S knows P, then S knows that P is not the product of a scenario that is subjectively indistinguishable from P. (2) I know I have hands. (3) The brain-in-a-vat scenario is subjectively indistinguishable from having hands, yet I do not know I am not in it. (4) So by (1) and (3), I fail to know I have hands, contradicting (2). (5) The skeptic resolves the tension by denying (2). (6) Apply the same closure principle to scientific knowledge: if researchers know atoms exist, they must know they are not in a high-fidelity simulation that reproduces every observable atomic interaction. (7) They cannot rule out such a simulation. (8) Therefore, either researchers do not know atoms exist, or principle (1) is false. (9) My friend says this proves too much: it does not merely undermine ordinary knowledge but collapses scientific knowledge as well, so (1) must be rejected. I feel a slide occurs between (6) and (8)—perhaps in what "indistinguishable" means across first-person and theoretical domains—but I cannot name the step precisely. Which premise or inference do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? Answer in American English.
