I've been working through the standard literature on the hard problem and keep running into the same stock examples—Mary in her black-and-white room, philosophical zombies, inverted spectra—so I'm trying to design something more surgically precise for a conceptual paper I'm sketching. Can you construct a thought experiment that isolates the explanatory gap specifically over phenomenal presence—the bare "that it is like something" aspect—rather than over qualitative character?

Here are the specifications. Build a single, realistic clinical scenario, no teleportation, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, involving a gradual implant-based neural intervention on one human subject across two phases. In Phase 1, the intervention systematically inverts the qualitative character of the subject's color experience—reds look green, greens look red—while leaving intact both subjective presence and all behavioral and reporting capacities; the subject notices, reports the inversion, and behaves accordingly. In Phase 2, using the same technology applied more extensively to thalamocortical integration loops, the intervention eliminates subjective presence entirely—the subject reports that nothing is missing, denies any experiential absence, behaves identically to Phase 1, and retains every functional and qualitative role that was present before—while, according to the scenario's setup, there is genuinely no longer anything it is like to be the subject.

Present the scenario in no more than two paragraphs, keeping it medically plausible rather than science-fictional. Then walk through the verdict each of three positions delivers: (a) a standard physicalist who identifies consciousness with integrated information processing in the relevant neural architecture; (b) a hard-problem theorist who accepts an irreducible phenomenal presence but allows that qualitative character might be functionally determined; (c) a strong illusionist who denies phenomenal presence altogether. For each, say exactly why Phase 1 is or is not problematic and exactly why Phase 2 is or is not problematic.

Next, do the diagnostic work: identify the single structural feature of Phase 2 that does all the labor in separating the hard-problem theorist from the physicalist—specifically, the feature that allows the physicalist to treat Phase 2 as continuous with Phase 1 while the hard-problem theorist must treat it as a radical discontinuity. Then say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, for instance if the subject in Phase 2 reports feeling an absence rather than denying it. Finally, show how the physicalist would attack the hard-problem theorist's reading of Phase 2, and whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard zombie argument or keeps it conceptually distinct.

Write your answer in American English, keep the reasoning explicit and the clinical details concrete, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—I want to see whether the conceptual architecture holds up on its own without leaning on named texts.
