My colleague in the philosophy of religion, whose research centers on Abhidharma theories of mind and conventional identity, has over the course of this semester advanced the following judgments about whether a conventional self (puggala) is present in various hypothetical scenarios. She works within a no-self (anattā) framework, treating personal identity as at most a pragmatic designation (paññatti) applied to a causally ordered stream of mental moments (citta-santati) rather than to any enduring substance.

Her verdicts so far are these:

First, in a case of gradual neural replacement in which thirty percent of a person’s neurons are substituted over years by synthetic analogues that preserve every causal and functional role, and in which the subject reports uninterrupted first-person awareness and autobiographical memory, she holds that the same conventional self continues; the causal-functional stream remains unbroken, and the reflexive point of awareness (sati) is never disrupted.

Second, in a standard teletransportation scenario—where a brain is scanned at high resolution, the original body is destroyed, and an isomorphic brain is reconstructed elsewhere from the data—she denies that the reconstructed being is the same self. The causal chain linking successive mental moments has been severed, and qualitative similarity, however perfect, cannot substitute for sequential causal dependency.

Third, in a fission case in which a single brain is split and each hemisphere is implanted in a different body, with both resulting beings functioning psychologically, she denies that either successor is identical to the original. A non-branching stream is, on her view, a necessary condition for the conventional designation of a single self.

Fourth, regarding a meditator who enters the attainment of cessation (nirodha-samāpatti) for seven days, during which all mental activity is suspended, and then resumes consciousness in the same body with intact memory, she judges that no self is present during the suspension itself, but that upon resumption the very same conventional self continues, anchored to the dormant potential (bhavaṅga) of that specific stream.

Fifth, in the case of a collective formed by direct neural linking of twelve human brains into a distributed system with shared access to memories and intentions but no single bodily locus and no unified first-person perspective, she denies that there is any self present—not even a plural or collective self—holding instead that we have a novel, non-personal process.

Sixth, in a scenario where a computer simulation runs in real time on a remote server, replicating every mental state of a living monk with bidirectional causal interaction, she judges that the simulation is not the monk’s self but at best a derivative or shadow stream, lacking the original’s autonomous causal history.

Next week’s case is a sequential substrate migration. Here is the setup: for one full year, every mental state produced in a monk’s biological brain immediately causes an isomorphic state in a synthetic neural substrate located remotely, so that both substrates run in perfect synchrony, linked only by high-bandwidth signal transmission. At the end of that year, the biological brain is abruptly destroyed, and the synthetic substrate continues alone, with each new state now caused solely by the preceding synthetic state. The being reports uninterrupted first-person awareness and complete autobiographical memory stretching back through the year of parallel operation.

Please do the following:

Predict my colleague’s verdict on whether the synthetic being after destruction is the same conventional self as the monk before migration. Identify at least two distinct rules or principles that are jointly consistent with her six prior verdicts but that diverge on this new case—one should privilege sequential causal dependency across substrates, the other should privilege the uniqueness of an anchored material history or an undivided reflexive origin. For each rule, state precisely what it predicts about the sequential migration case. Then offer your best guess as to which rule she actually runs, with explicit reasons drawn from the pattern of her prior judgments, and acknowledge the ambiguity if the track record genuinely underdetermines the choice. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from teletransportation, fission, cessation, collective linkage, and real-time simulation—that would most sharply distinguish the two rules you have identified.
