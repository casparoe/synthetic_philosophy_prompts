I spend a lot of time in rationalist-adjacent forums discussing forecasting, AI timelines, and macro-rationality, and I keep encountering a particular stalemate that does not map neatly onto the standard peer-disagreement literature I have read. Two interlocutors agree that they are epistemic peers—similar track records, identical access to evidence, shared reasoning norms—and they disagree on some substantive claim, say a long-range technological forecast. They also agree on the typical failure modes in this domain: hidden incentives, overfitting to recent data, neglect of base rates, and the like. Here is the pattern I keep seeing: both apply a conciliationist rule to their opponent—"since you are a peer who might be affected by bias X, I should shift my credence somewhat toward yours"—but they apply it asymmetrically to themselves. One reasons: "I do not observe bias X operating in my own reasoning, so in the absence of direct internal evidence I should not apply the same downward adjustment to my own view; I stay near my original credence while giving weight to yours." The other reasons: "My inability to introspectively detect bias X in myself is weak evidence that I am free of it, so I should apply the same downward adjustment to my own view that I apply to yours, which pulls us closer together." The result is that both claim to be honoring peer-respect norms, both accuse the other of insufficient self-skepticism, and the disagreement does not converge—it ossifies into a meta-dispute about whose reflexivity conditions are correct. I have seen this in existential-risk debates, in philosophy-of-science arguments about model selection, and in forecasting tournaments where two equally calibrated forecasters never merge despite years of interaction.

Is there an established name for this specific configuration—peer disagreement that persists not because one party rejects conciliation altogether, but because the parties operate with different "self-application rules" for the same conciliation principle? It feels different from straightforward steadfastness, since neither party ignores the peer; it is more like conciliationism being filtered through an asymmetrical reflexivity condition. It also does not seem to reduce to ordinary motivated reasoning, because both sides can articulate their meta-rules clearly and are genuinely trying to be rational. If the literature has no specific term for this asymmetry in reflexive conciliation, what should we call it—perhaps "reflexive conciliation failure," "asymmetrical peer discounting," or something more precise? More importantly, I want to understand its conceptual boundaries: how exactly should we distinguish it from cases where one party simply has a higher prior for being correct, from cases where the real disagreement is about the reliability of the reasoning framework itself rather than about its application, and from the broader psychological tendency to detect biases in others more readily than in oneself? A bare label will not satisfy me; I am after the carving—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie relative to conciliationism and steadfastness.
