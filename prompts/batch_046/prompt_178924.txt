Read Cormac McCarthy's The Road through the lens of Anthropocene periodization, treating the novel's refusal to name its catastrophe as a philosophical claim about how geological epochs are fixed. The story offers only an undifferentiated before and an ash-covered after, with no reference to whether the collapse began in the Industrial Revolution, the nuclear age, or the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration, and no surviving institution capable of ratifying such a boundary. Ask how this narrative erasure illuminates the debate over whether the Anthropocene is discovered in stratigraphic evidence or decided by bodies like the International Commission on Stratigraphy; in particular, examine the father's fragmentary memories, the absence of any archival or scientific authority, and the landscape's refusal to preserve readable layers. Where does the novel's silence about causes and authorities support the view that epochal boundaries are normative judgments rather than natural facts, and where does it resist that reading by implying that human agency has become so total that institutional decision is irrelevant? Conclude with what the work contributes to the philosophical dispute over who decides when an epoch has started that conceptual analysis alone cannot provide.
