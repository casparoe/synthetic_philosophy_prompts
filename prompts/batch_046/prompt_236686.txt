I've been working through arguments on political obligation with a small rationalist reading group, and we keep returning to a problem that feels systematically under-analyzed in the literature: if compatriots genuinely carry special moral weight for one another, what must an adequate theory of that weight explain without either collapsing into bare familiarity bias or licensing arbitrary exclusion? I'm trying to formalize the desiderata that any defensible account of compatriot-priority must satisfy—especially in hard cases where secessionist movements reject the shared values that liberal nationalists typically invoke.

Specifically, the criteria should cover: (1) a principled distinction between the moral force of co-national ties and mere proximity or in-group preference; (2) whether shared institutional reciprocity alone can ground obligations, or whether shared history and value convergence are necessary conditions; (3) consistency with the intuition that catastrophic need elsewhere can sometimes override local distributive priorities; (4) the ability to accommodate secessionist exit without treating every subgroup departure as either automatically legitimate or automatically destructive of the remaining polity's obligations; and (5) scalability—whether the account extends coherently to sub-national regions or supranational associations without contradiction. For each criterion, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a negotiable preference of a particular camp (civic republicans versus liberal nationalists, for instance), and identify whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable—e.g., whether grounding obligations strictly in institutional reciprocity necessarily undermines the normative force of secession by groups that reject those institutions. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories, though brief remarks on where Rawlsian political liberalism or David Miller's nationality thesis land relative to the list are welcome.
