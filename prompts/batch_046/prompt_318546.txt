I am drafting my final reflection for an advanced philosophy seminar at my high school, and I want to check whether I am understanding Daniel Dennett correctly. I recently read his essay The Problem With Counterfeit People, in which he warns that artificial intelligence can now generate entities that interact with us online and are virtually indistinguishable from real human beings, yet lack any genuine inner life or moral standing. This seems to imply that there is a meaningful ontological gap between a conscious person and a behaviorally perfect imitation—something very close to the philosophical zombie, a being that acts exactly like a conscious human but has no consciousness at all. Yet in Consciousness Explained and elsewhere, Dennett spent considerable effort arguing that the zombie idea is incoherent, insisting that once we adopt the intentional stance there is no further hidden fact about consciousness to discover or miss. So I am struggling with an apparent tension: if Dennett now claims that counterfeit people are dangerous precisely because they possess the full appearance of consciousness without the reality, has he not quietly reintroduced the appearance-reality distinction he once sought to dissolve? Or is his actual concern not about missing consciousness at all, but rather about deception, the erosion of social trust, and the vulnerability of democratic discourse? I would appreciate clarification on whether the counterfeit-person argument contradicts his broader anti-zombie stance, and on what exactly makes these entities dangerous in his view.
