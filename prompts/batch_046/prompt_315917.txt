I am delivering the opening remarks at tomorrow's workshop honoring the retirement of my doctoral advisor, a philosopher who spent four decades writing on continuity, temporal logic, and the conceptual structure of infinite processes. The audience will be divided between specialists in logic and philosophy of science who followed her arguments about supertasks, and her family, students from outside the department, and university administrators who have not. Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about Thomson's lamp: specifically, whether the paradox demonstrates that an infinite sequence of toggles in a finite interval genuinely lacks a determinate endpoint, or whether it only reveals that our binary predicates are not preserved under infinite iteration. My friend pressed the stronger reading onto human lives, claiming that if a lamp can undergo infinitely many state-changes with no final condition, then treating a career as reaching a completed retirement state is a category mistake, and that any speech pretending otherwise is structurally false. I need the remarks to honor her actual intellectual commitments—particularly her view that supertasks are conceptually coherent but physically unrealizable—without endorsing my friend's conclusion that accumulation is therefore an illusion, and without saying anything she would reject. The lamp paradox should do real structural work: it should constrain me from asserting that she has now arrived at a static endpoint, that her influence exists in some final on-condition or off-condition, or that the room should understand her career as a finished object with determinate boundaries; at the same time, it should not license me to say that her work left no trace, or that infinite divisibility erases achievement. I want a five-minute address that lets the philosophers hear that I grasp the distinction between conceptual possibility and physical realizability, that lets the family understand why I am refusing the usual metaphors of arrival and closure, and that offers them something genuinely consoling in place of those metaphors. Include one precise formulation of the lamp's logic that both audiences can follow without prior study, and close with a sentence appropriate to speak aloud in a room where half the listeners do not share the technical vocabulary.
