As a graduate student in formal epistemology, I have been tracking a specific failure mode in commonsense-reasoning formalisms that I cannot place in the literature. One builds a planning language with frame axioms: an action changes certain properties, and all others persist. Soon one discovers that a property previously treated as persistent—say, an object’s visible color—should actually change in a narrow subclass of cases, such as a chameleon under direct sunlight with elevated stress hormones.

The researcher therefore adds an exception clause, but that clause introduces new conditions—light intensity, thermal state, physiological markers—that themselves require persistence rules and further exception rules. Over iterations, the ontology of static conditions expands far faster than the ontology of actions, so that change dynamics become a negligible fraction of the formal system; the framework technically works, yet it has metastasized into a taxonomy of conditions rather than a theory of action. I have observed this pattern in extensions of situation calculus, in action-description languages, and in certain non-monotonic frameworks, but I have never seen it isolated and named as its own pathology distinct from the general frame problem or from the ramification problem. Does this specific “condition-inflation spiral” have an established concept in the planning or knowledge-representation literature, and if not, how should it be precisely defined and bounded away from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, from standard frame-problem solutions, and from ramification?
