A friend sketched an argument about Hegel's master-slave dialectic that seems to swallow every hierarchical relationship whole, and I cannot locate the leak. (1) Self-consciousness becomes certain of itself only through being recognized by another self-consciousness. (2) For that recognition to be authoritative rather than empty, the recognizing party must be free and independent—an equal, not a subordinate or a thing. (3) Therefore, genuine recognition can occur only between peers standing in relations of equality. (4) But hierarchical relationships—parent and child, commander and soldier, teacher and student, employer and employee—are precisely those in which one party is subordinate. (5) Hence in every hierarchical bond, either the subordinate's acknowledgment is not genuine recognition because they lack independence, or the superior's acknowledgment comes from someone not their equal. (6) So hierarchical relationships can never provide the recognition necessary for self-consciousness, which means family, civil authority, military command, and economic organization all fail to sustain the very consciousness they depend on. (7) Yet Hegel plainly treats these institutions as sites where recognition is achieved and mediated. The conclusion feels like a reductio: if correct, it dissolves not just mastery but every structured social role. I want to preserve (1) and (2)—the core insight that recognition requires an other who is genuinely self-conscious and free—because giving them up seems to collapse the dialectic into solipsism or thing-relations. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does the error lie in treating equality as a necessary condition rather than an achieved result, or is there a hidden equivocation between the logical structure of recognition and its empirical conditions?
