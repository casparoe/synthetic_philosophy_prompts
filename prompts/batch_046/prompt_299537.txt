I serve on the Joint Committee on Professional Accreditation and Epistemic Standards at our university’s school of medicine and public policy. We are reviewing a proposal to accredit a new Rapid Diagnostic Credential that trains clinicians to render preliminary judgments based on outputs from an opaque ensemble model. The model is well calibrated—its predictions are true in roughly ninety-four percent of cases, and trainees are taught to treat its outputs as presumptively authoritative when accompanied by a standard reliability briefing. The difficulty is that in a non-trivial subset of cases the model reaches the correct diagnosis through spurious feature correlations—for example, a confounding artifact in imaging noise—while the clinician’s justification tracks only the general reliability of the system, not the particular causal pathway that makes this specific judgment true. Structurally, this looks like a Gettier case: justified true belief that falls short of knowledge, except now it is distributed across an institution and embedded in a credentialing framework rather than held by an individual.

Half the committee argues that accreditation should certify only that practitioners can form beliefs that are justified and reliably true, which this program clearly achieves; the other half insists that institutional warrant requires an anti-luck condition—something like a safety or sensitivity constraint—so that accredited practitioners do not merely happen to be right, and that without it we are licensing knowledge in name only. Complicating the matter further, the proposal includes a redundancy protocol: every preliminary judgment must be confirmed by an independent, interpretable heuristic check. Some members believe this eliminates the Gettier problem by supplying an independent justification; others contend it merely creates a second-order Gettier case, in which both justifications happen to align with the truth by coincidence rather than tracking it, or that it relocates the luck into the selection of the heuristic rather than removing it.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion recommending whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions. The memo should specify a concrete decision with a clear implementation or review time frame, should treat the anti-luck position seriously rather than dismissing it as philosophical hair-splitting, and should engage specifically with whether the redundancy protocol genuinely removes epistemic luck or only displaces it. You need not cite literature—argue directly from the conceptual structure—but the reasoning should be precise enough that the researchers and clinicians could respond with concrete adjustments rather than general objections.
