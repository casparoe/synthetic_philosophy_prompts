I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, and I am preparing a polished sequence of interview questions for a podcast colloquium with Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher who defends a normative version of Herbert Simon's bounded-rationality framework applied to scientific theory choice. Voss calls her position lexicographic satisficing: she holds that scientists should not maximize over theoretical virtues—predictive accuracy, simplicity, explanatory breadth—using weighted compensatory trade-offs, but should instead apply a fixed, non-compensatory ordering, accepting the first theory that clears an independent adequacy threshold for virtue one, then virtue two, and so forth, stopping once all thresholds are met. She insists this is not merely a descriptive claim about cognitive limits but an independent epistemic strategy, on the grounds that compensatory weighing introduces framing-dependent weights that erode intersubjective stability, whereas lexicographic rules preserve it. I want questions that avoid biographical preliminaries and do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Specifically, please draft: (a) an opening question that distinguishes her normative claim from Simon's descriptive psychology, pressing on historical cases—such as the retention of Newtonian mechanics in applied engineering long after the acceptance of general relativity—where retention appears driven by pragmatic domain restriction rather than by a sequential virtue-check; (b) a sharp follow-up anticipating her reply that domain restriction is itself encoded in the lexicographic ordering, asking whether that move renders her view unfalsifiable; (c) a question about whether her rejection of trade-offs forces her to deny that rational scientists ever rationally accept a less simple but more predictively accurate theory, together with a follow-up keyed to her probable appeal to a threshold of sufficiency in predictive accuracy beyond which simplicity is not sacrificed; (d) a question drawing an analogy—without requiring expertise in economic theory—between her defense of decentralized, rule-governed theory choice and the socialist calculation debate, asking whether her argument that local lexicographic rules avoid global optimization echoes Oskar Lange's iterative trial-and-error pricing, and whether she consequently faces a parallel Hayekian critique about dispersed local knowledge being unable to determine the correct ordering of virtues across distinct scientific domains; and (e) a closing question asking what empirical pattern or historical case study could genuinely count as evidence against lexicographic satisficing, given her tendency to reclassify apparent counterexamples as misapplied domain restrictions. The questions should be sequential, suitable for an audience of both philosophers and working scientists, and should press precisely where the literature on scientific rationality and bounded rationality presses.
