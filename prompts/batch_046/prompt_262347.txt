Two philosophers at a seminar debated a modified aggregation case. The first asked us to choose between torturing one person and causing 3^^^3 people to feel a barely noticeable eye irritation, but added a technological twist: imagine a neural-link system that phenomenally binds all those irritated people into a single distributed conscious field, so that every micro-irritation is experienced within one unified awareness, though no individual node suffers more than a dust speck's worth. She argued that if this bound aggregate is not morally equivalent to torture, then the wrongness of torture cannot depend on the intensity or qualitative character of suffering alone, but must depend on something like phenomenal unification—yet since the bound case does unify the suffering, it should indeed be equivalent, showing that aggregation can bridge the gap when consciousness is shared. The second objected that this misdescribes the situation: there is still no one subject enduring intense pain, only many subjects each enduring trivial pain within a common frame, so the moral difference remains intact. He added that if her view were correct, then any large crowd experiencing mild discomfort together—everyone in a stadium feeling slightly warm—would generate a moral catastrophe comparable to torture, which is absurd. She replied that the stadium case lacks both intentional imposition and the specific qualitative profile of irritation, so the reductio fails, and that his appeal to discrete subjects begs the question against distributed moral ontology. They asked for an adjudication. Please assess their exchange move by move: evaluate the initial technological twist, the argument from phenomenal unification, the stadium reductio, and the reply about intentionality and qualitative profile. Say who is right about which specific point, identify what both sides miss—particularly regarding whether phenomenal binding actually transforms the moral arithmetic or merely redescribes it—and state which position is more defensible overall.
