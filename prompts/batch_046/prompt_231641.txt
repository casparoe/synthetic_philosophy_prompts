I am a high school student preparing an independent study presentation on the philosophy of consciousness, and I am trying to understand whether split-brain patients contain one conscious subject or two. To organize my analysis, I have reconstructed my philosophy professor’s verdicts across four classroom cases.

Case 1: A classic adult case of complete surgical section of the corpus callosum, leaving subcortical and brainstem pathways intact but removing all direct cortical interhemispheric connections. The professor’s verdict was that two distinct streams of conscious awareness operate in parallel within a single body.

Case 2: A congenital case of total agenesis of the corpus callosum, in which interhemispheric cortical connections never developed, yet the individual behaves in a fully unified manner and reports a single point of view. The professor’s verdict was that this is one consciousness; the absence of a pathway that never existed does not produce division.

Case 3: A partial surgical section that spares the anterior commissure and related subcortical links. The left hemisphere dominates verbal report and explicit reasoning, the right hemisphere recognizes faces and processes emotional tone, emotional responses integrate across visual fields, and there is no bimanual conflict. The professor’s verdict was that this represents one consciousness with domain-specific modular isolation, not a true split of the subject.

Case 4: A complete surgical section in an adult with intact subcortical pathways. The left hemisphere produces confabulating explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere, and there is clear bimanual conflict in which the two hands work at cross-purposes. The professor’s verdict was that there are two centers of narrative agency and volitional control, though shared subcortical mechanisms may preserve a unitary affective substrate.

Now consider a new hypothetical case. An adult patient has undergone complete surgical section of the corpus callosum in all regions, with the anterior commissure and brainstem pathways remaining intact. Post-operatively, the patient shows no bimanual conflict; both hands cooperate smoothly in manual tasks. The right hemisphere can match emotional expressions presented in the left visual field to those presented in the right visual field, suggesting cross-hemispheric affective integration. Nevertheless, stimuli presented exclusively to the right hemisphere remain verbally unreportable. Crucially, when asked why the left hand made a particular choice guided by the right hemisphere, the patient does not confabulate; instead, the left hemisphere states that it does not know. At the same time, in forced-choice paradigms, the left and right hemispheres can select different responses simultaneously, with each hand pointing to a different option.

Please do the following. First, identify at least two plausible general rules that could jointly account for the professor’s four verdicts, stating the core criterion of each. Fourth, give your best guess about what the professor should say for consistency, with the reasoning behind that preference. Fifth, state explicitly what principle the professor would be adopting if they conclude there is one consciousness, and what principle they would be adopting if they conclude there are two. Sixth, describe one further hypothetical case that would clearly distinguish between the two rules you identified.
