I am a senior in my school's philosophy elective, and our instructor assigned a peer-review exercise on the philosophy of fiction. The essay prompt asked students to address the classical paradox of fiction—how we can experience genuine emotional responses toward entities we know are not real—through the lens of a newer case: interactive narrative systems in which an AI-generated companion, whose dialogue is procedurally produced and whose personality evolves based on player behavior, is permanently erased by an in-game event. The student was asked specifically whether this scenario strengthens the formulation associated with Colin Radford, whether Kendall Walton's account of make-believe can accommodate emotional attachment to an entity that lacks both real existence and a fixed authorial script, and whether the player's knowledge that the companion is algorithmic rather than conscious resolves the tension.

My partner's answer begins with a clear summary of Radford's formulation: we seem to hold the belief that the fictional entity does not exist, yet we respond with what feels like genuine sorrow or fear, which appears irrational because rational emotion usually requires belief in the reality of its object. The student then explains Walton's theory well, describing it as a framework in which we participate in a game of make-believe that authorizes responses that are like emotions but not fully belief-committed, so that feeling pity for Anna Karenina is not contradictory but part of an imaginative practice. So far the analysis is accurate and well-structured.

Where the answer becomes problematic is in its application to the AI companion case and in a couple of attributions. The student claims that Walton's view depends on there being a single, stable fictional world established by an author, and therefore that procedurally generated characters fall outside its scope because no author fixes their traits. From this, the student concludes that the grief felt upon deletion is not a quasi-emotional response at all but a direct reaction to the real loss of accumulated interaction history—meaning there is no paradox, only ordinary sadness about lost time. Along the way, the student also writes that Radford himself proposed suspension of disbelief as the mechanism that allows emotional engagement with fiction, and that Peter Lamarque's alternative is to deny that we ever direct emotions at fictional objects, insisting instead that we only feel general sympathy for human suffering represented in art. The student gives this essay a B-range grade in my preliminary notes, but I am unsure whether the missteps are serious enough to lower it further, especially since the writing is clear and the comparison between the classical case and the algorithmic case is original.
