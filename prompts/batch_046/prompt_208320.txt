I am a doctoral student in moral epistemology, and I am trying to map the implicit framework my advisor uses when she evaluates reliance on moral testimony. Over the past semester she has delivered the following verdicts in our seminar discussions:

(1) A layperson who comes to believe that climate change is occurring solely on the testimony of climate scientists holds a justified belief; demanding that she personally evaluate the climate models would be an unreasonable form of epistemic exceptionalism.

(2) A person who accepts, purely on the testimony of a moral philosopher, the normative claim that torture is categorically wrong also holds a justified belief; there is no special first-personal access condition for normative truths of this kind.

(3) A young child who believes that hitting other people is wrong solely because her parents said so possesses genuine moral knowledge rather than mere parroting.

(4) A philosopher who rejects all testimony about wild-animal suffering on the grounds that only direct acquaintance with animal pain can ground moral belief about it is guilty of an implausible exceptionalism that we would never apply to testimony about human suffering.

(5) A person who accepts on expert testimony that conventional crop agriculture kills more sentient wild animals per calorie produced than pasture-based beef production, and who on that basis forms the belief that beef is morally preferable to grains, but who takes no corresponding action in her own life—she said this testimonial belief is acceptable, though she added that if the belief were to guide one's own dietary choices one should have at least a rough grasp of the reasoning behind the empirical comparison.

Next week we will discuss a variant of case (5): The same graduate student accepts the very same testimonial claim—that crop cultivation kills more wild animals per calorie than beef production—solely on the authority of an animal-welfare researcher. She admits she has not examined the mortality-estimate literature, the ecological density studies, or the comparative life-cycle analyses. Unlike the person in (5), however, she uses this belief as the direct premise in an argument that she herself, and others like her, ought to switch from plant-based diets to beef consumption in order to minimize wild-animal suffering. She also concedes that if the empirical comparison turned out to be false, her normative recommendation would collapse.

Predict my advisor's verdict on whether this testimonial belief is epistemically acceptable in this action-guiding context. Identify at least two distinct principles that could consistently explain her verdicts in cases (1) through (5)—for instance, a principle of universal legitimacy for moral testimony, and a principle requiring first-personal understanding for beliefs that directly guide one's own actions. Show how each principle predicts a different outcome for the new case. Give your best-guess prediction of her actual verdict, with the specific reasons that favor it over the alternative. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—distinct from (1)–(5) and from the new case—that would settle which of the two principles she is actually running.
