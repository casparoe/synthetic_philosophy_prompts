Please compose an opening address—approximately seven minutes in delivery—for the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop held in a university lecture theatre, entitled Contingency, Class, and the Consumption of Revolutions: Re-reading 1794. You are addressing the room as the chair, a philosopher of history; your audience is divided evenly between specialist social historians of the French Revolution and moral philosophers with no archival training. The workshop confronts the familiar claim that revolutions devour their children, and your welcome must treat this not as a rhetorical flourish but as a live conceptual problem: does it name a structural inevitability embedded in revolutionary ideology, a contingent failure of particular decisions taken between 1793 and 1794, or an epiphenomenon of deeper class struggle? This philosophical distinction—between structural inevitability and historical contingency—should do genuine structural work in the speech: it ought to determine what tone of commemoration is fitting, which questions may legitimately be put to the historians present, and which moral judgements about the Terror must be held in abeyance rather than asserted. To avoid mere textbook rehearsal, inject and develop one specific conceptual framing that is not standard curricular material: the notion of a ‘Thermidorian epistemic debt’, by which any interpretation of revolutionary violence incurs an obligation to reconstruct what was actually knowable to actors at specific decision points, rather than assessing them solely by outcomes. Use this framing to navigate among class-struggle, ideological, and contingency-based interpretations without treating any as settled. The address must be rigorous enough that the historians recognise conceptual precision, yet accessible enough that the philosophers are not lost in archival detail. Write entirely in British English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the audience to accept authoritative attributions of specific phrases.
