For my philosophy club’s independent study on political philosophy, I tried to extend Marx’s analysis of exploitation and alienation beyond factory labor, and the result seems to prove far too much. The argument runs as follows: (1) On Marx’s structural account, exploitation is not primarily about unfair wages but about the extraction of surplus value—labor produces more value than it receives back. (2) Alienation, in the same framework, is not merely feeling unhappy but the separation of the worker from her product, her productive activity, her species-being, and other people, because capital directs the labor process for its own ends. (3) If these two concepts are genuinely connected, then the core wrong of exploitation is that one party’s life-activity and developing capacities are directed by another party for the latter’s benefit. (4) Compulsory education fits this structure precisely: the state and institution design the curriculum; students realize intellectual capacities within parameters they do not control; and the resulting human capital benefits society and employers far more than the direct compensation students receive. (5) So compulsory education is structurally exploitative and alienating in Marx’s technical sense. (6) But this applies with equal force to voluntary apprenticeship, athletic coaching, or even self-directed study guided by a mentor—if any directed development of capacities counts as extraction for another’s benefit, then almost all pedagogy becomes exploitation. (7) Therefore either Marx’s framework collapses into an absurd overgeneralization, or we must arbitrarily restrict premise (3). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, but I want to know whether saving them requires abandoning the link between exploitation and alienation, or whether the mistake lies in a hidden equivocation elsewhere—perhaps between economic surplus-value and the metaphysical direction of human capacities.
