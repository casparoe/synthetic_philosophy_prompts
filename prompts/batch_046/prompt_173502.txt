There's this thing in logic education and proof-assistant design that's always struck me as philosophically loaded but nobody talks about it. When you write a formal proof — say in a natural deduction system or inside Lean — you list your premises up front and defend them, but the inference rules (modus ponens, universal elimination, whatever) are just... there. You apply them, but you don't cite them as premises that need backing. Carroll's tortoise dialogue points out the weirdness: if applying a rule requires accepting the rule, then shouldn't the rule itself be a premise? And then applying that premise needs another rule, ad infinitum. So the practice of keeping rules and premises in seperate boxes — rules in the kernel or in the textbook's sidebar, premises in the argument — what does that presuppose? Is it saying logical rules aren't truths but permissions? Or that they're self-justifying? Or is it just a pragmatic division with no deep philosophy behind it?

I want you to dig into the implicit epistemology/logic here. Does this practice commit us to conventionalism, or to some kind of inferentialism where rules are primitive? Could a logical realist — someone who thinks modus ponens is true in the same way a physical law is true — still accept this division, or do they have to treat rules differently? And if we actually redesigned logic courses or proof checkers to take the tortoise seriously — putting every applied rule into the explicit premise list with its own justification chain — what would break? Would it be incoherent, or just unwieldy?

Also, is the practice actually consistent with itself? In proof assistants like Lean there's this split between the small trusted kernel (with hard-coded primitive rules) and the tactic layer (where users apply complex derived rules without seeing every step). Does that architecture presuppose one view of deduction, while the way we teach students to write proof trees by hand presupposes another? Reconstruct the implicit theories, say where they're underdetermined, and tell me what'd have to change if we replaced them with a view that takes Carroll's regress seriously.
