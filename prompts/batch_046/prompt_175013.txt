As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to make sense of self-deception through analogies to distributed systems: a node can store data that the executive process never queries, and a monitoring module can suppress alerts that the system itself generated. This raises a conceptual puzzle—can a single agent genuinely deceive itself, or does the appearance of deception dissolve once belief is mapped onto something like cached state versus active working memory? Before I read any particular answers, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of self-deception must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: the specific phenomena it must cover—such as a manager who expresses genuine surprise at declining metrics she herself configured alerts to suppress, or a partner who systematically avoids confirming evidence of betrayal without ever making an explicit decision to avoid it—the distinctions it must preserve—between self-deception and mere ignorance, between motivated reasoning and strategic blindness, and between possessing an inert belief versus lacking belief altogether—and the established findings from cognitive psychology about belief fixation, dissonance reduction, and revision that any account must respect. For each requirement, state the motivating case or constraint behind it, and flag which are non-negotiable—for instance, preserving the subject’s first-person report of being caught off-guard upon correction—versus which depend on negotiable ontological commitments, such as whether the mind must contain literally separate sub-agents. In particular, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—say, maintaining full doxastic unity while also explaining genuine self-deception without contradiction—and lay out that tension explicitly. Then, briefly, score the main broad approaches against your list: a Freudian-style division-of-mind model, a deflationary or intentionalist account that treats the phenomenon as ordinary false belief aided by motivated bias, and a compartmentalization or partition model that allows belief storage without executive access. Finally, assess one niche framing I am injecting: must an adequate theory accommodate epistemic compartmentalization, in which a belief is retained but systematically excluded from the reasoning processes that govern action, without positing a fully separate sub-agent? Or does that framing merely smuggle in a computing metaphor that philosophical analysis should reject?
