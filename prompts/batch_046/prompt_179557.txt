I am a retired natural scientist—my career was in physics—who has only recently turned to philosophy, and I have adopted a specific interpretation of Keynes's vision of the good life. In his 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren, Keynes distinguishes absolute needs, which can be satisfied, from relative needs, which are insatiable because they depend on social comparison. I hold the stronger view that under conditions of widespread abundance, this boundary becomes psychologically unstable: leisure, contemplation, and cultural refinement—the very activities Keynes associates with the good life—function as positional goods. Their value derives partly from scarcity and from distinguishing the holder from others, so universalizing them would undermine their worth and generate new relative needs. Consequently, the good life is not merely postponed by inadequate growth, but structurally unattainable as a universal state. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings about the actual use of leisure, which conceptual arguments that might stabilize Keynes's distinction, or which accounts of non-positional intrinsic goods, would genuinely threaten it? Which objections should a defender concede as damaging, and which could be legitimately dismissed? Please answer in American English.
