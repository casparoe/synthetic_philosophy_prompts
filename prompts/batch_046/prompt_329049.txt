In my interdisciplinary seminar on deep time and human agency, a recurring frustration has emerged around the debate over whether the Anthropocene should be formally recognized as a geological epoch, and if so, where its boundary lies. On one side, stratigraphers associated with the Anthropocene Working Group have advanced arguments for a mid-twentieth-century start marked by globally synchronous radionuclide signals and plastic deposition, treating the question as empirical: can we identify a clear, worldwide stratigraphic marker that distinguishes this layer from the Holocene? On the other, historians and some earth scientists have proposed alternative boundaries—around 1610 with the transoceanic exchange of species, or around the late eighteenth century with the widespread adoption of fossil-fuel energy—arguing that these moments better capture the structural transformation of human-earth relations. Meanwhile, philosophers and critical theorists often treat the date as secondary to a normative question: whether declaring a new epoch serves to assign collective moral responsibility or to reconceptualize humanity as a unified geological agent. Despite years of exchange, the Working Group's recommendations have not achieved formal ratification, and the various camps rarely shift position, yet they also rarely address one another's central concerns directly.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly about sedimentary evidence, but about misaligned questions being pursued under a single heading. The stratigraphers may be asking what counts as sufficient empirical proof for a golden spike; the historians may be asking when human activity became the dominant driver of planetary change; and the philosophers may be asking at what point it becomes conceptually appropriate to treat humanity as a singular geological force. If so, then disputes about specific years—1950 versus 1610 versus 1784—might be symptoms of incompatible success conditions rather than competing answers to the same question. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden empirical disagreement about what kinds of signals are admissible, or perhaps a verbal disagreement about whether "epoch" is a purely descriptive category or one that can carry normative weight.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this impasse best explained as a hidden factual dispute about stratigraphic standards, a verbal confusion over the word "epoch," or a substantive but misaligned disagreement in which different disciplines are answering different questions with different criteria for success? Please support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with what each side actually says—what they treat as decisive evidence, what they treat as irrelevant, and what they seem to be trying to achieve. Given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it require a new formal distinction, an interdisciplinary decision procedure for boundary-setting, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed? And if the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about whether science should serve purely descriptive or partly normative ends, is there any reason to expect resolution, or should we treat the stall as permanent and even productive?
