Following the death of my grandmother last year, I have found myself responsible for deciding which of her personal effects, letters, and digital traces to preserve, archive, or allow to decay. This practical burden has led me to think systematically about the moral status of remembrance and forgetting. I would like you to compare two philosophical positions in this debate: Avishai Margalit's account of an ethics of memory, according to which shared remembrance is a condition for moral community and forgiveness requires a prior, active commitment to remembering wrongs; and David Rieff's defense of strategic forgetting, which holds that collective remembrance often fossilizes grievance and that flourishing societies sometimes require the past to recede rather than be continuously curated. To keep the comparison from being a simple restatement of familiar arguments, please frame your analysis through a conceptual lens I have been calling "mnemonic labor"—the idea that remembering is not solely an internal mental act but a distributed practice that can be delegated to archives, algorithms, monuments, or institutional rituals. Specifically, address whether outsourcing mnemonic labor to an external system preserves the moral value Margalit assigns to remembrance, or whether it transforms remembrance into precisely the kind of hollow, perpetuated trace that Rieff urges us to abandon. Furthermore, examine whether either framework can accommodate a middle position—what might be termed "curated amnesia," in which certain details are deliberately obscured while an overarching narrative is retained—without collapsing into internal contradiction. Please justify your assessment by considering concrete cases, such as the maintenance of digital legacies versus the deliberate dissolution of family archives, rather than by appealing to general authority.
