After a long argument with a friend about whether suffering and pleasure can even share an evaluative scale, I want to sharpen the issue into a precise philosophical problem. Take lexical threshold negative utilitarianism in a form that combines two commitments: first, that there exists a lexical boundary such that no amount of pleasure—no matter how vast—can justify allowing a severe suffering that crosses that boundary; second, that below this boundary, pleasure and pain are not cardinally commensurable and perhaps not even robustly ordinally comparable, since they lack a shared metric and resist aggregation into a unified utility function. My friend argued that the second commitment undermines the first: if pleasure and suffering are genuinely incommensurable, then there is no normative ground for ranking them lexically, and the very idea of a threshold that suffering must not cross presupposes a comparative framework that incommensurability denies. I am inclined to think the view might survive by grounding the lexical priority not in comparative magnitude but in a structural or deontic asymmetry—specifically, in the claim that moral agents have non-optional reasons to eliminate suffering that operate independently of any calculus of pleasure, functioning more like veto conditions than like weights on a scale.
