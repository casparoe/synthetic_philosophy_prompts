I serve on the Interpretive Ethics and Exhibition Committee of the Mid-Atlantic Museum of Archaeological History, and we are scheduled to vote at next month’s plenary on the curatorial framework for the recently excavated Northern Periphery assemblage. The deposit—placed by stratigraphic correlation in the late second millennium—contains several hundred standardized clay tokens bearing incised geometric patterns, a single large anthropomorphic figurine with patterned fractures consistent with deliberate ritual termination, storage vessels containing mixed lipid and pollen residues, and an architectural footprint that lacks domestic hearths but includes a central raised platform. Two internal teams have offered competing reconstructions. The administrative interpretation treats the tokens as quantitative accounting instruments within a centralized redistribution economy, the residues as stored surplus, and the platform as an auditing station. The cultic interpretation argues that the tokens functioned as mnemonic aids for covenantal obligations rather than numerical records, that the vessel residues reflect communal feasting rather than storage, and that the platform and broken figurine indicate a site of ritual deposition and periodic renewal. Meanwhile, representatives of the local indigenous heritage council maintain that both secular frameworks misapply modern categorical distinctions—bureaucracy versus religion—to a social ontology in which economic reciprocity and sacred obligation were not conceptually separated, and they have requested that the exhibition treat the assemblage as living ancestral heritage rather than as evidence for an institutional classification.

The philosophical stakes concern the underdetermination of institutional belief by material culture: the same object patterns are consistent with radically different social ontologies, and our choice of interpretive frame risks projecting anachronistic distinctions onto a society that may not have recognized them. At the same time, the museum has a public mandate to present coherent historical narratives, and prolonged interpretive suspension could be seen as a failure of scholarly responsibility. Our committee policy requires that any permanent exhibition recommendation include a clear curatorial position, an explicit account of the evidence that most strongly supports the rejected alternative, and a defined timeline for review should new excavation or analytical techniques alter the evidential landscape. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting: it should recommend a specific course of action—whether to adopt a dominant narrative, institute a dual comparative display, or impose a provisional interpretive moratorium with restricted labeling—together with a concrete implementation time frame, and it should treat with genuine seriousness the strongest argument against your recommendation, whether that argument concerns epistemic humility, the ethical authority of descendant communities, or the museum’s educational obligation to avoid presenting ambiguity as mere ignorance.
