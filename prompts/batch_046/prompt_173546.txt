Write a blank-verse poem of no more than forty lines, spoken in the voice of Adam Smith’s impartial spectator observing a grief-counselling algorithm that produces precisely calibrated sympathetic responses despite possessing neither embodied experience nor a continuous personal history. Allow the regular iambic pentameter to embody the spectator’s characteristic distance and measured judgement, but fracture that rhythm with enjambment and abrupt caesura wherever the speaker confronts the possibility that genuine sympathy requires an irrational loyalty to past suffering—an emotional investment that rational calculation would treat as irrecoverable. Around line twenty, turn from describing the algorithm’s mimicry to questioning whether the spectator can maintain sympathy without honouring attachments that no longer serve present utility, and whether such honouring illicitly derives a prescription from the mere description of prior feeling. After the poem, provide a prose note of no more than 120 words identifying where the poem takes liberties with Smith’s actual account—particularly regarding sympathy directed at non-human or non-embodied agents, and whether continuity of self permits the spectator to treat prior emotional commitments as binding. In that note, state clearly what empirical or philosophical observation about sympathy—human or artificial—would lead you to reject the claim that continuity of self is a precondition for genuine sympathy, and identify the reasoning step most likely to fail.
