I’ve been arguing with a friend about whether Golding’s Lord of the Flies ultimately supports Mencius or Xunzi on human nature. My view is that the boys initially display something like Mencius’s four sprouts—compassion, shame, deference, and discernment—but that these collapse not because human nature is inherently bad in Xunzi’s sense, but because ritual and learning (li) have been removed. The conch and Piggy’s glasses seem to function as fragile artificial technologies of order, and when they break the boys do not revert to an original evil; they revert to an unformed rawness that Xunzi calls xing. My friend counters that Simon’s private vision of the beast is exactly the kind of spontaneous moral intuition Mencius describes, and that its destruction proves the sprouts are too weak to survive without institutional reinforcement. I’d like a philosophical reading that takes both positions seriously—treating Xunzi’s claim that nature requires transformation rather than suppression, and Mencius’s claim that moral capacity is innate but needs cultivation—while staying faithful to the novel’s events. Does Golding show that violence emerges from the absence of ritual containment, or does he dramatize a deeper problem: that the interaction between innate capacity and collapsed social form produces something neither philosopher fully accounts for?
