I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a mid-size hospital and I keep getting called in when patients with substance-use disorders or compulsive conditions refuse treatment. The psychiatrists want a reusable procedure for deciding whether a refusal expresses "free will worth wanting" — not just legal capacity, but the philosophical kind — so they can document it consistently. I want it grounded in the hierarchical-endorsement view but with two tweaks I've been using: first, a diachronic-mesh condition where second-order volitions have to hold across at least two distinct decision-points seperated by sleep or significant time, not just in the moment; second, a non-induction constraint that discounts desires formed under direct neurochemical coercion or targeted manipulation even if the patient endorses them now. Build me a step-by-step decision procedure my team can run in about 15 minutes: the ordered questions, what counts as passing each, where the flow stops with "treat as autonomous refusal" vs "treat as non-autonomous," and the exact nodes where a human has to make a values call that the checklist can't cover. Apply it to three cases: (1) a patient with alcohol dependence refusing detox after a single clear statement of endorsement; (2) a patient with severe OCD refusing exposure therapy despite consistently endorsing the refusal over three weeks; (3) a patient refusing a new medication whose refusal formed immediately after a targeted social-media ad campaign about "big pharma" that they now fully endorse. Include one where the procedure gives an answer I probably won't like, and flag what the procedure flat-out can't determine — especially around whether diachronic stability should be measured in days or months, and whether endorsement of a harmful refusal ever counts as autonomous just because it's stable.
