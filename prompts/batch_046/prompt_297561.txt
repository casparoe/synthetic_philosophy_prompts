I've been taking a graduate course on philosophy of medicine, and a recurring disagreement with a classmate has led me to frame a narrower question that standard surveys of the naturalism-versus-normativism debate rarely address. We both reject the strong Boorsean claim that health is nothing more than species-typical functional efficiency, and we also reject the purely constructivist view that disease-status is entirely determined by social values without biological constraint. Instead, my classmate defends a hybrid position—let me call it "functional pluralism"—which holds that a condition qualifies as a disease if and only if it involves a statistically atypical functional deficit relative to evolutionary design and is simultaneously evaluated as harmful by the affected agent within that agent's own normative framework of flourishing. She argues that this dual-constraint view can cleanly separate cases like congenital deafness within a thriving signing community (not a disease, because the functional deficit is not evaluated negatively within that framework) from insulin-dependent diabetes (a disease, because the metabolic dysfunction is evaluated negatively regardless of cultural framing).

I'm not convinced this hybrid avoids either hidden biological essentialism or unexamined cultural bias, and I want your help thinking through my skepticism probabilistically. Please give me your credence—on a scale from 0 to 1—that functional pluralism, as defined above, can actually do the explanatory work required to yield determinate, non-arbitrary classifications in hard boundary cases without collapsing back into pure naturalism or pure normativism. Decompose your reasoning into three sub-estimates with brief justifications: first, the likelihood that any hybrid account can solve the "integration problem" (that is, combine biological and normative conditions without one merely masking the other); second, the likelihood that patient-autonomy norms can be formalized with enough precision to avoid circularity in cases where an agent's evaluative framework itself may be shaped by the very dysfunction in question; and third, the likelihood that evolutionary-functional baselines are sufficiently determinate to serve as a meaningful partial constraint rather than an empty placeholder. Then combine these into your overall number, identify which term dominates your uncertainty, and specify what kind of evidence—perhaps a formal model of multi-level selection applied to psychiatric taxonomy, or a cross-cultural study of diagnostic agreement on contested conditions—would most likely move your credence.
