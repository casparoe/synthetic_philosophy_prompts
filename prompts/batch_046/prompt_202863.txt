In a graduate reading group on nationalism and state legitimacy, a fellow participant keeps cutting off debates about secession with the line, 'all nations are imagined communities.' Depending on the day, the phrase is used to mean that national identity lacks biological roots; that modern states are recent ideological constructions; that large-scale solidarity is necessarily mediated and therefore 'fictional'; or even that because a nation is 'imagined,' it can command no special loyalty or territorial entitlement. I want to come back with a disambiguated menu rather than a rebuttal. Can you separate out the precise theses this slogan might express, ordering them from the near-tautological (e.g., citizens do not personally know one another) to the historically substantive (e.g., mass national consciousness emerged only with print capitalism and standardized education) to the normatively radical (e.g., the 'imagined' status of a community undermines its claim to sovereignty or to a right of self-determination)? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, empirically defensible, false, or conceptually confused; and then specify which of these readings, if any, actually supports the move from 'this nation is imagined' to 'therefore this secessionist claim fails.' I am not looking for a bibliography or a defense of any particular theory of nationalism; I need the conceptual map that shows exactly what work the slogan is being asked to do.
