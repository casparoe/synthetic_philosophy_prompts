I'm in this course on Black aesthetics and we got stuck arguing about Du Bois's claim that all art is propoganda, specifically from that essay Criteria of Negro Art where he ties beauty and truth together with racial uplift. I'm trying to figure out my actual gut-level verdicts on specific cases to see if they line up behind one principle, but my instincts feel kind of scattered.

Here's where I land. A Harlem Renaissance poem that just celebrates Black joy without mentioning racism: that's propaganda, because the beauty itself is making an argument for our humanity. A harsh anti-lynching cartoon meant for a rally: obviously propaganda, but I'm unsure Du Bois would even call it art since it might be ugly and purely instrumental. A jazz record that's just for dancing, no lyrics about race: still propaganda, though maybe a seperate kind, like presence rather than argument. A meticulously realistic portrait of a wealthy Black patron commissioned to hang in a white bank lobby: I feel like this is NOT propaganda, or at least not the good kind, since it's serving assimilation and comfort for elite viewers rather than truth. But then a children's fairy tale with Black characters in a magical world with zero racial conflict: that feels like propaganda to me, the kind that insists we belong in every narrative. Meanwhile a deliberately distorted, ugly painting made to shock white audiences into seeing Black suffering: politically it's propaganda, but Du Bois might reject it as art because it sacrifices beauty for message. So I've got beautiful non-political work that counts, ugly political work that might fail as art, and commissioned elite art that doesn't count even though it's beautiful and non-confrontational.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or do I have to pick between a complicated account that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns one? Specifically, what's the real difference between that bank portrait and the fairy tale - both avoid conflict and aim at respectability, yet I treat them differently. And if I settle on something like art is propaganda when it refuses to let Black life be invisible, but only counts as art when it also meets a truth-and-beauty standard, what does that predict for a case I haven't mentioned - say, an abstract sculpture by a Black artist with no representational content at all, displayed in a museum that never mentions race?
