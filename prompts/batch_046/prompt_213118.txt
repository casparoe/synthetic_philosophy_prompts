Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the lens of inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and epistemic modesty. Stevens justifies decades of service to Lord Darlington by appealing to the particularities of professional dignity, personal loyalty, and the uniqueness of his situation—the inside view—while systematically declining the outside view that would place him in the reference class of domestic staff serving a fascist-sympathizing aristocrat. Does the novel present this refusal as a failure of epistemic modesty, or as a necessary condition for the practical identity that makes his life coherent? Focus on the road trip to meet Miss Kenton and his retrospective assessment of his past choices: does he finally adopt an outside view of his own life, and if so, does it yield genuine self-knowledge or only a destructive form of regret? I want a reading that takes seriously the philosophical claim that modesty about one's own case may require treating it as one of a kind, and asks whether Ishiguro dramatizes the ethical cost of such modesty rather than its absence—without reducing Stevens to a simple illustration of false consciousness.
