I am preparing a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, and I want to test how well different interpretive frameworks account for what Kierkegaard calls the teleological suspension of the ethical in the case of Abraham. Please rank the following three readings according to their explanatory power—specifically, their ability to preserve Abraham's faith as genuinely transcending the universal ethical order without collapsing into either irrationalist fanaticism or a concealed ethical duty—using tiers A (strongest), B (moderate), and C (weakest): first, the classic knight-of-faith interpretation, which stresses Abraham's private relation to the absolute and the paradox of the absurd; second, a virtue-ethical reconstruction that treats Abraham's readiness to sacrifice Isaac as an extreme orientation toward the highest good, analogous to heroic virtue; third, a contractualist-suspension reading I have been developing, according to which Abraham's silence does not indicate the absence of ethical reason but rather a temporary bracketing of the social contract of moral reciprocity in favor of a direct, non-generalizable covenantal obligation. For each tier assignment, provide a concise justification—no more than two sentences—focusing especially on whether the reading can explain Abraham's return to ethical life with Isaac after the ordeal without treating the suspension as merely revoked or illusory.
