I am preparing a long-form feature for an ideas magazine on whether empirical findings in moral psychology can settle philosophical debates about moral objectivity, and during my reporting a professor of philosophy shared an exam she administered in an upper-division course on cross-cultural ethics. The exam asks students to evaluate the claim that WEIRD populations—Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic subjects—are psychological outliers in treating harm and fairness as the core of morality while weighting loyalty, authority, and purity far less heavily than many non-Western groups, and whether this descriptive pattern undermines moral objectivity. I have been given one student response that sounds plausible to a non-specialist, but I want a rigorous point-by-point assessment before I reference any part of its reasoning in print, particularly because I am trying in my article to distinguish cross-cultural disagreement about which moral rules apply from disagreement about the relative weight assigned to shared moral concerns—a distinction that seems central to the objectivity debate but is rarely made explicit in popular coverage.

Here is the exam question: "Evaluate the argument that cross-cultural differences in folk morality—especially the WEIRD bias in treating care and fairness violations as paradigmatically moral while other cultures assign greater weight to loyalty, authority, and purity—show that morality is culturally constructed and therefore not objective. In your answer, explain what the WEIRD finding actually demonstrates, address whether descriptive moral variation entails normative relativism, and discuss whether moral objectivity requires universal agreement on specific moral judgments."

And here is the student answer: "The WEIRD finding demonstrates that Western populations are cognitive outliers in moral judgment. As documented in cross-cultural research that informed the moral foundations framework, these groups rely mainly on individualizing foundations—care and fairness—whereas most other societies treat binding foundations—loyalty, authority, and purity—as equally central. Some philosophers infer from this descriptive variation that morality lacks objective status. That inference fails. Moral objectivity is a metaphysical thesis about whether moral claims can be true or false independently of belief; it does not require that every culture agree on specific rules. By analogy, disagreement about physical theories across cultures does not render physics subjective. Moreover, the moral foundations framework indicates that all humans share the same innate moral capacities—the five foundations are universal—so the observed variation is best explained as different activation patterns of a common human endowment rather than arbitrary cultural invention. This shared architecture actually supports objectivity: it grounds moral judgment in universal human nature. Therefore, the WEIRD data undermines moral universalism—the view that all cultures share identical moral codes—but leaves moral objectivity intact, since objectivity concerns the status of moral truths, not the uniformity of belief across populations."

Please grade this answer as an instructor would, assigning a specific letter grade and providing detailed comments that identify both accurate reasoning and any subtle errors—especially any conflation of the psychological universality of moral foundations with metaphysical objectivity, any misattribution of the WEIRD finding to the moral-foundations literature specifically, and any unexamined assumption in the analogy between moral and scientific disagreement. I am not looking for a general impression but for a precise, point-by-point evaluation that a journalist could quote with confidence.
