I am currently drafting a historical novel set within the court of Rudolf II, where the boundary between astronomical calculation and prognostication was far more permeable than contemporary accounts tend to admit. In the course of my research, I have been studying a passage—preserved in an older English rendering of Johannes Kepler's reflections on celestial influence—that has left me genuinely uncertain about its philosophical architecture. The passage presents Kepler's argument that the planets do not act upon human affairs through any tangible physical mechanism, such as rays or effluvia, but rather that their geometric configurations constitute a kind of cosmic notation. According to this account, the human soul, being structured according to the same mathematical ratios that govern celestial motion, can perceive these configurations in the manner that an ear perceives harmonic consonance. Astrology is therefore not a predictive science grounded in physical causation, but an interpretive art that demands the practitioner cultivate an internal mathematical harmony in order to translate celestial patterns into human significance. The passage further implies that astrological error stems not from false premises about stellar power, but from a form of hermeneutic illiteracy—when the interpreter lacks the geometric and musical education required to read the sky correctly.

What perplexes me is the precise ontological status Kepler assigns to this symbolic correspondence. Does he intend that the soul's apprehension of planetary harmony is merely a subjective aesthetic projection onto an indifferent cosmos, or does he maintain that there exists an objective, divinely instituted isomorphism between celestial geometry and the structure of human reason, such that correct interpretation is genuinely truth-tracking? Moreover, how ought one to reconcile this apparently non-causal, hermeneutic conception of astrology with Kepler's simultaneous commitment to physical causation in celestial mechanics—does he partition reality into a mechanical domain governed by force and a symbolic domain governed by meaning, or is there a subtler integration of these registers in his thought? I am especially interested in whether this distinction might illuminate why Kepler persisted in casting horoscopes even as he dismantled the physical foundations of Ptolemaic astronomy, and I would be grateful for any clarification that might help me render this tension faithfully in my narrative.
