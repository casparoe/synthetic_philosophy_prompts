Consider the institutional practice—common in biosafety regulation across several jurisdictions—where a customer may purchase or download a digital text file specifying the base-pair sequence of a synthetic gene with minimal oversight, on the grounds that it is “mere information,” yet the same customer must submit to screening and licensing if they ask a synthesis firm to manufacture the corresponding physical DNA molecule. The practice treats the sequence-as-data and the sequence-as-chemistry as falling under radically different regulatory categories, despite their identical causal potential once the data is synthesized. What philosophical commitments about genetic information does that bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat DNA as a literal code—semantic content that remains the same regardless of substrate—or as a chemical structure whose biological significance is inseparable from its material form? Is the distinction sustainable without committing to an information-matter dualism that has little place in molecular biology? Assess whether the practice actually requires that ontology, or whether it could be defended on purely practical grounds (ease of screening data versus molecules). Then describe what a consistent regulatory regime would look like if it rejected the code metaphor entirely: would it regulate digital sequences based on predicted biochemical effects, treat synthesized DNA and its digital blueprint identically, or abandon sequence-based screening altogether? And identify one tension within the current practice—for instance, between allowing unrestricted access to pathogenic sequence data and restricting the tools that can read or write it—that reveals an unacknowledged philosophical inconsistency.
