I have just come from a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend, and I find that I can defend my position only in emotional terms, not in conceptual ones. I need help seeing whether my resistance is philosophically coherent or merely a form of self-exemption.

For the past twelve years I have maintained an independent, unfunded archive: I record, transcribe, and contextualize oral histories from a specific, now-scattered community of craftspeople who practiced a particular regional reed-weaving technique that is no longer taught anywhere. The work is solitary, produces almost no public recognition, and absorbs nearly all my discretionary time and modest income. Recently, I was offered a full-time role with an organization that deploys low-cost health interventions in regions with high child mortality; accepting it would require me to abandon the archive, because the travel schedule and emotional demands are incompatible with the continuity that preservation requires.

My friend argues that if I genuinely care about reducing suffering, I am required to take the position. She claims that any reason I offer for staying—loyalty to the remaining members of the community, the irreplaceability of my embodied knowledge, the sense that this project is constitutive of who I am—is merely a disguised preference for my own identity or comfort. She insists that moral reasoning should be entirely third-personal: I should ask which course produces the best outcomes, apply that calculus uniformly, and treat my attachment to the archive as one factor among others, weighted solely by its causal contribution to overall welfare. If I refuse, she says, I am inserting an extra consideration—something like this is mine—that has no independent moral weight and functions only to privilege myself.

What unsettles me is not simply that I want to stay, but that her framework seems to require me to justify staying by demonstrating that it maximizes good—either because the archive eventually benefits people, or because my fulfillment feeds back into productivity. If I remain only because I have calculated that my presence here produces marginally more welfare than my absence there, that feels like a betrayal of the very commitment that makes the work worth doing. It seems I ought to be able to say: this is my project, not because it is optimal, but because it is mine, and that fact itself supplies a reason that does not depend on a prior comparison with every alternative use of my life.

My friend responds that this is precisely the kind of reasoning that collapses under scrutiny—privileging mine over best is arbitrary unless mine tracks some independently valuable property. She asks what I would say to someone whose project was collecting rare stamps while children died of preventable disease nearby.

Can you help me identify the precise philosophical structure of our disagreement? In particular, is there a coherent defense of the claim that a long-standing, identity-constituting commitment can provide a reason for action that is not reducible to its contribution to an overall good? And if such a defense exists, what does accepting it cost in terms of impartial moral demands—does it license indifference to distant suffering, or can it be constrained without collapsing back into the calculus my friend demands? I want to understand whether my instinct corresponds to a recognized philosophical position, and whether it can survive without becoming the kind of self-exemption she alleges.
