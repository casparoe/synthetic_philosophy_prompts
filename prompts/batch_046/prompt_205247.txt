As a novelist constructing a historical narrative, I have invented a seventeenth-century Genevan sect called the Silent Ledger, whose members record every transaction in three columns—worldly debit, spiritual credit, and divine election—treating the arithmetic balance of the books as direct evidence of salvation. I am trying to use Weber's Protestant ethic thesis not as historical sociology but as a narrative rule, and I have built the following argument that now threatens to make my plot impossible. (1) If a cultural value can be translated without remainder into the economic rationalization it produces, then it is not an independent cause but merely a description of that effect. (2) Weber shows that religious values caused capitalist development only by generating a rationalized, calculable labor orientation; absent that rationalization, the same values produced no economic change. (3) Therefore, in my fiction, the Silent Ledger's bookkeeping-prayer can do causal work only by producing economic rationality, and if it fails to do so it is causally inert. (4) So culture is either reducible to the economic structures it creates or powerless to direct them. (5) Yet I intend to write that the sect's arithmetic ritual genuinely directs a merchant's refusal to forgive debt on the Sabbath without being identical to that economic decision. (6) Hence Weber's logic implies my intended scene is incoherent. I want to know which premise I should abandon, why it seemed defensible, and what material reductionism, strict Weberian dualism, and emergent narrative causation each have to pay to reject it. Give me your diagnosis in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
