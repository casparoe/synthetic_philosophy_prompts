Read Dostoevsky’s The Idiot through the lens of what philosophers call the moral saint—not merely a good person, but one whose life is organized around the relentless maximization of moral value, leaving little room for personal projects, aesthetic pursuits, or partial attachments. Focus on Prince Myshkin: he is compassionate to the point of self-annihilation, yet the novel treats his goodness as both luminous and destructive. Where does Myshkin approximate the moral saint that Susan Wolf criticized—someone whose non-moral virtues are stunted by an overdeveloped moral sensibility? But also consider a less-discussed tension: Myshkin’s goodness operates through a kind of radical transparency and immediate emotional identification with others, a condition we might call affective permeability. Does the novel suggest that such permeability, taken to its extreme, makes moral agency unstable—because one cannot maintain the boundaries necessary for practical judgment? In particular, examine the scenes in which Myshkin intervenes on Nastasya Filippovna’s behalf and the subsequent collapse of his marriage plans into an epileptic crisis: does the narrative present his breakdown as a failure of character, or as the inevitable structural consequence of a life without defensive distance? I want a reading that takes both Dostoevsky’s narrative architecture and the philosophical literature on moral saints seriously, without reducing the novel to an illustration of Wolf’s thesis. What does the work add to the debate—perhaps something about the aesthetic and relational costs of maximal goodness—that philosophical argument alone tends to miss?
