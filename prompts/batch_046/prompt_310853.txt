I'd like you to adjudicate an exchange between two members of a philosophy reading group, Maya and Leo, about whether death is bad for the one who dies. Maya defends a deprivation account modified by what she calls project-based harm: death is bad not merely because it subtracts future goods, but because it frustrates an ongoing teleological arc—life-projects that require temporal extension to achieve their value—so the harm is borne by the living subject whose trajectory is severed, even after death. Leo presses the Epicurean no-subject objection: harm requires a coincident subject, and since the dead do not exist, there is no bearer for this harm; he adds that if past initiation creates a protective reason for future continuation, then prenatal non-existence should symmetrically be a harm because it frustrates the same potential projects before they begin. Maya distinguishes the two cases by arguing that birth establishes a normative baseline—once a life-project is actually initiated, there is a reason to preserve its trajectory—whereas prior to initiation there is no subject or project to be protected. Leo replies that this baseline is arbitrary unless we already presuppose that future goods matter, which collapses her view back into standard deprivation and leaves the Epicurean challenge untouched. They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain what both are missing—particularly about whether the project-based framing genuinely avoids the coincidence problem or just renames it—and say who is more right overall.
