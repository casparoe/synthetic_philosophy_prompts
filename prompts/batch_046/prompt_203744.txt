Write a deadpan parody of a bureaucratic "Best-Practices White Paper" (approximately 700 words) issued by a fictional international body—the Global Institute for Productive Governance—proposing the universal adoption of what it terms optimized disciplinary architectures. Format the piece with numbered sections, bullet points, and an executive summary, using the bloodless, jargon-heavy prose of contemporary institutional consulting reports.

The satire should target Michel Foucault’s analyses of power, knowledge, and discipline: specifically the claims that power is productive rather than merely repressive, that institutions generate the truths about the subjects they study, that the examination and the norm operate as rituals of normalization, and that panoptic mechanisms induce self-surveillance. Rather than inventing caricatures, exaggerate these actual conceptual moves. Recommend, for instance, that schools replace grades with continuous biometric norm-deviation tracking; present the clinical gaze as a cost-saving hospital protocol; and describe prison reform not as reducing incarceration but as refining the micro-physics of power within carceral spaces. Include a sidebar on Knowledge-Production Metrics in which the report celebrates the circular fact that the more data is gathered about citizens, the more categories of deviance are invented—treating this as an operational success. Maintain a consistently earnest, deadpan voice; the parody should be legible through the relentless, literal application of Foucauldian logic to education, medicine, and criminal justice.

The document should be readable to someone unfamiliar with Foucault—no unexplained jargon without context—while rewarding a specialist who recognizes that every exaggeration is a faithful extension of arguments about discipline, normalization, and the production of subjects. Do not cite specific texts, page numbers, or named scholars within the report (the fictional institution may refer only to a generic founding theoretical framework), and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. By the end, the report should implicitly raise the standard objections—that such architectures erase autonomy, that knowledge here is inseparable from control, and that the language of optimization masks domination—without ever stating them outright.
