I am a novelist applying for a four-month interdisciplinary residency that pairs fiction writers with philosophers of action, and I need a one-page proposal on a precise conceptual question. In standard Frankfurt cases, the principle of alternative possibilities is undermined by showing that an agent acts freely even though an external intervener stands ready to block any deviation. Existing debates stall because both compatibilists and incompatibilists treat “could have done otherwise” as a single modal notion. I propose to investigate whether splitting it into “counterfactual divergence” (a genuinely different world-branch in which the agent chooses otherwise) and “structural accessibility” (a reasoning-path still available within the agent’s actual deterministic framework, even if externally blocked) either dissolves the counterexample or merely relocates it. The angle is drawn from narrative craft: in fiction, characters exercise agency inside deterministic plot architectures, yet readers intuit that some choices are genuinely open to the character’s reasoning while others are authorially foreclosed regardless of internal deliberation. I will use this distinction to reconstruct canonical Frankfurt cases and ask whether the intervener eliminates structural accessibility or only counterfactual divergence, and whether moral responsibility tracks the former rather than the latter. The method is conceptual analysis with explicit narrative-structure analogues, not empirical study or historical exegesis. A successful result would be a clear criterion for when an agent retains structural accessibility despite lacking counterfactual divergence, together with an argument that this either saves a modified principle of alternative possibilities or definitively abandons it. Failure would occur if the distinction collapses—if every structurally accessible path proves counterfactually available, or vice versa—leaving the original stalemate untouched. Given the four-month scope, I would spend the first month formalising the distinction, the second applying it to standard cases, the third testing objections, and the fourth drafting a thirty-page essay for an interdisciplinary philosophy audience.
