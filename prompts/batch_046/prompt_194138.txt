I'm trying to think through whether there's any coherent way to hold onto a weak teleogical view of history — like, not full Hegelian providence, but the idea that institutional development has some long-run bias toward more inclusive or less arbitrary arrangements. I built this argument and it keeps landing me in a place I hate.

(1) If there's a directional bias in institutional history, then outcomes that persist for generations are more likely to be ones that align with that direction, since misaligned ones face corrective pressure.
(2) The Bolshevik consolidation, the Meiji state's coercive modernization, even the instutional lock-in of colonial extractive structures — all persisted for decades and shaped modern states.
(3) So by (1), those outcomes align with the directional bias toward justice, at least structurally.
(4) But if they align with justice, then condemning them as unjust is just a catagory error: they're instruments of the arc, not deviations from it.
(5) That collapses normative judgment into descriptive success, which makes the whole "arc bends toward justice" claim empty or morally grotesque.
(6) So either there's no directional bias, or we have to accept (4).

I'd rather keep (1) in some form and definitely keep (5) — that Stalinist consolidation wasn't just. Where does this actually fail? Is it an equivocation on "align with justice," a hidden assumption that persistance requires moral legitimacy, or something about collective agency that doesn't transfer? And what does giving up the weakest link cost if I still want to say history isn't totally random?
