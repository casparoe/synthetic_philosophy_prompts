I am in the midst of drafting a novel that turns on a diplomatic negotiation between a Dutch trade representative and a Japanese cultural liaison, and I keep encountering a single, overused sentence that one of my characters deploys like a conversational weapon: 'honesty is the best policy'. He invokes it whenever the Japanese liaison offers an indirect refusal or a politely fabricated excuse — what my Dutchman dismisses as a 'white lie' — and he treats the phrase as though it were an unanswerable moral theorem rather than a folk proverb. Before I can write his dialogue with any conviction, I need to understand exactly what precise theses that sentence might be hiding, because at the moment it feels to me like a slogan that could mean anything from a trivial reminder about long-term reputation to a radical demand for total communicative transparency.

Could you separate the distinct claims that 'honesty is the best policy' could actually be making? I should like a menu of readings — not a single interpretation — specifying for each what kind of 'honesty' is at stake (epistemic, ethical, or perhaps what I have been calling in my notes 'communicative directness', a distinction between stating facts plainly and stating one's moral judgements plainly), whose 'policy' is being prescribed, and over what scope of interaction. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false; and tell me which of these readings my Dutchman is most likely relying upon when he uses the proverb to shut down an argument about whether a face-saving falsehood is ever permissible. I am particularly keen to see whether any defensible version of the principle can survive contact with cross-cultural norms in which indirectness is not deception but a form of respect.

In my manuscript I am trying to avoid reducing either culture to a caricature, so I do not want a simple verdict that Dutch directness is honest and Japanese indirectness is dishonest. Rather, I want to know whether the proverb, on its most plausible reading, presupposes a universal communicative standard, or whether it secretly embeds a culturally specific ideal of transparency that looks like virtue in one context and brutality in another. If the slogan turns out to be ambiguous between these possibilities, that ambiguity itself is something I could dramatise; but I need the ambiguity laid out with philosophical precision, not dissolved into vague advice about being kind. Please do not offer bibliographies or citations — I am writing fiction, not an academic treatise — but do ground your analysis in the actual logical and moral content of the phrase as it might function in a room where two people genuinely disagree about what respect requires.
