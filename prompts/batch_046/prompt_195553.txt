I'm working through a philosophy-of-language assignment on whether statistical machine translation involves anything properly called understanding, and I'm stuck between two frameworks. On one side is Douglas Hofstadter's familiar skepticism: statistical translation, however fluent, operates without semantic grounding, lacking any internal model of the world, of speaker intent, or of the referential relations that make a sentence about something rather than merely correlated with other sentences. On the other side is a position I want to call the structural isomorphism defense, which holds that if a system's distributed representations preserve the same inferential, analogical, and relational structures that characterize human conceptual networks, then the system possesses a functional analog of understanding—not consciousness or embodied experience, but a genuine grasp of meaning—regardless of whether its operations are purely correlational.

Please compare these two positions with precision. First, articulate Hofstadter's central objection: what specific capacity is absent from statistical translation that makes its outputs shallow rather than merely mechanistically different? Second, lay out the structural isomorphism defense and explain exactly how it answers Hofstadter without simply begging the question—in particular, how it distinguishes predictive correlation from representational structure. Third, apply both frameworks to a single concrete phenomenon: the translation of culturally embedded metaphors or long-range polysemy, where statistical models often produce locally coherent but globally inappropriate renderings. Show how Hofstadter interprets such failures as evidence of missing understanding, and how the defense might reinterpret them as limitations in representational resolution rather than proof of semantic emptiness. Then adjudicate: does the defense genuinely preserve the normative force of understanding, or does it succeed only by redefining the term downward? In your answer, also consider whether Hofstadter's standard implicitly demands an impossible Cartesian transparency that human translators themselves do not achieve. Conclude by stating which view you find more defensible and identifying the unresolved ambiguity that prevents a decisive resolution.
