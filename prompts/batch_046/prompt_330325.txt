As a novelist developing a character study around an aging philosopher who, in her fifties, drafted an advance directive refusing all life-sustaining measures should she ever fail to recognize her own published writings—only to reach her eighties with advanced dementia yet display what her caregivers consistently describe as a serene, present-tense engagement with sensory experience—I need an intuitive but conceptually rigorous explanation of why the authority of the earlier self is not simply dissolved by the apparent well-being of the present self. Specifically, I want to understand the philosophical tension between precedent autonomy as a form of diachronic sovereignty and the counter-claim that personal identity across time is not a corporate entity but a sequence of psychologically continuous yet metaphysically distinct stages, each bearing its own present-tense normative authority. Please explain the conceptual issue at stake: under what conditions, if any, does an earlier self possess binding normative power over a later self who no longer retains the conceptual apparatus required to formulate—or even to comprehend—the values that originally motivated the directive? I wish to move beyond the standard bioethical framing of best interests versus autonomy and instead address the deeper problem of whether continuity of narrative, rather than continuity of memory or psychology, is what grants the directive its force, and whether a rupture in narrative coherence transforms that force into something analogous to posthumous editorial control exercised over a character who no longer shares the author's intentions.
