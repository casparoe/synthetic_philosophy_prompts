I'm putting together a diagnostic handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral motivation, and I want an instrument that sorts students by what they actually believe about Glaucon's challenge rather than by the labels they have learned. To keep it from being a rote recitation of textbook positions, I want the tree built around a distinction that almost never appears in standard taxonomies: the difference between mere "social invisibility" (no witness can catch or blame you) and what we might call "normative self-invisibility" (the agent can still recognize her own act, but no moral demand registers with her as genuinely binding). The scenario should remain the ring of Gyges—absolute immunity from detection and social consequence—but the branches should test whether a position survives the stronger condition in which even the agent's own self-assessment is stripped of moral weight.

Please construct a decision tree or branching questionnaire with six to eight leaves, designed so that a student can answer each node as a concrete case or claim without already knowing which philosophical view it leads to. Use binary or trinary splits that map the logical space first, then locate named positions within it. The four dimensions that should drive the splits are: (1) whether moral reasons survive the complete removal of external sanctions and social recognition; (2) whether those reasons are ultimately reducible to the agent's own long-term welfare or belong to a fundamentally different normative kind; (3) whether they depend on the agent's continuing self-knowledge of what she has done, or whether they apply even under conditions of "normative self-invisibility"; and (4) whether justice is valuable primarily for the state of affairs it produces or for the character it expresses in the agent. Once the combinations are laid out, place within the tree Glaucon's conventionalist view, a Hobbesian contractarian view, a Kantian rationalist view, an Aristotelian virtue view, and a moral-realist internalist view; for any combination that remains unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate—especially if two positions share answers across all four dimensions but diverge on some further point that this instrument does not capture. The deliverable should read as a polished seminar flowchart, with each leaf carrying a single sentence describing its most awkward commitment.
