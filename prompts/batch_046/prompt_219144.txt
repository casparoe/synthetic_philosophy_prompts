After a long argument with a friend about whether poetry survives translation, I am trying to determine whether his summary of Douglas Hofstadter’s Le Ton beau de Marot is defensible or a distortion. He presented Hofstadter’s study of Clément Marot’s 1537 poem À une damoyselle malade as advancing what he called formal monism: the thesis that in poetic translation, formal properties—meter, rhyme scheme, syllable count, and prosodic rhythm—are not merely important but exhaustively constitutive of the poem’s identity, while lexical and semantic content is essentially fungible. On this reading, Hofstadter treats literal semantic fidelity as a kind of false precision, arguing that preserving the dictionary meanings of Marot’s lines typically destroys the structural architecture that actually carries the poem’s effect. Therefore, Hofstadter’s own English versions are meant to be evaluated not by their proximity to the French vocabulary or syntax but by whether they achieve what my friend termed prosodic isomorphism—line-for-line mirroring of rhyme and meter—even when this requires morphosyntactic calquing that bends English grammar away from ordinary usage. From this, my friend concluded that Hofstadter has demonstrated translation to be impossible in principle: one must either preserve the form and abandon the meaning, or preserve the meaning and destroy the poem, so every translation is ultimately a new poem wearing the old one’s clothes.

I find this too extreme, but I lack the familiarity with Hofstadter’s text to locate the errors. Is this summary fair? In particular, does Hofstadter genuinely treat semantic content as fungible rather than as a competing constraint that must be negotiated? Is prosodic isomorphism an accurate characterization of his practice, or does he allow structural adjustments when they serve other values? Does his argument actually entail the impossibility of translation, or does he defend a more modest claim about the inevitability of loss and trade-off? And if one examines the specific translations he offers of Marot’s poem, do they support my friend’s strong formal monism, or do they reveal moments where lexical accuracy is deliberately preserved at the expense of perfect formal symmetry?
