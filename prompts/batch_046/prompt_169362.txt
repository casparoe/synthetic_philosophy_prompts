my draft splits them into three. first, coordination norms: queing is visible, everyone gains from the line, and a visitor who cuts in is defecting on a shared game they can literally see. i think there's a real obligation here, and ignorance isn't a great excuse.

second, subsidy norms: tipping in the US. the menu price hides the real cost; the customer covers part of the wage. but if you come from a place with normal wages, you don't sense that hidden contract. breaking this doesn't mess up coordination, it just violates a local distributive scheme. i call this normative opaciy — the obligation is real for locals but opaque to outsiders because the background economic arrangement is invisible.

third, risk norms: jaywalking. in berlin it's legal on some streets, in tokyo it's social death, in new york it's just practical if the light's broken. the moral weight depends on actual danger, not the sign.

so my argument is that visitors should follow coordination norms right away, risk norms based on real harm not local signage, and subsidy norms only weakly, since normative opaciy weakens the moral force.
