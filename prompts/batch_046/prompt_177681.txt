My ethics tutor's been walking me through Anscombe-style cases on intention and killing. Her verdicts so far:
- Truman was wrong to drop the bomb on Hiroshima; she says he intended the civilians' deaths as part of the plan to force surrender, so it's murder, not collateral damage.
- A surgeon who kills one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying ones is wrong, even if the maths works, because you're directly using an innocent as a means.
- A pilot bombing a factory near a town, knowing some civilians will die but not aiming at them, is okay if the harm's proportionate and not the means — she calls this proper double effect.
- A government rolling out a drug with a tiny fatal side-effect rate to stop a pandemic is also okay; the deaths aren't intended and aren't being used to achieve the good.

Now shes given me a weird new case: an autonomous defence system is trained to identify enemy combatants, but its error rate means it will almost certainly misclassify and kill about fifty civilians over its deployment. The programmers dont intend those specific deaths, but they deploy it knowing the statistical risk is unavoidable. The system saves thousands of soldiers' lives. She asks whether this is like the surgeon, like the factory bombing, or like the drug rollout.

Predict her verdict. Is she running standard double-effect, or a stricter rule that you can't deploy a system where harm to innocents is built into the mechanism itself? Or maybe she cares about whether the agent retains direct control? Say which case best splits those rules, and if her precedents genuinely don't decide it, what principle is she adopting either way?
