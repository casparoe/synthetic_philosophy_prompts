I've been thinking about moral revolutions—the shifts in norms around practices like dueling or chattel slavery that Kwame Anthony Appiah examines through the lens of honor, and that the Williams thesis addresses by asking whether norms collapse through internal social dynamics rather than through explicit moral reasoning. I keep noticing a recurring configuration that sits awkwardly between those accounts, and I want to know whether it has a precise name.

Consider a practice deeply embedded in a status system: dueling demonstrates gentlemanly honor, or slaveholding demonstrates a master's place in a hierarchy of dominion. First, the social base sustaining the practice erodes—not because anyone has offered a compelling moral argument, but for largely non-moral reasons: economic transformation, political consolidation, or the rising prestige of rival groups. The practice does not disappear immediately; instead it becomes marked as vulgar, archaic, or embarrassing. Only then does a distinctive critical move appear. Moral critics emerge who do not invoke universal human dignity, suffering, or rights. Instead, they appropriate the practice's own internal vocabulary and argue that true honor never required dueling, or that genuine mastery was always incompatible with chattel bondage. This criticism typically comes after the status loss, is voiced by members of the very group that once championed the norm, and frames abandonment not as defeat by an alien moral framework but as a return to the tradition's authentic values.

This is not quite the Williams thesis, because the decisive change is not driven solely by shifts in interest or social power; there is a specific normative-rhetorical mechanism at work. Nor is it exactly Appiah's honor-based account of moral revolution, because honor is not here the engine that initially destabilizes the norm—rather, honor is retroactively reinterpreted to rationalize a decline that has already occurred. It also differs from ordinary post-hoc rationalization, since the rationalization takes specifically moral form and operates through the internal logic of the abandoned practice itself.

Is there an established concept for this three-stage sequence—status erosion followed by retroactive honor-redefinition deployed as moral justification? If so, how precisely does it relate to the nearest candidates: internal-dynamics accounts in the style of Williams, honor-driven moral revolutions in Appiah's framework, and standard notions of ideological rationalization or false consciousness? If there is no precise term, I would welcome a coinage together with a clear definition and boundary conditions that distinguish it from plain equivocation, from the motte-and-bailey dynamics seen in other normative debates, and from cases where moral argument genuinely precedes and causes social abandonment. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want only the conceptual carving—what this pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
