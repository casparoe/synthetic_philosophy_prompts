Write an exam question with a model answer and marking scheme for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar final (in-class, 90 minutes; one essay of about 800 words; 40 marks out of 100). The domain is the argument—associated with the Chase-Sanborn line—that qualia are an incoherent posit, particularly the claim that phenomenal properties cannot consistently be characterized as intrinsic, ineffable, private, and directly accessible.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard four-feature list or by summarizing familiar textbook responses. Instead, require students to do three things.

First, reconstruct the strongest conceptual tension within the incoherence charge: if a quale is genuinely non-relational (intrinsic), it cannot figure in comparative judgments of similarity or difference (e.g., that this experienced shade is closer in phenomenal space to that one than to a third), yet phenomenal experience apparently requires exactly such comparisons. Explain why an appeal to direct acquaintance or introspective access does not dissolve the tension but rather presupposes a subject-content relation that intrinsicness was meant to exclude.

Second, apply an injected distinction between thin phenomenal determinates (particular, demonstratively given instances: this specific ache, this precise shade now) and thick phenomenal determinables (the overall structure of phenomenal space treated as a non-physical domain). Ask whether relocating phenomenal content into thin determinates removes the contradiction, or whether it merely pushes the problem into the determinable/determinate relations that organize even particular experiences.

Third, determine the scope of a successful incoherence charge: does it undermine only a metaphysically thick dualist theory of consciousness, or does it also undermine the minimal claim that there is something-it-is-like?

Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words. Then provide a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-range answer from a B-range answer by naming specific required moves: for an A, correct identification of the relational presupposition hidden in acquaintance, explicit use of the determinable/determinate structure in part two, and a reasoned judgment about scope in part three. For a B, correct listing of the four properties with only generic discussion of tension and no structural analysis. Name the specific errors that should cost marks: treating the charge as an empirical claim about neuroscience or brain imaging; resolving part one by simply asserting that introspection is non-relational; ignoring the determinable/determinate distinction in part two; and failing to address whether the minimal phenomenological claim survives. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
