In my philosophy of history seminar we are studying Plutarch's Parallel Lives as a technology for ethical self-formation rather than as mere historical record. For our midterm, each student must select one figure—say Alcibiades or Coriolanus—reconstruct the narrative arc of a decisive moral failure, and translate it into a counterfactual decision protocol that we could deploy in an analogous contemporary situation. The rubric treats historical distance as an engineering advantage: we extract the failure mode from the biography, treat the subject's life as open-source moral firmware, and apply a patch to our own reasoning without requiring the figure to be a uniformly admirable exemplar. What view of moral learning from biography does this pedagogical practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates—particularly regarding whether the exercise depends on a hagiographic logic that demands sanitized heroes, or whether it can survive on the kind of unresolved, contradictory portrait Plutarch often draws. Then tell me what would have to change in the assignment's design if we replaced that implicit view with one that treats biography solely as documentary evidence about past agents, with no normative purchase on present decision-making.
