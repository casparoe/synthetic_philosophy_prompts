I've been trying to understand the debate about why some nations are wealthy and others remain poor, and I ended up constructing an argument that seems to undermine the entire discussion—including beliefs I don't want to give up. Here are the steps as I see them. (1) To genuinely establish that institutions cause prosperity, one must observe the same society with and without those institutions, holding geography, culture, and prior conditions constant. (2) History never provides this, since institutions co-evolve with geography, culture, and accumulated wealth, and they never change in isolation. (3) The same limitation applies to geographic explanations: to prove climate or disease ecology determines development, one would need to observe the same territory under different environmental conditions, which is impossible. (4) Therefore neither institutional nor geographic theories can satisfy the standard requirements of causal identification; both depend on counterfactual comparisons that are in principle unobservable. (5) When researchers claim to have isolated an institutional effect by comparing neighboring regions with different colonial histories, they are merely substituting one unobservable comparison—what if this region had not been colonized in that particular way?—for another. (6) If this is right, then the long-running disagreement is not about evidence but about which hidden counterfactual assumptions each side finds more intuitive. (7) Worse, the same reasoning applies to historical claims I strongly hold, such as that extractive colonial institutions damaged long-run growth: those too rely on comparing actual history to an unobserved path without extraction. (8) So my argument seems to prove that no causal knowledge about national development is possible, which feels absurd.

I want to know which step is the weak one—perhaps the assumption that causal identification requires laboratory-style isolation, or the claim that statistical comparison methods cannot approximate counterfactuals?—and what giving up that step costs me. If I reject it, do I recover a meaningful institutional debate, and do I also have to admit that my conviction about colonial harm is just an untestable preference rather than something supported by reasoning?
