me and two friends from my poli-sci seminar were arguing about democratic backsliding after class last week. Maya started by saying the term is basically useless now—just a partisan slur that liberals slap on right-wing governments they don't like. She pointed out that people screamed backsliding about Trump in the US even though he left office after losing, and that Hungary's Orban and Poland's PiS both won elections fairly recently, so if voters keep picking them how is democracy actually sliding? Jonah pushed back hard: he said there's a real institutional pattern—executive aggrandizement, packing constitutional courts, capturing media regulators—that's visible in Hungary and Poland and is qualitatively different from normal partisan disagreement. Maya said that sounds precise but falls apart on inspection: court packing was literally proposed by US Democrats for the Supreme Court, and media capture happens everywhere—look at public broadcasters in France or Germany being accused of bias. So Jonah is just dressing up his political preferences in institutional language.

Jonah replied that she's conflating expansion with capture—adding seats is different from creating disciplinary chambers for judges, like Poland did, that let the ruling party punish dissenting jurists. He said backsliding should be defined not by policy disagreement but by whether changes specifically lower the cost for the incumbent to stay in power regardless of voter preference—like Hungary's gerrymandering and electoral law tweaks that made it structurally harder for opposition to win even with pluralities. Maya shot back that this cost of staying in power metric is circular: it assumes we can measure structural difficulty independently of election results, but Turkey's Erdogan passed a presidential system via referendum with majority support—was that backsliding or democracy working? If it's backsliding, then democratic majorities can democratically backslide, which makes the concept self-undermining. Jonah said that's not circular, it's just showing backsliding can be popular, like democratic suicide. Maya said then backsliding is just democracy I dislike, and asked Jonah why he doesn't apply the same frame to left-wing centralization in other contexts—say, emergency decrees in some Latin American left governments—unless the concept is selectively deployed. Jonah admitted the concept is used asymmetrically but said that doesn't falsify the core mechanism.
