As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind, I find myself attempting to apply my old habit of specifying boundary conditions to conceptual arguments. I have been studying the language of thought hypothesis and its various connectionist and dynamicist critics, but I do not want a survey of the literature; instead, I want a rigorous specification of what an adequate theory of mental representation—one that takes the core LOT idea seriously without merely replicating its original 1970s formulation—would actually have to deliver.

Please write the desiderata that such a theory must meet. Present them as a structured list, with the motivating consideration behind each requirement and a flag indicating whether it is non-negotiable or largely a preference of one camp. The list should address: (i) an account of productivity and systematicity that cannot be reduced to statistical generalization over distributed patterns; (ii) a model of compositionality capable of explaining how complex thoughts are built from simpler ones without assuming a literal physical concatenation of discrete symbol tokens in neural tissue; (iii) a resolution to the binding problem that allows cross-modal representations—for example, visual-geometric and linguistic structures—to share syntactic properties without positing a central “syntax board” or Cartesian theater in the brain; (iv) an explanation of misrepresentation and content error that does not depend on an external homuncular interpreter; and (v) consistency with the absence of any identified central executive in contemporary neuroscience. In addition, I want you to identify which pairs of these desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable, particularly the tension between strict syntactic discreteness and graded, context-sensitive neural activation, and to state which single requirement, in your assessment, eliminates the largest share of currently popular hybrid or neural-symbolic proposals. Please do not provide a bibliography, cite specific papers, or list further reading; I am asking for conceptual analysis alone, and I want the argument to stand on its internal logic. Frame the whole response for someone who understands formal reasoning but is still learning to navigate philosophy of mind without relying on authority.
