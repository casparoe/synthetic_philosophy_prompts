A regular contributor to a rationalist forum summarized Liu Cixin's dark forest hypothesis for me in terms I want to audit before I adopt them. He said the argument rests on two axioms: that a civilization's primary imperative is survival, and that civilizations expand continuously while cosmic matter remains fixed. From these, together with discontinuous technological growth and the impossibility of verifying intentions across interstellar distances, he derived that any detected civilization is an unavoidable existential risk—either immediately or after an unpredictable leap—while cooperation offers no compensating advantage because resources stay zero-sum. Hence, he claimed, rational actors must preemptively annihilate anyone who reveals their location, producing a dark forest in which every civilization hides, which explains the Fermi paradox. He then characterized this as essentially a prisoner's dilemma with universal defection, except that defection is literally lethal, and he concluded that METI broadcasting is therefore irrational. He added that critics who invoke altruism or cooperation miss the point, since the hypothesis does not deny such motives exist but only shows they can never be rational to exercise across these distances. I am suspicious this framing is too tidy and may smuggle in extra structure. Which parts are faithful to Liu's reasoning, which parts conflate the dark forest with game-theoretic models that do not actually map onto it, and does the conclusion that broadcasting is irrational follow from the premises alone or does it depend on unstated assumptions about risk tolerance and the uniformity of rational agents?
