In my school's science and humanities departments, grading practices seem to treat the relationship between a word and what it refers to in strikingly different ways, and I am trying to understand what implicit philosophy of language makes one practice coherent while the other appears to contradict it. In chemistry exams, the rubric awards full credit for identifying a sample as water or writing its formula H₂O even when a student's explanation reveals that they conceive of it as an elementary substance rather than a compound, or when they associate the term with properties drawn from an entirely different chemical. The graders treat the reference of the term as fixed by something external to the student's mental content—apparently the actual molecular structure present in the environment—so that the student has said something true about H₂O despite holding false beliefs about it. By contrast, in literature and history assessments, a student who interprets a symbol or event in a way that diverges sharply from the instructor's view is not credited for having "referred correctly" to the symbol's objective meaning; instead, evaluation turns on the internal coherence and textual support for the student's own conception. The institution thus appears to operate with one model of reference in the laboratory and another in the classroom.

What philosophical commitments about meaning, reference, and speaker knowledge does this bifurcated grading policy presuppose? Does the science rubric commit the school to a form of semantic externalism—something like the view tested by the Twin Earth thought experiment, where "water" picks out H₂O in virtue of the speaker's causal or environmental connection to that substance rather than in virtue of any internal description? Or could the same grading practice be sustained by a social-deference theory, according to which students are merely borrowing experts' terms, without taking a stand on whether reference is fixed in the head or in the world? Is the humanities practice genuinely incompatible with that externalist picture, or could an externalist about natural-kind terms be an internalist about interpretive meaning without contradiction? Reconstruct the implicit theory of reference that best explains why the school separates "reference accuracy" from "conceptual accuracy" only in science, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the institution is philosophically consistent or whether it implicitly endorses two rival theories of meaning that cannot both be true. Finally, if the school were to adopt a uniformly internalist policy—treating all terms as fixed by the speaker's own beliefs and associations—what specific changes would have to be made to the chemistry grading rubric, and would those changes render the rubric more fair or less?
