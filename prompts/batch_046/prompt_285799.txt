The frame problem, in its classic form, asks how an artificial reasoner can update its beliefs about the world after an action without having to enumerate every property that remains unchanged—every untouched wall, every unbroken law of physics—as an explicit representation. For decades the literature has split between formalists who propose successor-state axioms or circumscription to compute non-effects, and critics—often from embodied or situated cognition—who treat the problem as a pseudo-problem arising only from the false premise that intelligence requires explicit propositional world-models. Despite extensive exchange, neither camp has persuaded the other, and recent work often abandons the vocabulary rather than resolving the conflict. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether explicit representation is necessary for rational action—or is it partly verbal, with "representation" and "model" meaning different things to each side? Or are the camps answering different questions under one heading: the formalists treating an engineering problem of belief update in a closed domain, and the critics treating a philosophical question about whether cognition needs internal propositional structures at all? There may also be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether embodied agents genuinely avoid combinatorial explosion, or merely displace it into learning dynamics and physical interaction that are not transparent. To sharpen the diagnosis, consider a synthetic variation: a planner solves the physical frame problem with axioms for object permanence and momentum, but then faces an implicit normative frame—social conventions and institutional rules that do not change with its actions, yet are too numerous and context-sensitive to enumerate, so that any axiomatization either omits critical constraints or produces an unmanageable proliferation of conditions. Does this show that the formalist solution is domain-limited to physics, or does it show that the critics' dismissal applies only to physical dynamics and fails for normative constraints? Take a committed position: is the frame problem a single ill-posed question that should be dissolved into separate technical and philosophical issues, or is there a unified problem that current approaches genuinely fail to solve? Give concrete reasons, engaging with what each side actually claims about computation, representation, and the role of explicit models.
