I have been working through an argument about karma understood specifically as a theory of moral desert, and it now seems to drive toward a conclusion I cannot accept: that karma is either metaphysically self-defeating or collapses into an unrecognizable form of moral bookkeeping. I want most of all to preserve the claim that karma is genuinely about personal desert rather than about systemic cosmic balance, and I also want to keep, if I can, the standard rebirth denial of an enduring self, though I am more firmly committed to the desert interpretation than to that metaphysical picture. Here is the chain as it stands. (1) For karma to function as a theory of moral desert rather than as a mere causal regularity, the benefits and burdens distributed must belong to the very agent who performed the actions that earned them; otherwise the suffering or flourishing assigned is not deserved in the relevant backward-looking sense but is arbitrarily placed. (2) Yet in the rebirth doctrines that typically frame karma, there is no enduring self—only a causal-psychological continuum across lives, structurally analogous to the psychological-continuity account of personal identity that permits fission and fusion without strict numerical identity over time. (3) So if an agent in life L1 performs a harmful act and an agent in life L2 receives suffering in response, the recipient in L2 is not strictly identical to the agent in L1 who performed the act; at best they stand in relations of causal and psychological continuity. (4) Therefore, if karma assigns that suffering to the L2 agent, it assigns it to someone other than the one who earned it, violating the condition laid out in (1). (5) A defender might reply that psychological continuity is sufficient for desert, just as it is often taken to be sufficient for prudential concern and for attributions of moral responsibility across temporal stages. (6) But prudential concern is forward-looking and can be justified by relations of connectedness without requiring strict identity; desert, by contrast, is backward-looking and seems conceptually tied to the idea that the same subject who acted is now being repaid, not merely that someone causally linked receives the payoff. (7) If we weaken the identity requirement for desert to allow mere psychological continuity, then we must also allow that distant causal descendants, or even strangers woven into the same causal web, could be appropriate recipients of desert-based rewards or punishments, which collapses karma into a broad consequentialist or systemic account of moral accounting with no distinctive link to the individual agent. (8) Thus either karma requires an enduring self that rebirth doctrines deny, or it abandons its claim to be a distinctive theory of personal desert and becomes indistinguishable from general moral bookkeeping. (9) Since the latter seems to strip karma of its explanatory power—why this particular suffering is justified for this particular sufferer rather than merely instrumentally useful for cosmic order—the argument concludes that karma as a theory of personal desert is either internally inconsistent or conceptually empty. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to reject either (6) or (7) if I can, because I want to hold both the continuity-friendly metaphysics and the desert interpretation, but I need to know exactly which step is weakest and what giving it up costs.
