I just finished a long and exhausting argument with a friend about climate obligations and people who do not yet exist. She insisted that if our carbon-intensive policies cause climate damage that leaves future people worse off, then we clearly harm them, and that harm gives us a direct reason to change course. I pressed the nonidentity problem: because our choices affect who is conceived, the specific individuals who live in the damaged world would not have existed had we acted differently, so there is no same person who is made worse off. She conceded the identity point but then shifted ground, saying that even if no particular individual is harmed, the state of affairs is still made worse in an impersonal sense, and that should suffice for moral urgency. I think we left a deeper conceptual knot unresolved. Could you explain, precisely, the tension between the person-affecting intuition that harm requires an identifiable victim and the impersonal evaluation that a world can be worse without being worse for anyone? In particular, I want to understand whether introducing a criterion of modal proximity—the idea that we compare the actual world not to any counterfactual but specifically to the nearest possible world in which the same individual exists—genuinely rescues the harm claim, or whether it merely relocates the problem by making harm depend on an arbitrary metric of world-similarity. I also want clarification on whether accepting this impersonal evaluation conceptually commits one to obligations to create additional happy people, or whether there is a principled boundary that separates making the world worse from merely failing to make it better. I am not asking for a survey of authors or texts; I want the conceptual architecture laid out with its pressure points exposed.
