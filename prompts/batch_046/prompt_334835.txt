I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about health and disease, going by my gut on concrete cases. A broken femur is obviosly a disease—something is physically wrong. Pregnancy is not a disease, even though it's constantly medicalized. Being 5'2" and perfectly healthy is not a disease, just short, though society treats average height as the default. Severe alcohol dependence is a disease, even if behavior plays a big role. Grief that leaves you unable to function for months after a partner dies feels like suffering, not sickness—I'd want support available, but I wouldn't call it a disease.

But then gender dysphoria with no detectable biological lesion: I want to say it's not a disease in the "broken part" sense, yet medical intervention still feels appropriate, not cosmetic. Meanwhile someone who wants purely cosmetic limb-lengthening: also not a broken part, but intervention feels wrong to me. So there's a tension I can't resolve. Same with long COVID that has clear biomarkers versus chronic fatigue with identical suffering but no biomarker—I think both are real diseases, but I feel way more sure about the first one, which is probably inconsistent. Then there's "pre-diabetes" flagged only by algorithmic risk scores when you feel totally fine: are you already sick, or just being watched? And the one I keep flipping on—autism without any distress, just a different neurotype: is that a health condition or just variation?

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these, maybe mixing natural dysfunction with some normative element? What would it say about cases I haven't mentioned, like voluntary cognitive enhancement or adaptive dissociation in high-surveillance environments? And which of my verdicts do I have to drop to keep it simple? Please write your answer in American English.
