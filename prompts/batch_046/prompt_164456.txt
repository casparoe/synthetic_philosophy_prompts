Analyze the 1971 Pentagon Papers episode through the lens of Hannah Arendt’s theory of modern political lying, developed in her essay reflecting on those documents. The salient facts are common knowledge: classified Department of Defense records revealed that successive U.S. administrations had systematically misled Congress and the public about the scope, costs, and military prospects of the Vietnam War, and that internal analyses frequently contradicted the optimistic public narratives advanced by officials. Arendt distinguishes traditional concealment—hiding specific secrets from enemies—from modern organized lying, in which bureaucratic “problem-solvers” replace a shared factual reality with a self-consistent scenario, eventually becoming self-deceived and destroying the common ground required for political judgment.

Apply this framework with precision. First, map the episode onto Arendt’s model: identify who functioned as the problem-solvers, what factual record (the internal assessments of unwinnable conflict, inflated body counts, or underestimated enemy strength) was displaced by the public scenario, and how the institutional split between classified analysis and public image operated. Second, ask whether the Papers confirm Arendt’s diagnosis of collective self-deception, or whether they reveal a different mechanism—specifically, institutional compartmentalization, in which contradictory assessments coexisted across agencies, some officials genuinely believed the optimistic projections, and others knowingly suppressed dissent without any unified narrative being constructed. Does this fragmented deception fit Arendt’s image of reality being replaced wholesale by a fabricated scenario, or does it show that democratic bureaucratic deception operates through epistemic fragmentation rather than through a single, collectively inhabited lie?

Then run the analysis in reverse. If the deception was compartmentalized rather than uniformly self-deceived, what does that do to Arendt’s claim that lying undermines the “common world”? Does it suggest her framework, developed with totalitarian regimes in view, misdescribes democratic bureaucracies, or can it be adapted by treating each compartment as its own enclosed reality? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent: do not force Arendt’s concepts onto institutional dynamics she never addressed. Conclude by assessing whether the Pentagon Papers strengthen her distinction between traditional and modern lying, expose a limitation in her reliance on collective narrative replacement, or transform the distinction itself—perhaps by showing that modern political lying need not destroy one shared world so much as multiply incompatible ones.
