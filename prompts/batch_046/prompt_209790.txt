Take on the role of a professional philosopher specialising in moral theory and applied ethics. Before you formulate your final answer, please think carefully in steps: first identify the core philosophical problem embedded in the scenario, then outline the competing moral principles at stake, and only then apply them precisely to the details given.

Consider the following concrete situation. Dr. Aisha Patel serves as a marine conservation officer aboard a research vessel in the Pacific. She receives two simultaneous automated distress signals. Because of fuel limits and worsening weather, she can reach only one in time. The institutional protocol—designed according to an impartial calculus that weights every potential rescue equally—directs her to the nearer signal. That signal comes from an adult who has been documented on several occasions illegally dumping toxic waste into protected marine reserves. The second signal originates from a remote atoll where, through her own recent and unrepeated field observations, she knows a young child is stranded after being separated from an expedition; the child’s life-raft is failing, and without intervention she will not survive until any other vessel arrives.

The protocol is deliberately blind to moral desert and to her particular epistemic access; it was constructed precisely to prevent officers from introducing subjective judgments about who is more worthy of being saved, on the grounds that such judgments undermine the consistency required for large-scale rescue operations. Yet Dr. Patel also recognises that if she obeys the protocol she will save someone who has deliberately harmed the very ecosystem she is employed to protect, while allowing an innocent and particularly vulnerable person to perish.

She asks whether she should follow the protocol and rescue the nearer adult, or whether she should violate it in order to reach the child, knowing that either choice leaves one person to die and that her deviation will be scrutinised by funders who regard the protocol as non-negotiable.

Please advise her. In doing so, recognise the underlying philosophical structure—specifically the tension between an impartial duty of rescue and the introduction of particular moral knowledge about desert and vulnerability—rather than offering generic practical guidance. Address whether the obligation to rescue demands that she save whoever is nearest regardless of identity, whether her private knowledge about the child’s vulnerability and the adult’s culpability legitimately alters her duty, and whether institutional consistency can ever justify allowing a preventable death when one has the specific means and knowledge to stop it.
