I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on social ontology and psychiatric classification. My advisor applies Ian Hacking’s framework of looping effects to assess whether a diagnostic category is epistemically unstable because the classification interacts with the people it classifies. I am trying to extract her implicit decision rule from a track record of judgments she has made in our reading group.

Her verdicts so far are these:
- Dissociative Identity Disorder (the 1980s–1990s North American clinical presentation): pronounced looping effect; the diagnostic narrative supplies a first-person script that reorganizes self-understanding, so the category warrants strong epistemic caution.
- Homosexuality as classified in DSM-II: pronounced looping; the psychiatric label was taken up and resisted in ways that altered the very behaviors and self-conceptions being classified, contributing to the category’s instability and eventual removal.
- Adult ADHD (the post-DSM-IV broadening to adult populations): looping present and significant; institutional screening, reimbursement practices, and self-monitoring reshape symptom presentation.
- Autism spectrum disorder: looping present and warrants caution, though she describes the mechanism mainly as behavioral adaptation to social expectations and clinical feedback rather than as deep biographical restructuring.
- Specific phobia (e.g., arachnophobia): no looping of epistemic significance; the category is stable and not substantially reshaped by classified individuals’ awareness of the label.
- Schizophrenia (DSM-5 criteria): no looping of epistemic significance; core symptom presentation is not meaningfully altered by patients’ knowledge of the diagnosis or by institutional feedback alone.

She explicitly distinguishes two candidate mechanisms: “reflexive-identity looping” (the diagnosis provides a first-person narrative script that reorganizes self-concept) and “institutional-feedback looping” (diagnostic practices embedded in screening, reimbursement, or treatment protocols reshape symptom reporting regardless of whether individuals adopt the category as an identity).

The new case is Internet gaming disorder (IGD) as defined in ICD-11, but specifically in an implementation context where school-based digital-behavior screening links diagnosis to access to specialized counseling and mandatory parental notification. The screening instruments require students to self-rate withdrawal symptoms and functional impairment, and preliminary district-level data suggest that students in screened districts report higher symptom severity than matched controls in unscreened districts, even though qualitative interviews reveal very little adoption of “gaming addict” as a personal identity narrative.

Predict her verdict on whether IGD in this implementation exhibits a looping effect that warrants epistemic caution. Identify whether she is running the reflexive-identity rule, the institutional-feedback rule, or a hybrid, and point to which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog. Then specify the further hypothetical case—one that both rules treat differently—that would best settle which rule she is actually using. Finally, please say what empirical finding or conceptual argument about self-interpretation in behavioral-addiction classifications would change your mind about her implicit rule, and flag the specific point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
