I serve on the Graduate Examination and Curriculum Policy Board of our philosophy doctoral program, and we must decide a concrete case that has forced us to clarify what counts as disciplinary understanding when research is mediated by generative systems. Our candidate—who works in epistemology and philosophy of science—submitted her written comprehensive exam on scientific explanation. Rather than a conventional literature review, she used an AI system to construct what she terms “argumentative topologies”: network representations of premises, objections, and revisions across roughly thirty primary texts. Her written submission was sophisticated, and in the oral defense she navigated these networks with fluency, anticipating counter-arguments and tracking dependencies with precision. Yet when the examining committee removed the scaffold—asking her to reconstruct a specific debate about mechanistic explanation from her own grasp, without reference to nodes or edges—her performance collapsed. She could recall that an objection existed, but not why it was structurally compelling, nor how it related to the underlying metaphysical commitments of the authors in question.

The board is divided. One faction holds that this is a legitimate form of distributed cognition: she has built a reliable external scaffold, and her mastery consists in knowing how to navigate and correct it, not in internalizing every internal relation. They warn that requiring “first-person structural authority”—the capacity to regenerate the argumentative landscape from one’s own conceptual grasp—ignores how philosophers actually work with archives, indexes, and collaborative tools. The opposing faction argues that doctoral-level understanding requires precisely that regenerative capacity, and that without it she is merely epistemically dependent on an opaque mediator. They further worry that certifying her exam would institutionalize a form of “hermeneutic flattening,” in which students come to understand the map rather than the territory, and that our program would be accrediting competence in tool-use rather than philosophical judgment. Both sides agree that the issue is not mere plagiarism or misconduct, but a deeper question about whether epistemic dependence on an uninterpreted synthetic system erodes the kind of understanding our degree is meant to guarantee.

We must issue a formal decision memo before next month’s faculty meeting, with a clear recommendation—whether to pass her with conditions, require a revised oral examination under scaffold-free conditions, or institute a program-wide policy on AI-mediated synthesis in comprehensive exams—together with a realistic implementation time frame. The memo should treat the distributed-cognition objection seriously rather than dismissing it as technological anxiety, and should avoid generic appeals to “balance” or “critical thinking.” Please address the board directly, recommend a concrete course of action, and include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side, so that we can determine whether our current criteria for understanding are adequate to cases where the cognitive labor is genuinely divided between human agent and system.
