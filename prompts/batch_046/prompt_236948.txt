Consider the institutional practice, now common in democratic education systems, of mandating that state-approved school history textbooks present “balanced” or “multi-perspectival” accounts of nationally contested origins and conflicts: for example, requiring American state standards to treat both 1619 and 1776 as competing “foundational moments” without granting either historiographical primacy, or requiring Japanese and Korean textbooks to include diplomatically negotiated “balanced” treatments of wartime conduct that allocate narrative space according to political representation rather than scholarly weight. The curriculum boards that enforce these rules typically justify them not as compromises but as expressions of democratic pluralism and pedagogical objectivity.

What philosophical commitments about historical knowledge, democratic legitimacy, and the authority of the classroom does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat historical significance as a perspectival property distributed among interpretive communities, or does it smuggle in a realist view of historical fact that it then parcels out across “sides”? Does it presuppose that democratic legitimacy requires equal narrative representation regardless of evidential support, or that pedagogical neutrality consists in reproducing the political distribution of memory rather than the distribution of scholarly argument? Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it comfortably—whether, for instance, it is genuinely incompatible with a straightforward evidentialist historiography in which the best-supported narrative is taught and “balance” is interpreted as presenting the range of peer-reviewed debate rather than allocating space to politically defined positions. Finally, if the underlying view were replaced by such an evidentialist framework, what specific institutional mechanisms—approval-board composition, dispute-resolution criteria, teacher-training norms—would have to change, and which parts of the current practice could survive that replacement intact?
