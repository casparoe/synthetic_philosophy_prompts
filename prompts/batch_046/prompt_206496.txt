I was arguing with a friend late into the evening about the democratic boundary problem—specifically, whether a proposed transboundary climate assembly should include not just citizens of participating states, but also future generations represented by guardians, non-citizen residents of affected watersheds, and collective proxies for severely impacted ecosystems. At one point, frustrated with my objections about circularity, she kept falling back on the line: "If you're affected by the decision, you belong to the demos that makes it." She seemed to think this settled the membership question, but I felt it was smuggling in commitments about causation, representation, and standing that she never defended.

Can you lay out the different precise theses that slogan could be expressing—distinguishing, for instance, causal-impact accounts, coercion-or-subjection accounts, legitimate-interest accounts, and more expansive relational or ecological readings that might license including non-human representatives? For each interpretation, say whether it actually solves the boundary problem or merely relocates it (for example, by requiring a prior decision about who counts as affected and how severely), whether it risks absurd over-inclusion in the climate-assembly case I described, and which version my friend was most likely invoking when she used it to justify ecosystem proxies. I want a menu of readings with verdicts on plausibility and practical coherence, not a defense of a single one.

Please also say explicitly what would change your mind about which interpretation is most defensible or about whether any version genuinely avoids circularity—whether that is an argument about the inescapability of prior boundaries, about the anthropocentric bias hidden in selecting representatives for future or ecological interests, or about some other weakness in the reasoning. And tell me where your analysis is most likely to fail, particularly if it assumes that affectedness can be sharply demarcated or treats democratic membership as binary rather than graded.
