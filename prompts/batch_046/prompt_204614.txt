Construct a thought experiment that extends the structure of Lewis Carroll's tortoise-and-Achilles regress into an ordinary practical-reasoning context, engineered so that three accounts of deductive justification—direct grasp of logical form, explicit hierarchical premises, and procedural entitlement through a reliable but resource-bound cognitive architecture—deliver different verdicts on whether an agent is rationally entitled to act after a chain of five sequential modus ponens inferences. Constraints: the setting must be realistic (a hospital or workplace, not a trolley problem or science-fiction scenario), the chain must lead to a concrete actionable conclusion (e.g., administering a treatment or signing a contract), and you must introduce a single novel psychological feature—premise fatigue—whereby each explicit articulation of a logical rule as a separate premise reduces the agent's confidence in the final conclusion by a fixed, non-negligible increment. Present the scenario in one paragraph without naming the three theories. Then demonstrate, for each account, whether the agent is justified in acting and why; identify the precise structural feature of the case that forces the three verdicts apart. Next, explain what happens to the divergence if premise fatigue is removed—does it collapse back into a standard Carroll case, or shift to a different disagreement? Finally, show how a defender of the account that comes out worst would attack the scenario, and assess whether that attack succeeds without begging the question against the other two.
