I've been taking this philosophy of democracy seminar and we're stuck on Popper's tolerance paradox — you know, the classic idea that if you're totally open then intolerance eventually wipes out openness itself. But our instructor pushed us further into the weeds: she said the real problem isn't just that intolerance crashes the party from outside, it's that the *procedures* we build to stay neutral (giving every position a platform, refusing to exclude anyone in advance) actually become the delivery system for the intolerant view. So neutrality is structurally self-defeating, not just vulnerable.

I want to close our last session with a poem that actually performs this logic instead of just naming it. Can you write a pantoum — the form where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, cycling back until they collapse together in the end — about that specific twist? Use repeating lines like "the rule that lets every voice recieve air" and "the air, once filled, cannot be cleared", and let the repetition literally do the work: each time those lines return, their meaning should shift so that the mechanism of inclusion gradually becomes indistinguishable from the exclusion it was meant to prevent. Five stanzas, stark tone, don't resolve it with some sermon about free speech — leave the tension hanging, since that's honestly where we ended up. Make it accurate enough that someone who knows the paradox recognizes the procedural point, but don't just drop Popper's name and call it done.
