I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of history, and next Thursday I present to our department’s philosophy of science workshop a thesis I call stratified overdetermination. The core claim is that standard analyses of causal redundancy—two independently sufficient conditions for one outcome—misrepresent how multi-causal explanation actually works in history. I distinguish structural conditions, which establish what I term a causal field (making some macro-outcome highly resilient across nearby counterfactual worlds but leaving its timing and specific trajectory indeterminate), from triggering events, which are modally fragile but necessary for the particular path taken. On this view, events like the collapse of the Weimar Republic or the outbreak of the First World War are not flatly overdetermined in the philosophical sense; rather, they exhibit layered modal properties, with robustness at the macro-structural stratum and fragility at the micro-triggering stratum. Treating these as redundant sufficient conditions collapses explanatory architecture that historians actually care about—why this regime fell in 1933 rather than 1929, or why the assassination in Sarajevo produced a continental war rather than a localized crisis. The audience will be precise and skeptical: mostly analytic philosophers of science with strong metaphysics backgrounds, one historian of science who resists importing modal machinery into archival work, a political theorist worried I am smuggling determinism back in through structural language, and several graduate students familiar with the standard causal-redundancy literature but not with historical explanation specifically. They are not hostile, but they will press hard on whether my distinction is ontological or merely descriptive, and whether it solves anything that counterfactual historiography does not already cover.
