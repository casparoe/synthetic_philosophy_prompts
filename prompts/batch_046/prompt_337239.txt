At a recent writers' retreat, two fellow novelists argued over the ethics of dramatizing a real, dead surgeon from the 1870s whose journals confirm that he performed an experimental amputation without ether, claiming that patient consciousness aided surgical precision. The novelist in question had invented an interior monologue attributing the choice not to clinical conviction but to an unacknowledged desire to dominate suffering. The debate that followed turned on what obligation, if any, artists owe such figures.

Elena held that the dead retain a form of posthumous dignity that prohibits what she called representational ventriloquism: assigning them psychological motives that the archive neither supports nor denies violates a duty of epistemic humility. Because they cannot correct misapprehension, inventing interiority that reframes their documented actions transforms them from historical agents into narrative instruments, and this constitutes a dignitary harm distinct from mere factual error.

Marcus replied that dignitary harm requires a subject capable of being harmed, and the dead are not subjects in any philosophically robust sense. The only relevant injury is to living readers who might mistake fiction for biography. If the external events remain accurate—the amputation occurred, ether was available, the clinical justification was offered—then supplying an interpretive psychology is no more unethical than a historian proposing an unverified causal thesis. To forbid this is to confuse narrative interpretation with archival forgery.

Elena answered that the comparison to historiography misses the point, because historians argue with evidence and invite correction, whereas fiction presents itself as aesthetic experience and thereby smuggles interpretation into collective memory without the safeguards of scholarship. The dead are uniquely defenseless: a living person may publish a rebuttal, but the dead can only be spoken for, never speak. This asymmetry creates an obligation not merely to avoid falsifying actions, but also to avoid colonizing interior lives with motives that serve dramatic irony rather than historical understanding.

Marcus then distinguished between archival corruption—altering dates, inventing actions, or denying documented intentions—which is clearly wrong, and interpretive dramatization—supplying emotional textures consistent with known behavior but unverified by record—which is inevitable in any biographical art. He argued that Elena's standard, applied consistently, would prohibit any novel about a real person, since every selection and emphasis is an interpretation. The real ethical boundary, he said, lies between inventing internal states that contradict the subject's explicit self-understanding and inventing those that merely complicate it. In this case, since the surgeon's journals never address personal vanity, the invented motive does not contradict his account; it only illuminates a shadow the archive leaves dark.

Elena conceded that not all interpretation is colonization, but insisted that there is a difference between complicating a record and substituting one's own thematic architecture for the subject's agency. She proposed a criterion she called the principle of non-substitution: a novelist may imagine what the dead might have felt, but may not replace the historical agent's own framework of meaning with one that makes the agent serve the novel's moral design. Here, she argued, the surgeon's clinical framework is erased and replaced by a narrative of domination that renders him a more convenient antagonist for modern readers.

Marcus responded that every historical narrative, including scholarly ones, selects frameworks that serve present concerns; the surgeon's clinical framework was itself a product of his era's values, not a sacred self-definition. To treat it as inviolable is to commit what he termed the curatorial fallacy—treating biography as museum preservation rather than living argument. The obligation to the dead, he concluded, is not to preserve their self-image intact but to avoid demonstrable falsehood about their actions, while allowing art to ask questions the archive cannot answer.

Then say which party is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled line between legitimate interpretive dramatization and the kind of representational substitution Elena fears, or whether Marcus's curatorial fallacy dissolves the problem entirely.
