As a novelist who has spent the better part of a decade constructing fictional societies that must feel historically coherent without being deterministically plotted, I have become increasingly preoccupied with a debate among economic historians and development scholars that seems, to my eye, less like an empirical dispute and more like a disagreement about what kind of story history allows. For at least three decades, the literature on why some nations are rich and others remain poor has circled the same triangle—institutions versus geography versus culture—producing ever more sophisticated regressions, instrumental-variable strategies, and natural experiments, yet the camps have barely moved from their founding positions. My working hypothesis is that this stall is not primarily a failure of data but a conceptual deadlock about causal explanation in macro-history: the institutionalists implicitly treat development as a sequence of contingent critical junctures that could have broken differently; the geographers treat deep structural constraints as almost narratively inevitable; and the culturalists invoke path-dependent meanings and collective self-understandings that resist counterfactual testing altogether. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement—about identification strategies, about the appropriate unit of analysis, about whether counterfactual reasoning is even licit at this temporal scale—that makes this a genuine empirical impasse in disguise, with the conceptual confusion merely ornamenting it?

I want a structural diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature: tell me whether these sides are actually answering different questions under the single heading “development,” whether the disagreement is partly verbal around the word “cause,” or whether there is a substantive clash about standards of evidence that neither side can resolve because history offers no laboratory and no true repetition. Be concrete about what each camp actually assumes—do the institutionalists require a kind of narrative contingency that geographers reject as romantic fiction, and do the culturalists smuggle in normative commitments about collective identity that make their claims unfalsifiable by design? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would genuinely count as progress: a formal result in causal inference applicable to non-repeatable macro-events, a new conceptual distinction that dissolves the triangle into separate inquiries, or an honest admission that the question is ill-posed for the kind of knowledge human societies can have about themselves? Please separate carefully what is settled in the methodological literature—particularly around the identification problems that plague cross-national comparison—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who understand those problems deeply, and do not present any one camp’s framework as if it were the consensus view. Before you begin, take a moment to think step-by-step about the logic of historical explanation itself, and approach this as an expert philosopher of social science who respects the novelist’s intuition that every macro-historical outcome feels overdetermined the moment you try to write it into a coherent plot with a single cause.
