I've been teaching this grad seminar on AI governance and we keep circling back to the competitiveness-constraint argument — the idea that if one lab spends real resources on alignment it gets outpaced by rivals who don't, so the 'alignment tax' isn't just technical but a kind of market trap. My students read the usual essays and come away convinced it's either an obvious prisoner's dilemma or an overblown bogeyman, and neither camp ever moves the other. But I'm starting to think the stall isn't about empirics at all.

Here's my hunch, though I could be totally off: one side is talking about a short-run product disadvantage (aligned models are slower, more cautious, less flashy for users), while the other is talking about long-run research-path dependency (once you commit to a safety-first architecture you can't pivot to the scaling paradigm that dominates). They both call it 'the alignment tax,' but they're measuring different things — quarterly revenue versus trajectory lock-in over decades. Meanwhile there's a weird third position I sketched: that the tax might actually be negative in some regimes, meaning alignment work produces capability spillovers that accelerate rather than retard progress, but only if you define 'alignment' as mechanistic interpretability rather than behavioral guardrails. Nobody in the literature sits here comfortably.

So is this impasse verbal? Are we just using 'competitiveness' to cover product-market fit, talent retention, and compute-optimal scaling all at once? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about whether safety investments actually slow capability gains — something we could settle with internal lab data we'll never see? Or are the sides answering different normative questions: one asking 'what should a rational actor do?' and the other 'what institutional design makes rationality collective?' Diagnose it using what each side actually says, not strawmen. And tell me what progress would look like — a formal model of multi-lab dynamics, a conceptual distinction that splits the tax into seperate components, or should we admit the question is ill-posed because 'alignment' and 'capability' aren't really seperable metrics? I'm genuinely unsure if we're stuck because the problem is hard or because we're talking past each other.
