I was arguing with a friend about the nonidentity problem and I think we accidently built a monster. Here's the chain — tell me which link to break and what breaking it costs me, because right now it feels like I either have to deny that climate change can harm anyone or that parents can ever do wrong.

(1) If an act is morally wrong, it must wrong some actual, existant person — you can't wrong a merely possible person who never gets born.
(2) In reproductive or long-range environmental choices, any difference in conditions, timing, or genetics produces a numerically different person; call this the Genetic Identity Shift.
(3) So if a parent conceives now with a condition that causes suffering, the child who results wouldn't exist had they waited; the "alternative" is a different child entirely, not the same child unharmed.
(4) The same applies across centuries: if I burn coal today, the climate in 2200 shifts conception conditions, so the people living then are not the ones who'd live if I biked.
(5) Therefore, for any specific future person harmed by climate change or disability, had the "bad" choice not been made, that very person wouldn't exist at all.
(6) So no actual person is made worse off relative to non-existence — they have lives worth living, and non-existence isn't a deprivation they suffer.
(7) Now add: if an act doesn't wrong any actual person, and doesn't violate rights of present people, it's not morally wrong.
(8) So neither reproductive negligence nor individual carbon emissions can be wrong.
(9) But we both think it's wrong to recklessly conceive a suffering child when you could easily wait, and wrong to burn coal for fun.
(10) Therefore (7) is false — but if we keep (7), we have to bite the bullet on (8).

I feel like there's a slide between "this specific person isn't worse off" and "no one is wronged," but I can't name it. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if I can, since they feel like common sense, but I'm not sure what I'm paying if I reject (7). Also, if the whole argument rests on treating identity like it's locked to a genetic fingerprint, is that the weak spot? And please push back hard if I'm mixing up the nonidentity problem with something else — I'm not a philosopher, just someone who took one course and is now overthinking.
