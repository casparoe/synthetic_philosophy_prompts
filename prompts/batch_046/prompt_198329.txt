Adopt, for the sake of argument, a strong Braudelian structuralism: the claim that in macrohistorical explanation the longue durée—geographic morphology, climatic rhythms, and demographic regimes—functions not as mere background conditions but as interventionist structural causes, such that counterfactual alterations to political events (the death of a monarch, the outcome of a naval battle, a dynastic crisis) leave long-range socio-economic trajectories invariant, while counterfactual alterations to slow geographic or climatic variables propagate downward and reshape the space of possible events. Treat this as the dominant framework for understanding the sixteenth-century Mediterranean world. Now play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Which of its implicit commitments—about scale-invariant causal asymmetry, the modularity of social structures, or the non-historical constitution of geographic constraints—would need to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure: are there live conceptual objections (for instance, that slow variables are themselves path-dependent and historically constituted), live empirical challenges (institutional innovations that decouple economic rhythms from climate), or formal results (from complex systems theory) that undermine the claimed downward invariance? Distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward anomalies, and indicate whether defenders of the view could concede certain failures—say, partial path-dependence or regional decoupling—without abandoning structural priority altogether.
