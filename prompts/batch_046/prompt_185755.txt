At a recent dinner, two friends—both well-read but neither a professional stratigrapher—got into a sustained argument about whether the Anthropocene should be formally ratified as a geological epoch, and I listened without taking sides. The first friend argued that the only defensible start date is around 1945, marked by the global plutonium-239 and cesium-137 spike, because stratigraphy requires a synchronous, readable boundary in the rock record; diachronous markers like the spread of agriculture fail this test, and the International Commission on Stratigraphy is right to demand a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point or at least a Global Standard Stratigraphic Age rather than letting historians vote on a concept. The second friend replied that selecting 1945 is exactly the kind of Whig history the prompt warns against: it privileges Western nuclear modernity and instruments that happen to leave traces scientists like, while ignoring equally global signals like the carbon-dip associated with the Columbian Exchange around 1610—the so-called Orbis hypothesis—simply because that narrative implicates colonial extraction rather than atomic physics. She added that turning the decision over to a small guild of stratigraphers in Lyon makes a democratic, planetary ethical problem into an exercise in methodological conservatism, and that describing multivariate convergence—plastics, radionuclides, nitrogen cycles—smuggles in a holistic historical judgment that is not actually stratigraphic. The first friend countered that every taxonomy involves curation, that the Periodic Table does not include every isotope, and that post-1945 human impacts genuinely exceed Holocene variability across independent proxies simultaneously, which is a multivariate fact rather than a narrative choice; he also said that her democratic framing confuses who should describe reality with who gets to define it. The second friend closed by saying that calling convergence an "objective" feature of the sediment already assumes what needs proving—namely, that human geological agency is best captured as a sudden rupture rather than a long, uneven process—and that if the boundary is genuinely about system states rather than discrete events, then the entire epoch framework is the wrong tool, which the ICS will never admit because its authority depends on preserving it.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and adjudicate who is right at each specific step—not just who wins overall? In particular, assess whether the Whig-history charge actually lands against the stratigraphic argument, whether the "guild decision" objection undermines the authority claim or just changes the subject, and what both of them are missing about the relationship between stratigraphic utility and historical explanation. Also say whether their disagreement is genuinely about evidence and method, or whether they are talking past each other because one is defending a taxonomy and the other is defending a historical narrative, and if so, whether there is any synthesis that respects both constraints without collapsing into vagueness.
