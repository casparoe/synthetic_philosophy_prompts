I am a high school student serving on my district’s curriculum review committee, which meets monthly to decide how historical figures should appear in our required history courses—whether standard biographical treatments should stand, be accompanied by critical contextual notes, or be replaced with alternative representatives. We have about thirty minutes per case, limited philosophical training, and a mandate to be consistent rather than reactive; what we lack is a reusable procedure that translates philosophical analysis of the man-of-his-time defense into practical steps non-specialists can run. I need you to construct a decision flowchart in words that operationalizes this defense not as a single vague intuition but as three distinct, testable axes: epistemic accessibility, meaning whether credible moral objections to the figure’s conduct were actually articulated and available within their own intellectual environment rather than being purely retrospective; normative accessibility, meaning whether viable alternative courses of action were institutionally and culturally live options for someone in their position, not merely logically conceivable from our present standpoint; and structural coercion, meaning whether the figure’s institutional role made the harmful action practically mandatory rather than genuinely discretionary. For each axis, specify the questions to ask in sequence, what evidentiary threshold counts as a sufficient answer, the point at which the procedure halts with a concrete recommendation—retain standard presentation, add contextual framing, or substitute the figure—and the explicit points where it must defer to a value judgment because the axis yields an ambiguous or contested result. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and flag the hard cases where the axes conflict, such as high epistemic accessibility paired with high structural coercion.

Then apply the full procedure to Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus, ensuring that at least one case produces a recommendation that will likely feel uncomfortable to committee members who entered with strong prior views. After presenting the procedure and the three worked applications, provide a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following schema: procedure_steps (array of strings describing each ordered step), judgment_flags (array of strings noting where judgment is unavoidable), cases (object with keys Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, each containing axis_scores—an object with keys epistemic_accessibility, normative_accessibility, and structural_coercion, each with value high, low, or mixed—plus conflict_points as an array of strings and recommendation as a string), and limitations (array of strings describing what the procedure cannot settle).
