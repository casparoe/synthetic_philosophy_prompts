I am a high school student presenting at my school's philosophy symposium. I defend a hybrid view of dreams: they are not experiences in Malcolm's strict sense, because they lack the waking verification criteria that confer authority on first-person reports, but neither are they mere Dennett-style retrospective cassettes replayed upon awakening. Instead, I argue that REM states are proto-experiential simulations—neural-phenomenal structures that remain incomplete during sleep and acquire full experiential status only through a constitutive act of waking narration, where the narration completes rather than reconstructs an unfinished phenomenal form. The audience is my philosophy teacher, who takes a phenomenological line and generally trusts first-person reports; two classmates who have read Dennett and will defend the anti-experiential, confabulation-friendly account; and a visiting graduate student in cognitive science who believes dream content is always a post-hoc construction with no genuine phenomenal core during REM. Prepare me for the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased as each of these three would phrase them, ordered by how damaging they are. For each, tell me what I should concede, what I must hold, where my reply is weakest, and flag the single question I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, the challenge that decoded REM neuroimaging can identify specific dream content without relying on any waking verbal report. Keep the prep sheet strictly conceptual, since I cannot cite specific studies, authors, or page numbers from memory.
