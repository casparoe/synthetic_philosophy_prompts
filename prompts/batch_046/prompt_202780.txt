Following a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend over dinner—she works in technology policy and I am currently taking a graduate seminar in philosophy of technology—I find myself stuck on a meta-question about artificial intelligence alignment that neither of us could resolve. We were not disagreeing about any particular empirical result, but about what the problem itself comprises. She maintained that alignment is fundamentally a single problem: ensuring that an advanced system’s behavior tracks the intentions of its designers rather than optimizing for some misaligned proxy. I countered that this framing collapses at least three distinguishable challenges—specification of values, robust internalization of those values across distributional shift, and preservation of alignment through potential recursive capability gains, which I have been calling structural alignment—and that treating them as one obscures both the distinct failure modes and the different methods required to address each.

The debate stalled in a way that felt familiar from the literature: both of us could invoke the same canonical examples—reward hacking, goal misgeneralization, instrumental convergence—yet she read them as instances of the same underlying failure, while I read them as symptoms of different pathologies. This makes me wonder whether the long-standing impasse over the nature of alignment is genuinely substantive—perhaps an empirical disagreement about whether progress on one front reliably transfers to the others—or whether it is partly verbal, with different research communities using the alignment problem to pick out different objects of study. It also seems possible that a normative disagreement is hidden inside the conceptual one: maybe we prioritize different failure modes because we hold different implicit judgments about which risks are catastrophic versus merely serious, and about what control ought to mean in the first place. Can you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Specifically, I want to know whether the unity-versus-plurality debate is best understood as a dispute about facts, about conceptual boundaries, about standards of evidence, or about bedrock intuitions; whether either side is missing a distinction that would dissolve the tension; and what—if anything—would actually count as progress, be it a formal separation result, a case study showing coupled versus decoupled failures, or a reframing of the question itself.
