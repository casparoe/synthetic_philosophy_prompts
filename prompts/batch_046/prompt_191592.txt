Below are notes for a short essay I am drafting, titled "Architecture-Level Intervention and the Limits of Modularity in Social Causation." The aim is to press the standard contrast between counterfactual-dependence theories (roughly the Lewis-Stalnaker line) and interventionist-manipulation theories (Pearl, Woodward) into a domain where neither is usually tested: institutional causation, specifically cases in which the "variables" are constitutively intertwined legal and economic structures.

The notes run as follows. First, the familiar problem: counterfactual accounts handle preemption poorly in social settings. If reform R occurs, and both a grassroots mobilization G and a legislative compromise L are each sufficient, the counterfactual "if not-G then not-R" fails even though G genuinely contributed. Interventionists avoid this by requiring that an intervention on G change R while other mechanisms are held fixed; but in institutional contexts, holding other mechanisms fixed is often conceptually impossible, because altering property-rights enforcement without altering contract-adjudication norms may not be a coherent institutional state.

Second, my proposed move—this is the part I want scrutinized—is to relax the modularity constraint not by abandoning interventionism but by reconceiving the intervention target. Instead of manipulating a single variable within a fixed causal graph, we intervene on what I call the "governance architecture": the higher-order rules that determine which lower-order dependencies are active. This is still an intervention in the agency-centered, manipulationist sense—an external actor reconfigures the institutional design—but it is non-modular with respect to the first-order variables. The claim is that this saves interventionism for macro-social explanation without reducing causation to counterfactual dependence across closest possible worlds, since the evaluation criterion remains "what changes if we redesign the architecture?" rather than "what happens in the nearest world where G is absent."

Third, I argue that this move comes at a cost. Once architecture-level redesign is admitted, the distinction between "intervening on X" and "changing the background conditions that define X" blurs. Counterfactualists can then argue that all we have done is smuggled in a holistic counterfactual—"what would the institutional outcomes be under architecture A versus architecture B?"—disguised in interventionist vocabulary. I reply that the difference remains in the direction of analysis: counterfactual evaluation moves from effect to cause via world-comparison, whereas architecture-level interventionism moves from a designed change in rules to an observed reconfiguration of outcomes, preserving the asymmetry of manipulation.

(2) Is the preemption problem genuinely more severe for counterfactual accounts in institutional domains than in physical ones, or am I overstating the asymmetry? (3) Identify the weakest argumentative move in the notes—whether it is the architecture/intervention distinction, the claim about conceptual impossibility of holding variables fixed, or the defense of asymmetry—and explain what would need to be repaired. Do not hedge across all options; choose the weakest link, say why, and suggest a specific repair or concession. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature, but for a rigorous internal assessment of whether the conceptual machinery holds together.
