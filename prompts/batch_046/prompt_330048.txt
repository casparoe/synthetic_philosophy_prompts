In philosophy of art, the debate over whether an original painting or sculpture holds value that a perfect, undetectable forgery lacks seems to me to have reached an impasse: participants repeatedly invoke causal connection to the artist, the familiar idea of an artistic aura, and the fact that observers react identically, yet no side appears persuaded. I want to suggest a framing I have been developing—call it the distinction between "counterfactual creative dependency," by which an original is tied to the specific contingent path of choices that actually produced it, and "phenomenal identity," by which two objects share every observable property—and ask whether this clarifies the stall. Is the disagreement actually about ontology, about epistemic access, about moral psychology, or is it partly verbal, with one camp asking about the object's history and the other about the viewer's experience under the single word "value"? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement as concretely as you can, indicate what—a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about viewer response, or a reframing of the question—would genuinely advance it, and please distinguish what is well established in aesthetics from what specialists still genuinely dispute, without treating any contested position as the settled view.
