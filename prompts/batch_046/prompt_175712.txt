I have just emerged from a prolonged and somewhat fractious debate with a friend over a practical decision I must resolve by Friday. I have been offered a senior post in Edinburgh, contingent upon my relocating within the month. Before I commit, I have the opportunity—entirely without charge—to consult an independent algorithmic forecast that predicts, with reasonable accuracy, whether a proposed municipal tax on non-resident professionals will pass in the local referendum due next month. Crucially, the forecast is published in real time, and my friend argues that the very act of widespread observation alters the outcome: if the prediction proves unfavourable, a sufficient number of professionals will withdraw from the local housing market in anticipation, thereby depressing prices and triggering the economic conditions that make the tax more likely to be enacted. She therefore contends that I ought actively to prevent myself from viewing the forecast—not merely to avoid personal anxiety, but because my observation contributes to a reflexive loop that harms the collective outcome and, consequently, my own long-term welfare. I counter that, from my individual perspective, the information remains costless and that I retain full discretion over how I act upon it; yet I am uncertain whether this individual-level reasoning collapses once the information environment is interactive in this way. Could you examine the philosophical structure of our disagreement? In particular, I should like to know whether there is a formal principle governing the expected utility of acquiring sample information prior to a choice, whether that principle survives when observation itself modifies the state being predicted, and whether my friend's advocacy of deliberate ignorance—both personal and collective—is defensible. Please provide a formal, precise analysis written in British English.
