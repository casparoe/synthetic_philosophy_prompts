I have been discussing institutional design with colleagues in a reading group on democratic theory, and we recently turned to Jon Elster's analysis of Ulysses contracts—situations in which an agent deliberately restricts her own future options in anticipation of predictable failures of rationality. Several of us began to wonder whether constitutional precommitment, by which a founding generation entrenches legal provisions that bind subsequent democratic majorities, can be illuminatingly understood as a collective or intergenerational analogue of this self-binding strategy. The comparison seems attractive, yet I am concerned that stretching the analogy across generations rather than within a single continuing agent might obscure more than it reveals.

Could you please set out what you take to be the most important implications of treating constitutional precommitment through the lens of Ulysses contracts? I am particularly interested in whether this framing genuinely clarifies the normative authority of entrenched provisions—such as those safeguarding minority rights or imposing fiscal discipline—over present-day citizens, or whether it mistakenly presupposes that a democratic polity is a unified agent with a continuous will across time. Furthermore, I should like you to address whether the analogy survives the objection that a democratic people can always 'untie itself' through formal amendment procedures, and what the persistence or absence of such mechanisms might imply for the legitimacy of precommitment in constitutional design. Please focus on the conceptual and normative implications rather than on historical case studies.
