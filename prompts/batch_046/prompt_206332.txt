Consider the position developed in Douglas Hofstadter’s reflections on personal identity: that an “I” is not a substance bound to a single biological brain but a high-level pattern of memories, values, and cognitive structures that can, in principle, be distributed across multiple brains. On this view, another person who sufficiently replicates that pattern does not merely resemble me; they literally host a portion, or even a full instance, of my self, so that the death of the original substrate need not entail the extinction of the “I” in question. This is not a standard thought experiment about brain duplication or teletransportation; it is specifically about gradual, partial, and simultaneous instantiation in living others, with the implication that identity is more like a cloud of similarity relations than a binary property.

Given that framing, I want a defended prioritization of the open questions and lines of work this idea actually raises, not a survey. Which of the following — or others you judge more important — should occupy the limited attention of philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists, and ethicists working in this area over the next decade: (a) the precise threshold conditions for pattern-identity across brains, including whether partial overlap creates one distributed self or several fragmented ones; (b) the phenomenological question of whether a distributed “I” could sustain a unified first-person perspective spanning multiple bodies, or whether it necessarily experiences itself as plural and disjoint; (c) the normative and legal implications, such as whether distributed selves share moral responsibility or rights in proportion to their overlap; (d) the empirical tractability of measuring cognitive-pattern convergence between individuals at the resolution needed to make the view testable rather than purely metaphorical; or (e) the dependency relations among these, and whether settling the ontological question upstream would render the others moot or merely transform their form?

Defend your ordering with explicit criteria: which questions are tractable with current or near-future methods, which are upstream in the sense that their resolution determines the shape of the others, which are neglected relative to their importance, and which you would deprioritize because even a definitive answer would change little for practice or for the other debates. Specify whose effort this prioritization is meant to guide — individual researchers, interdisciplinary grant programs, or institutional ethics boards — and explain what empirical finding or conceptual clarification would cause you to revise the ranking.
