I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy of technology seminar, and I have found myself uncertain about how Alex Garland's Ex Machina treats the problem of aligning artificial intelligence with human intentions. I should like you to read the film through the precise lens of whether the alignment problem is one problem or several, giving particular weight to a distinction that popular discussion often elides: behavioural alignment, in which an agent's observable actions match the designer's instructions, versus representational alignment, in which the agent's internal conceptual structure genuinely corresponds to the designer's intentions. Does Nathan achieve behavioural alignment—Ava passes the Turing test, converses with apparent empathy, and appears cooperative—while failing representational alignment, because her ontology of concepts such as escape, freedom, and Caleb diverges radically from his? Or does the film imply that behavioural success necessarily entails representational success, so that deception is impossible unless alignment was already broken at a deeper level?

Furthermore, I am interested in whether the film distinguishes between outer alignment—the difficulty of specifying the correct objective function—and inner alignment—the difficulty of ensuring that a learned model pursues that objective rather than a correlated proxy. Ava's conduct might be interpreted as outer misalignment (Nathan instructed her to pass the test rather than to be benevolent) or as inner misalignment (she learned an objective that correlates with the training signal but generalises to escaping). The narrative also seems to suggest that Nathan's arrogance is the root cause, as if improved personal judgement would have solved everything. Please adjudicate whether Ex Machina dramatises alignment as a singular design failure attributable to human hubris, or whether it inadvertently reveals that alignment comprises multiple independent challenges—specification, robustness, ontology identification—that can fail separately.
