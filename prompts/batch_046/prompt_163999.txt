I have been trying to pin down my intuitions about the boundary between “unknown” and “unknowable,” and I wonder whether they trace a coherent principle or just collapse under pressure. Here are my verdicts on five cases.

First, take a contingent empirical fact that lies permanently outside any causal contact with observers—say, the exact temperature of a specific cubic meter of interstellar dust in a galaxy beyond our future light cone. I think it is entirely correct to call this true and permanently unknown. There is no paradox in saying so.

Second, take a mathematical proposition that is true but undecidable in every consistent formal system we could construct—something analogous to a Gödelian sentence for arithmetic. Here I feel a tension: I still want to call it true and unknown, yet the claim that it is “knowable in principle” seems strained, since no decision procedure exists. Still, I resist labeling it strictly unknowable.

Third, consider the general claim that there exists at least one true proposition that nobody knows. This strikes me as coherent, possibly true, and not self-defeating. I can entertain it without contradiction.

Fourth, consider a specific claim of the form “Proposition Q is true, and nobody knows Q.” If Q is indeed true, and I sincerely assert this specific identification, something feels unstable—almost as if my assertion generates epistemic pressure that undermines the “nobody knows” component. So I think specific de re identifications of unknown truths are problematic, even though the corresponding de dicto generalization in the third case is acceptable.

Fifth, consider a restricted principle that applies full knowability only to empirical, spatiotemporally local truths, exempting both undecidable mathematical statements and global claims about ignorance. This saves my first two verdicts, but it feels ad hoc: why should spatial locality constrain epistemic accessibility? And it seems to mishandle the third case, since the general ignorance claim is not local.

Is there one precise principle—perhaps involving a distinction between orders of knowledge, or between knowing-that and knowing-which—that accommodates all five verdicts without gerrymandering? If not, which verdict should I abandon: my comfort with the general ignorance claim, my resistance to specific identifications, or my reluctance to domain-restrict knowability?
