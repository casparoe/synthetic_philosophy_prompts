I've been trying to work out what heritability actually means, and I keep circling back to the same confusion. When I read that a trait is, say, seventy percent heritable, my instinct is to picture one person's body split into a genetic portion and an environmental portion, as if the number describes an individual recipe. But then I encounter the correction: heritability is not about one person, but about the spread of differences within a particular group living in a particular environment. And there's a further twist that I can't shake—if you were to make everyone's environment perfectly equal, heritability would rise, sometimes all the way to one hundred percent, because you've removed environmental variation rather than strengthened genetic influence. That seems to turn my first intuition inside out: the number gets bigger precisely when the world gets fairer, not when genes become more powerful.

Would you write a poem for me—blank verse, roughly twenty lines, with two recurring phrases that keep cutting into each other like arguments that never finish? The first phrase should voice my initial misunderstanding: something about the seed being fixed and the soil being powerless to change the nature within. The second should voice the statistical reality: something about the measure holding only when the ground is uneven, and shifting when the ground is leveled. Let them meet and part without resolution. I'd like it to be precise enough that someone who actually studies genetics and statistics would recognize the real conceptual tension being staged, not a simplified caricature, yet clear enough that the contradiction feels lived rather than technical. I don't need citations or references—just the poem, with its two voices left talking past each other.
