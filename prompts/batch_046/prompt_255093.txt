I have recently become involved with a neighbourhood planning forum in which disagreements about housing density, green-belt adjustments, and community land trusts do not map cleanly onto party labels, yet participants consistently invoke “left” and “right” as if they named real coalitions rather than convenient shorthand. I am drafting a short essay for the forum’s newsletter in which I argue that the distinction is indeed real, but not because either side possesses a shared positive doctrine. Instead, I propose that left and right are held together by what I term “antagonistic co-constitution”: each camp defines its central commitments primarily through opposition to the other, producing an internal coherence that is relational rather than substantive. To make this precise, I borrow Wittgenstein’s notion of family resemblance, but apply it not to the members within each side—where overlap is indeed weak—but to the pattern of mutual negation that links the two sides across domains. Thus, a left position on economic redistribution and a left position on cultural pluralism need not derive from an underlying principle; they cohere because both reject a conservative ontology of “natural hierarchy” that the right, in its mirror-image form, defends through commitments to market freedom and traditional social forms that likewise need not share a single axiom, but rather a shared resistance to egalitarian abstraction. I then ask whether this relational account dissolves the distinction into pure rhetoric, or whether it actually explains its persistence better than standard ideological-cluster or single-axis models do.

Could you comment on whether this line of argument is conceptually coherent, particularly regarding the move of applying family resemblance to a relational structure rather than to a category of items? I would also value your assessment of whether the account overlooks a well-known objection from political philosophy or the sociology of ideology—perhaps one that shows left and right do in fact share hidden substantive commitments that my antagonistic framework misses—and whether there is a particular conceptual tension in claiming that both sides are defined negatively without thereby collapsing them into symmetrical emptiness. I am not seeking references or a reading list, but rather a direct evaluation of the idea’s defensibility and its possible blind spots.
