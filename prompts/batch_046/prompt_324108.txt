I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on the emotional architecture of responsibility, and I'm trying to determine whether my intuitions about guilt and shame form a coherent pattern or whether I need to abandon some of them. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, when someone deliberately deceives a close friend and then privately confesses and seeks to repair the harm without any public exposure, I think guilt is exactly right: it targets the specific act, motivates direct restitution, and preserves the wrongdoer's social standing in a way that allows reconciliation. But when the same deception is revealed by others and the wrongdoer feels primarily humiliated about being seen as untrustworthy—what I think of as a collapse into status shame—the emotion now targets the self's visibility rather than the harm done to the other, and it strikes me as morally worse, not because exposure is never relevant but because the emotional structure has lost its reparative pathway.

Second, in collective contexts I see a sharp distinction. When members of a society acknowledge historical injustice through formal apology and reparative measures, with the emotional burden distributed but no ritual degradation of individuals, that strikes me as institutional guilt: appropriate, even obligatory, and directed at victims rather than at social standing. By contrast, when a culture uses public ritual degradation for minor norm violations—producing what I call distributive shame—the practice seems coercive and morally suspect, even if it regulates behavior effectively. Yet I also recognize cultures where honor shame is cultivated as a virtue: failing to defend one's family honor brings collective disgrace, and this is treated not as pathology but as the backbone of moral life. So a culture can apparently choose shame as its dominant emotional register.

Third, survivor guilt—feeling responsible for living when others died despite having done nothing wrong—strikes me as a misfiring of guilt, but still less damaging than the shame of feeling one's very existence is tainted. And there's a borderline case I'm unsure about: a professional who plagiarizes a minor section of a report, feels deep private remorse, but upon discovery is subjected to permanent professional shunning. The private guilt seems appropriate; the public shunning seems to convert it into destructive shame. But I'm not sure whether the culture that chooses shunning is making a rational moral selection for coordination, or simply institutionalizing cruelty.

What principle, if any, captures all of these verdicts? Is it that guilt is appropriate precisely when the emotional state's internal structure includes a reparable pathway back to moral standing directed at the victim, whereas shame is appropriate only when the self's social identity genuinely requires reconstruction—and that a culture can "choose" only insofar as it can design institutions that preserve that reparability profile rather than collapsing emotional response into visibility-based degradation? Or do I have to split the account, accepting that some cultures rationally prefer shame for coordination reasons even though it lacks the reparative structure? Please state your principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn, predict what it says about a case I haven't listed—say, an anonymous donor who secretly gives away stolen funds, feels no exposure, but experiences persistent remorse that never reaches the victim—and tell me whether the idea that cultures can deliberately choose between these emotions survives or collapses under your account.
