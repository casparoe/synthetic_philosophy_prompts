I'm taking an upper-division seminar on consciousness and causation, and we've spent several weeks on the paradox of phenomenal judgment—the worry that if phenomenal states are causally inert, they can't cause the judgments we make about them. But in our discussions I've kept running into a related pattern that doesn't seem to be the paradox itself, and I'm not sure whether philosophers have given it a specific name.

The pattern shows up like this. Someone grants that on an epiphenomenalist view my verbal report "I am in pain" is driven entirely by neural mechanisms, with no causal contribution from the pain itself. Instead of treating this as pure skeptical pressure, however, they turn it into a justification for an unusually dense and self-contained first-person taxonomy. They argue that because phenomenal properties are decoupled from behavior and physical interaction, introspection is freed from "behavioral noise" and can construct ever-finer distinctions—subtle shades of color-feel, precise structural relations among qualia—without needing to match those distinctions to any causal or functional role. I've seen this move in zombie-argument discussions, where the absence of phenomenal causation is paired with lavish phenomenological description; in introspection debates, where causal isolation is framed as enhancing rather than undermining descriptive precision; and in inverted-spectrum scenarios, where the lack of behavioral divergence is used to motivate richer internal mapping rather than skepticism.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for this specific configuration—where the causal inertness of phenomenal properties is invoked not just to generate skeptical doubts about knowledge, but to license or explain a hyper-refined, behaviorally unconstrained first-person conceptual scheme. How exactly does it sit relative to the standard paradox of phenomenal judgment? Is it a variant of that paradox, something closer to a claim about introspective autonomy, or perhaps a distinct rhetorical failure mode? I don't want only a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is and is not, where its boundaries lie, and which of the nearby ideas—explanatory exclusion in first-person discourse, introspective illusion, or recognitional phenomenal concepts—fit best and where they diverge. Please answer in American English, say how confident you are in your claims, and identify the point about which you are least certain.
