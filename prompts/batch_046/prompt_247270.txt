I've been chewing on supererogation since a weird arguement with a friend who thinks all "above and beyond" talk is just moral marketing, and I want to check if my verdicts actually share a principel or if I'm just pattern-matching. Here are the cases: a stranger donating a kidney is clearly supererogatory, but if your sibling needs one and you flat-out refuse, that feels blameworthy—so donating to a sibling might actually be obligatory rather than supererogatory. At the same time, a firefighter who saves a child from a burning building at enormous personal risk: even though rescue is her job, the extreme danger pushes the act back into supererogatory territory for me. Then there's a billionaire who funds an orphanage purely for tax write-offs—zero moral credit, I think—but the same billionaire anonymously funding a cure for an obscure disease that affects almost nobody, costing him essentially nothing relative to his wealth, still feels genuinely supererogatory. The pair that really bugs me is collective action: ten people each spend one hour stacking sandbags and together save a town from flooding. Individually, nobody sacrificed much, but the group achievement looks heroic—so can there be supererogation at the collective level without any at the individual level? I also think that if you do something heroic mainly to get praise, the praise should be reduced, but the act doesn't stop being supererogatory—though I'm shaky on that. Is there a coherent account behind all this? What does it say about further cases, like refusing to join a collective rescue when your own contribution wouldn't make a difference? Please be explicit about how confident you are in whatever principle you land on, and single out the point you're least sure about—especially if it hinges on a contested view of how moral cost and obligation interact.
