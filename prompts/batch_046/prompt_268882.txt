There's this protocol some startups call radical ownership — not the usual buzzword, but a specific practice where if a project tanks, the lead has to write a public memo breaking down exactly which of their own choices (not budget cuts or market shifts) caused the failure, including things like choosing to feel overwhelmed or choosing not to push back on scope. It's framed as anti-bad-faith: you're not allowed to hide behind circumstances. I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments this is actually carrying. Does it presuppose a Sartrean view of radical freedom, where facticity is always transcended by choice? Or does it secretly assume a fixed essential self that just needs to own its nature? Is it consistent with Sartre's claim that we are condemned to be free, or is it using freedom-language to deny structural determinism in a way that might itself be bad faith? And if you took Sartre seriously, wouldn't you have to admit that the employee's choice to accept the job was made under economic duress, not pure freedom? So what's the implicit theory of responsibility and selfhood here, wether it's coherent, and where does the mapping between corporate accountability and existantial freedom actually break?
