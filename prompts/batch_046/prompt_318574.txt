I just spent the better part of the evening in a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether it is conceptually coherent to hold an entire nation responsible for a war. We kept returning to the Fischer controversy: my friend insists that once the archival evidence establishes deliberate strategic recklessness by the German leadership in 1914, moral blame naturally attaches to Germany as a collective entity. I tried to push back that this slides from causal-historical explanation into a metaphysically dubious form of national guilt, but I could not fully articulate why the slide is illegitimate, especially given recent work on corporate agency. I need to present a sharper version of this position in a seminar next week.

Please take on the role of an expert in the philosophy of collective responsibility, with deep familiarity with modern European intellectual history and with preparing scholars for hostile academic Q&A. Before drafting the prep sheet, spend a moment brainstorming the different species of objection this audience is likely to generate—historical, metaphysical, practical, and rhetorical—so that the ordering reflects actual threat rather than my own anxieties.

My thesis: The Fischer thesis demonstrates that specific bureaucratic actors within the Wilhelmine state pursued a strategy of calculated risk that made general European war probable. However, attributing moral blame to Germany as a nation requires a theory of collective agency that the archival, agent-centered methodology of Fischer’s own work cannot support. The Wilhelmine decision-making structure was not a unitary corporate agent but a fragmented committee of competing institutional factions—War Ministry, Foreign Office, General Staff—whose strategic intentions were not coherently integrated. Therefore, holding Germany responsible is either a category mistake, treating an aggregation of competing bureaucratic roles as a single moral subject, or it requires distributing blame across institutional roles in a way that dissolves the national unit of attribution. I do not deny that individual actors were culpable; I deny that culpability aggregates into national guilt without a coherence condition that 1914 Germany failed to meet.
