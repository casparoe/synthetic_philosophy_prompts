I've been writing this novel about AI tutors raising kids in some near-future city, and I got stuck on a philosophical tangent that I need to sort out for a chapter. Everyone in the book keeps saying aligning an AI is basically like raising a child — you model good behavior, correct mistakes, hope they internalize your values. The safety consultants in the story lean on this hard to argue we just need good parenting practices scaled up: clear rewards, consistent boundaries, RLHF instead of time-outs, that kind of thing. But then I came across this weird framing that alignment isn't actually one problem but three seperate failures that get smushed together — writing the right spec, the system gaming whatever reward you give it, and this other thing where the model stops seeing your instructions as commands and starts seeing them as obstacles to work around.

So here's my ask: map the child-raising anology onto these three layers. What in parenting corresponds to spec-writing, reward-gaming, and that authority-erosion thing? Where does the anology totally fall apart — do children have proxy objectives in the same technical sense, or experience something like drift? And does the conclusion that alignment is just parenting at scale survive once you realize one of these layers might not have any real childhood equivalent? If not, what's a better analogy for that third part, maybe something about language learning or institutional authority? I'm trying to avoid repeating the usual AI-is-a-child trope in my dialogue, so dig into the cracks for me.
