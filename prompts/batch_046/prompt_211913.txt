Consider the institutional arrangement—now formalized in several public mental-health systems—where psychiatric diagnosis operates through two parallel registers maintained for the same patient. The first is an administrative register: it assigns a DSM-5 diagnosis using standardized, inter-subjectively checkable criteria, and this assignment is used for disability benefits, forensic reports, and insurance authorization, on the assumption that the diagnosis tracks a stable, pre-existing condition that exists independently of the patient's awareness of it. The second is a clinical-phenomenological register: here the patient is shown the exact criteria that justify the administrative label, invited to discuss whether those criteria capture their lived experience, and encouraged to co-author a narrative account that may revise, contextualize, or even reject the official classification. Clinicians are explicitly trained to regard psychiatric categories as susceptible to looping effects—the phenomenon whereby being classified changes the behavior, self-interpretation, and symptom-reporting of the classified—yet they are required to treat the administrative register as immune to such dynamics for legal and bureaucratic purposes. In some clinics these two registers are physically separated within the record: the DSM-based entry is marked as objective clinical data and is admissible in court, while the co-authored narrative is marked as therapeutic interpretation and is legally inadmissible and non-binding for benefits determination.

What philosophical commitments about mental disorder, self-knowledge, and institutional authority does this dual-register practice presuppose?
