I am a novelist working on a philosophical novel about a writer who gradually realizes that her artistic commitments have begun to displace every other loyalty in her life. To build the intellectual architecture of a key dialogue, I have constructed an argument that extends Bernard Williams's integrity objection — the claim that moral reasoning can require an agent to have one thought too many — into the domain of artistic creation. The argument feels suspicious to me because it seems to prove far more than Williams intended: not merely that utilitarianism threatens integrity, but that integrity itself eliminates any genuinely other-regarding constraint. Yet I cannot locate the precise misstep, and I need to know which premise is least costly to surrender before I put the reasoning into my character's mouth.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) For Williams, a ground project is constitutive of an agent's identity when abandoning it would destroy the very self that deliberates about abandonment.

(2) The integrity objection holds that utilitarianism demands that agents be prepared to sacrifice any ground project for the sake of aggregate welfare; in doing so, it requires the agent to treat what should be non-negotiable as dispensable — to have one thought too many.

(3) Now consider the novelist whose ground project is artistic truth: she refuses to alter the tragic ending of her protagonist because doing so would violate the internal logic of the narrative, even when she knows that publishing the tragedy will cause measurable psychological harm to vulnerable readers.

(4) If this refusal is genuinely an expression of integrity in Williams's sense, then her commitment to the protagonist's fate is non-negotiable in exactly the way the husband who saves his wife treats that rescue.

(5) But the protagonist lacks any independent metaphysical existence; the novelist's loyalty is directed entirely at a fictional construct that is a product of her own imagination. Therefore what appears to be loyalty to another is in fact loyalty to her own artistic vision — a purely self-constituting preservation of identity.

(6) By parallel reasoning, the husband who saves his wife is not genuinely other-directed either; he is preserving the self constituted by that particular marriage and its associated projects.

(7) Therefore every ground project, including those that appear altruistic, collapses into self-constitution, and Williams's distinction between self-constituting commitments and external moral demands dissolves entirely.

(8) If that distinction dissolves, there is no coherent standpoint from which integrity can object to utilitarianism, since integrity no longer permits any genuinely other-regarding constraint that utilitarian calculation could violate.

This conclusion strikes me as too extreme — I want to preserve the intuition that the novelist (and the husband) can be genuinely committed to something outside themselves — but I am unsure whether the error lies in (5), in the analogical move from (5) to (6), or elsewhere. I need a diagnosis that respects the philosophical structure rather than simply declaring the argument absurd.
