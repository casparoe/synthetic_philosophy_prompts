A recent essay summarizes Darwin's position in The Descent of Man like this. Darwin held that the moral sense in humans arose entirely from social instincts shaped by group selection, which means moral judgments have no objective normative authority and are merely expressions of evolved sentiment. He further argued that religious belief is a direct evolutionary byproduct of hyperactive agency detection, destined to disappear once science explains the unknown. And though he maintained public agnosticism, he privately regarded revealed religion as an actively harmful superstition that obstructs moral progress. The author adds an anachronistic twist, suggesting that Darwin's evolutionary naturalism implies moral judgments about artificial agents are also just adaptive expressions rather than truth-apt claims. From this the essay concludes that Darwin's view makes normative ethics impossible and that his hidden anti-religious stance undermines any attempt to ground morality in tradition. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what accurately reflects Darwin's published arguments and private correspondence from common overstatements, conflations with social Darwinism, or modern projections; and evaluate whether the conclusion about normative ethics—or its extension to artificial agents—actually follows from his evolutionary account.
