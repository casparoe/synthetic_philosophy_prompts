I serve on the governing board of a small worker-owned cooperative with five equal members. We have identified two structural safety issues: a pervasive low-grade environmental exposure that affects all of us, and a severe localized risk that threatens only four members based on their assigned stations. Our budget allows us to remediate only one. The four members facing the severe risk propose that we eliminate it, financing the repair by reducing the fifth member's scheduled hours and corresponding earnings. They argue that the aggregate harm prevented is substantially greater, and they further pledge to redistribute part of their own future earnings to offset his loss, so that the collective welfare is maximized and, in net terms, no one is disadvantaged. The fifth member rejects this framing, insisting that his diminished autonomy and income are not fungible credits in a communal ledger that can be canceled by benefits accruing to others. I sense he is invoking a genuine constraint on how moral reasons aggregate across distinct lives, but I lack the vocabulary to defend or assess his objection. Does the cooperative's reasoning commit a specific error in moral aggregation, and does the proposed compensation alter the underlying ethical structure?
