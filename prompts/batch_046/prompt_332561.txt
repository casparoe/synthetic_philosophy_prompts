I retired from running a marine ecology lab about five years ago and lately I've been poking around in philosophy of mind and decision theory, mostly on my own. I got stuck on Buridan's ass—the classic donkey between two identical hay bales. I think I've landed on a view, but I want to stress-test it.

My position is basically this: the paradox only looks like a problem about rational choice, but it's actually a problem about physical symmetry breaking. If the ass's sensory and motor system is deterministic, and the two bales are genuinely identical in every relevant parameter—including spatial position relative to the ass's body axis—then there is literally no causal pathway that can initiate movement toward one rather than the other. The ass doesn't choose to starve; its hardware recieve zero differential input, so no motor command gets triggered. In other words, choice without preference isn't just hard, it's physically impossible for any deterministic agent, because every real decision mechanism has some hidden asymmetry—maybe just the order neurons fire, or a thermal fluctuation, or even the left-right wiring of the brain. The preference is always there, just buried in the implementation.

What would actually count against this? I'm not looking for the usual just-flip-a-coin answer—I'm asking what evidence or argument would show that a system can genuinely break symmetry without relying on some hidden weighting, whether that's in neuroscience, formal decision theory, or even physics. Would it take a demonstrated case of spontaneous symmetry breaking in a macroscopic biological system with no hidden variable? Or is my view already unfalsifiable because I can always claim there's a hidden asymmetry I haven't measured yet? And if it is unfalsifiable, is that a real problem for a claim about physical decision mechanisms, or just a feature? I'd especially like to know which objections a defender of this view would concede as genuinely damaging versus which ones they'd brush off as missing the point. Rank them if you can, from fatal to merely awkward. I was discussing this with my neighbor—he's an engineer and thinks I'm overfitting philosophy onto biology—but maybe he's right.
