Write a poem of approximately 20 to 28 lines, divided into two alternating voices: a subject reporting an experience of a sharp, unified visual field with a clear boundary between self and world, and a heterophenomenologist who records that report. Use a single refrain — a phrase describing the boundary — that appears in both voices, but shift its grammatical mood: in the subject's lines it should be declarative and ontologically committed, and in the scientist's lines it should appear bracketed or embedded in a descriptive clause that suspends its reality. The subject's stanzas should follow a strict rhyme scheme (ABAB CDCD), while the scientist's stanzas should be in loose, unrhymed free verse, so that the formal contrast mirrors the methodological contrast between naive report and interpretive reconstruction. The poem should render Dennett's heterophenomenological method with enough precision that a philosopher of mind would recognize it — specifically, the distinction between the heterophenomenological world (the world as the subject takes it to be experienced) and the absence of direct access to any inner reality — and should inject the somewhat niche point that confabulation and phenomenological illusion are not errors to be filtered out but constitutive data for the text being reconstructed. Do not resolve the tension at the end: the closing lines should leave the reader uncertain whether the scientist's bracketing is epistemic humility or a covert denial of the experience's reality. Avoid bibliographic references, page numbers, or requests for citations; the philosophical content should stand on its own.
