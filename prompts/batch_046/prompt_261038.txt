Having spent my career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have lately been working through evolutionary game theory and the philosophy of cooperation, and I find myself holding a cluster of judgments about when cooperation is rational or evolutionarily stable that I cannot fully reconcile. I should like a precisely stated principle, or a small family of candidates, that either unifies these verdicts or tells me which ones must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts, stated concretely.

First, in a strictly one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between strangers with no causal or institutional link to any future interaction, mutual defection is the only rational outcome; cooperation is a mistake.

Second, in an iterated game with a commonly known, fixed number of rounds, backward induction applies: rational agents defect in the final round, hence in every preceding round, so cooperation never gets started.

Third, in an iterated game with an indefinite horizon—where the continuation probability is common knowledge and sufficiently high—conditional strategies such as tit-for-tat can be rational, and cooperation is sustained by the shadow of the future.

Fourth, in an evolutionary setting with clonal reproduction in a well-mixed population, altruistic cooperation is evolutionarily stable because the beneficiaries are genetic copies; strategic reasoning is unnecessary.

Fifth, in an evolutionary setting with non-clonal agents arranged on a spatial lattice with local interaction only, cooperation can spread even without genetic relatedness, but only if the neighborhood structure is stable and the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds the lattice connectivity.

Sixth, introducing a costly punishment option into the well-mixed evolutionary setting does not rescue cooperation, because the cost of punishing renders punishers vulnerable to second-order free-riding, and the equilibrium collapses back to universal defection.

Seventh, consider a one-shot interaction in which my counterpart's decision rule is fully transparent to me—I can inspect that she runs a deterministic program that cooperates if and only if I cooperate—yet there is no causal pathway by which my present choice affects her payoff or any future encounter. Here I feel cooperation is rational, because the correlation is perfect and I prefer mutual cooperation to mutual defection.

Eighth, consider a one-shot interaction that is statistically identical to the seventh—the counterpart's behavior is drawn from a distribution that yields cooperation exactly when I cooperate—but the underlying mechanism is opaque; I observe only the correlation ex post. Here I revert to the first verdict: defection is rational, and cooperation is unjustified.

Ninth, in an iterated game with implementation noise—where intended actions are misexecuted with small but common-knowledge probability—I think strict tit-for-tat is no longer optimal; more forgiving strategies such as win-stay-lose-shift become rational, and the sustaining principle shifts from exact reciprocity to error-corrected reciprocity.

These nine judgments generate tensions that trouble me. The seventh and eighth cases are observationally equivalent in outcome distribution, yet I assign opposite rational verdicts, which suggests that my principle is not purely consequentialist. The sixth case contradicts the common institutional intuition that punishment can stabilize cooperation, yet I insist that well-mixed punishment fails. The second and third cases depend sharply on whether the endpoint is known, which feels like a discontinuity rather than a graded response.

My questions are as follows. Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps framed in terms of credible causal linkage, epistemic access to the counterpart's decision structure, and evolutionary payoff geometry—that captures all nine verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn: the distinction between transparent and opaque mechanisms in one-shot settings, the failure of punishment in well-mixed populations, or the sharp discontinuity between known and unknown endpoints? Furthermore, apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: an iterated interaction with an unknown endpoint, but where each round is separated by a random time delay that is common knowledge, and where monitoring is imperfect so that I observe my counterpart's intended action only with probability 0.8. What does your principle predict—cooperation, defection, or a conditional strategy—and why?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and present your prediction for the unlisted case with explicit reasoning.
