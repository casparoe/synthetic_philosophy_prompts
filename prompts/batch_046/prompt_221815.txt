Analyze the June 2020 toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol and the subsequent debate over renaming Colston Hall as a case study in commemorative ethics, but do not treat it as a generic dispute over whether slave-traders ought to be honored. Instead, apply the following paired framework. First, the visibility asymmetry thesis: commemorative objects in public space do not merely express approval of a historical figure but structurally allocate historical attention, rendering certain narratives hyper-visible while suppressing others, so that the ethical question is not only who is praised but whose suffering is rendered perceptually absent. Second, diachronic moral authority: a monument’s legitimacy depends not on the intentions of its original donors but on an ongoing, revocable consent by the affected community, where consent can be withdrawn when the monument’s presence systematically distorts that community’s shared historical self-understanding. Map the episode with discipline onto these concepts: identify the precise affected community with standing to revoke consent; determine what the physical removal of the statue and the renaming of the hall respectively do to the visibility asymmetry—whether they correct it, displace it onto an absence, or reproduce it elsewhere; and examine how the statue’s subsequent recovery, its museum display, and the economic legacy of Colston’s wealth funding civic institutions interact with the idea of revocable authority. Then provide the residue report: which features of the episode—the divergence between Bristol residents and national political discourse, the distinction between symbolic and material redress, or the problem of commemorating absence without creating a new monument—does the framework illuminate, which does it misdescribe or leave silent, and whether the episode ultimately supports the claim that commemorative ethics should center visibility and diachronic consent, or reveals that these concepts miss something essential about how public memory actually operates.
