I serve as the institutional continuity referee for a regional neuro-rehabilitation consortium that performs staged cortical-substitution therapies—synthetic neural arrays, cross-hemispheric grafts, and limited full encephalic transfers—for patients with progressive neurodegenerative conditions. Every quarter my office must certify, for consent validity, clinical liability, and property-rights continuity, whether a given post-operative entity counts as the same person who entered the protocol, and the board has adopted, in broad outline, the conceptual framework behind Dennett’s brain-transplant puzzle: personal identity is anchored not in continuous biological tissue but in a functional-narrative center that can in principle migrate with its supporting substrate. What we lack is a reusable operational method; I need a decision procedure my two-person team can execute in roughly ninety minutes using only a standard neurological report, a transcript of a structured narrative interview, and a social-recognition questionnaire, with explicit pass-fail thresholds at each stage, clear termination rules, and marked points where human judgment must override the audit because values rather than facts are at stake.

The procedure should be structured as an ordered audit built around three checks—first, causal-dependency of present mental states on pre-operative brain architecture; second, coherence and first-person ownership of autobiographical narrative; and third, stability of recognized social relationships—specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, what partial or contradictory results trigger, and where an earlier failure stops the audit entirely. It must preserve the ambiguity that Dennett’s intuition pumps expose, avoiding collapse into either pure psychological-continuity theory or pure bodily-continuity theory, and it must explicitly flag split-successor scenarios and substrate-replacement cases where the framework underdetermines the answer. Then apply the audit step-by-step to three proposals on my desk: case A, a patient whose entire cerebrum was replaced by a synthetic array that replicates his neural dynamics but retains no original tissue, where I anticipate the audit will recommend discontinuity and I want to see whether that result can be defended without invoking an immaterial soul; case B, a split-hemisphere intervention in which the left hemisphere was fully replaced but the right retains pre-operative memory traces and the patient reports a unified first-person perspective; and case C, a full encephalic transfer into a biologically identical clone body, with the original body maintained in vegetative state by an artificial brainstem implant. For each case, show where the audit stops with a definitive assignment, where it must hand the decision to a person, and include at least one—preferably case A—where the procedure delivers a verdict I am likely to reject on intuitive grounds.

Finally, conclude by stating precisely what categories of identity dispute lie outside the procedure’s reach, particularly normative judgments about which form of continuity ought to be treated as authoritative, and note any practical constraints—time pressure, limited neurological expertise on my team, or the need for a signed certification within forty-eight hours—that should shape how the audit is documented and appealed. I was discussing this with a colleague who suggested simply tracking the brain wherever it goes, but that strikes me as too quick; I want the procedure to show exactly why such a rule fails in the hard cases rather than merely asserting it.
