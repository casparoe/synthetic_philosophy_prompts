I am preparing an interview for a philosophy podcast that examines the intersection of economic forecasting and ethical theory. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a particular reading of John Maynard Keynes's conception of the good life—specifically, the view that Keynes's well-known essay on future economic abundance should be interpreted not merely as macroeconomic speculation but as an argument rooted in G. E. Moore's ethics. On her account, the ultimate purpose of reducing labor is to make space for intrinsically valuable states of consciousness—friendship, aesthetic appreciation, and unpressured intellectual engagement—rather than for the maximization of preference satisfaction. The guest further maintains that Keynes underestimated the institutional and social scaffolding required to sustain these goods, and she therefore proposes what she calls the structured-leisure problem: the possibility that abundance, by dissolving the shared temporal rhythms and cooperative practices embedded in economic labor, may actually erode rather than enable the very conditions for the good life she takes Keynes to have valued.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this position. I want at least one question on the tension between Keynes's technological optimism—his expectation that rising productivity would eventually reduce the working week to roughly fifteen hours—and her claim that labor structures the social relationships necessary for these Moorean goods; include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer, namely that cooperative work is not the only source of shared rhythm. I want at least one question that presses her on how this interpretation distinguishes Keynes both from utilitarian welfare economics, which treats leisure as an instrument for aggregate preference satisfaction, and from Aristotelian accounts that treat productive activity as itself constitutive of human flourishing; the follow-up should anticipate her likely appeal to Moore's distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value. I want at least one question focused specifically on the structured-leisure problem: ask her to address the objection that if abundance really undermines the conditions for friendship and aesthetic experience, then Keynes's vision collapses into a paradox in which achieving the economic conditions for the good life destroys the social conditions for it, and include a follow-up that assumes she responds by distinguishing voluntary association from economic cooperation. Finally, include at least one question that anticipates the standard scholarly objection that Keynes's essay was partly ironic or rhetorical rather than a serious ethical program, with a follow-up that addresses her likely reply that even rhetorical texts embed normative commitments about human ends.

All questions should be phrased for a colloquium-style conversation rather than a general-audience chat. Avoid generic biographical or career-oriented questions. Where appropriate, frame the questions so that they turn on concrete distinctions rather than broad agreement or disagreement, and press precisely where the existing literature on Keynes, Moore, and the ethics of labor tends to press.
