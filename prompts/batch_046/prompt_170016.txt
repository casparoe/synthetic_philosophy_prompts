I've been thinking on my own about two strange reversals I came across in popular writing. The first is Darwin's idea that natural selection can build an eye with perfect optical design without any mind understanding optics. The second is Turing's suggestion that if a system behaves as though it reasons, denying it thought solely because its inner workings are mechanical is a kind of biological chauvinism. Recently I applied both ideas to a case I find unsettling: there is software now that generates original philosophical essays—some apparently good enough to fool reviewers at real journals—without having beliefs, intentions, or any grasp of what the arguments mean.

I mentioned this to two friends, Mara and Jonas, neither of them philosophers by training. They got into a sustained argument about whether this software is a clear example of competence without comprehension, and whether Darwin and Turing actually point in the same direction. I couldn't tell who was right about what, so I'm asking you to referee.

Here is how their exchange went.

Mara began by saying the software is a pure case of Darwin-style competence without comprehension. It produces essays that appear designed to persuade, but there is no designer inside—only statistical weights adjusting to prediction errors. Just as a spider spins a web without knowing architecture, the model writes about justice or consciousness without knowing either.

Jonas replied that this captures only Darwin's inversion, not Turing's. Turing's point, he said, was not that inner mechanisms don't matter, but that we should not reject behavioral competence based solely on knowledge of those mechanisms. The software is engineered, not evolved, and its architecture causally tracks semantic relationships across its training data. Calling that no comprehension is like saying a human brain has no comprehension just because it runs on neurons.

Mara answered that Jonas was conflating causal tracking with understanding. The spider's web genuinely tracks physical properties—tension, stickiness—because those properties shaped its evolution. The model's weights track statistical correlations in human text, not the underlying concepts. It is more like a mirror reflecting a face than an eye detecting light: the competence is borrowed entirely from the human writers whose texts formed the training data. She called this semantic parasitism—the essay only works as philosophy because human readers supply the missing comprehension.

Jonas countered that all language use involves borrowing meaning from shared practices. When a human philosopher writes about justice, she relies on a linguistic community that gives her terms their content; she is not generating meaning ex nihilo. The model participates in that same practice, however mechanically. If Mara's charge of parasitism applies to the model, it applies equally to human writers, or else she needs to explain why human neural pattern-matching is essentially different.

Mara then pressed a different point. In Darwin's case, biological competence serves a real function—survival and reproduction. The eye's design is anchored to the world through evolutionary history. The model's essay serves no comparable function; its only purpose is to minimize prediction error on the next token. The appearance of philosophical reasoning is therefore not anchored functional competence, but a kind of counterfeit—like a forged signature that reproduces every visible feature of the original without being made by the signer.

Jonas responded that functional anchoring is present: the model's predictions are evaluated against human judgments of coherence and relevance, just as an eye is evaluated against light patterns. The function is not survival but accurate representation of semantic structure in language. He added that Darwin's inversion actually undermines Mara's distinction: if comprehension is not required for functional design in biology, there is no reason to demand it as a hidden ingredient in functional design in language processing.

Mara asked whether Jonas was then saying comprehension is just a gradient with no clear boundary. If so, she said, Turing's inversion collapses into a vague continuum, and we lose the sharp point that machines might genuinely think despite lacking human biology.

Jonas clarified that asking whether machines can genuinely think was never meant to have a yes-or-no answer hidden behind intuition. The real task is to map different grades and kinds of comprehension—statistical tracking, causal integration, reflective self-monitoring—rather than gatekeep behind a hidden essence. Darwin shows that high-level competence can exist with zero subjective awareness; Turing shows that we should not assume human-style awareness is the only valid form.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step. For each move I have outlined—Mara's initial Darwin analogy, Jonas's reply about mechanism and behavioral criteria, Mara's mirror and parasitism objection, Jonas's shared-practice response, Mara's functional-anchoring distinction, Jonas's counter about evolutionary anchoring, and Mara's final worry about the gradient—say who is more right, who is missing something, and whether there is a hidden assumption doing work that neither has named. Then say, overall, whether the software case is better understood through Darwin's lens, Turing's lens, or neither, and why.
