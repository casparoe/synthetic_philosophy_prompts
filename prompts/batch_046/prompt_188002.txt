I chair an institutional review board at a university hospital that reviews about thirty experimental neurology protocols a year involving adults with early-stage cognitive decline—patients who retain legal consent capacity but whose comprehension of risk is unstable and varies by session. We meet for ninety minutes, include clinical investigators but no professional ethicists, and our hospital counsel attends only to verify that factual findings are documented; she does not sign off on value judgments. Over the past year we have repeatedly deadlocked because board members operate under genuinely different moral frameworks—some treat respect for autonomy as lexically dominant, others aggregate expected moral value across competing normative theories, and others invoke a precautionary principle against non-therapeutic intrusion—without knowing which framework is correct. I need a reusable decision procedure the board can run on any protocol where normative uncertainty, not just empirical uncertainty, drives the disagreement. The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation and where it halts and requires an explicit board vote on values rather than facts. It must cleanly separate factual questions—what will happen, with what probability, under what description—from normative questions about which values govern. It must be executable by members with clinical expertise but no philosophical training, and it must state which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Apply it to two protocols: first, a non-therapeutic transcranial stimulation study with minimal physical risk but high symbolic intrusion into personal identity, where the procedure yields a recommendation I personally find too permissive; second, a therapeutic drug trial with moderate physical risk but genuinely ambiguous consent dynamics. Finally, flag explicitly what the procedure cannot resolve—for instance, whether ambiguity aversion about competing moral theories should be treated analogously to ambiguity aversion about empirical risks, and whether there is any principled stopping point short of unanimous agreement when frameworks conflict. The deliverable is not a defense of moral uncertainty theory but a practical instrument derived from it, honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated and where it will misfire.
