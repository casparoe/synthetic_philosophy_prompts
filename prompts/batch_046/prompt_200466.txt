I've been thinking about that idea linked to the french poet Marot—Le Ton beau de Marot, or whatever—about how translating poetry with tight rhyme and meter isn't just swapping words. People usually say that if you have to change the vocabulary to keep the rhyme scheme, you're loosing the original meaning. But I want to argue the opposite: that the formal constraint is actually part of the meaning, not just a container for it.

Specifically, I think we should use something I call "prosodic anchoring." Instead of anchoring a translation to the dictionary definitions of each word, you anchor it to the stress peaks and rhythmic architecture of the original line, and let the actual vocabulary drift wherever it needs to go. So the meaning that survives isn't the literal propositions but the sequence of mental emphasis—the cognitive shape the poem forces on you. If Marot uses a rhyme pair as a kind of logical punchline, a translator should preserve that punchline structure even if the specific concepts have to change completely.

Please make the argument that in formal-constraint translation, prosodic anchoring is more faithful to the original than a literal prose rendering that keeps all the words but destroys the architecture. Address the obvious objection that this is just writing a new poem and calling it a translation. I'm not asking for reading lists or citations, just work out the reasoning. Also, do you think this applies only to poetry, or could it work for something like translating a very rhythmic political speech? I find that part confusing.
