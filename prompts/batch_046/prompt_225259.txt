Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find the post-Quine literature on the analytic–synthetic distinction curiously static. Quine’s 1951 essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism is treated by many as having dissolved the boundary entirely, yet a persistent minority insists that some form of the distinction survives—though they rarely agree on which form. The debate has persisted for decades without obvious convergence: one camp treats analyticity as a relic of logical empiricism, the other treats it as an unavoidable feature of how theoretical frameworks constrain evidence. My own suspicion, shaped by laboratory practice, is that the disagreement is not about language at all but about whether scientific theories contain what I would call load-bearing joints—structural commitments that are not empirical hypotheses but that determine how evidence can even be articulated, analogous to the way unit conventions or coordinate choices function in physics. If that analogy holds, the two sides may be answering different questions: one about the semantics of individual sentences, the other about the architecture of belief revision. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a genuine, hidden disagreement about the dynamics of confirmation? I want a precise diagnosis—verbal, substantive, or a case of parallel monologues—supported by what each camp actually claims about cases such as logical truths, mathematical axioms embedded in physical theory, or definitional expansions of observational terms. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything—an experiment, a formal model of belief revision, a conceptual distinction—could actually move the dispute, or whether the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned for a different framing altogether.
