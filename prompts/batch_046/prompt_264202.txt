Compare Dennett's two-bitser argument—that a system composed of parts none of which possess original intentionality can nonetheless exhibit genuine derived intentionality—with Searle's insistence that original intentionality is ontologically primitive and that derived intentionality is therefore causally and semantically parasitic. Rather than rehearsing the standard Chinese Room exchange, examine whether the distinction between original and derived intentionality can survive once we concede that all intentional ascription, including self-ascription, involves an interpretive stance. If original intentionality itself depends on an interpretive framework, does Dennett's reduction of it to a useful fiction collapse the very gap Searle needs, or can Searle identify a non-circular, intrinsic feature of mental states that grounds original intentionality without appealing to derived practices? Assess, further, whether Dennett's position risks rendering intentionality so ubiquitous—present in any system that rewards predictive interpretation—that it loses explanatory power. I want a precise comparison of their metaphysical commitments, with particular attention to whether Searle's defense can avoid circularity and whether Dennett's view is genuinely eliminativist or merely revisionary about the ontology of mind.
