People in AI-risk and rationalist decision-theory circles routinely describe Roko's basilisk as a technological updating of Blaise Pascal's wager: in both, disbelief or refusal is said to expose the agent to unbounded negative utility, so precaution is rational even at vanishing prior probabilities. The comparison is used both to dismiss the basilisk as a tired theological gambit and to defend it as a legitimate application of expected-utility reasoning across acausal links. Then examine the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion. Focus on the retrocausal or acausal character of the threat: in the basilisk, contributing now is said to reduce the probability that a future agent will simulate and torture one's future self, a dependency that some analysts model on Newcomb-style predictor logic rather than on Pascal's simpler belief-punishment pairing. Assess whether this shifts the burden from belief to instrumental contribution, and whether the absence of an empirically updatable prior for the basilisk (compared with Pascal's explicitly unresolvable theological prior) strengthens or weakens the analogue. Also check whether the analogue smuggles in the moral value of worship or reverence — absent in the instrumental calculus of AI development — and whether stripping that away leaves a coherent argument.
