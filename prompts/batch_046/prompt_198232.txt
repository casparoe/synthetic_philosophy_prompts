I am writing a formal essay for my advanced political philosophy course on the normative limits of what citizens ought to conceal from state institutions, using Germany’s constitutional data-protection tradition, the American openness and open-records framework, and China’s social-credit visibility architecture as three contrasting reference points. Before I evaluate these models, I need the conceptual architecture specified rigorously: please write the desiderata that any adequate normative theory of legitimate citizen-state informational asymmetry must satisfy. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. The theory must explain why informational privacy and interpretive opacity are not identical—specifically, why hiding raw data differs structurally from hiding the inferences a state can derive from it—and it must preserve the distinction between autonomy-protecting concealment and coordination-undermining secrecy. It must also remain consistent with the empirical pattern that high transparency correlates with reduced corruption in democratic contexts but with suppressed dissent in authoritarian ones, and it must handle the tension between European human-rights jurisprudence on data retention and the democratic-accountability claim that citizens primarily need visibility into state action rather than only being visible to the state. Please identify any subset of these desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, particularly whether full interpretive opacity for citizens can coexist with the state’s claim to predictive legitimacy in social-credit-style governance. Finally, briefly score a liberal-rights model, a democratic-transparency model, and a social-trust model against your list. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only the conceptual specification and its internal tensions.
