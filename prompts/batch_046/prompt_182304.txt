Essay question (90 minutes, approx 1000-1200 words):

Scanlon holds that an act is wrong if it violates a principle that no affected person could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. His "individualist restriction" compares principles by asking what can be justified to each person, not by summing welfare across people.

Consider this modified case. A factory emits noise that gives each of 100 local residents a mild but persistent headache — a harm that, taken alone, falls just below the threshold any one person could reasonably reject a principle permitting it. If the residents could coordinate, however, they would jointly reject any principle allowing the factory to run, since together they bear a massive distributed burden. Shutting the factory prevents all 100 headaches but kills one worker, the only person who knows how to disable the dangerous machinery.

First, explain Scanlon's individualist restriction and why he thinks it blocks standard additive aggregation. Second, apply it to the case: can the 100 residents together reject the principle that saves the worker, even though none can do so alone? Distinguish carefully between pairwise justification (each resident compared to the worker) and group-level justification (the residents as a collective compared to the worker), and explain why blurring that distinction undermines Scanlon's view. Third, evaluate whether "threshold contractualism" — where complaints do not accumulate like utility units but can trigger a collective veto once a severity line is crossed for the group — saves contractualism or collapses it into rule-utilitarianism. Defend your interpretation.

Model answer (approx 800 words):

Scanlon's contractualism defines wrongness in terms of justifiability to others. A principle is rejectable if an affected individual has a reason to reject it that is not outweighed by the reasons others have to accept it. Crucially, Scanlon imposes an individualist restriction: we assess principles by comparing what they mean for each person, not by aggregating benefits and harms across persons. This is why he rejects the idea that five small headaches can be added up to outweigh one death. Numbers matter only indirectly — for example, saving five lives rather than one gives each of the five a reason to reject a principle that saves only one — but there is no additive calculus of welfare.

In the factory case, the individualist restriction initially seems to favor the worker. Each resident's headache is too minor to ground a reasonable rejection of a principle that permits the factory to operate and thereby saves a life. Pairwise, each resident is asking the worker to endure death for the sake of avoiding a mild headache; that rejection is clearly unreasonable. So if we stick strictly to pairwise comparisons, the factory should stay open.

The difficulty arises at the group level. The residents, taken together, suffer a serious collective burden. One might think they can jointly reject the principle because the pattern of harm is massive, even if each slice is small. But Scanlon is skeptical of group-level complaints that are not reducible to individual ones. Contractualism is addressed to each person as an individual; there is no "group person" that holds rights over and above its members. If we allow the residents to pool their complaints into a collective veto, we risk treating the group like a single utility-bearer whose total suffering can be weighed against the worker's death. That looks like rule-utilitarianism in disguise: we are aggregating harms, just with a non-linear threshold rather than a linear sum.

A "threshold contractualist" might reply that the aggregation is not of welfare but of rejectability. On this view, a principle is rejectable by a group not because their headaches sum to a large utility loss, but because the principle permits a structural pattern that each member can recognize as unfair when viewed from the group's perspective. The veto is collective but not additive: it activates only when the distributed harm crosses a qualitative line that makes the principle intolerable to those affected together.

Whether this saves Scanlon's view depends on whether the collective veto can be explained without appealing to summed welfare. If the residents reject the principle because they can see that they are all being subjected to a systematic imposition for the sake of one life, and if that recognition is available to each of them as a reason they share, then the rejection might still be individualist in spirit — each person rejects being part of a pattern that treats them as expendable for minor gains. But if the veto works only by treating the 100 headaches as collectively equivalent to a death, then the distinction from rule-utilitarianism collapses. The threshold view then becomes a disguised aggregation rule with a cutoff.

The specific mistake to avoid is conflating pairwise with group-level justification. A student might argue that since each resident cannot reject the principle alone, the group cannot reject it either — but that assumes that group-level reasons must be decomposable into pairwise ones, which is precisely what the threshold view denies. Conversely, one might slide from "the group suffers a lot" to "therefore the principle is rejectable" without explaining how that collective reason is available to each individual without additive weighing. Scanlon's view demands that every rejection be justifiable to each affected party; a collective veto that cannot be cashed out in those terms violates the individualist restriction.

My view is that Scanlon should resist the threshold modification. Once we allow collective rejection based on distributed patterns that no individual can reject alone, we lose the clear boundary between contractualism and aggregation. The factory case should be resolved in favor of the worker, not because the residents' suffering does not matter, but because contractualism does not provide a mechanism for turning many small, individually tolerable harms into one large, rejectable harm. The cost is that contractualism may seem cold in cases of widespread minor injustice, but that is the price of refusing to sum people up.

Marking scheme (20 marks total):

1. Accurate exposition of Scanlon's contractualism and the individualist restriction, with correct explanation of why additive aggregation is blocked. (4 marks) Typical miss: describing contractualism as a social contract about consent or confusing it with Rawlsian justice.

2. Clear distinction between pairwise justification (each resident vs worker) and group-level justification (residents collectively vs worker), with explanation of why the distinction matters for Scanlon. (5 marks) Typical miss: conflating the two levels — either assuming group rejection automatically follows from summed individual complaints, or denying group-level reasons can exist at all without argument; this specific confusion should cost 3 marks.

3. Application to the factory case, showing how the individualist restriction handles (or fails to handle) the collective headache burden. (5 marks) Typical miss: applying a utilitarian calculus directly without noticing that Scanlon rejects welfare aggregation.

4. Evaluation of "threshold contractualism" and whether it collapses into rule-utilitarianism, with a defended position. (4 marks) Typical miss: asserting that any group consideration is utilitarian without analyzing whether the veto is structural rather than summed.

5. Overall coherence, structure, and explicit defense of an interpretation rather than a summary of both sides. (2 marks) Typical miss: ending with "it depends" or listing positions without taking a stand.
