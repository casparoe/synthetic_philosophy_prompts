Question: Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction is often taken to threaten scientific realism, especially the version that says we should believe in the unobservable entities postulated by our best theories. Explain the pessimistic meta-induction, then discuss whether the realist's 'no miracles' reply survives if we shift to structural realism (Worrall) rather than standard entity realism. Is the induction then blocked, or can it be reformulated at the level of structures?

Student answer:
Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction points out that many past scientific theories that were empirically successful turned out false, like the caloric theory of heat or the ether theory. From this he infers that our current successful theories are probably false too, which undermines the realist claim that success tracks truth.

Realists respond with the no-miracles argument: if our theories weren't approximately true, there predicitve success would be a miracle. But this reply assumes that success requires truth about entities.

Worrall's structural realism offers a different defense. He says we should be realists about the mathematical structures of theories, not about the entities. The structures survive theory change—Maxwell's equations kept there form even when the ether was dropped—so we can explain success without committing to entities that get abandoned.

But there's still the divide et impera strategy, which Worrall uses to split a theory into its true structural core and its false ontological parts. Once we do that, Laudan's induction is blocked because the false parts are exactly the ones that change, while the true parts remain.

This means structural realism doesn't just save the no-miracles argument for structures; it actually rehabilitates entity realism too, because if we know which parts are true, we can read the entities back in. So the pessimistic meta-induction fails altogether.
