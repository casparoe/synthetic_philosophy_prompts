I was discussing with a colleague in economic history whether path dependence ought to be understood as a gradient or as a threshold phenomenon, and I have settled—provisionally—on a threshold view with a particular twist: I now hold that in complex socio-technical systems there exist genuine “irreversibility thresholds” which, once crossed, do not merely render reversal costly but alter the ontology of the available choices, so that the putatively “superior alternative” can no longer be evaluated within the same framework that sustained the incumbent. I have in mind not only familiar technological lock-ins, but also the embedding of particular risk-assessment protocols in regulatory bodies or the persistence of specific measurement conventions in scientific practice, where the standard has become so interwoven with complementary practices, credential structures, and even the vocabulary of evaluation that abandoning it requires not merely adopting a new tool but a partial reconstruction of the institutional world that deploys it. I do not deny that history sometimes jumps tracks—catastrophic disruptions can do it—but I claim that within functioning, non-catastrophic conditions, the lock-in is effectively absolute.

Given this position, I want a precise map of its exposure. What observations, formal results from evolutionary game theory or institutional economics, or conceptual distinctions would genuinely count against the claim that such irreversibility thresholds exist? I am not asking for the familiar reminder that QWERTY eventually faced challengers, but for deeper defeaters: perhaps documented cases of smooth, non-catastrophic transition away from deeply embedded standards despite intact network effects, or philosophical arguments that the “framework-dependence” I describe is an illusion generated by treating contingent norms as constitutive. Please rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed defender of my view could legitimately absorb—say, by arguing that apparent reversals were in fact disguised collapses of the supporting ecosystem rather than genuine internal transitions.
