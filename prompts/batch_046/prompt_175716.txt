In literary studies, academic interpretation is governed by a strong norm against the “intentional fallacy”: students learn to justify readings through textual structures rather than appeals to what an author meant, and biographical evidence is typically excluded from analytical essays. By contrast, in copyright law courts regularly invoke authorial intent to distinguish infringement from transformative fair use, and in MFA creative-writing workshops feedback is organized almost entirely around clarifying the writer’s aims—what did you intend this scene to do?—with the author’s own explanation treated as authoritative. These three practices—scholarly exegesis, legal adjudication, and artistic pedagogy—operate within the same disciplinary ecosystem yet appear to presuppose incompatible theories of textual meaning.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of authorial intention that allows them to coexist: does the practice treat intent as causally explanatory but semantically irrelevant, or does it presuppose that “meaning” is context-relative, functioning descriptively in criticism but normatively in law and production? Distinguish what this institutional arrangement strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify which rival semantic theories—say, a strict anti-intentionalism or a robust authorialism—would be ruled out by one or more of these practices. Finally, imagine a university that adopted a “semantic autonomy” policy extending the critical norm into workshops (banning intent-talk in feedback) and into copyright reasoning (replacing intent-based tests with purely structural similarity metrics). Which commitments of the current practice would that expose as inessential, and which inconsistencies would it reveal?
