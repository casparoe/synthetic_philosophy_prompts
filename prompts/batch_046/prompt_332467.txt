So I've been dealing with some personal stuff lately—my dad's health went downhill fast, then a breakup, the usual mess—and it's made me rethink the whole "meaning of life" thing in a way that feels more lived-in than philosophy-class abstract. I've come around to this view that the absurd isn't something you solve; it's more like bad weather you build in. Meaning isn't an achievement state or a checklist of objective goods (you know, the usual list theories), but a kind of maintainence work: you keep small commitments alive precisely because you know they don't matter cosmically. I also think we misapply hyperbolic discounting—people chase "legacy" meaning because they overvalue distant future valuation, but real meaning might live in micro-horizons, like this afternoon's choice to show up for someone. So the position is: meaning is resistance-based maintainence against cosmic independance, evaluated in short time-scales, not accumulated worth.

But I'm not sure if this is actually a view or just a mood. What would count against it—seriously? I want the defeaters ranked: which psychological results about human motivation, which intuitions about heroic sacrifice or legacy-building, which arguments from value theory about whether meaning requires objective grounding, would be fatal versus just awkward? Would a committed defender of this "maintenance model" concede that if people genuinely can't sustain motivation without some illusion of cosmic importance, that's a real problem? Or can the view absorb everything—"oh that's just more maintainence"? I need an honest read on whether this risks being unfalsifiable, and whether being unfalsifiable is actually a defect for a view about how to live. Also please write in American English, and I don't need citations or a reading list—just the arguments and where they land.
