I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of consent, and I'm struck by how textbook treatments tend to treat deception, coercion, and incompetence as three independent failure modes that rarely interact in subtle ways. I'd like you to design a thought experiment—realistic, set in a clinical-trial or specialized workplace context, no science-fiction elements—to the following specifications.

Construct a scenario in which Pat, a fully competent adult, explicitly consents to procedure X. There is no coercion: no threats, no economic duress beyond ordinary employment conditions, and no authority figure exploiting dependency. However, Pat is deceived about a structural feature of X—specifically, about the institutional framing under which X is performed (for example, whether it is conducted under a public-health protocol versus a private-commercial protocol, or whether the data generated will be routed through a particular regulatory pathway). Crucially, this framing feature is causally irrelevant to the physical risk, benefit, and experiential quality of X itself; Pat's subjective goals and risk tolerance would not change if they knew the framing. The deception must be intentional on the part of the practitioner, not an accidental omission.

The case must be engineered so that three accounts of consent deliver the specified verdicts: (1) a pure voluntariness-plus-competence account holds that consent is valid because Pat is uncoerced and understands the act's physical nature; (2) a strict full-information account holds that consent is invalid because any intentional deception, even about framing, vitiates authorization; (3) a normative-scope account holds that consent is valid but only because Pat's authorization extends precisely to the physical act they understand, and the hidden framing falls outside the normative scope of what they authorized—making the validity conditional on a boundary that the other two accounts reject. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, walk through each verdict with explicit reasoning, then identify exactly which feature of the case—decoupling physical act-description from institutional framing—does the theoretical work. Finally, explain what collapses if that decoupling is removed (e.g., if the framing feature does affect risk), and how a defender of the full-information view would argue that the decoupling is illusory because framing always constitutes part of the act's identity.
