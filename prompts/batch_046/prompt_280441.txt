I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about celebrating versus cutting down successful people, weighing the American Dream narrative of individual ascent against norms like Jantelagen and tall-poppy syndrome that treat standing out as a collective threat. Praising a baker who built a regional chain from scratch and is now profiled nationally feels right. Celebrating a scientist whose peer-reviewed breakthrough changed her field feels right. Mocking that scientist in her hometown for acting superior — a clear tall-poppy reflex — feels wrong. Offering loud public praise to a colleague who won a major prize mainly because her well-connected mentor lobbied for it feels wrong; a quiet private acknowledgment is enough. Shaming a wealthy heir who bought a sports team and flaunts it feels appropriate. Calling a tech CEO brilliant after his net worth doubles from a stock swing he did not cause feels wrong, though actively mocking him feels excessive — perhaps one should simply withhold praise. Telling a student with perfect grades that she should not think herself special, purely to suppress her, feels wrong. A corporation that ranks every employee and throws a gala for the top ten percent feels toxic and wrong. By contrast, a university awarding a single public valedictorian medal feels acceptable. A community that enforces Jantelagen-style equality through social silence rather than direct mockery feels oppressive when applied to outsiders, though I understand its appeal for insiders. I keep going back and forth on my neighbor, who founded a genuinely effective local nonprofit and is now receiving a city-council honor with a plaque and ceremony: part of me wants to cheer, part thinks public honors always breed resentment and praise should stay private.

Is there a precise principle — or a small competing set — that captures most of these verdicts? If so, what does it imply about the neighbor case, and about further cases such as a lottery winner who donates anonymously versus one who demands a named building? Be explicit about which of my judgments the best principle cannot save — the corporate-ranking judgment, the valedictorian judgment, or another — and whether saving it requires an unacceptable cost in complexity.
