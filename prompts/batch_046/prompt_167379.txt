Philosophers addressing the paradox of fiction often liken our engagement with a tragic narrative to a child’s game of make-believe with dolls: within the frame of play we treat representations as if they were real, experience something like grief or fear, yet remain fully aware that no actual harm is occurring, and defenders use this to argue that fictional emotions are not genuinely paradoxical but merely rule-governed responses inside shared pretense. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the child, the doll, the rules of play, the adult who permits the game, and the emotional response itself? Then identify the fractures: the absence of an explicit agreement to play among adult readers, the resistance of framing devices to fully suppress unconscious emotional arousal, and the difference between voluntary suspension of disbelief and the directedness of real grief. Assess whether the analogy dissolves the paradox — that we genuinely mourn for Anna Karenina despite knowing she does not exist — or whether it smuggles in the assumption that all emotional engagement requires a real object, thereby begging the question against the cognitivist. If the make-believe model fails on these points, propose a more precise analogy that preserves the insight about representational engagement without reducing fictional affect to childlike pretense.
