I have just spent a long evening arguing with a close friend about what makes consent valid in medical and legal contexts. He offered the following summary, which he insists captures the mainstream philosophical position: consent should be understood not merely as a mental state of willingness but as an act of normative authorisation—granting another party permission to cross a boundary they would otherwise wrongfully transgress. For this authorisation to be valid, he said, three conditions must be met: the agent must possess competence, the choice must be voluntary, and the agent must not be deceived. He then made several more specific claims. First, deception vitiates consent only when it targets the 'normative content' of what is being authorised—misrepresenting the procedure itself invalidates consent, but misrepresenting incidental properties, such as the surgeon's credentials or personal views, does not, because the bodily intrusion remains authorised. Second, coercion must take the form of direct, intentional threats of harm; structural pressures—such as being offered the only available life-saving treatment on condition of joining a study, or severe economic necessity—do not count as coercive, since the patient physically retains the option to refuse. Third, competence is a global rational threshold rather than a domain-specific capacity: if one is competent to consent to one serious medical intervention, one is competent to consent to any other. From these premises he drew a conclusion: if a patient is fully and correctly informed about the operation but deceived about the surgeon's identity, they have given valid consent to the procedure itself. Therefore, the surgeon has not committed a bodily wrong, only a separate wrong of deception.

I am not sure how much of this is faithful philosophy and how much is my friend stitching ideas together. Please audit the summary: separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation—paying particular attention to the treatment of deception, the boundaries of coercion, and the nature of competence. Please also assess whether his conclusion about the deceived patient follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it depends on the errors. Please answer in British English.
