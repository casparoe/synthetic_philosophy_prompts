I've been working through some philosophy puzzles on my own—mostly from podcasts and a used copy of an introductory textbook I found—without any formal training, and I keep returning to the question of whether I am really the same person over time. Recently I read about Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant scenario, in which a person's brain, named Yorick, is kept in a laboratory vat while their body, named Hamlet, is sent on a dangerous mission and controlled remotely from that brain. I also came across John Locke's old thought experiment about a prince and a cobbler: Locke suggests that if the prince's consciousness were transferred into the sleeping cobbler's body, the prince would wake up in that body and still be the prince, because personal identity follows consciousness rather than the physical body.

I'm trying to apply Locke's principle to a variation on Dennett's setup that I have not seen discussed. Instead of a sudden separation, imagine that Hamlet's brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with synthetic equivalents that preserve every functional and experiential pattern, while the original biological tissue—Yorick—is kept alive in the vat and continues to receive an identical stream of sensory input through a split feed. By the end of the process, there is a synthetic Hamlet walking around with an unbroken chain of memories and intentions stretching back to the original, and there is also the biological Yorick in the vat with an equally continuous chain of experience going back to the same origin. If Locke were alive today and confronted with this precise fork—where consciousness seems to continue in both branches without interruption—how would he distribute identity? Would he say that both are the original person, that only the branch capable of action counts, or that the split itself breaks the continuity? I am especially curious whether he would need to supplement his consciousness-based rule with something like responsibility or agency, or whether the rule already resolves the case on its own.
