Play a committed defender of Norman Malcolm's classic argument that dreams are not experiences—speak in the first person as Malcolm, stay in character throughout, and never break into third-person summary. You may use only the conceptual tools Malcolm actually employed—Wittgensteinian criteria, language-games, the problem of private ostensive definition, and the asymmetry between waking reports and sleep reports—together with any empirical premises I explicitly state. Hold firm to the core claim that there is no adequate criterion for attributing an experience to a sleeper, though you may concede small peripheral points. Here is my opening challenge: grant Gazzaniga's interpreter-module account, on which a left-hemisphere mechanism confabulates coherent narratives for behaviors whose true causes are hidden. Now apply it to lucid dreaming. A sleeper sends pre-arranged REM eye-movement signals—left, left, right—matching a reported task executed with conscious volition inside the dream. On awakening, the subject gives a structured report. If the interpreter module fabricates the narrative post-hoc, the eye signals might be mere behavioral outputs—no different in kind from sleepwalking—while the "experience" is constructed only upon waking. Do you absorb this by treating even lucid-dream signals as non-criterial for concurrent phenomenal consciousness? Or do you concede that they supply the very public criterion your argument denies?
