Consider the institutional architecture of modern parliamentary democracy: political parties are rarely mentioned in constitutional texts, yet they dominate candidate selection, organize legislative voting through party discipline, and structure public life into antagonistic blocs. At the same time, these systems maintain rituals and doctrines—secret ballots, individual representation, deliberative debate—that ostensibly treat each citizen and each legislator as an autonomous moral agent bearing irreducible personal responsibility. Simone Weil argued, in her radical critique, that parties function as mechanisms for manufacturing collective passion and suppressing the individual capacity for moral reasoning and truth-seeking that democratic legitimacy claims to honor; she regarded them as structurally incompatible with justice because they substitute factional loyalty for attention to reality. Given this tension, what philosophical commitments about collective agency, representation, and moral responsibility does the actual practice of party-governed democracy presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that collective will is ontologically prior to individual judgment, that representation can only operate through organized blocs, or that moral responsibility can be distributed across groups without being diluted?

Examine whether these commitments are internally consistent—particularly with the simultaneous legal and moral insistence that each voter and each deputy bears personal accountability for their choices—and determine whether Weil’s critique exposes a genuine contradiction or merely an uncomfortable but stable trade-off. Then say what would have to change in constitutional design, electoral procedure, or legislative norm if one took Weil’s underlying presuppositions seriously: not merely weakening party power, but restructuring the conditions under which collective decision-making could claim democratic legitimacy without relying on the psychological mechanisms she condemned. Be precise about which parts of current practice are philosophically underdetermined and could survive the critique, and which parts would have to be abandoned entirely.
