I've been working through a version of the paradox of fiction that keeps spilling over into other domains, and I'm not sure where the leak is. Here is the argument my study group constructed.

(1) For any emotional response R directed at an object O's state S to be genuinely rational, the subject must hold a belief that O actually exists, that S actually obtains, and that S is undesirable (for negative R) or desirable (for positive R).

(2) In standard fictional engagement — say, feeling grief at Anna Karenina's death — the subject does not believe Anna actually exists or that her death actually occurs; at most they entertain these propositions within the fiction.

(3) Therefore, by (1) and (2), grief at Anna's death is not genuinely rational; it is at best a simulated or quasi-emotional state.

(4) The same structure applies to emotional responses directed at merely possible people (counterfactual grief over a child never conceived), distant actual people whose existence is only mediated by testimony (sympathy for a famine victim one will never meet), and future states of affairs (anxiety about climate outcomes in 2100).

(5) So if (1) holds, then the vast majority of our emotional life — moral sympathy, anticipatory anxiety, historical pity — collapses into quasi-emotion or irrationality.

(6) But this conclusion is intolerable: we do not want to say that mourning a miscarriage that never happened, or feeling horror at a hypothetical genocide, is always defective.

(7) Therefore, (1) must be false: genuine emotion does not require belief in actual existence.

But here is where the argument seems to prove too much in the opposite direction. If we reject (1) and allow rational grief without belief in actual existence, then there seems to be no principled barrier to rational grief about logically impossible objects, or about entities in internally inconsistent fictions. More specifically, if the rationality condition for pity is merely that the subject entertains the proposition that O suffers undeservedly within some appropriate mental mode, then someone who sincerely pities a round square's suffering, or who weeps for a character in a story they know to be contradictory, is not irrational — they have satisfied the same modal-entertainment condition. The only way to block this is to reintroduce a reality constraint somewhere, but that looks like smuggling (1) back in through the back door.

So my question is: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am inclined to keep (6) — I do not want to dismiss counterfactual and mediated emotions as defective — and I am inclined to keep (3) — I do think fictional grief is in some important way different from real-world grief, not merely identical in rational structure. If I must choose, I would rather weaken (1) than abandon (3) or (6). But I want to know exactly what weakening (1) commits me to.

Explain why that step looked plausible: is there an equivocation between de re and de dicto belief conditions, a scope shift over modal operators, or a modal slide that treats the rationality conditions for emotion as invariant across actual, fictional, and counterfactual contexts?

Price the alternatives. If I weaken (1) by saying that emotion requires only a belief in possibility or in a counterfactual scenario rather than in actual existence, what do the standard positions — the pretend-theory of fiction (Walton-style make-believe), the thought-theory (Carroll-style), and the illusion-theory — each have to pay? In particular, does relaxing the existence condition force any of them to treat fictional and counterfactual emotions as rationally on a par, and is that a cost or a feature?

If such indexing exists, what determines the appropriate index for a given emotional episode, and does it restore a principled distinction between grief for Anna Karenina and grief for a round square without reintroducing a blanket actual-existence requirement?
