As a doctoral student working at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of physics, I have been trying to formalize the standard Black-sphere counterexample to Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to force anti-haecceitism into an untenable global monism. The reasoning looks valid to me, yet its conclusion — that quantum exchange statistics are impossible — is clearly wrong, so there must be a hidden slide. I would like help locating the exact step where the argument overreaches, and then an assessment of what it costs to reject that step.

Here is the argument:

(1) Consider a possible world W containing exactly two macroscopic spheres, A and B, with identical intrinsic properties (mass, radius, temperature, color) and separated by two miles of empty space.
(2) Every relational property that A bears to B is matched by the converse relational property that B bears to A; there is no irreflexive or asymmetric relation available, since W contains no third object or background structure against which to orient them.
(3) Therefore A and B share all qualitative properties — both intrinsic and relational.
(4) Leibniz's Law states that if x and y share all properties, then x is identical to y.
(5) Anti-haecceitism denies that there are non-qualitative, primitive thisnesses (haecceitates) that could distinguish A and B.
(6) So by (3), (4), and (5), A and B are identical.
(7) But (1) stipulates that A and B occupy distinct locations; if they are one object, then W contains a single sphere in two places, or W is misdescribed, which is contradictory.
(8) To avoid contradiction, the anti-haecceitist must reject (1) as impossible: no world can contain two qualitatively identical objects in distinct locations.
(9) Now apply this to quantum mechanics: in a symmetric two-electron state, the particles share all qualitative properties (charge, mass, spin) and stand in symmetric relations.
(10) By (8), they cannot be numerically distinct; there is only one electron, or the state is metaphysically impossible.
(11) This eliminates the exchange symmetry required by quantum statistics and violates the Pauli exclusion principle's application to pairs.
(12) Hence anti-haecceitism is false, and every object must possess a primitive individual essence.

The conclusion in (12) strikes me as far stronger than the premises warrant. I am particularly suspicious of the move from (6) to (8) — it seems like a modal slide from "they are identical in this world" to "this world is impossible" — but I am also unsure whether (2) illicitly assumes that relational properties require a third reference point, which might be the niche mistake. Alternatively, the error might be in (4)'s unrestricted reading of Leibniz's Law across modal contexts, or in (9)'s assimilation of quantum particles to macroscopic spheres.

Could you tell me which specific premise or inferential step I should reject? Please explain why that step initially looks plausible — especially given recent work on weak discernibility via irreflexive relations — and then map out what the main competing positions have to pay to reject it: specifically, the defender of Saunders-Muller weak discernibility, the relationalist who grounds identity in spatiotemporal continuity, and the quantum anti-haecceitist who accepts symmetric states but denies individual essences. I want to understand whether saving the argument requires buying haecceitism outright, or whether there is a more minimal repair.
