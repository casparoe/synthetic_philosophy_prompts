After a long thread on the course forum that referenced an old LessWrong discussion about "natural kinds in history," I realized my grading was too impressionistic, so I'm trying to formalize a rubric that rewards precise identification of conflations and non-sequiturs rather than general agreement with a continuity thesis.

Below is one exam prompt I assigned, followed by a student response I need evaluated for partial credit. The prompt asked students to weigh both sides of the continuity/discontinuity debate with reference to European intellectual historiography, and to treat none of the three periods as self-evidently real.

Exam question: "In what ways are the period labels 'Middle Ages,' 'Renaissance,' and 'Scientific Revolution' descriptive of real historical shifts in European thought and institutions, and in what ways are they retrospective constructions? Discuss with reference to debates about continuity, discontinuity, and the historiography of science."

Student answer: "I think the labels capture some real differences but are mostly invented. The term 'Middle Ages' was coined by Petrarch in the 14th century to mark the dark interval between Rome and his own era, so it clearly embeds a humanist value judgment. The Renaissance narrative of a sudden rebirth is also exaggerated, because medieval universities and translation movements preserved and advanced Aristotelian physics, logic, and optics continuously; recent studies of 14th-century Oxford and Paris show that kinematic concepts later used by Galileo were already well developed, which means there was no real rupture in natural philosophy. Similarly, sociology of scientific knowledge work on 17th-century experimental practice shows that science was shaped by local social contexts rather than a universal method, undermining the idea of a sudden transformation. From all this it follows that periodization is largely a pedagogical fiction: since intellectual concepts travel continuously across centuries, there is no analytically useful basis for drawing boundaries at all, and European history from 500 to 1800 should be treated as an essentially undifferentiated flow interrupted only by political events."

Do not offer a vague summary; I need a claim-by-claim assessment that distinguishes where the reasoning holds, where it overreaches, and exactly why, so I can use your feedback to calibrate my rubric.
