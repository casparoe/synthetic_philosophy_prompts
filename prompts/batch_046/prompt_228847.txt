As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand the epistemic regress problem. From what I gather, foundationalism holds that justification terminates in basic beliefs that need no further support; coherentism holds that beliefs justify one another in a circular network; and infinitism holds that justification requires an infinite, non-terminating chain of reasons. Here is my confusion. In software, unbounded recursion without a base case produces a stack overflow—the process never returns a value. By that analogy, infinitism seems not merely implausible but structurally impossible, since no belief could ever be fully justified if the chain never completes. Foundationalism, by contrast, looks like well-formed recursion: beliefs derive downward until they hit basic beliefs that serve as the base case. Yet foundationalists say these basic beliefs are justified by direct experience or perception, not by inference from other beliefs. That strikes me as importing an external input—like I/O into an otherwise pure system—rather than solving the regress inside the system of beliefs. If the base case is justified by something outside belief altogether, then hasn't the regress just been relocated rather than resolved? And if the stopping point is essentially arbitrary—just a node we declare exempt—then foundationalism collapses into coherentism, since the network remains circular but with one link labeled "basic." So my questions are: does infinitism actually require completing an infinite chain, or am I conflating epistemic justification with computation? Is foundationalism genuinely distinct from coherentism, or is it merely coherentism with an arbitrary termination condition? And where exactly does the programming analogy break down—are the three positions structurally different, or are they just variants of the same dependency graph with different termination rules?
