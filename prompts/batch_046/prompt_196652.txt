I'm noticing a recurring argumentative slide in discussions about whether large language models exhibit Popperian cognition — the kind Dennett distinguishes, in which a creature can generate and test hypotheses internally so that erroneous ones may fail without harming the creature itself. The pattern runs like this. An interlocutor challenges a model with a counterexample or an alternative framing within a single conversation; the model adjusts its output in response. Observers then cite this flexibility as evidence that the system is not merely Skinnerian, shaped by external reward through trial and error, but genuinely Popperian, capable of internal hypothesis testing. Yet the adjustment never extends beyond the prompt window: there is no persistent internal world-model being revised, no domain-general exposure to risk, and no consequence for the system itself if the counterfactual fails. The behavior looks more like a Skinnerian creature operating over an unusually rich, linguistically mediated reward landscape than like a creature with an internal architecture that can be genuinely falsified. I want to know whether this specific misattribution — upgrading a system to Popperian status on the basis of localized, prompt-bound counterfactual fluency — has an established name in philosophy of mind or cognitive science. How does it differ from ordinary anthropomorphism or from a simple category mistake? If there is no precise term, can you propose one, with a clear definition and boundary cases that distinguish it from both genuine Popperian cognition and from standard Skinnerian conditioning?
