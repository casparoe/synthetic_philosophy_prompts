I am in the middle of drafting a novel about a maritime archive—an institution that preserves only the acoustic reverberations of a submerged civilization, never the objects themselves. The narrative turns on a philosopher-character who must decide what, if anything, exists in the depths now that direct observation is permanently impossible, not merely hidden. I have sketched five competing stances she might defend, and I would like you to rank them from the most to the least defensible, assigning each a brief justification that appeals to internal coherence rather than to authority.

The first is structural preservationism: only the relational patterns captured in the acoustic traces warrant ontological commitment, while the intrinsic nature of the submerged entities is permanently bracketed. The second is modal rationalism: we should postulate whatever entities are necessary to make the modal space of the traces maximally consistent, even if those entities possess properties never manifested in the data. The third is ontological deflationism: the question of what exists in the inaccessible region is not false but empty, like asking about the color of a sound, and should be dissolved rather than answered. The fourth is pragmatic reconstructionism: we adopt an ontology not because it is true but because it yields the most fruitful predictions about future acoustic anomalies, treating existence as a regulative fiction calibrated to expectation. The fifth—this is the position I have invented for the novel, though it draws on familiar intuitions—is indexical anchoring: the claim that ontology is fixed not by properties or relations but by a network of irreducible indexical pointers (this shadow, that echo) that survive information loss, so that to identify an entity is not to describe it but to preserve its unique coordinate in the trace-matrix, regardless of whether any descriptive content remains.

Please order these five—structural preservationism, modal rationalism, ontological deflationism, pragmatic reconstructionism, and indexical anchoring—by how seriously a philosopher of science should take them when empirical access is not merely hidden but structurally destroyed. Express the result as a tier list or ordered list with a relative weight or score, and attend specifically to the problem of ontology identification under inaccessibility rather than to general epistemological caution. If you think two positions are essentially tied, say so explicitly and explain what further consideration—perhaps about the preservation conditions for indexical reference or about the cost of modal proliferation—would break the tie.
