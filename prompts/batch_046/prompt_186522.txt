Write a dialogue between Mira and Jonas, two grad students in philosophy of mind who just left a long seminar on predictive processing. Mira defends a specific version of the "consciousness is a user illusion" view: she thinks the brain doesn't just build a simplified desktop-world, but actually constructs a recursive control-avatar for the organism itself, and subjective experience is nothing more than the feedback loop between that avatar's predictions and bodily action. She calls this the recursive avatar loop and insists there's no inner audience watching a show, just a model that systematically mistakes itself for the system running it.

Jonas is sceptical. He accepts that the brain builds models, but he thinks calling consciousness a user illusion smuggles in a hidden homunculus. His counter-argument turns on interface transparency: in real interfaces, like a computer desktop, there's always an underlying layer you can inspect if you know how to look. But with consciousness there is no analog to viewing the source code—the qualitative feel isn't hidden behind a representation, it just is what's there. So either consciousness isn't an interface at all, or the metaphor collapses because the user and the interface are identical in a way that makes "illusion" meaningless.

Have them actually respond to each other point by point. Mira should push back that Jonas is treating transparency as essential to interfaces, when biological interfaces evolved specifically to be opaque—the avatar doesn't need source access to steer the body. Jonas should reply that if the interface is totally opaque even in principle, then we're not explaining consciousness, we're just renaming it "avatar feedback" and declaring victory. They should both be sharp, neither should be a strawman, and maybe they end up circling the real disagreement: whether an adequate explanation can dissolve phenomenal data into functional architecture, or whether it has to preserve the qualitative feel as something real but mislocated.

Keep it casual, like they're arguing over coffee. A typo or two is totally fine.
