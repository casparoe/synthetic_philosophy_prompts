Having spent my career in condensed-matter physics before turning recently to metaphysics, I find myself drawn to Leibniz’s claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds, yet puzzled by the mechanics of comparison he seems to presuppose. Most discussions I encounter focus on moral evil or free will, but I am interested in a more structural conceptual difficulty—one that arises when we take seriously the idea that possibility is not merely a discrete set of worlds but a continuous space ordered by degrees of perfection, much like an energy landscape with local minima and separated attractor basins.

Give an example of a conceptual issue within this framework that involves precisely such a topological complication. Specifically, imagine two regions in the space of compossible worlds—call them region A and region B—each representing a local maximum of overall value according to a distinct, internally coherent metric of goodness. In region A, the goods realized are cognitive richness and structural complexity, optimized to such an extent that any nearby world introducing more moral simplicity necessarily collapses into contradiction with the laws governing that region; in region B, minimal suffering and maximal harmony dominate, but in ways that are genuinely incommensurable with the goods of A, so that no common scale exists to rank a world in A against one in B. The landscape therefore contains separated basins with no continuous path of monotonic improvement connecting them. The conceptual problem is whether Leibniz’s God, guided by the principle of sufficient reason, can have a rational basis for selecting one global peak when the topology of possibility itself permits multiple, incomparable local optima, and whether this introduces a form of modal underdetermination that the doctrine of the best world was meant to eliminate.

Please provide a concrete, worked-out instance of this issue—describe the specific goods or structural features that generate these incommensurable basins, and take a definite position on whether this genuinely undermines the uniqueness of God’s choice or can instead be dissolved by appealing to the internal logic of compossibility alone. I want a committed answer with explicit reasoning, not a survey of options; and since I have no access to external libraries, please avoid citations, bibliographies, or appeals to obscure texts, so that the argument stands or falls on pure conceptual analysis.
