At a workshop on European governance, two scholars—Elena and Marcus—became locked in a dispute over whether the EU’s democratic deficit is a structural consequence of pooled sovereignty or a remediable institutional failure. Their exchange unfolded in five moves.

(1) Elena argued that pooling sovereignty in areas such as monetary policy or competition law necessarily produces what she called “regressive legitimacy”: national parliaments lose legislative authority over those domains, while the European Parliament lacks the partisan coherence, direct electoral mandate, and legislative initiative required to replace them. Democratic control, she said, moves upward without being reconstructed at the higher level.

(2) Marcus replied that this treats sovereignty as zero-sum, which is exactly the misconception the integration project was meant to overcome. Pooling sovereignty is not losing it but exercising it collectively; democratic authorization is therefore preserved through the Council and European Council, where elected national governments sit. Any deficit is one of transparency or media coverage, not of democratic legitimacy.

(3) Elena countered that the Council’s democratic chain is too indirect to satisfy the control condition for democracy: a voter in one member state cannot remove a government in another member state that blocks legislation she cares about, so the link between electoral choice and policy outcome is severed. She added that ever-closer union deepens the problem by expanding pooled sovereignty—through successive treaty revisions—without a corresponding expansion of transnational democratic mechanisms.

(4) Marcus conceded the indirectness but accused Elena of smuggling in a unitary-state template of democracy. He proposed that EU legitimacy should be assessed as “demoi-cratic”: each national demos retains its own democratic life, and the union’s legitimacy derives from the consent of these plural demoi rather than from a single European demos. On this view, the democratic deficit vanishes once we stop measuring the EU against the institutional form of a nation-state.

(5) Elena responded that multi-level legitimacy collapses into a dilemma. If each demos retains a genuine veto or exit option, collective action fails and ever-closer union becomes impossible; if it does not retain such an option, then its sovereignty is nominal and democratic consent is hollow. She noted that withdrawal—exemplified by Brexit—is treated institutionally as a failure of integration rather than as a legitimate exercise of democratic sovereignty, which reveals that Marcus’s “consent of the demoi” is constrained in ways that make it incompatible with true democratic authorization.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether the democratic deficit is structural or contingent, identify what both parties are missing about the relationship between pooled sovereignty and democratic authorization, and explain whether Elena's dilemma in move (5) genuinely undermines Marcus's demoi-cratic proposal or can be answered without abandoning multi-level legitimacy.
