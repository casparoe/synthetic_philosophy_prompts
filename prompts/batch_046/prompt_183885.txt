I've bought into the Deweyan line that democracy is less a form of goverment and more a continous social experiment—like the Great Community trying to solve its own problems through co-operatve intelligence rather than through fixed rules or majority voting. The legitimacy of democratic institutions, on this view, comes from their functioning as distributed inquiry: they work like peer review for social policy, not like a popularity contest. I want to stress-test this. What observations or arguments would actually break the analogy between democratic deliberation and scientific inquiry? For instance, science eventually converges on answers through shared reality checks, but politics often doesn't—does that just mean we need better democracy, or does it show the model is wrong? And if democratic participation is justified because it builds citizens' judgment capacities, what if empirical work shows participation makes people more polerized rather than wiser? Rank these defeaters from fatal to awkward. Which ones would a serious Deweyan concede, and which would they dismiss by saying the case wasn't real democracy? I'm also worried the view might be insulated: every failure gets explained away as not being true co-operatve inquiry. Is that insulation a problem for an experimentalist theory, or is it exactly what you'd expect?
