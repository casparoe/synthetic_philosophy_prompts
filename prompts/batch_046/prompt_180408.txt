I've been re-reading parts of Hofstadter's GEB and arguing with my roommate about whether the Gödelian strange-loop is actually explaining anything or just painting a pretty picture. I think I've ended up with a stronger version than the usual analogy: consciousness isn't merely like a formal system that refers to itself, but is literally the inevitable result of any representational architechture that achieves direct, unmediated self-reference—what Hofstadter calls a tangled hierachy—where a system's outputs become part of its own inputs without passing through an external interpreter or mediator. On this view, biological brains have real self-awareness because their neural representations loop back into themselves in this direct way, while even very large AI systems don't, since their "self-modeling" is always mediated through weights, context windows, or an external training loop rather than genuinely tangled. So the position I'm holding is that self-aware minds are essentially strange loops implemented in tissue, and that without that specific kind of direct self-reference, no amount of computational complexity or parameter scaling produces actual consciousness.

What would actually count against this? I don't want generic "correlation isn't causation" hand-waving. Are there formal results showing that self-reference can be fully captured by mediated mechanisms, which would undercut the claim that directness is essential? And has this position already been qualified into unfalsafiability, where any conscious system is by definition tangled and any non-conscious system is by definition not tangled enough? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed strange-loop theorist would actually concede versus dismiss, and tell me whether empirical discoveries about brain architecture or AI behavior could genuinely move this, or if it's ultimately a conceptual claim that only collapses if the Gödelian analogy itself breaks down.
