hey so i'm a retired physicist who got into philosphy of science recently and i'm trying to pin down my view on Godel before a reading group discussion. here's roughly where i landed:

1. Godel's incompleteness theorems apply literally only to formal axiomatic systems with recursive rules. using them directly on brains or on physics is basically a category error - Penrose-style arguments that consciousness must be non-computational are misapplying the math.
2. that said, i think there's a looser "Godelian" point worth holding onto: any system complex enough to represent itself will have blind spots that can't be resolved from inside the system.
3. i'm a physicalist, but i take the hard problem seriously. subjective experience isn't non-physical, it's just that third-person models have an explanitory gap that more data or axioms won't close.
4. i think this gap is structurally analogous to undecidability: not identical, but similar in that a formal model of mind will always miss the first-person perspective in a principled way.
5. at the same time, i think physics is asymptotically approaching a complete empirical description of nature - not formally provable as complete, but practically exhaustive.
6. so i reject literal Godel arguments for dualism, yet i think the analogy between formal undecidability and the hard problem actually illuminates why physicalism doesn't dissolve the gap.

do these hang together? i feel like (1) is pulling against (4) - if it's a category error to apply Godel to minds, why am i treating the hard problem as structurally analogous to undecidability? and is (5) compatible with (3), since if physics can be practically complete but minds still have this permanent gap, aren't i giving consciousness a special status my physicalism can't afford? rank the tensions by severity and say what the cheapest fix is for each. also there's probably a fake tension in there that dissolves once you look closer - point it out. thanks!
