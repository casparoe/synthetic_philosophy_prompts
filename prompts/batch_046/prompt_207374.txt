I'm preparing the final examination for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar—third-year undergraduates, ninety minutes, worth forty percent of the course grade. Over the past three weeks we have worked through Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his positive proposal that consciousness consists of multiple drafts being edited in parallel without a central observer. To prevent students from simply reproducing lecture summaries, I want an essay question that forces them to apply these distinctions to a novel hybrid account. Embed in the exam the following hypothetical theory, which I will call the Staggered Projection Model (SPM): according to SPM, there is indeed a single unified "screen" of conscious experience, but it is not watched by a homunculus; rather, the screen is reconstructed every 200 milliseconds from competing draft fragments, with a "temporal gatekeeper" that permits only one version to be projected into immediate awareness, while earlier drafts survive only as "ghost traces" accessible to memory but not to present consciousness. Crucially, the gatekeeper itself has no conscious access to its own selection criteria.

Write me a forty-mark essay prompt (suggested length: 800–1000 words) that asks students to evaluate whether the Staggered Projection Model successfully avoids Dennett's objections to the Cartesian theater, or whether it collapses back into that model, or whether it introduces new problems that Dennett's multiple drafts account avoids. The question must require students to distinguish carefully between (i) the spatial or unified-location problem of the theater, (ii) the homuncular-observer problem, and (iii) the problem of the "Cartesian spotlight" of immediate, all-at-once access. Along with the exam question, provide (a) a model answer that specifies the philosophical moves a top-scoring response must make—including an accurate diagnosis of where SPM stands relative to both the theater and the drafts model, and the identification of at least one genuinely novel difficulty that SPM introduces that pure multiple drafts do not face; and (b) a marking scheme out of forty that assigns points for correct application of Dennett's arguments, precise conceptual discrimination among the three problems listed above, accurate evaluation of SPM's status, identification of a new problem, and that penalizes students who conflate the Cartesian theater metaphor with the multiple drafts alternative or who treat the gatekeeper as unproblematically non-homuncular without argument. The difficulty should arise from conceptual discrimination rather than from recalling obscure literature, and the prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
