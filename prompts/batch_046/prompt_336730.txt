Illusionism, as defended by Keith Frankish and related thinkers, denies that there exist intrinsic qualitative properties—qualia—that constitute phenomenal consciousness, but it does not reject consciousness altogether in the manner of global eliminativists like Paul Churchland, who deny propositional attitudes entirely. Instead, illusionists hold that mental states are fully real in their functional and access roles, and the appearance of qualitative feel arises because introspection systematically misrepresents these functional states: our cognitive architecture generates representational models in which accessible information is portrayed as having non-physical qualitative characters. To explain why these representations feel so compelling, illusionists adapt the phenomenal concept strategy—originally developed by realists—to show that our concepts for experience can apply even in the absence of real phenomenal properties, producing a kind of empty but vivid reference. This framework is fully compatible with Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness. Illusionists accept that the distinction tracks a real difference in how we conceptualize mental life: access consciousness corresponds to the actual functional organization of cognition, while phenomenal consciousness corresponds to the projected, misrepresentational layer we add to it, so the distinction is ontologically spurious yet conceptually useful.
