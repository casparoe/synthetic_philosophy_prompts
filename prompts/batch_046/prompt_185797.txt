I have been attempting to formalise the interaction between Bostrom-style simulation reasoning and longtermist moral frameworks, and I find myself caught in a tension I cannot quite dissolve. Suppose one accepts the disjunctive premise that we are either in a base reality or inside an ancestor simulation executed by technologically mature civilisations. It then seems that assigning a non-negligible credence to the latter branch should substantially weaken the normative urgency of far-future interventions: if our temporal horizon is confined to a simulated substrate, the welfare of entities existing millennia hence within this run possesses, at best, derivative moral significance relative to the axiological framework of the base-reality operators. Wouldn't rational agents who take the simulation argument seriously therefore be compelled to dampen their far-future fanaticism, redirecting concern instead toward near-term behaviours that minimise the risk of simulator termination?

Moreover, given that the argument implies simulated minds vastly outnumber unsimulated ones, shouldn't our prior for inhabiting a simulation be so dominant that the very concept of an unsimulated 'far future' becomes ontologically unstable? In that case, longtermist planning would not merely be probabilistically discounted but conceptually incoherent, since the object of concern — a distant, unsimulated posterity — might lack any well-defined referent. I am trying to see whether this line of reasoning genuinely undermines far-future fanaticism, or whether it rests on a confusion about the level-dependence of moral status and the strategic opacity of simulator preferences; could you help me locate the precise error?
