In planning debates about upzoning and neighborhood preservation, the same empirical studies—on displacement risk, carbon intensity, and supply elasticity—are cited by both sides, yet the deadlock persists across cities with very different housing markets. My working theory is that the disagreement is not about housing economics at all, but about whether the physical form of an existing neighborhood generates a kind of morphological right: a claim by present residents, and perhaps by the place itself, against alteration that is independent of property titles and survives resident turnover. The growth coalition treats zoning as a distributive mechanism for access across generations; the preservation coalition treats it as stewardship of collective memory embedded in built scale and street life. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—say, about the causal pathway from upzoning to actual displacement—masked by shared rhetoric? Or are the sides answering different questions altogether, one about intergenerational equity among potential residents and the other about the intrinsic value of urban continuity? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, not a survey of planning literature, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—an empirical result, a formal distinction between access rights and form rights, or a reframing of the question—could move the impasse, or whether the conflict is best understood as a clash of bedrock intuitions about what a city owes to its future versus its present inhabitants.
