I am drafting briefing materials for a constitutional convention in a multinational state, and the commission is deadlocked over whether to entrench what some members call a two-referendum rule for regions seeking independence. The proposal holds that a first referendum may pass by simple majority, but any subsequent vote on separation within a single generation—defined here as twenty-five years—would require a supermajority of sixty percent, on the theory that repeated polling risks destabilizing democratic commitment without allowing genuine generational renewal. Critics contend that this unfairly privileges territorial integrity over self-determination, particularly in cases like Scotland, Quebec, and Catalonia, where prior referenda or declarations have produced contested outcomes and where demographic turnover arguably constitutes a new people with a fresh moral claim. Defenders reply that democratic consistency obliges a losing side to accept defeat for a substantial interval, lest secession become a perpetual bargaining instrument rather than an exceptional remedy.

Write an essay weighing these competing considerations. Reach and defend a clear verdict: either endorse the two-referendum supermajority rule as a necessary safeguard for democratic stability, or reject it as an illegitimate entrenchment of the status quo that violates the principle of self-determination. Your conclusion should explicitly address whether generational turnover genuinely resets the moral authority of the original electorate, and whether a simple-majority first vote followed by a supermajority second vote strikes an acceptable balance between continuity and change. Do not end on a non-committal note.
