Draft the joint statement that an Orwellian and a Stalinesque theorist of conscious temporal experience could both sign after an adversarial collaboration focused on the color phi phenomenon and the related cutaneous-rabbit illusion. Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims about the psychophysics and the experimental conditions, followed by a disagreements table in which each row states one precise disagreement phrased in wording its own side accepts.

In the agreed-claims section, cover: the stimulus parameters (two spatially separated color changes or tactile taps with brief interstimulus intervals); the subjective report (subjects report a single moving or traveling percept rather than two discrete events); the dependence of the report on the second stimulus (if the second stimulus is absent or masked, the intermediate percept does not occur); and what both sides agree is not in dispute (for example, that the brain processes both stimuli and that the report is genuinely produced).

In the disagreements table, include at least: (1) whether there exists a first-draft conscious representation of an intermediate state at the location of the first stimulus that is subsequently revised or overwritten by information arriving with the second stimulus (the Orwellian view), versus whether no such first-draft representation ever enters consciousness and the narrative of motion or travel is constructed entirely post-hoc without any revision of prior conscious content (the Stalinesque view); (2) whether introspective reports in these cases should be treated as direct data about the temporal structure of experience or as post-hoc judgments that need not mirror the temporal order of conscious states.

Then identify the cruxes — specifically, whether neural correlates of an intermediate-state representation can be detected at a time before the second stimulus has been processed, and whether the absence of such correlates would count as decisive for either side. For each crux, specify the concrete experimental design or piece of evidence both camps agree would bear on it, and record each side's advance prediction about what that evidence will show.

Finally, include a brief section addressing whether any part of the dispute is verbal: for instance, whether disagreement about when consciousness of the intermediate state occurs dissolves once both sides specify whether they mean neural activity time, report time, or attributed experience time. Say which part, if any, is verbal and why, without asking either side to concede the substantive point.

Keep the wording neutral: do not frame either position as obviously wrong, do not ask for a bibliography or citation list, and do not invoke theories or data so specialized that they could not be evaluated from general knowledge of cognitive science and philosophy of mind.
