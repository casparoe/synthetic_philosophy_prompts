I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have begun working through questions in political philosophy with a small reading group. One analogy that keeps surfacing in our discussions of direct democracy—particularly when we compare the Swiss referendum system, the Brexit vote, and California ballot propositions—is that a popular referendum functions like a controlled laboratory experiment. The claim, usually advanced by enthusiasts of direct democracy, is that by isolating a single policy question from the noise of party bargaining, coalition-building, and legislative amendment, a referendum allows the electorate to measure the “true” popular will with something like the precision and neutrality of an instrument reading a physical constant. Parliamentary politics, on this view, is the messy real world full of confounding variables; the referendum is the clean bench-top trial.

I would like you to audit this analogy thoroughly. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in the referendum corresponds to the apparatus, the sample population, the control conditions, the replication protocol, the measurement error, and the observer? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down—consider, for instance, the absence of random assignment, the self-selection of voters, the framing effects of ballot language, the lack of reproducibility across jurisdictions or time, and the fact that campaigns are not neutral conditions but engineered interventions. Third, say which features of laboratory science the analogy quietly imports—objectivity, value-neutrality, reproducibility, the separation of fact from norm—and whether those imports are warranted in the political case. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion that referendum outcomes are therefore more legitimate, more “true,” or more authoritative than parliamentary decisions actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the laboratory image fails, suggest what analogy—if any—better captures the epistemic and normative status of a referendum result.

Please organize your response with the correspondence table first, then the disanalogies separated into those that matter for the intended normative use and those that do not, and finally your assessment of the conclusion. End with a single-sentence verdict enclosed exactly as \answer{...} stating whether the analogy survives as a justification for treating referendum outcomes as purified measurements of popular will.
