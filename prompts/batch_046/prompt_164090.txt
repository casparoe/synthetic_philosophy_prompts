I have recently begun caring for a parent with a progressive degenerative condition, and in trying to think clearly about what actually benefits someone in prolonged decline, I have been drawn to the philosophical position sometimes called tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive achievements, pleasures, or fulfilled desires add nothing to well-being. What I need is not a general defense or critique, but help with a conceptual distinction that seems to be collapsed in most discussions and that has direct bearing on clinical judgment: is tranquilism primarily a structural claim about the composition of well-being (that negative states alone are goods, and positive states are axiologically inert), or is it an ontological claim about value itself (that positive states literally do not exist as goods, rather than merely being outweighed)? More specifically, I want to understand the divergence between what might be called craving-elimination tranquilism, which treats the extinction of desire as valuable even when low-level discomfort persists, and suffering-elimination tranquilism, which treats the absence of pain as valuable even when latent preferences and attachments remain intact. Could you explain how these two versions conceptually come apart in edge cases—such as deep sedation that suppresses suffering without dissolving preference structures, versus adaptive preference extinction that eliminates craving while leaving subtle somatic distress? I am especially interested in whether the distinction survives once we clarify what counts as a genuine craving rather than a mere preference, and whether one version ultimately collapses into the other or into a broader form of negative hedonism. Please do not provide a bibliography or literature survey; I need a careful conceptual mapping of the logical space, the clinical implications of each branch, and where a defender of each version would say the other has mislocated the source of value.
