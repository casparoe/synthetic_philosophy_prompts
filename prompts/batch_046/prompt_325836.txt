I have been drafting a short essay on the ethics of voting when every viable candidate supports policies that cause serious harm, and I would like your direct comments on the reasoning, its coherence, and whether I have overlooked an obvious objection or smuggled in an unstated assumption. The essay distinguishes three layers of moral involvement in an election: causal contribution, which is negligible for any individual voter; symbolic legitimation, which occurs when participation is interpreted as acceptance of the electoral process itself; and institutional authorization, which happens when the winner can claim a democratic mandate derived from the aggregate pool of voters, including those who selected the losing option. I argue that the familiar 'lesser evil' justification addresses only the first layer, treating the vote as a private harm-minimization calculation, but it neglects the other two. Because the winner's mandate is constructed from total turnout, casting a ballot for the less harmful candidate still supplies a unit of democratic authorization to the system that generated harmful alternatives in the first place. I then examine the expressive defense—that one votes not to secure victory but to register dissent—and reply that in a winner-take-all framework, dissent expressed through a losing vote is structurally converted into support for the legitimacy of the outcome, since high participation strengthens the mandate regardless of how the votes are distributed. From this I conclude that there is an unresolved conflict between a duty to reduce harm and a duty not to authorize harmful institutions, and that resolving it requires either accepting complicity in the authorization layer or accepting that abstention, though it fails to reduce harm causally, avoids the symbolic and institutional layers. I am especially uncertain about the inference from individual vote to aggregate mandate: does my single ballot genuinely contribute to the winner's authorization in a meaningful way, or does this reasoning commit a fallacy of division? I also wonder whether the expressive defense can be salvaged by distinguishing between voting for a conventional candidate and voting for a protest option, assuming one is available.
