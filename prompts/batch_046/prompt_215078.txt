Because of my recent work with a collective advising on long-horizon technology governance, intergenerational decision theory has become personally relevant in a way it never was during my formal training, and I have agreed to help a colleague—currently away—who teaches a graduate seminar on ethics across infinite populations. I am serving as an external grader for one exam and want a rigorous point-by-point assessment without inserting my own judgment about whether the answer is broadly acceptable.

The exam question asks students to consider two deterministic policies affecting a countably infinite sequence of generations. Policy A assigns welfare 2 to every second generation and 0 to all others; Policy B assigns welfare 1 to every generation. Because both yield divergent aggregate sums, standard total utilitarianism cannot rank them. The prompt requires an evaluation of whether a limit-average (Cesàro) aggregation rule consistently orders A and B; an explanation of how the principle of indifference—interpreted here as a normative commitment to equal moral weight per generation—interacts with the mathematical fact that Banach limits for bounded sequences depend on the choice of a free ultrafilter; a precise statement about whether rearranging the generational ordering affects the limit-average; and a discussion of whether temporal discounting resolves any tension between mathematical non-uniqueness and normative equality.

The student submitted the following response. They begin by correctly observing that both policies produce infinite total welfare, leaving simple summation silent. They then compute the Cesàro mean for Policy B as 1, and for Policy A also as 1, since exactly half the generations receive welfare 2 and half receive 0, and they conclude that the policies are therefore tied under limit-averaging. At this point they invoke "the principle of indifference" to justify assigning identical moral weight to every generation, arguing that without distinguishing information we must treat each position in the sequence equally. They extend this reasoning to the non-uniqueness of Banach limits: because different free ultrafilters produce different extensions of the limit, and because ethical theory supplies no criterion for selecting one, they claim that the principle of indifference requires distributing credence uniformly across all admissible Banach limits, yielding an undefined expected ranking that confirms moral indifference between A and B. To break the tie, they propose introducing a temporal discount factor, which they say resolves the ambiguity by breaking symmetry; they attribute this solution to Rawls's savings principle in A Theory of Justice, describing it as an application of the original position across generations that prohibits imposing arbitrary differences on future people. Finally, they correctly note that rearranging the generational sequence in Policy A does not alter its Cesàro mean, so rearrangement invariance is preserved.

Please grade this answer on a standard graduate scale and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the score. Assess each claim individually: identify where the mathematical reasoning is sound, where a concept is conflated with another, where a view is misattributed, and where a proposed resolution fails to follow from the premises. Deliver the evaluation as a grader would record it in the margins—do not offer a broad verdict of "pass" or "fail," and do not indicate your own agreement or disagreement with the student's conclusions.
