Following a lengthy argument with a friend about whether the exposure of a forgery eliminates all artistic value or merely shifts its kind, I need a reusable decision procedure for my role on the acquisitions and display committee of a regional museum. Our board includes both a philosopher of art and an attorney, and they require that any decision to deaccession, relabel, or retain an object for study be consistent, defensible, and completed within a single meeting, but we currently have no framework that translates philosophical principle into an actual vote. I want the procedure grounded in the three-layer distinction my friend insisted upon: genetic value, meaning the object’s actual causal connection to the artist’s hand and deliberate choices; semiotic value, meaning its function as a token that carries the artist’s intended artistic statement or meaning; and experiential value, meaning the quality of the perceptual and aesthetic encounter it affords, regardless of origin or history. For any object discovered or suspected to be inauthentic, the procedure should specify, in this exact order: the empirical or documentary questions to ask about each layer; what counts as sufficient evidence to answer yes, no, or indeterminate for that layer; the precise point at which the remaining question becomes a judgment of institutional values rather than fact, and must be flagged as such for board deliberation; and the decision branch that follows—retain with corrected attribution and context, relocate to a study or teaching collection, or deaccession. The procedure must be runnable by a curator without philosophical training, should take no more than thirty minutes per case once the evidence is assembled, and must say explicitly where it will misfire or produce an answer the committee will not want. Apply it to three cases: a landscape painting executed in correct period pigments and brushwork but actually painted in 1952 by an unknown hand copying a documented lost original; a bronze cast made from the artist’s own molds but produced without authorization by a foundry after the artist’s death; and a painting genuinely executed by the named artist but subsequently overpainted by a restorer to the point that its current visible surface is mostly not the artist’s work. Include a worked example in which two layers conflict and the procedure demands retaining an object the committee is inclined to reject, and state what the procedure cannot do—particularly where empirical evidence is silent and value judgment is unavoidable. Do not include bibliographies, named texts, citations, or references that require searching a library; the deliverable is the procedure itself, with its tests, branches, and honest failure points.
