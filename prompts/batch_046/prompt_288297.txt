I've been thinking about the Condorcet jury theorem—the idea that if each voter is slightly more likely right than wrong and decides independently, the majority is more accurate than any individual—and I recently watched 12 Angry Men and can't stop turning it over. The jurors open with an eleven-to-one guilty vote, then through deliberation reach unanimous acquittal. At first that looks like collective wisdom, but Condorcet requires independence, and the entire plot is about influence, shared reasoning, and social pressure, which seems to violate the theorem's central condition. Meanwhile, Juror 8 doesn't just persuade; he introduces private signals—the switchblade, the old man's gait, the glasses marks—that the others missed entirely, so the correction feels less like averaging independent judgments and more like one person breaking a group-wide correlation of error. I keep coming back to this tension: the film celebrates the jury reaching the right answer together, yet the mechanism is exactly what the theorem warns against. Does the movie implicitly defend deliberation as epistemically superior despite destroying independence, or does it accidentally show that collective accuracy depends on hidden heterogeneity in competence and information rather than on majority rule itself? I'm also struck by how the jurors' initial errors are highly correlated—shared fatigue, class prejudice, and a desire to finish—so that breaking that correlation is what saves them, not the vote count. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the logic of the theorem seriously—the independence condition, the problem of correlated mistakes, and whether deliberation improves judgment or merely replaces one bias with another—and tells me where the film holds up as an argument about groups and where it collapses under scrutiny. I'm discussing this with a friend who studies political philosophy, and I want to avoid saying something naive about crowds being automatically wise, so please point out where my framing might be missing something the film actually shows.
