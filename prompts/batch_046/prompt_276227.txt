I'm presenting next Tuesday to a philosophy-of-history reading group I've been attending for about a year—mostly historians of early modern thought who work on Locke, Hume, and the Scottish moralists, plus one ethicist who understands decision theory well but hasn't read Franklin closely. I have no formal academic background in history or philosophy, but over the last several months I've worked through Franklin's Autobiography and some older scholarship on his virtue project. The thesis I want to defend is this: Franklin's well-known scheme of thirteen virtues, tracked in a small book with weekly grids and pursued despite his admission that he never achieved perfection, should not be read as a version of Aristotelian habituation aimed at cultivating character. Instead, it operates as an internal precommitment technology—what we might call rational irrationality turned inward—where Franklin binds himself to rigid rules precisely because he expects his future self to update away from them in the moment. He treats his own future preferences as unreliable agents to be constrained by prior rules, not as capacities to be developed through practice.

The audience is deeply skeptical of "presentism" and allergic to importing formal frameworks—especially decision-theoretic ones—into eighteenth-century texts. The historians will care about period vocabulary, textual fidelity, and whether Franklin could have intended anything resembling this framing; the ethicist will grasp the Parfit-style concept immediately but will press me on whether I'm attributing to Franklin an idea he could not have possessed. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a full defense of the thesis itself. Please order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each the way someone in that room would actually put it, and for each tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and the specific point where that reply is weakest. Please also include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks—probably something about whether I'm just redescribing Stoic askesis with modern vocabulary.
