I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether a traditionalist, procreative account of marriage can consistently exclude same-sex unions. She kept returning to an analogy I want to examine more rigorously: marriage, she claimed, is like a single biological organism—eyes and heart, say—where reproductive and affective capacities are not interchangeable parts but functionally integrated capacities of one whole. A same-sex couple, on her view, is analogous to two eyes without a heart: they may cooperate closely, but they lack the specific complementary integration that makes the whole capable of its defining function, so extending the institution to them is like calling a lichen a single reproductive organism merely because its fungal and algal components live in intimate symbiosis. I objected that this imports a teleological biology we no longer accept, and that modern evolutionary theory is replete with non-reproductive symbioses—mitochondrial endosymbiosis, for instance—that are still treated as unified organisms despite never producing offspring together. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—spouses, reproductive act, institutional recognition, state sanction, biological parts—then identify precisely where the source domain and the target diverge, especially with respect to intentionality, legal constructibility, and the normative weight given to reproductive potential? In particular, does the analogy quietly smuggle in the claim that only reproductive integration counts as true organic unity, and if so, does the exclusionary conclusion survive once we acknowledge that biology recognizes many forms of functional integration that have nothing to do with reproduction? If the analogy fails to carry the argument, what would be a more defensible comparison for distinguishing marriage from other durable partnerships without presupposing the conclusion?
