In major Holocaust memorial institutions, curators typically maintain a strict architectural and narrative separation: the genocide of European Jews is given its own chronological pathway and dedicated spaces, while the persecution of Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, and homosexual victims is placed in peripheral galleries or separate memorials, despite the fact that the same bureaucratic and industrial apparatus targeted all of them. What implicit theory of uniqueness—historical singularity, moral incomparability, or ontological distinctness—does that design presuppose? Must it entail that comparing these victim groups risks dilution, or can it be justified on purely pedagogical grounds? Would a Bauman-style thesis—that the Holocaust was not a regression but an extreme expression of modern rational-bureaucratic organization—license the same separation, or does it undermine the claim that the event is incomparable? And is the practice consistent with these same institutions' insistence, in their educational programming, that the Holocaust yields universal human-rights lessons?
