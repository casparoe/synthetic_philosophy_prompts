my study group came up with this argument about Carroll's tortoise and now we're stuck. (1) Deductive justification requires that every step is licensed by a rule we can explicitly state. (2) Carroll shows that stating modus ponens doesn't apply itself; you need a meta-rule to apply it. (3) That meta-rule needs its own meta-meta-rule, so justification is either an infinite regress or circular. (4) Circular justification is useless, and an infinite regress never delivers an actual ground. (5) So the only real ground is a brute acceptance of some base rule B. (6) But B is just a syntactic pattern; we could swap in B' with the same formal shape but which gives licence to contradiction — say, from P and not-P derive Q and not-Q. (7) So if B is brute, there's no rational constraint stopping B' from being equally brute. (8) Therefore deduction either has no rational foundation, or it's compatible with deriving contradictions. (9) Yet in actual mathematical practice we treat deduction as rationally constrained and contradiction-free. (10) So we must reject the idea that deduction needs rule-based justification at all — which makes logical knowledge either non-cognitive or mystical. Which step should I attack? I want to keep the idea that logical rules aren't just syntactic habits, but I'm also not ready to say deduction's mystical. Is the slip between (5) and (6) — that a brute rule is arbitrary — or is it somehwere else? What does fixing it cost?
