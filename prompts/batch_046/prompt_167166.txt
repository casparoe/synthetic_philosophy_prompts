I have been trying to formalize an argument that chaos theory does not merely limit our predictive reach but dissolves the empirical content of determinism altogether, and I cannot locate the precise overreach. The steps are these:

(1) Determinism holds that the complete state of a system at time t, together with the dynamical laws, fixes a unique state at every other time.

(2) In any real epistemic situation, our access to the state at t is mediated by measurement with non-zero error bounded by ε.

(3) For a chaotic system, the set of initial conditions within ε of our measured value contains trajectories that diverge exponentially and, after a finite time T, occupy macroscopically distinct regions of state space.

(4) Because the actual trajectory is just one member of that ε-set, and our evidence cannot discriminate among them, any deterministic law applied to our measurement is compatible with every one of those divergent futures.

(5) A stochastic model that assigns a probability distribution over the same ε-set and evolves it according to the same Lyapunov structure yields exactly the same predictive distribution over future macrostates.

(6) Therefore, for any chaotic domain, determinism and this parameterized indeterminism are observationally equivalent: no experiment can adjudicate between them.

(7) If two theories are permanently observationally equivalent in all domains where they apply, they differ only in metaphysical ornament, not in scientific content.

(8) But measurement error is universal; even in non-chaotic systems there is an ε-set of initially indistinguishable states that eventually diverge, even if the divergence time exceeds cosmological scales.

(9) So the reasoning in (3)–(6) applies, mutatis mutandis, to every physical system.

(10) Hence determinism is either false or empirically empty across the board.

This proves far too much: it does not merely trouble determinism about weather or turbulence, but about planetary orbits and every other domain. I would most like to preserve (1), (2), and (6), because they seem to capture something genuine about chaos and evidential access. Which step should I reject? Is the flaw a hidden modal slide between “compatible with evidence” and “equally supported by evidence” in (4), or is (7) an overly strong verificationist principle that conflates predictive equivalence with theoretical identity? And if I reject (7) to save determinism’s scientific status, does that commit me to saying determinism is empirically meaningful only where chaos is absent—which seems to concede the argument for meteorology—or is there a different repair, perhaps by distinguishing predictive equivalence from explanatory equivalence?
