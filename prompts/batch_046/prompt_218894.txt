For my science-and-ideas column, I am commissioning a satirical feature: a deadpan corporate policy memo of about 600 words, issued by the fictional Office of Temporal Governance at a management consultancy. The memo should announce an “Intertemporal Preference Sovereignty Framework” that treats weakness of will—akrasia—not as moral failure but as rational hyperbolic discounting, and should redesign employee commitment devices to protect present-self authority from future-self interference. Invent one concrete bureaucratic mechanism to carry the logic, such as “reverse escrow” retirement penalties or a “present-self veto clause” that weights immediate preferences exponentially over delayed ones. The satire must stay entirely in character: exaggerate the real philosophical tension that, if akrasia collapses into time-inconsistent preference ordering, then “strengthening the will” becomes mere discount-curve flattening and autonomy dissolves into present-biased governance. The standard objections—whether normative language survives, whether commitment devices turn coercive, and whether future selves are disenfranchised—should emerge solely through euphemistic policy detail, never stated outright. It should be readable for someone unfamiliar with intertemporal bargaining or incontinence theories, yet fair and recognizable to specialists. Maintain deadpan bureaucratic consistency; do not break the frame.
