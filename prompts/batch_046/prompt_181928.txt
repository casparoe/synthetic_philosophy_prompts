I've been working through Schlick's idea that life is meaningful when it involves activity pursued for its own sake—playful absorption—rather than as a mere instrument for external ends, though I'm unsure whether the distinction survives hard cases. Here are my judgments about whether an episode or life-pattern is genuinely meaningful rather than instrumentally valuable or empty.

A musician practicing scales for a decade without ever performing, for the pleasure of finger-work and harmonic patterns: clearly meaningful. A scientist spending her career on a theory eventually falsified, dying unaware, yet describing the work as joyful obsession: meaningful. A parent raising a child solely to secure elder care, and succeeding: hollow, not genuinely meaningful despite the achieved goal. A soldier in decades of active combat without a decisive victory, reporting every battle as pure presence: meaningful. A worker enduring thirty years of hated labor to fund a leisure-filled retirement: the retirement years are meaningful, the working years are not, even though they enable the leisure. A philosopher writing an unread treatise for the pleasure of constructing arguments: meaningful.

But I keep returning to two conflicting cases. A terminally ill patient spends her final months organizing her estate so her heirs avoid bureaucratic pain; she takes no pleasure in it, yet insists she would not have it otherwise. I want to call this meaningful, yet it seems purely instrumental and painful. Conversely, a gambler spends every day in pure risk-thrill, loses everything, and harms his family; he is absorbed in play, yet to me his life is destructive, not meaningful. Is there a coherent principle—Schlick's repaired, or a different one—that captures the first six judgments and resolves this tension? State it precisely, say what it implies for unconsidered cases (for example, a monk meditating only to escape anxiety, or an artist who immediately destroys every completed work), and say honestly which of my verdicts, especially the estate-organizing patient or the gambler, must be sacrificed if simplicity is preferred.
