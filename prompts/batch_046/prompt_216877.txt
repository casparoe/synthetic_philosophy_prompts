Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them Mara and Jules—who are arguing about whether "Western values" is a coherent category at all. Mara thinks there is a real cluster here: not a universal human inheritance, but a specific historical bundle that includes things like constitutional limits on power, a certain secular framing of public reason, and an individual-rights vocabulary that actually co-evolved in specific legal traditions. Jules thinks that's just post-hoc mythmaking—he wants to say the phrase collapses incompatible things (Catholic corporatism, Scottish Enlightenment liberalism, American slaveholding republicanism, etc.) into one imaginary bloc, mostly to do boundary-policing work in immigration or culture-war debates.

Dont make either one a strawman. They should actually respond to each other. So Jules should press Mara on whether her cluster survives including Southern European corporatist traditions or the fact that many "Western" founders held explicit racial hierarchies. And Mara should push back by asking Jules whether he's missing that people who invoke the phrase are often tracking a specific institutional attitude—like a particular way of seperating religious authority from state coercion—rather than just waving a flag.

Then inject a niche twist: about halfway through, have one of them propose that "Western values" might not function like a normal descriptive category at all, but more like an inverted family-resemblance concept—where the point isn't to list shared features that members have, but to establish who gets to claim ownership of the tradition and who is excluded from it. Have them argue over whether that functional explanation saves the coherence of the phrase or just makes it more suspicious—like a membership test disguised as anthropology.

Keep it casual, like you're asking a friend to draft something for a seminar. Maybe include a typo or two.
