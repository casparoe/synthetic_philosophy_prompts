Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of historical knowledge about the distant past—particularly societies without continuous written archives—must meet, specifying the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. Your list should address at least the following: (i) how to account for the asymmetry of preservation, in which durable materials (stone, metal, ceramic) survive while perishable practices (wood, textile, oral performance) vanish, without allowing that bias to determine the historical narrative; (ii) how to treat testimony that has passed through generations of oral transmission, tracking the tension between Herodotean collection of logoi and Thucydidean insistence on autopsy, without collapsing oral tradition into either pure myth or transparent record; (iii) how to adjudicate between asymmetrical sources—such as an official state chronicle and a subaltern oral tradition—without defaulting to the victor's archive or to an uncritical relativism that renders reconciliation impossible; (iv) the conditions under which negative evidence, that is, the absence of an expected artifact or record, genuinely undermines a claim rather than merely reflecting archival silence; and (v) what consistency with modern archaeology, paleogenetics, and climatology requires, distinguishing where those sciences correct historical interpretation from where they only supplement it. For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (for example, scientific realism versus hermeneutic suspicion). Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the largest class of popular approaches—whether radical skepticism, naive archival positivism, or strong historical relativism—and briefly explain why satisfying it is conceptually costly. Present the answer in formal American English prose. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
