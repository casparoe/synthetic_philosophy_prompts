I'm a journalist who writes about science and the philosophy of science, and I've been invited to deliver the opening remarks—roughly eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Formal Boundaries: Where Proof Ends and Judgment Begins." The room is deliberately split: half are mathematicians and logicians who work on foundations, and half are legal scholars and policy analysts studying algorithmic governance and regulatory formalization. I need the remarks to do real structural work, not merely decorate the program with a famous theorem. Specifically, I want to address Gödel's incompleteness theorems with enough precision that the specialists don't wince—so I should mention the actual technical preconditions, including recursive axiomatizability, the arithmetization of syntax, omega-consistency in relation to the first theorem, and the requirement that a system be able to represent its own provability predicate for the second—while remaining intelligible to the non-mathematicians. At the same time, I must draw a sharp line around what the theorems do not demonstrate: they do not prove that human cognition transcends formal computation, that ethical reasoning is necessarily unformalizable, or that legal codes contain inherent Gödelian lacunae, since none of those domains satisfy the requisite formal conditions. Rather than stopping at debunking, I want to use that precise boundary as a pivot. The organizing claim should be analogical but disciplined: that attempts to fully formalize decision-making or regulatory systems often confuse procedural completeness with substantive adequacy, and that Gödel's result, read correctly, warns about a divergence between syntax and semantics rather than licensing mysticism or human exceptionalism. The remarks should open with this distinction, carry it through the middle as the workshop's central tension, and close by asking the two halves of the audience to collaborate—logicians to help formalists recognize the hard limits of their tools, and policy scholars to resist importing those limits as excuses for abandoning rigor altogether. Please write the remarks in polished, formal American English, appropriate for a speaker who respects both audiences without lecturing either. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not invoke specific scholars by name unless essential; and avoid verbatim quotations from real texts.
