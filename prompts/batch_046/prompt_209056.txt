I am writing the final for an upper-level seminar on epistemic logic and the philosophy of belief, open-book but without web access. I need one essay question on the surprise exam paradox that students cannot answer by reciting the standard backward-induction story or by dropping the phrase "common knowledge" as if it were an explanation. To force them into the machinery, I have constructed a variant I am calling the Graded-Surprise Announcement: the teacher states on Monday that a surprise exam will occur on exactly one day, Tuesday through Friday, but "surprise" is now defined in terms of calibrated predictive expectation rather than mere absence of belief. Specifically, each student holds subjective credences over the four days, and an exam on day D counts as surprising only if the student's credence in D on the morning of D falls below a threshold θ, where θ and the credence-updating rules are themselves common knowledge among the students. The teacher further reveals that she has already selected the day according to a constraint: she will not choose any day on which the students' reasoning, given the announcement, would push their credence in that day to or above θ.

The exam question should present this scenario and ask students to do three things in about 800 to 1000 words: first, trace the students' reasoning about Thursday and Friday under the graded condition, showing whether the backward induction still eliminates those days or merely relocates the threshold; second, determine whether the teacher's selection constraint makes the announcement self-defeating or instead produces a stable fixed point—most plausibly Wednesday—where the exam can occur and still satisfy the surprise condition; and third, distinguish sharply between surprise as a doxastic state (simply not believing the exam is that day) and surprise as an affective or attentional state (being caught off guard despite holding a non-zero, below-θ credence), explaining why conflating these two senses is the root error in both naive student reasoning and in many standard resolutions of the paradox. The answer must be constructed without citing specific authors or texts by name; paraphrase of familiar arguments is sufficient.

Please provide the exam question itself (suitable for a 45-minute essay, roughly 30 of 100 total marks), a model answer of approximately 600 words that makes the specific conceptual moves an A-grade response requires, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer—one that correctly distinguishes the two senses of surprise, traces the credence dynamics through at least Wednesday and Thursday, identifies the fixed-point condition created by the teacher's constraint, and explains why the standard resolution misses the affective/doxastic split—from a B answer, which correctly describes backward induction but treats "surprise" as a unitary concept and fails to engage the self-referential selection constraint.
