I'm working through origin essentialism for a dissertation chapter in modal biology, and I've constructed an argument that seems to collapse the view into an implausibly rigid genetic essentialism. Here is the chain:

(1) If origin essentialism holds, then for any person P there is a specific sperm S and egg E such that P could not have originated from any other gametic pair.
(2) Gametes are individuated not merely by spatiotemporal position but also by genetic constitution; a sperm carrying allele a at locus L is numerically different from one carrying allele a' at the same locus.
(3) So if P could not have come from different gametes, P could not have come from gametes with different genetic constitutions.
(4) But P's genome is causally derived from the genomes of S and E; hence if S and E had different constitutions, P's genome would differ accordingly.
(5) Therefore, in any world where P exists, P has exactly the same genome as in the actual world.
(6) If P has the same genome in every world of existence, then P could not have had brown eyes instead of blue, or been two inches taller, since those differences require genomic variation.
(7) This makes personal identity modally invariant with respect to every genetic trait, turning every phenotypic property into an essential property of the person.
(8) But that result is absurd: we routinely and coherently imagine ourselves with minor genetic differences.
(9) Therefore origin essentialism is false.

I feel like something slipped between (3) and (5), or perhaps in (2)'s account of gamete identity. I'd rather keep (1) than (8), since abandoning origin essentialism entirely costs me the metaphysical picture I'm defending, but I also don't want to pay the price of (7). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way to maintain that the origin is essential without inheriting essentiality of every genetic feature?
