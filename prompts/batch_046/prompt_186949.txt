Write the opening remarks (about ten minutes) for a workshop titled "Process Pedagogy After Predictive Text." The audience is split evenly between philosophers of education who know Whitehead intimately and high-school teachers and AI researchers who have never read him. Use Whitehead’s three-stage rhythm of education—romance, precision, and generalization—as the actual architecture of your speech, not as decoration. First, romance: open with a concrete moment of wonder about what learning becomes if large language models are treated as partners in inquiry rather than answer-machines, addressing the non-philosophers without explaining the reference. Second, precision: diagnose the specific failure mode Whitehead called "inert ideas," now visible when students submit LLM-polished work they have not tested against their own judgment; here the experts should recognize the concept, but you must make its present-day form legible to everyone else. Third, generalization: propose one concrete, non-obvious classroom practice that preserves romance while using precision tools, and honestly state what resisting that proposal costs in institutional time or assessment norms. Do not quote Whitehead verbatim, do not supply a bibliography or reading list, and do not use "process philosophy" as a label. The speech must land as an invitation to the experts and as a revelation to the rest.
