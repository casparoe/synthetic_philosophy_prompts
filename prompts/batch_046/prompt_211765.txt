I've been working through Davidson's radical interpretation stuff for my diss, and I've landed on a kind of hybrid view that I can't shake. Basically I think the principle of charity isn't a global constraint on belief attribution but works locally inside what I'd call interpretive neighborhoods — clusters of shared practical engagement where disagreement is meaningful because there's enough common ground to make error detectable. Outside those neighboorhoods you don't get incommensurable schemes, you just get noise or strategic behavior that doesn't call for interpretation at all. So the classic Davidsonian anti-relativist conclusion survives but for practical rather than rationality-based reasons. What would actually count against this? I want the arguments and cases that should move me, ranked by how fatal they'd be. For instance, is there evidence that interpretation succeeds across genuinely disjoint practical contexts — like deep cross-cultural understanding without shared practices? Or formal results showing that local charity collapses into global charity? Also, has anyone shown that the neighborhood boundary is so vague that the view just becomes unfalsifiable — and if so, is that actually a problem for a view that's supposed to be about conditions of interpretation rather than empirical psychology? I'd also like to know what Davidsonians themselves would concede counts against their view versus what they'd dismiss, and whether my practical twist saves them from those objections or just inherits them.
