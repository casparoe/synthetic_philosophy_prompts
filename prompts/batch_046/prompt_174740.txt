I hold a qualified version of the view that the Löbian obstacle prevents rational self-trusting agents from achieving full reflective stability, but that this obstacle is locally circumventable through a tiered doxastic architecture. Specifically, an agent can assign high conditional reliability to its first-order reasoning modules for empirical and mathematical claims, while assigning any modal claim about the global reliability of those modules—including the claim that if the agent believes p then p is true—to a separate, lower epistemic tier that is not imported back into the first-order system. On this view, practical self-trust is available without satisfying the premises of Löb's theorem, because the trust relation is indexed to claim-type rather than uniformly distributed across the belief hierarchy.

What would count against this position? I want the arguments and observations that should move me, ordered by how decisive they would be. In particular:

(1) Is there a formal result showing that tiered indexing collapses under iterated reflection, or that any rational closure condition forces the tiers to merge?

(2) Are there concrete models of bounded rationality in which such tier separation is unstable—for instance, agents that gradually upgrade meta-beliefs and thereby trigger the obstacle?

(3) Does the position risk being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure of self-trust is explained away by misclassification of the claim's tier, does the view predict anything that a uniform-skepticism view does not?

(4) Which concessions should a defender of this tiered view make to critics—such as about the cost of withholding global self-trust for practical decision-making—without abandoning the core claim?

Please distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones, and assess honestly whether the position is well-insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
