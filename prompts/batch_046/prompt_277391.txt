After retiring from the lab I've been reading Plato's Laws, which I never touched as a biochemist. There's this institutional design in Books I-II that feels almost like a psychology experiment: the state runs symposia where citizens drink together under supervision, with a sober symposiarch watching everyone. Plato treats wine as a pharmakon that relaxes the soul and reveals true character, so the legislator can test citizens' virtue by seeing how they behave when drunk. I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments that presupposes. Does it need a hidden true self that alcohol uncovers, or could it work with a purely behavioral or constructivist view of personality? Is Plato's tripartite soul doing the heavy lifting, or is the practice compatible with modern ideas about disinhibition and context-dependent behaviour? And if we dropped the assumption that there's a stable character behind the social mask, what would have to change about the instutition? Would it collapse as useless, or could it be redesigned as pure data-gathering? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not just "Plato was a virtue ethicist," and I want to know whether the practice strictly needs that theory or is philosophically underdetermined.
