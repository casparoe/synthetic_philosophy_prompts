I am preparing a conceptual chapter for an interdisciplinary project on causal explanation in historiography, specifically on how microbial epidemics, long-duration climate shifts, and large-scale volcanic aerosol injections can be treated as historical actors without collapsing into environmental determinism. The framing I am working with rejects both the older fallacy of reducing human societies to passive responses to temperature or pathogen curves, and the opposite tendency to treat germs, climate regimes, or volcanic winters as mere background scenery for exclusively human drama. Within this middle space—where non-human forces possess genuine explanatory weight but do not possess intentions, strategies, or moral responsibility—there remain several unresolved conceptual issues that block coherent multi-causal narration.

If you were advising a researcher with about three years to spend on the conceptual architecture rather than on empirical archival work, which open questions should be prioritized, and in what order? In particular, I want a defended agenda addressing at least these three candidates: first, the criteria for attributing causal efficacy to entities that lack agency in any intentional sense; second, the problem of temporal scale-mismatches between geological or epidemiological processes and the shorter rhythms of political decision-making; and third, the normative risk that assigning explanatory weight to environmental factors will be misread as excusing human moral or institutional failure. For each, explain whether it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is through conceptual analysis alone, and what kind of resolution—if achieved—would actually change downstream work versus merely tidying a corner. I am not asking for a survey of authors or a reading list, but for a dependency-aware prioritization of the conceptual labor that still needs doing.
