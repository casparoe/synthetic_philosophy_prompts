I'm working through Kant's absolute prohibition on lying for a graduate seminar on moral philosophy, specifically the classic case of the murderer at the door. A fellow student presented the standard defense—Kant's claim that falsehood violates perfect duty regardless of consequences—but then pushed it into territory that now strikes me as either overreaching or unstable, and I want to see where the fault line is. Here is the argument as it stands, with premises numbered.

(1) Kant holds that lying is always impermissible, even when a murderer asks after an innocent person hiding in your house, because the wrongness of falsehood is formal rather than consequential: it treats the listener's rational nature as a mere instrument to one's own ends rather than respecting it as an end in itself. (2) If the ground of the prohibition is this formal disrespect for rational autonomy—substituting the speaker's will for the listener's own rational judgment—then the wrongness should attach to any communicative act that deliberately engineers a false belief in another rational agent, not solely to explicit utterances of false propositions. (3) Consequently, truthful misleading—deliberately framing a technically accurate statement so that one foresees it will be interpreted falsely—should be morally equivalent to lying, since it produces the same subversion of rational judgment by design.

(4) Yet truthful misleading is pervasive in ordinary moral life without clear fault: a physician who answers a distressed patient's broad question about prognosis with a technically true but selectively framed remark; a host who, to spare feelings, answers "How was the dinner?" by commenting on the table setting rather than the food; or a negotiator who uses precisely worded legal language to foster a false impression about liability. (5) If we follow the formal reason in (1) consistently, we must condemn all such practices; but if we excuse them, we need a principled distinction between lying and truthful misleading that does not collapse into consequentialist reasoning about harm. (6) Moreover, if we attempt to save the distinction by saying that lying involves an explicit breach of the social contract of language while truthful misleading respects literal truth, we have introduced a conventional, institution-dependent criterion that seems incompatible with Kant's insistence that the duty of truthfulness is unconditional and grounded in pure practical reason. (7) Therefore, the Kantian prohibition on lying either proves too much—extending to ordinary decency and strategic ambiguity—or it relies on a distinction that its own formal foundation cannot support.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am inclined to hold onto (1) if possible, but I want to understand exactly what I am paying to keep it.
