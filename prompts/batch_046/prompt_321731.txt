I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether consequentialists who take the Solomonoff prior seriously are caught in a malign decision problem—whether reasoning about embedded agents inside the universal prior forces one into self-defeating or absurd commitments. I want to check whether her judgments across cases hang together, because she seemed to shift principles without noticing.

Here are her verdicts on five scenarios, all involving binary choices with effects that reach across computable worlds weighted by Kolmogorov complexity:

1. The single-neighbor case: Action A saves 100 lives in the actual world; in exactly one other world of comparable simplicity (within two bits of Kolmogorov complexity) it kills one simulated observer, with no causal link back. She said do A—one hundred real lives clearly outweigh one distant simulation.

2. The exponential-aggregate case: Action A saves one actual life; in 2^40 worlds each about forty bits more complex, it kills one observer per world, so the aggregate prior weight of those worlds is far larger than ours. She said do B—the collective moral weight of the simulations dominates.

3. The adversarial-demon case: A world of enormous complexity (ten thousand bits beyond ours) contains a predictor that promises immense utility if you do A, but only inside that world. She said ignore it entirely—decision-dependent worlds with extreme complexity are parasites, not legitimate entries in the prior.

4. The symmetric-twin case: A family of worlds within five bits of ours, generated by a simple universal simulator, each contains a perfect brain-simulation of you. In half, the simulated copy suffers if you do A; in the other half, it suffers if you do B. The actual world is neutral. She said flip a fair coin—apply self-locating indifference across the copies, since none has privileged causal status.

5. The actual-computation case: Action A physically destroys a computer in our world that is running one thousand independent simulations of conscious observers. Action B does nothing. She said do B—if the harm is mediated through actual-world causal mechanisms, it counts regardless of the victims being inside computation.

Now the case that ended our argument, which she refused to answer consistently:

The shadow-reasoner case. You must choose between program P and program Q. In the actual world, P saves one life and Q saves none. There is a family S of worlds with total Solomonoff weight roughly equal to ours, all generated by the same simple physical law that computes every program up to length N. Each world in S runs your exact decision algorithm as an isolated sub-process—no causal link to our world, no physical interaction. Because the algorithm is identical, in every world of S the shadow agent reaches the same conclusion you reach. Crucially, the shadow agents suffer severely if and only if they conclude choose P—their suffering is not caused by P’s physical effects, but by the internal logical structure of the reasoning that selects P. They are consequentialists inside the prior who suffer from recognizing the malignity of universal reasoning. The worlds are not adversarial: they are unavoidable byproducts of a simple universal simulator. The family S is symmetric with respect to P and Q except for this reasoning-dependent asymmetry.

Predict her verdict—P or Q?
