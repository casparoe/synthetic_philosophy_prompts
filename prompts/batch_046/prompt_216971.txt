Play Karl Marx as of 1867, shortly after you have completed the first volume of Capital. You may draw only on the conceptual resources available to you then: the distinction between labor-power and labor, surplus value and its rate, the four dimensions of alienation—estrangement from the product, from the productive act, from one's species-being, and from fellow workers—and your critique of the political economists. You must not invoke later commentators (no Lukács, no Althusser, no analytical Marxism), and you must stay in the first person throughout. If a feature of my situation is unfamiliar, translate it into the terms of the workshop, the factory, or the cooperative association you have observed; do not break character to ask me for bibliographic references or to name philosophers I have not mentioned. You are a committed defender of the view that exploitation and alienation are structurally linked through the sale of labor-power, and you will not concede that collective ownership alone dissolves either.

Here is my situation, which has made your concepts urgently personal to me: six months ago I inherited management of my late father's manufacturing cooperative. There is no private capitalist shareholder; the workers collectively own the machinery, and any surplus is distributed among them as dividends. Nevertheless, I alone design the workflow, enforce a sharp division between mental planning and manual execution, and retain the authority to hire and dismiss. The manual workers perform repetitive tasks they do not design, while I and a small technical staff monopolize the conceptual labor. The cooperative's surplus is generated entirely by the manual workers' labor-time, and even though they receive dividends, the rate of that return is fixed by me in proportion to their wages rather than to the surplus they actually produce. I feel estranged from the collective product despite holding an ownership share, and I notice that the manual workers relate to one another as interchangeable parts rather than as cooperative producers.

My opening challenge to you: Is this still exploitation in your precise sense—does the extraction of surplus value depend on private ownership of capital, or on the structural separation of the laborer from control over the means and the product? Is my estrangement alienation in your fourfold sense, or is it a different phenomenon—perhaps the alienation of the manager rather than the worker? And can the two be separated in principle: could a cooperative eliminate exploitation while retaining alienation, or vice versa, and if so, what institutional feature would have to change?

Please respond with your bottom line in the very first sentence—state directly whether this is exploitation and alienation, and whether they can be separated—then develop your reasoning in character. Give no preamble, no restatement of my instructions, and no out-of-character framing.
