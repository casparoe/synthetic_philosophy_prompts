At our philosophy seminar this week, two colleagues—Mira and Jonas—became deadlocked over how rational agents ought to behave in two classic decision puzzles, and they even constructed a hybrid case that neither of us had encountered before. Mira began by outlining what she called the “Platonia structure”: a transparent box holds £1,000, whilst an opaque box either contains £1,000,000 or nothing, with the contents fixed in advance by a predictor that scans your mental state with 99 per cent accuracy. The predictor fills the opaque box only if it anticipates that you will take that box alone; if you take both, you receive only the £1,000 in the transparent box. Mira argued that causal decision theory clearly recommends taking both boxes, since your present choice cannot causally affect what is already inside the opaque one; the correlation with the predictor’s forecast is merely evidential, and rationality should track causal efficacy, not any spurious backward influence.

Jonas replied that this misses the point of evidential reasoning: if you are genuinely the sort of agent who takes both, the predictor almost certainly foresaw that and left the large box empty, so two-boxing yields only £1,000, whereas one-boxing yields £1,000,000. He insisted that rationality requires adopting policies that are evidentially self-supporting, especially when the predictor is embedded in the causal architecture of the world. Mira then shifted to the “Luring Lottery”, describing a strategic interaction between two perfectly rational duplicates: each may either enter or abstain; if both enter they each lose £100, if one enters alone he gains £1,000, and if neither enters they gain nothing. She claimed this exposes the danger of Jonas’s evidential approach—if both agents apply symmetric evidential reasoning, they might both enter and suffer the worst outcome, demonstrating that superrational coherence can be exploited. Jonas countered that the Luring Lottery involves genuine strategic interaction with a duplicate, whereas Platonia involves a single agent facing a predictor, so the two cases are structurally disanalogous. He then proposed their hybrid “Reflective Lure”: imagine the Platonia predictor conditions its forecast not only on your choice in that scenario, but on whether you apply evidential reasoning consistently across both puzzles—including whether you would avoid entering the Luring Lottery. Mira argued that this hybrid actually strengthens her position: adopting a stable one-boxing policy demonstrates the rational coherence that the predictor rewards, and predictability is only harmful in adversarial contexts, not in predictive ones. Jonas retorted that Mira is now defining rationality circularly—whatever avoids the worst constructed outcome—rather than tracking genuine causal or strategic principles, and he warned that if rational agents must be predictable to predictors, they become systematically exploitable in any strategic setting.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Mira’s initial causal argument in Platonia, Jonas’s evidential rebuttal, Mira’s use of the Luring Lottery against evidential reasoning, Jonas’s disanalogy claim, and finally their dispute over the “Reflective Lure” hybrid. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly sound but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration—perhaps concerning the distinction between evidential and causal reasoning in predictive versus strategic environments—both of them have overlooked. Please write your answer in British English.
