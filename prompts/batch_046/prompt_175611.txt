I keep encountering a particular argumentative structure in discussions of whether present-day citizens can bear responsibility for historical injustices committed by prior generations of their state. The pattern runs like this: first, it is insisted that the nation-state is a continuous institutional agent—its legal personality, its treasury, its territorial jurisdiction persist—so that the state itself can inherit a form of guilt that outlives any individual wrongdoer. Second, when asked whether this means any living citizen is personally guilty, the answer is an emphatic no: moral guilt, on this view, is strictly non-transferable and dies with the agent. Third, when asked why citizens are nevertheless obligated to contribute to reparative measures, the reply is that the obligation belongs to the guilty state and is discharged through its members, not because the members are blameworthy but because citizenship is the mechanism by which the state's duties are executed. The result is a guilt that is strong enough to generate binding, enforceable obligations for current members, yet too weak—indeed, explicitly denied—to constitute any form of personal or shared culpability among them. This is not quite Jaspers’s familiar distinction between political and moral guilt, since Jaspers treated political guilt as genuinely shared by citizens in virtue of their membership; here, membership is said to be merely the conduit for an obligation that attaches to an abstract institutional entity, leaving the citizens themselves in a kind of liability-without-culpability position. Nor is it straightforward collective responsibility in the usual philosophical sense, because the collective is not being held responsible for its members’ actions but for its own continuous existence. Is there an established concept or literature for this exact configuration—institutional guilt mediated by membership but decoupled from personal blame—and if not, how should we delineate its boundaries against Jaspers’s taxonomy, against social-connection accounts of collective responsibility, and against standard theories of inherited state obligations?
