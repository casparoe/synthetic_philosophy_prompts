In a recent discussion about regulating digital platforms, a colleague argued that targeted institutional reform is conceptually naive because, on a Foucauldian view, power is everywhere and comes from everywhere: if power is genuinely diffuse and relational, then fixing one site—say, a social-media company—merely displaces it rather than reducing it. I suspect this conflates several different claims hiding inside the slogan, but I want to be precise. Could you lay out the distinct, precisely formulated theses that the idea summed up as power being everywhere and coming from everywhere might express? I am particularly interested in distinctions between: (i) an ontological claim about what power is (for instance, relational rather than possessed); (ii) an empirical claim about its distribution across social relations; (iii) a claim about its productivity, or about its entanglement with knowledge and discipline; and (iv) any normative implication for resistance or reform. For each thesis, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or irrelevant to my colleague’s conclusion that local regulation is futile. Please present the theses as a numbered menu, with a brief verdict on plausibility and on whether it actually supports the dismissal of targeted intervention. Finally, end with a single line beginning Verdict: that identifies which reading, if any, genuinely underwrites the argument against reform, or write none if the slogan carries no such weight.
