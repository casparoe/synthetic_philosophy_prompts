Draft the joint statement that two opposing camps within care ethics could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Camp A defends the view that care is irreducibly particular: moral value arises from direct, responsive attention to specific others, and any formalization of care obligations into algorithmic or distributive rules necessarily erodes the relational quality that makes care ethical. Camp B defends the view that care ethics must be universalizable: without explicit principles for distributing attention and resources across populations, care collapses into arbitrary partiality that can justify neglect of distant or unpopular dependents, and algorithmic tools can be ethically integrated if they are designed to extend rather than replace human responsiveness.

The collaboration should focus on a concrete constructed scenario rather than abstract principle alone: a predictive allocation system in eldercare that ranks patients by a composite "connection need" score derived from biometric and interaction data, then distributes limited human caregiver hours to maximize aggregate emotional-attunement metrics. Both camps agree this scenario is technologically plausible within current affective-computing limits and ethically urgent.

Structure the statement as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the phenomenology of attentive care, the empirical unreliability of current affective-computing proxies for emotional attunement, and the moral risks of substituting algorithmic ranking for human judgment. Second, a disagreements table: for each camp, the precise formulation of its core claim in wording that camp would accept, with the disagreement explicitly located—specifically, whether quantifiable "attention" retains moral status as care, and whether partiality toward specific relations is a non-negotiable feature or a correctable bias. Third, the cruxes: identify at least two sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp. Suggested cruxes include whether scaling care requires abandoning particularity, and whether an algorithm can serve as a transparent instrument of human care rather than its replacement. Fourth, for each crux, present one concrete case or argument both camps accept bears on it—such as a comparison between algorithmically triaged eldercare and informal family-based triage in resource-scarce settings—along with each camp's explicit prediction about what the case demonstrates. Fifth, a brief concluding assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—e.g., about the definition of "care" versus "service delivery"—and, if so, which portion and why.

The prompt should be self-contained, requiring no external citations, bibliographies, or specialized literature beyond general familiarity with care-ethical reasoning. Write in a formal, polished register.
