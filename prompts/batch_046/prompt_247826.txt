Write a dialogue between Dr. Lena Voss and Dr. Tomás Reeves, both philosophers of medical ethics, discussing whether valid consent can be decomposed into domain-specific compartments rather than treated as a single holistic authorisation. Voss defends a framework she calls "compartmentalised authorisation": consent is a structured vector across distinct normative domains—somatic, informational, and institutional—so that deception or incompetence in one domain invalidates only the corresponding component unless the agent's values explicitly link the domains. Reeves argues for holistic integrity: consent is a unified normative transfer that takes place within a specific relational and institutional context, meaning deception about funding, data-sharing, or institutional embedding contaminates the entire act regardless of whether the agent cares about those details. They should examine a concrete case: a research participant, M, with domain-specific cognitive limitations, consents to a neurostimulation trial after truthful clinical disclosure but is deceived about a defence-sector grant and about anonymised data-sharing with a third party. Voss should claim that M's somatic authorisation remains valid because the deception is confined to the institutional compartment and does not affect clinical risk disclosure; Reeves should reply that the relational conditions of trust are falsified, and that competence must cover the institutional embedding of the act, not merely its bodily mechanics. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: Voss should respond to Reeves's "poisoning" metaphor by demanding which specific feature is constitutive for M, and Reeves should answer that normative authority, not subjective preference, determines constitutiveness. They should also address coercion—whether institutional pressure (e.g., rapid enrolment demands tied to the grant) seeps into the somatic compartment—and conclude without either conceding entirely, but with each clarifying what empirical or normative evidence would change their view. Use British spelling and maintain a formal, polished register throughout. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to obscure literature; build the argument from the constructed framework and the case of M alone.
