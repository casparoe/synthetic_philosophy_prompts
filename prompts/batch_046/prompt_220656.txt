I'm working through my own judgments about democratic membership and want to see whether they cohere under a single principle or whether some must be abandoned. Here are my concrete verdicts.

First, a citizen who emigrated at age twenty-five, has lived abroad for forty-five years, holds dual nationality, pays no taxes to her state of origin, and follows its politics only sporadically. I think she should not vote in that state's national elections.

Second, a permanent resident who arrived at age eight, is now sixty, never naturalized due only to bureaucratic barriers, served in the military, and has paid taxes continuously. I think he should hold full voting rights.

Third, a sixteen-year-old who works full-time, pays income tax, and demonstrates a sophisticated grasp of current policy debates. I think she should vote.

Fourth, people who will be born in thirty years and will live with the climate consequences of today's carbon policies. I think they should be represented in present decision-making, though not through direct ballots, but through institutional mechanisms that weight long-term impacts.

Fifth, members of an indigenous nation whose traditional territory spans an international border; they belong to the nation's own government and also hold citizenship in one of the bordering states. I think they should be able to vote in both systems for matters that affect them, without being forced to choose one membership.

Sixth, a case I keep reversing: a permanent treaty-based deliberative council, composed of scientists and local stakeholders from three different countries, governs a shared transboundary watershed. Its members are not citizens of any of the three states in the ordinary sense, yet the council's binding decisions affect all residents of those states. For elections that select representatives specifically to oversee water policy in each state, I feel these council members ought to have voting rights; but I also feel that democratic membership should not extend to non-citizen experts solely because they administer a shared resource.

Is there one account—stake-based, all-subjected, affected-interests, consent-based, or a hybrid—that delivers all six verdicts without strain? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the best available principle predict for a case I have not listed: temporary residents who stay exactly one year, are permanently excluded thereafter, but are heavily affected by the state's labor and housing regulations during that year?
