I want a fully runnable in-class simulation, not a lecture, that makes participants feel the actual pull of heterophenomenology: treating first-person reports as interpretable texts rather than as authoritative windows onto private experience. Split the group into "heterophenomenological interpreters" and "phenomenal-access defenders," assign printed reports with staged contradictions, and require both sides to produce a joint decision about whether to include "reversed-flow experience" in an official scientific record, knowing they have no independent access to any inner state. The rules and incentive structure—using only social reputation points, not real money—should be arranged so that the interpreters can build a predictive, behaviorally coherent model only by treating certain first-person corrections as confabulation or noise, while the defenders discover that insisting on direct phenomenal access collapses into an untestable private-language claim.

Please include a minute-by-minute timeline, explicit role descriptions, the debrief that maps each critical moment onto Dennett's interpretive stance and its bracketing of ontological commitment to raw qualia, and an honest admission of which feature of heterophenomenology—such as the long-term, iterative refinement of theory across actual empirical studies—the simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Also warn me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely students conflating the methodological bracketing with outright skepticism about consciousness itself—and how to correct that in the moment. Keep all materials printable on standard paper; do not ask students to look up citations or bibliographies, and avoid referring to specific published passages that I would need to verify.
