You serve as a senior evaluation officer at a multilateral development bank. Each quarter you review institutional-intervention proposals that justify large-scale funding by invoking supposed “laws of history”—for example, claims that societies necessarily pass through fixed stages of modernization, that democratic consolidation becomes irreversible once specific economic and electoral thresholds are crossed, or that statistical patterns extracted from centuries of regime data constitute predictive laws of political development. Your board requires you to flag historicist reasoning before funding is approved, but you have only about two hours per proposal, no dedicated philosophy staff, and must produce a written justification that a non-specialist director can audit. Drawing specifically on Popper’s critique of historicism—particularly his distinction between conditional trends (which depend on specific initial conditions and can be disrupted) and unconditional universal laws, and his argument that the growth of human knowledge makes long-range historical prediction in principle impossible—design a reusable decision procedure your office can apply to any proposal. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions with clear stop/no-stop rules, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, identify the exact points where evaluation must be handed to the board because it turns on normative value judgments rather than factual or logical checks, and include explicit instructions for treating machine-learning forecasts or large-scale statistical regularities that are presented as if they were historical laws.

Please structure your response exactly as follows, with approximate lengths indicated:

1. Practitioner context and stakes (about 120 words): briefly restate the office’s time and expertise constraints and why Popper’s trend/law distinction is operationally decisive here.

2. The decision procedure (about 350 words): present the ordered checklist in plain language a non-philosopher can run. For each step, state the question, what evidence would count as a sufficient “yes” or “no,” the stop rule (approve, reject, or escalate), and the specific misfire modes (e.g., a proposal disguising a statistical correlation as a necessary stage).

3. Worked applications (about 500 words): apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a claim that once a country reaches roughly $15,000 GDP per capita with fifteen continuous years of multiparty elections, authoritarian reversal is historically impossible; (b) a machine-learning model trained on 200 years of regime-type sequences that forecasts democratic backsliding in a specific region and is offered as a “law-governed” prediction; (c) a literacy-funding proposal arguing that because literacy rates and industrialization have moved together across historical cases, funding literacy alone will necessarily trigger industrialization. Include at least one case where the procedure returns “approve with caution” but the reasoning still feels suspicious to you—explain precisely what the procedure fails to catch.

4. Limits and honest misfires (about 200 words): state clearly what the procedure cannot do, which kinds of legitimate historical reasoning it might wrongly flag, and where human judgment is irreplaceable.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not ask for quotations from specific texts; paraphrase any philosophical claims in your own words.
