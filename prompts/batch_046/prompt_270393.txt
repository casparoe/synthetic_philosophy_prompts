I am working through some metaphysics of mind for an advanced reading course, and I keep returning to a line of reasoning about panpsychism that my study group found compelling but that I worry proves too much. I would like you, acting as a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in the combination problem and constitutive explanation, to examine the argument carefully. Before giving your verdict, please begin with some preliminary brainstorming about how composition relations might behave differently for phenomenal properties than for physical ones, and then work through the steps systematically.

Here is the argument as it was presented to me:

(1) Panpsychism holds that macro-consciousness is not an emergent novelty but is constituted by the consciousness of fundamental entities.
(2) If X is constituted by Y, then any feature of X that is not present in Y must be explained by the relations among the Ys, not by X alone.
(3) Macro-consciousness possesses a feature we might call phenomenal transparency of access: the subject can, in principle, introspectively access the full structural layout of its experience as an integrated whole.
(4) No micro-subject—whether at the level of neurons, molecules, or quantum fields—possesses this transparency; each is, by definition, a minimal point of view with no internal parts to access.
(5) Therefore, if panpsychism is correct, the transparency of macro-access must arise purely from the relational organization of micro-subjects that individually lack it.
(6) But relational organization among subjects that lack internal access cannot generate access to the organization itself: a collection of blind spots arranged in a pattern is still a collection of blind spots, not a seeing eye.
(7) Hence panpsychism cannot explain macro-consciousness without attributing transparency to the micro-subjects, which collapses the micro/macro distinction.
(8) If that distinction collapses, then either every particle is as conscious as I am (making the view absurd) or there is no special problem of macro-consciousness to solve, which undermines panpsychism's original motivation.
(9) So panpsychism either fails to explain what it promises or dissolves into the claim that consciousness is uniformly present at every scale with identical structural features.

My intuition is that something slips between (5) and (6)—perhaps an equivocation about what "access" means when distributed across subjects, or a hidden modal slide about what relations can generate—but I cannot pinpoint it. Is the argument valid? If so, which premise is least costly to abandon, and does rejecting it require giving up on constitutive explanation altogether, or can one accept constitution while denying that the constituted feature must be transparent to the parts? Please think through the alternatives carefully before delivering your diagnosis, and be explicit about what each option costs for the broader panpsychist project.
