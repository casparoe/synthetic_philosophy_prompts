Consider the well-known case of Jahi McMath, the adolescent patient declared brain-dead in California in 2013 following complications from a tonsillectomy, whose family subsequently relocated her to New Jersey, where statutory religious exemptions allow rejection of brain-death determination in favor of circulatory criteria. Analyze this episode through the conceptual distinction between brain death and circulatory death, treating the two not merely as clinical protocols but as competing metaphysical accounts of when a human being has died. I want a disciplined mapping: identify the relevant decision-makers (the treating physicians, the family, the state medical boards, and the patient herself), specify what options and payoffs each faced, and articulate precisely what the integrated-function and higher-brain formulations of brain death say about McMath's biological status during the period she was maintained on ventilatory support in New Jersey. Then tell me where the theory is silent—does it provide any resources for adjudicating between California's authority to declare death and New Jersey's permission to withhold that declaration?—and what the episode reveals about the theory's own commitments. In particular, examine whether the divergence between states produces a genuine ontological pluralism, in which the same body is dead in one jurisdiction and alive in another, or whether it reveals only a legal pluralism that a consistent biological theory of death should treat as jurisdictional error; and assess whether that distinction between ontological and legal pluralism collapses once we recognize that death determination is always mediated by institutional criteria. Conclude by saying what this case shows about whether brain death is best understood as a natural-kind boundary that medicine discovers, or as a normative decision that medicine and law jointly construct.
