I am working on a conceptual framework for classifying cognitive architectures—biological and artificial—according to how they generate adaptive behavior, and I want the full specification of what such a taxonomy must achieve to be adequate rather than merely descriptive. Taking as my point of departure Dennett's well-known division of minds into Darwinian creatures (hard-wired repertoires with selection over variants), Skinnerian creatures (reinforcement-shaped behavior), Popperian creatures (capable of internal hypothesis testing and representational failure in place of physical harm), and Gregorian creatures (whose cognition is amplified by culturally transmitted tools and structured representations), I need the complete list of desiderata that any theory in this space must satisfy. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual failure mode it prevents: for instance, what goes wrong if a taxonomy collapses internal model-based selection into sophisticated conditioning, or if it treats cultural transmission as merely accelerated learning? Indicate clearly which conditions are non-negotiable—such as consistency with evolutionary continuity and with the empirical limits of associative learning in non-linguistic animals—and which reflect camp-specific preferences, like whether explicit symbolic representation is required for Gregorian status or whether distributed neural encodings can suffice.

I am especially interested in whether a subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: is there a tension, analogous to a no-go result, between demanding evolutionary gradation among the four kinds and simultaneously requiring sharp representational boundaries, particularly at the Popperian-to-Gregorian transition? Keep the focus on the criteria and their justifications rather than on scoring particular frameworks, though if one prominent account clearly fails a core requirement, mention it briefly. Finally, distinguish what is settled about these categories from what is genuinely contested—such as whether Gregorian cognition necessarily involves language, or whether artificial systems can instantiate Popperian dynamics without biological embodiment—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists.
