Assume the role of an experienced interviewer and conceptual analyst preparing for a philosophy-and-history podcast. Your guest is an invented historian, Dr. Amara Osei, who defends a specific, refined version of the invention-of-tradition and imagined-communities literatures. Her core thesis is that nations are not merely symbolic or cognitive constructs but specifically archival communities: their durability depends on state-controlled mnemonic infrastructure—standardized census regimes, cartographic normalization, and centralized record-keeping—without which even strong linguistic or ethnic solidarity rarely consolidates into a durable nation-state. She applies this framework to explain the failure of several postcolonial pan-ethnic movements that enjoyed widespread popular identification but lacked autonomous bureaucratic archives.

Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm: identify the two or three strongest objections that scholars working in the Hobsbawm and Anderson traditions would actually press against her, and note where she is likely to deflect—perhaps by claiming that oral cultures maintain their own archives, or that her claim applies only to modern nation-states rather than all collective identity. Your questions should anticipate these deflections and turn them into pressure points rather than escape routes.

Now write a polished, numbered sequence of eight to ten interview questions for a serious colloquium-style conversation. Do not include generic biographical questions. Each entry must contain a primary question and, clearly marked, a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The sequence must cover the following angles:

- The boundary between her archival claim and Anderson’s idea of imagined communities: ask whether she takes symbolic imagination to be causally inert without bureaucratic archives, or merely insufficient, and specify what empirical case would distinguish these two positions.
- A concrete historical counter-example—such as a pre-modern polity or an anti-colonial movement that achieved durable national consciousness despite lacking modern state archives—followed by her probable reply (for instance, invoking religious institutions or oral tradition as substitute infrastructures) and then your follow-up testing whether that reply saves the view or renders it unfalsifiable.
- The normative implication for compatriot partiality: if a nation’s coherence depends on state-controlled mnemonic systems, does this strengthen cosmopolitan arguments against special obligations to compatriots, or does it merely redescribe the moral problem?
- The predictive stakes of her view for digital-era transformations: could decentralized identity systems or social-media archives substitute for state mnemonic infrastructure and thereby allow new, non-territorial forms of nationhood?
- A direct challenge about falsifiability: what specific historical or sociological finding—if any—would lead her to abandon the claim that mnemonic infrastructure is necessary rather than merely typical?

Maintain a formal, intellectual tone appropriate for an academic podcast. Think carefully about how each follow-up tightens the pressure if she offers her standard reply. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Present the final output as the sequence itself, with each question and its follow-up clearly labeled.
