In discussions of climate change, factory farming, and democratic voting, I keep encountering the same analogical move: an individual’s contribution is said to be like a single drop of water falling into a bucket that is already overflowing. The bucket does not fill because of that drop; had the drop never fallen, the overflow would have occurred just the same; therefore the drop is causally redundant and, by extension, morally inert. In my course on collective action, this image is used to license what is called the inefficacy objection—that because my carbon footprint, my ballot, or my purchase of animal products does not alter the aggregate outcome, I lack any moral reason to change my behavior. I want to press on whether the analogy actually does the argumentative work being asked of it.

Could you lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the bucket, the drop, the overflow, the measuring observer, and the causal law linking them? Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures: does the bucket model treat harm as purely linear and quantitative, thereby smuggling in the assumption that collective damage is just the sum of independent parts? How does the analogy handle discontinuous or threshold effects—cases where a single additional unit tips a system from stable to catastrophic, or where individual actions function not as direct causes but as nodes in a signaling or institutional structure? I am especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports a view about the value of the whole versus the parts—say, that only the aggregate state matters and that the distributive pattern of contributions is normatively invisible. Once these disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that individual action is morally pointless still stand, or does it turn out to have been carried almost entirely by the bucket image? Finally, if the analogy collapses under scrutiny, is there a more accurate substitute—perhaps one that treats collective harm as a woven structure in which each thread maintains tension—capable of preserving the intuition about distributed causation without licensing the inefficacy conclusion?
