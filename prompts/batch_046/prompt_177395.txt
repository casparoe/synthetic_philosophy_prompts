I am preparing an essay for my philosophy-of-mind course in which I defend Dennett's multiple-drafts model of consciousness against the Cartesian-theater picture, taking the central claim to be that there is no single spatiotemporal point at which unconscious processes become a unified conscious display for an internal observer. I want to understand what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the standard objections, but the observations, arguments, and cross-domain commitments that expose it to risk. In particular, I am curious about whether predictive-processing frameworks in cognitive neuroscience, which sometimes posit a global workspace or singular prediction-error resolution mechanism for reportable content, pose a structural challenge to distributed drafts, or whether the model can absorb them. Beyond that, please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which empirical findings (for example, about split-brain integration or about artificial neural architectures with genuinely distributed representations lacking central integration), which first-person intuitions about the unity of experience, and which commitments in other domains (say, about belief persistence or about the nature of scientific explanation) a committed multiple-drafts theorist would concede as damaging versus dismiss as question-begging? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind.
