Act as a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology and the ethics of blame. Before giving your audit, briefly list my five commitments and flag which pairs are in tension. I just finished a long argument with a friend about standing to blame and hypocrisy, and I want to know whether my own position is consistent or whether I’m smuggling in incompatible motivations. Here is where I’ve landed:

(1) I think hypocrisy undermines standing, but not because blame requires moral perfection; rather, it fails a counterfactual consistency test—if I wouldn’t refrain from X in your circumstances, I lack the standing to blame you for X.

(2) I want to extend this beyond direct hypocrisy to structural complicity: if I benefit from a systemic wrong I condemn (e.g., supply-chain abuses or carbon-intensive practices) and fail to take reasonable steps to extricate myself, I also forfeit standing, even if I never committed the discrete wrong myself.

(3) At the same time, I think standing is scalar and can be regained: a reformed wrongdoer can recover it, but only if the reform tracks the right reasons (genuine moral recognition, not social pressure), and the degree recovered depends on “moral proximity”—how structurally similar their past situation was to the target’s present one.

(4) I also think that if we make standing depend on actual moral purity or complete extrication from all complicity, then in conditions of pervasive systemic injustice almost nobody can blame anyone, which is a reductio I want to avoid.

(5) Finally, I think the normative framework here shouldn’t depend on when I happen to discover my own inconsistencies: if I learn I’m hypocritical after issuing blame, that retroactively undermines my standing, but this shouldn’t vary with whether I discovered it before or after I spoke—it’s a fact about my position, not my epistemic access.

Does this hang together? I’m especially worried that (2) expands standing-loss into structural conditions that are nearly unavoidable, while (3) imposes fine-grained psychological conditions for restoration (right-reason reform, moral proximity) that seem impossible to verify in structural cases. Meanwhile, (4) warns against over-demanding conditions, yet (3) looks demanding. Is (5) in tension with (1), or is it just an epistemic gloss on the same counterfactual property? Order the tensions by severity, say exactly which commitment must give if we want consistency, and note the cheapest repair and its cost. Please separate what is well-established in the literature on blame and hypocrisy from what is genuinely contested or depends on a non-standard interpretation of counterfactual consistency, moral proximity, or discovery-order independence. Do not present any contested view as the consensus.
