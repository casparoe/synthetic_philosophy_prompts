I am trying to formulate a transcendental argument that the very possibility of recognizing one's own representational errors requires a non-derivative first-person authority over mental content. The reasoning is broadly Kantian: if error-correction is possible at all, there must be a distinction between how things seem and how they are, and this distinction requires that some representations are immediately given to a single subject rather than inferred or distributed across subsystems. But I am concerned that the argument, if sound, proves too much: it appears to entail that any system that corrects errors through distributed cross-checking—without a central integrator—is conceptually impossible, which feels like an empirical claim about cognitive architecture smuggled into a conceptual proof. Is the mistake in assuming that transcendental conditions can dictate the structural architecture of error-correcting systems in general, or should the argument be restricted to human cognition in a way that avoids this overreach without collapsing into mere empirical psychology?
