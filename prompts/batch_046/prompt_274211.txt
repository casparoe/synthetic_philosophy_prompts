I am currently taking an upper-level seminar in philosophy of religion, and I am drafting an essay on Wittgenstein's account of religious belief. Rather than rehearsing the standard non-cognitivist interpretation—that religious utterances express attitude rather than assert factual claims—I want to defend a more structural reading. On this view, the grammatical autonomy Wittgenstein assigns to religious language-games is not merely local or disciplinary; it has an architectonic function. Religious pictures operate as scaffolds that establish the conditions of intelligibility for certain moral and existential judgments, analogously—though not identically—to the way transcendental structures frame experience in Kant, except that Wittgenstein offers no corresponding transcendental deduction and insists that these conditions are internal to practices rather than legislated by reason.

My argument draws on his observation that religious belief is not a hypothesis evaluated against empirical evidence but a framework governing a form of life. I extend this by proposing that such frameworks possess cross-domain structural authority: they do not only organize ritual and devotional discourse but also set grammatical preconditions for particular kinds of normative reasoning outside strictly theological contexts. This allows me to claim that religious disagreement is not best modeled as a conflict over competing hypotheses, but as a clash between incommensurable architectonic structures—each internally coherent yet lacking a neutral tribunal for arbitration.

I am concerned, however, that this reading may distort Wittgenstein's resolutely anti-theoretical stance by importing Kantian machinery he would reject. I also worry that emphasizing structural authority risks collapsing his position into a strong fideism that renders inter-religious disagreement conceptually impossible rather than practically intractable.

Could you comment on whether this reasoning holds together? Specifically, I would value feedback on whether the architectonic analogy illegitimately systematizes Wittgenstein's notion of grammatical autonomy, and on whether there is a principled distinction—within his philosophy—between legitimate grammatical independence and mere epistemic insulation. I am not seeking a bibliography or external reading suggestions; I want to assess the coherence of the argument on its own terms.
