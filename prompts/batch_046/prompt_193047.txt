I am leading a seminar on Kant’s moral philosophy, and a student has begun invoking the phrase ‘What if everyone did that?’ as a blanket objection whenever a maxim seems to license an exception for the speaker—whether that means breaking a promise for minor convenience, free-riding on a collective effort, or adopting a practice that only succeeds if others refrain from it. Rather than dismiss the intuition outright, I want the class to see exactly what philosophical work the slogan might be doing and where it collapses distinct arguments into a vague gesture. Can you lay out the different precise principles that ‘What if everyone did that?’ might express within Kant’s universalizability tests—distinguishing, for example, between universalization of a maxim versus universalization of an action; between a logical contradiction in conception and a practical contradiction in will; between applying the test to all rational beings, to all humans in analogous circumstances, or only to agents with identical preferences; and between requiring mere logical possibility of universal adoption versus requiring that universal adoption be actually willed without self-defeat? For each reading, please indicate whether it is a plausible interpretation of Kant, whether it is trivial or substantive, and whether it genuinely supports the student’s move of blocking an exception in the cases raised. I am especially interested in situations where universal adoption produces a bad collective outcome—such as universal stagnation or universal defection—since the student seemed to treat that consequence as if it were a contradiction in conception, and I want to know whether that reading is faithful to Kant or whether it smuggles in an unacknowledged consequentialist or empirical premise about actual behavior.

Please state how confident you are in each of these distinctions and identify the specific point about which you are least sure—particularly regarding whether Kant’s contradiction-in-will formulation genuinely captures the moral intuition behind the slogan, or whether the slogan depends on a hidden prediction about what people would in fact do if everyone adopted the maxim.
