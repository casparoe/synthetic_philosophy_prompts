You are an expert in the philosophy of social science and in the metaphysics of causation, with particular familiarity with Weber's methodology, Durkheimian social ontology, and contemporary debates about mechanistic explanation and mental causation. Before responding, take a moment to map the conceptual terrain: distinguish Weber's logical apparatus (ideal types, adequate causation, elective affinity) from empirical causal claims, and separate questions about the ontology of culture from questions about the logic of explanation.

Here is the difficulty I am trying to resolve. Weber's study of the Protestant ethic is standardly read as claiming that Calvinist religious values played a causal role in the emergence of modern capitalist conduct. If we take this as a literal causal statement, however, it seems to run into a metaphysical obstacle. Cultural values—such as the anxiety produced by the doctrine of predestination—are not spatiotemporally localized events; they are normative orientations or collective representations. In the philosophy of causation, efficient causes are typically either particular events or structural conditions that make a difference under intervention. A value-orientation does not seem to fit either category, since it lacks physical location and does not enter into physical interactions with labor practices or investment behavior.

One might try to save the causal reading by reducing values to distributed individual psychological states, each of which then causes its bearer to act rationally. But this seems to violate Weber's own insistence on the irreducibility of meaning-contexts and his rejection of psychologism in sociology. The alternative is to treat culture as exercising downward causation: a non-physical collective representation constrains the physical practices of economic life. Yet this immediately raises the interaction problem familiar from philosophy of mind—how can a non-physical cultural state influence physical behavior without violating the causal closure of the physical domain? The exclusion argument developed for mental causation seems to apply here with equal force: if every physical action already has a sufficient physical cause, there is no causal work left for the value-state to do.

Some interpreters resolve the tension by denying that Weber was offering causal explanation at all, and saying he was providing only interpretive understanding of subjective meanings. But this resolution strikes me as too quick. If values are not causes in any robust sense, then Weber's thesis collapses into a bare correlation: capitalism happened to develop in regions where Protestantism was present, and actors subjectively interpreted their economic behavior in religious terms. That does not explain why similar material conditions elsewhere failed to produce capitalism, nor does it account for the directional influence Weber claims to identify.

So I want to ask: Was Weber implicitly committed to a metaphysical picture of downward causation—perhaps closer to Durkheim's collective representations—despite his methodological individualism? Or is there a coherent third option, perhaps drawing on recent accounts of social mechanism sketches, in which values function not as efficient causes but as structural causes that organize the space of possible actions without themselves being physical events? And crucially, if we take that third option, does it genuinely avoid the exclusion argument, or does it merely relocate the interaction problem from individual mental states to the ontology of social structures? Please work through the options carefully, identifying and correcting any false presupposition in my framing, and say what survives once the confusion is cleared away.
