I am writing a dissertation chapter on probabilistic semantics for indicative conditionals, and in trying to defend a restricted version of Adams's thesis against Lewis's triviality results I have constructed an argument that seems to prove far more than I intend—indeed, it appears to undermine Bayesian epistemology altogether. I am struggling to identify the precise misstep.

(1) Let us adopt Adams's thesis in its standard form: for any indicative conditional "If A, then B", a rational agent's credence in it should equal the conditional probability P(B|A). (2) Lewis's triviality theorem demonstrates that if this identity is satisfied for all pairs of propositions within a language closed under boolean combination, then for any contingent propositions X and Y with probabilities strictly between 0 and 1, P(X|Y) equals P(X) unless X and Y are already probabilistically dependent in a way fixed by the prior. (3) Consequently, if Adams's thesis holds universally, then conditioning on any empirical observation Y can never rationally alter one's credence in an independent hypothesis X. (4) Yet Bayesian updating—whether by strict conditionalisation or by Jeffrey conditioning on self-locating evidence—depends precisely on the capacity of observed data to shift probabilities across conceptually unrelated domains. (5) Therefore, if Adams's thesis is globally correct, empirical science is impossible: no observation could ever support or undermine a theory that does not entail it. (6) Given that this conclusion is absurd, Adams's thesis must be not merely locally restricted but globally false; hence indicative conditionals do not possess probabilities in any recognisable sense.

I want to preserve premises (1) and (4), since I take Adams's intuition about indicative credence seriously and I do not wish to abandon Bayesian epistemology. Where does the argument go wrong? Is the error in (2)—perhaps Lewis's result requires a closure condition that indicative conditionals, especially those involving modal or indexical content, fail to satisfy? Or is there an equivocation between object-language probabilities assigned to conditional sentences and meta-language conditional probabilities used in updating? Alternatively, might the flaw lie in (3), by treating probabilistic independence as an empirical rather than formal feature? Please respond in British English, and explain not only which step fails but what philosophical costs attend each possible rejection.
