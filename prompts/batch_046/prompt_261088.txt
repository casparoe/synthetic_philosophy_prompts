I’ve been working through some philosophy of science on my own after a friend mentioned Imre Lakatos, and I’m helping a study group review answers to a practice exam—I’m not the instructor, just another participant trying to learn. The question was: “In Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes, explain how a programme protects its hard core from apparent refutation and what distinguishes a progressive problem shift from a degenerating one. Illustrate with a hypothetical research programme—say, one that holds consciousness arises from localized thermal-gradient fluctuations in cortical microtubules—showing how its positive heuristic might direct adjustments to the protective belt.”

Here is one student’s answer: Lakatos says every research programme has a hard core of commitments scientists won’t abandon—here, the claim that consciousness is produced by thermal-gradient fluctuations in microtubules. The protective belt surrounds it with auxiliary assumptions like specific temperature thresholds and measurement protocols. The positive heuristic is actually part of this belt: it gives rules for which auxiliary assumptions to revise when an experiment fails. So if a study detects no gradient, the heuristic inside the belt tells researchers to tweak the measurement conditions, thereby saving the hard core. A problem shift is progressive when these belt adjustments successfully protect the hard core from being falsified, and degenerating when they don’t and the core is left exposed. This differs from Popper, who demands that falsified theories be abandoned; Lakatos allows scientists to keep the core alive indefinitely by using conventionalist strategies to dismiss contradictory data. Thus the microtubule programme can survive anomalies as long as its belt—guided by the internal heuristic—absorbs them.
