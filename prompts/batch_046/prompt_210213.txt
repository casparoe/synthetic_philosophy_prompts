You are an expert philosopher of economics and intergenerational ethics with experience designing final examinations for upper-level moral philosophy courses. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm carefully: identify the precise conceptual fault line that separates a student who genuinely understands pure time preference from one who has merely memorized standard objections. Specifically, pinpoint why conflating pure temporal discounting with risk-weighted or uncertainty-based discounting is a category error, and articulate the exact argumentative move needed to show that a non-arbitrary cutoff for future moral consideration cannot be defended without either reintroducing pure time preference in disguised form or accepting obligations so expansive that present action becomes impossible.

Your task is to construct one essay exam question for a 90-minute final exam in an upper-level undergraduate moral philosophy module, with an expected answer length of approximately 1,200 words. The question must not be answerable by reciting familiar summaries of temporal neutrality, Ramsey-style critiques, or Parfit-style reasons-based objections. Instead, introduce a specific, somewhat unconventional framing—the idea of a normative threshold policy that assigns equal moral weight to welfare occurring within a 200-year horizon and exactly zero weight beyond it, not because of epistemic uncertainty, physical impossibility, or resource limits, but as a deliberate normative boundary of moral community membership.

Write the following three components in a formal, polished exam-board style:

1. The exam question itself. Ask students to evaluate whether this threshold policy can be defended consistently without smuggling pure time preference back in through the concept of a bounded moral community, and whether it avoids the problem of infinite or paralyzing obligations to the distant future. They should also be required to address whether temporal distance is genuinely analogous to spatial distance or group membership, and what costs attend either accepting or rejecting the sharp 200-year boundary. Do not instruct students to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the question must be fully answerable from conceptual analysis alone.

2. A model answer of about 600 words. It should make these specific moves: (i) precisely distinguish pure time preference—discounting welfare solely because it is later in time—from other forms of future-weight reduction such as risk discounting or diminishing predictive confidence; (ii) explain why treating temporal location as morally analogous to spatial proximity or social membership fails under standard universalist moral theories; (iii) analyze whether a sharp 200-year cutoff can be non-arbitrary, identifying the specific cost of accepting it and the specific cost of rejecting it; (iv) assess whether an appeal to a bounded moral community successfully avoids pure time preference or merely renames it, paying attention to the risk of circularity; (v) conclude with a clearly reasoned judgment about the coherence of the threshold view.

3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks. Separate an A-grade answer (first class, roughly 16–20 marks) from a B-grade answer (upper second, roughly 13–15 marks) by naming the specific moves each must contain. For each criterion, state the marks available, the precise content an excellent answer includes, and the particular error or omission that should cost marks. Include explicit notes on predictable mistakes: for example, treating the threshold as a variant of exponential discounting; failing to distinguish a normative cutoff from epistemic uncertainty about distant outcomes; asserting temporal neutrality without engaging the community-boundary objection; or describing the problem without taking a reasoned stance on coherence.

Keep the prompt self-contained. Rely on widely discussed concepts—pure time preference, temporal neutrality, intergenerational obligation, moral community—but frame them through the novel threshold scenario you have introduced. Do not reference obscure texts, highly specialized authors, or specific dated publications that a model without web access could not be expected to have encountered in training. Think step by step, present the exam question first, then the model answer, then the marking scheme, and begin your response immediately with the exam question.
