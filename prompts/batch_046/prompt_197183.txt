Consider the institutional practice in modern constitutional democracies in which courts apply rigorous rights-based scrutiny—proportionality tests, strict scrutiny, and rational-basis review—to ordinary social, economic, and administrative legislation, yet systematically invoke doctrines of non-justiciability, political question, or act of state to place military deployment, emergency decrees, and core security allocations beyond any judicial examination. The same tribunals often justify their authority as a defense of fundamental justice against majoritarian coercion, but they treat the state's direct exercise of coercive power as a domain where justice has no purchase. The asymmetry is puzzling: if justice genuinely constrains power, why does its authority seem to vanish precisely where state force is most concentrated? And if coercive power is the final arbiter in those exceptional domains, why do courts assume it is not also the hidden foundation of the rights they enforce everywhere else?
