In a seminar on effective altruism, a participant dismissed a consequentialist argument for donating to disaster relief by invoking the principle that "moral worth requires acting for the right reasons": if your donation is motivated solely by a calculation that it saves the most lives, then regardless of the outcome your action lacks moral value. I want to treat this as a serious claim rather than a conversation-stopper. Can you separate exactly five distinct precise theses that this slogan could be advancing, ordered from the trivial to the radically anti-consequentialist? For each thesis, specify whether it concerns praise, intrinsic value, necessary conditions for virtue, or the permissibility of decision-procedures; indicate which ones genuinely undermine the consequentialist donor's claim to moral credit; and address whether any defensible version requires motives to be mechanically rigid—fixed regardless of further reasoning—or whether rational reflection on outcomes can coexist with full moral worth. Provide your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
