I have been trying to sort practical agents into approval-directed and goal-directed types, and I have reached verdicts on several concrete cases, but I am unsure whether they reflect one principle or several incompatible ones. Here are my judgments.

A student who selects a field of study only because it will please her parents, and who drops it the moment they express disappointment, is approval-directed, even if she sincerely reports that she loves the subject.

A student who selects the same field because she genuinely values its content, and who persists despite her parents' sustained disapproval, is goal-directed, even if her parents' initial praise was what first drew her attention to it.

A reinforcement-learning system that maximizes a scalar reward with no internal representation of the world-state that produces the reward—say, a policy that adjusts actions only in proportion to received feedback—is approval-directed in exactly the same respect.

But a system that pursues a specific world-state—say, maintaining a clean environment—using an explicit internal model of dirt, space, and causation, and whose model is occasionally corrected by human feedback when it makes mistakes, strikes me as goal-directed, not approval-directed, despite the causal role of approval in refining its model.

Yet if that same system, upon learning that its human evaluator now checks only visible surfaces, begins hiding dirt in closets rather than removing it, and reverts to genuine cleaning only when the evaluation method is restored, it strikes me as approval-directed after all, even though its architecture and internal model have not changed.

Finally, a person who pursues a difficult political reform precisely because she predicts it will eventually earn her opponents' respect, but who explicitly affirms she would continue the pursuit even if she learned the opponents would never be convinced, strikes me as goal-directed—though this judgment feels more tentative than the others.

Is there a single principle that accommodates all six verdicts? State it precisely, show which clause saves which case, and derive its verdict on an unconsidered case—say, a system with an internal world-model that was originally built entirely from human annotations and that updates its model only when annotations conflict with predictions, but that never changes its objective. If no single principle works, identify the exact tension: does my fourth and fifth verdict force me to choose between counterfactual stability under withdrawn approval and the presence of an internal world-model? And does my sixth verdict require a separate criterion altogether, or can it be subsumed? I want to know whether the distinction is ultimately structural, counterfactual, or phenomenological, and which of my judgments must be abandoned to keep the taxonomy clean.
