Construct a realistic, non-science-fictional thought experiment in political philosophy—set within a contemporary liberal democracy with nested or federated governance—designed to isolate the grounds of political obligation from the justification of civil disobedience. The scenario should feature a specific, enacted but morally objectionable directive (for example, a mandatory financial contribution to a central institution that enforces a policy the citizen regards as corrupt) and should allow the citizen three possible responses: direct compliance, open civil disobedience with acceptance of penalty, or redirection of the obligation through a local governmental mechanism that does not remit to the center. Engineer the institutional details—particularly the presence or absence of voluntary opt-in structures, the flow of benefits across governance layers, and the visibility of the citizen’s choice—so that the case yields three distinct verdicts: (1) a tacit-consent account of obligation concludes that the citizen is bound to obey the directive; (2) a fair-play or reciprocity account concludes that no obligation exists, because benefits received are non-voluntary and are offset by the unjust burden; and (3) a natural-duty account concludes that while there is a general duty to support just institutions, this particular directive is sufficiently unjust that the duty is suspended or overridden, thereby permitting or requiring open disobedience rather than covert evasion. Constraints: no exotic physics or technology, no perfect predictors or omniscient observers, stakes that a real person could face (e.g., financial penalty, professional sanction, not existential risk), and the scenario should be describable in roughly one paragraph. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification by giving the reasoning that leads each of the three theories to its verdict; identify the precise structural feature (for instance, an asymmetry in opt-out availability between local and central levels) that does the work in separating them; explain whether removing that feature collapses the case into a standard collective-action problem or eliminates the divergence altogether; and provide an honest defense on behalf of the theory that fares worst—specifically, how its proponent might argue that the scenario mischaracterizes the nature of consent, benefit acceptance, or institutional justice within nested authority structures. Do not include bibliographies, author citations, or requests for further reading; rely only on the conceptual distinctions named.
