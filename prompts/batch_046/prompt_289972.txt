Question: Consider the epistemic regress problem and the three standard responses—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. First, explain the arbitrariness objection to foundationalism, and assess whether infinitism genuinely escapes it or merely relocates the problem to the availability of an endless chain. Second, examine whether coherentism can accommodate the intuition that perceptual beliefs enjoy a kind of default entitlement—a resistance to revision that does not depend on prior justification—without collapsing into a disguised foundationalism. Third, address whether infinitism's demand for a non-terminating chain of reasons makes justification psychologically unattainable, or whether this objection confuses the structure of justification with the process of conscious reasoning.

Student answer:
Foundationalism stops the regress with basic beliefs that need no further support. The arbitrariness objection, famously pressed by Sellars against Chisholm-style views, holds that if basic beliefs lack justification, they are arbitrarily selected; if they have it, they are not basic. Infinitism, as defended by Peter Klein, escapes this by requiring an infinite, non-terminating chain of reasons: since no belief is the endpoint, none is arbitrarily left unsupported. But this comes at a cost: to be justified in believing P, one must actually have traversed an infinite sequence of prior reasons, which no human can do. So infinitism avoids arbitrariness only by making justification impossible in practice.

Coherentism, as developed by Laurence BonJour, rejects linear justification altogether. Beliefs justify one another through mutual coherence. The view can accommodate the idea that perceptual beliefs are more central or resistant to revision, because centrality is just a matter of having more coherence links, not of being epistemically basic. However, if we say that perceptual beliefs enjoy default entitlement—meaning they are rational to hold unless specifically defeated—this introduces a non-coherentist privilege. Once we allow such entitlement, we have reintroduced a foundational tier, since these beliefs are justified independently of the web. Thus coherentism must reject default entitlement entirely, or it collapses into foundationalism.

In conclusion, foundationalism faces arbitrariness, infinitism faces psychological impossibility, and coherentism can preserve centrality only by denying any form of default entitlement.
