I am a high school junior in an interdisciplinary seminar that pairs moral philosophy with speculative fiction, and for my final project I must produce a short story that dramatizes a conceptual tension rather than resolving it neatly. I want to explore whether brain size—specifically the distinction between absolute cranial volume and cortical neuron density—has any legitimate bearing on moral status, but I need the premise to avoid collapsing into a familiar allegory about intelligence or species membership. Please set the story in a near-future municipality where citizenship tiers are assigned not by species or consciousness tests, but by a metric called the Neuro-Scaling Index, which weights encephalization quotient against synaptic pruning efficiency. The protagonist should be a young archivist with benign macrocephaly: her brain is large in absolute volume but exhibits lower neuronal packing density, and after a mandatory audit her index falls just below the first-tier threshold, placing her civic rights in jeopardy despite her unremarkable cognitive and emotional capacities. Her closest interlocutor should be a microcephalic colleague whose brain is small but hyper-dense, placing him in the first tier despite a limited working memory and a notably restricted emotional range. The narrative should not deliver a moral; instead, it should stage at least two genuinely conflicting intuitions—that physical scale feels morally arbitrary, and that quantitative neural architecture might still track something we genuinely care about—through dialogue that sounds like people actually speaking rather than philosophers delivering thesis statements. Write this in a restrained, literary style, keeping the philosophical problem embedded in the characters’ practical disagreements about housing allocations, voting eligibility, and medical priority rather than in abstract debate.
