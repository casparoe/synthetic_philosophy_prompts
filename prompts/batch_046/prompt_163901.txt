My doctoral supervisor has ruled consistently on five cases concerning rational credence under logical uncertainty, and I should like her prediction on a sixth. In the first case, a specific arithmetic claim that is computable but practically intractable—say, the parity of the 10^100th digit of π—she held that a bounded agent ought to maintain near-uniform credence rather than adopt an arbitrary point estimate. In the second, a self-referential sentence within a fixed formal system that asserts its own logical probability is below one-half, she permitted assignment of exactly one-half and treated this as coherent rather than paradoxical. In the third, an open conjecture supported by strong heuristic analogies but lacking proof, she allowed a non-extremal credence guided by those analogies. In the fourth, a direct logical contradiction asserting its own falsehood, she insisted that assignment must be withheld entirely rather than set to zero. In the fifth, a statement describing the output of a deterministic computation guaranteed to halt but only after the agent’s lifespan, she ruled that the agent should regard it as logically determined yet epistemically open, adopting the limit that a well-behaved logical induction market would converge to.

The new case is a syntactically well-formed sentence S that asserts, of a particular logical induction scheme running over a universal Turing machine, that the scheme’s price for S at stage n+1 will be strictly lower than its price at stage n, where the truth value of S is defined independently of the scheme’s own prices. I am less interested in a precise numeric credence than in the principle she is implicitly applying—whether she privileges inductive coherence across stages, permits downward self-referential price spirals, or enforces a calibration constraint tied to computable baselines. Please predict her verdict, identify the rule that best accommodates her earlier five rulings, articulate an alternative rule that fits those rulings equally well but yields a different outcome for S, and propose one further case that would distinguish the two rules. Present your answer as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
