I sit on the scientific review panel of the Meridian Foundation for Evolutionary Research, and we must vote by Thursday on a proposal titled 'Adaptive Architectures of Problem-Solving in Cephalopods: A Five-Year Program.' The investigators seek $2.4 million to test explicit adaptationist hypotheses about complex metacognitive behaviors in cuttlefish—specifically rapid reversal learning and analogues to tool use—treating each capacity as a direct product of selection for flexible foraging. Two reviewers have endorsed the proposal, arguing that an adaptationist heuristic is methodologically legitimate and that critics should bear the burden of demonstrating spandrel status rather than demanding it be disproven in advance. A dissenting reviewer, invoking Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, contends that the proposal commits what she calls a 'Panglossian architecture' error: it assumes every observed neural feature is optimally engineered for cognition without first ruling out that the circuitry might be a structural byproduct of selection for rapid growth and camouflage-related sensory integration—what she distinguishes, in her written assessment, as a 'developmental spandrel' rather than a direct adaptation or an exaptation. The panel is split. Some members worry that requiring explicit spandrel-null models will paralyze an otherwise promising empirical program; others argue that funding exclusively adaptationist research without such safeguards misallocates resources and misrepresents evolutionary methodology. Please draft the panel's formal written opinion recommending approval, conditional approval, or rejection. The memo should specify a concrete course of action with a clear timeline—for example, requiring the investigators to incorporate a competing non-adaptive origin model within the first eighteen months, or restructuring the grant to include an independent theoretical workstream assessing spandrel hypotheses—and should treat the strongest consideration on each side with intellectual honesty rather than restating the debate in abstract terms. In particular, address whether insisting on spandrel-testing at the funding stage is a legitimate methodological safeguard or an unreasonable barrier to empirical progress, and explain what conditions, if any, would make approval responsible.
