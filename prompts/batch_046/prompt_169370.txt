Daniel Dennett describes the self as a center of narrative gravity. Explain this metaphor, contrast it with the Cartesian theater model, and then discuss whether this view can accommodate our ordinary intuition that we are genuine authors of our actions, or whether it collapses into eliminativism about the self. Be careful to distinguish Dennett's view from the minimal self defended by phenomenologists like Zahavi.

Here's the student answer:

Dennett's center of narrative gravity is basically the idea that the self isn't a little person inside your head watching a movie (the Cartesian theater). Instead, it's like the center of gravity of a physical object — not a thing itself, but a point where all the forces and stories about you converge. So the self is a fictional character, but a useful fiction, like Sherlock Holmes. This is different from Zahavi's minimal self, which is pre-reflective and immediate. Dennett thinks even the minimal self is just part of the narrative overlay.

Now, about authorship: since the self is just a story, it can't actually cause anything. The brain does all the causal work. So Dennett is basically an eliminativist — he thinks there is no self, only the story we tell. But he avoids the bad consequences because the story is still real in the same way that centers of gravity are real. So we are authors in a metaphorical sense, like a character writing themselves into existence. This is actually very close to Hofstadter's strange loop, where a symbol system refers back to itself and creates an I. So Dennett and Hofstadter are saying basically the same thing: the self is an illusion that arises from self-reference.

Overall, the narrative gravity view is better than Cartesian dualism because it fits with neuroscience, but it does mean we have to give up the idea that we are genuinely resposible agents.
