In discussions of why norms like dueling and chattel slavery collapsed—whether through argument, shifting material interests, or changes in honor codes (Appiah’s thesis, contrasted with Bernard Williams’s more rationalist picture)—people sometimes treat an honor-based practice like a currency without a central bank: its value rests on collective acceptance, so it can suffer a sudden “run” when confidence breaks, leaving the old behavior worthless overnight. This is used to argue that dueling died not because anyone refuted it, but because the social guarantee failed: once enough gentlemen refused the challenge, honor-through-combat lost its exchange rate with prestige. Audit the analogy. Give an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the currency, the central bank, the bank run, individual holders, counterparty risk, and reserve assets? Then show where the financial model breaks when mapped onto honor: especially on intentionality, collective-action dynamics, and whether moral argument can trigger or only follow the collapse. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten Appiah’s anti-rationalist conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. If the analogy smuggles in a picture of honor as purely conventional that the history of abolition and dueling’s decline does not support, say so. Finally, state whether the conclusion survives, and propose a tighter analogy if one exists. Answer in American English.
