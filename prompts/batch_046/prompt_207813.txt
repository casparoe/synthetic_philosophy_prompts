As a retired physicist who has lately begun reading philosophy of science, I have become struck by a debate that seems to circle endlessly without resolution: should our ontology include entities or states for which all information is permanently inaccessible—such as configurations behind an event horizon that will never influence our past light cone, unobservable branches of an Everettian multiverse, or hidden-variable states that reproduce quantum statistics without leaving any empirical trace—or should ontological commitment be restricted to what can, at least in principle, be identified? Realists appeal to theoretical indispensability and explanatory coherence; anti-realists invoke epistemic humility and parsimony. My own suspicion is that this is not a substantive disagreement about physics or metaphysics proper, but a hidden divergence over what identification itself requires: actual observational access, structural embedding within a predictive theory, or counterfactual accessibility under some physically permitted transformation. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a masked empirical or formal question doing the work—perhaps about informational closure conditions? I want an honest diagnosis of the disagreement's structure—whether verbal, substantive, or cross-purpose—supported by what each side actually says, together with an assessment of whether any conceptual distinction or formal result could actually end the stalemate, or whether the question is ill-posed. Please do not provide a reading list or citations; I wish to assess whether the impasse is structural rather than informational.
