I'm not an academic, but I've been working through some of Kierkegaard's ideas about the press and the public on my own, mostly from essays and lecture notes I've found, and I keep running into a wall when I try to apply them to the world now. Recently I was talking with two friends, Maya and Jordan, both sharp but without formal background in philosophy—like me, they're piecing things together from what they've read and heard. We ended up in a disagreement about whether algorithmic social media is essentially the modern fulfillment of Kierkegaard's warning about leveling and the anonymous crowd, or whether it actually breaks the conditions he was worried about.

Maya's position is that recommendation algorithms intensify the very leveling Kierkegaard described. She argues that in the press era, everyone became a passive spectator to a single abstract crowd opinion; today, algorithms turn every user into a quantifiable node in a unified engagement economy, flattening truth, seriousness, and qualitative difference into the same metric—clicks, shares, dwell time. For Maya, the public hasn't disappeared; it has become a statistical abstraction optimized for virality, and this makes the leveling more total because it operates invisibly and globally. Jordan disagrees. He says Kierkegaard's fear was specifically of an anonymous, reflexive mass that exerts social pressure to conform, stripping individuals of commitment and seriousness. Algorithmic feeds, he claims, do the opposite: they fragment the audience into personalized bubbles, destroying any shared present age. There is no visible crowd to level you; instead, an invisible system isolates each person in their own curated timeline. Jordan thinks this is a different pathology—fragmentation and isolation—not leveling, and that applying Kierkegaard's critique here misses what is actually distinctive about modern media. Maya pushed back: she said fragmentation is just segmented leveling. Even inside bubbles, content is stripped of intrinsic worth and valued only by engagement potential. The algorithm doesn't care about truth, only about capturing attention, which is the quantitative flattening Kierkegaard warned about. The bubbles are just market segments for the same leveling logic. Jordan's reply was that Maya is conflating mechanisms. Leveling requires social pressure from a visible, anonymous norm. In a personalized feed, there is no social pressure—just an opaque curation system. The user isn't being leveled by the public; they're being removed from it. That's not Kierkegaard's problem but a new one.
