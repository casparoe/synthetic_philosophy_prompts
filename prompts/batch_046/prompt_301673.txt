I have recently learned that members of my own family held administrative positions during a period of state-sponsored injustice two generations ago, and I now serve on a national commission that must recommend whether our government should formally acknowledge collective guilt for those historical wrongs. Draft the policy memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action—either endorsing a formal declaration of collective guilt, rejecting such language in favor of political responsibility alone, or proposing a conditional framework—with a specific implementation timeline and clear conditions for review. The memo must take seriously Karl Jaspers's distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, applying each category precisely to the intergenerational case rather than treating them as interchangeable. It must also engage the strongest opposing view: that a nation, lacking continuous intentional agency across generational turnover, cannot properly bear moral guilt and that attributing it risks a category error. In particular, evaluate whether the notion of diachronic collective intentionality—the capacity of institutional structures to maintain directed normative commitments despite complete membership change—can genuinely bridge that gap, or whether invoking it here merely collapses moral guilt into institutional liability or metaphysical regret. Keep the reasoning rigorous enough for legal advisors and historians on the commission, but ensure the bottom-line recommendation is unambiguous, actionable, and responsive to both the moral urgency and the conceptual risks.
