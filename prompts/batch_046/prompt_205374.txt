Assume the role of a historian of philosophy with deep expertise in David Hume's epistemology. Before answering, briefly outline Hume's core probabilistic reasoning about miracles: the standing conflict between uniform experience that establishes natural laws and the inherently weaker, derivative reliability of human testimony. Then predict how Hume would respond to three modern developments he never encountered. First, hierarchical Bayesian analyses that treat widespread, independent testimony streams as jointly updating a prior rather than as a single chain of hearsay. Second, synthetic media technologies capable of generating statistically regular, high-fidelity visual "evidence" of apparently law-violating events. Third, the analytic philosophical proposal to redefine a miracle not as a suspension of natural law but as a maximally surprising event that nonetheless remains probabilistically coherent with a theistic prior. For each development, indicate whether Hume would treat it as fatal to his argument, as calling for a minor qualification, or as a red herring. Conclude with a single synthetic judgment: would he still maintain that testimony can never rationally establish a miracle, or would he concede a narrow, qualified exception? Do not provide citations, page references, or a reading list. Write formally, as if addressing a graduate seminar, and think carefully through each step.
