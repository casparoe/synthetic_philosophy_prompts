You are to deliver the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop convened to revisit John von Neumann’s legacy, titled “Computation and Cognition”. The audience is split evenly: half are neuroscientists and philosophers of mind familiar with von Neumann’s comparison of the brain and computer developed for his unfinished 1956 Silliman lectures; the other half are university administrators, donors, and family members with no technical background.

Your address must make von Neumann’s claim do real structural work. He argued that the brain achieves reliability not through error-free components, as in a digital computer, but through massive redundancy and statistical averaging—yet he also resisted reducing cognition to mere mechanism. You must navigate this precisely: you may not claim the brain is “just a computer”, since that betrays the nuance he insisted upon; but you may not retreat into mystical vitalism, because the workshop’s premise is that computational thinking genuinely illuminates neural processes.

To meet these demands, inject and develop the following niche conceptual framing: treat von Neumann’s brain-computer comparison not as a metaphor of equivalence but as an argument about “architectural forgiveness”—the principle that coherent intelligence can emerge from systems whose individual parts are continuously failing, provided the overall architecture is designed to preserve meaning despite partial breakdown. Use this to explain why engineers, clinicians studying neurodegeneration, and philosophers are gathered together: each studies a different face of the same problem—how function persists when the substrate is unreliable.

Write the address in British English. It should last about five minutes when spoken, open with a concrete image rather than an abstract definition, and close by posing one specific question the workshop must answer if it is to honour von Neumann’s legacy honestly—a question that sounds technically precise to the scientists but carries obvious human weight for everyone else. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not ask the audience to consult specific texts.
