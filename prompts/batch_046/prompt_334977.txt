I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I am trying to think clearly about a disagreement at work that feels larger than a simple technical dispute. We maintain a legacy data pipeline that includes a validation module comparing two SHA-256 hashes of what are now identical upstream streams; five years ago these streams came from separate vendors, but after a consolidation they are produced by the same microservice. The module logs nothing, triggers no alerts, and appears to have no effect on correctness. Removing it would reduce pipeline latency by roughly fifteen percent. I want to delete it and document the simplification. Several senior engineers object—not because they can identify a present failure mode it prevents, but because they say it was added after a 2011 outage, that "it was put there for a reason," and that stripping away safeguards whose purpose we no longer understand is exactly how complex systems collapse in ways no one predicts. I tend to think that if a mechanism has no observable function, rational design demands we eliminate it; they treat the absence of understanding as a reason for caution rather than for removal. Is there a principled way to distinguish legitimate epistemic humility about evolved systems from mere conservative inertia? I sense the disagreement maps onto some deeper philosophical structure about reform and hidden purpose, but I do not know how to articulate it or apply it to this decision. What framework should govern whether an apparently useless safeguard should be preserved?
