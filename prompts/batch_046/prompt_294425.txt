I am a university student in a seminar on the Holocaust, modernity, and historical comparison—material that includes the Historikerstreit and Bauman's analysis of bureaucratic rationality. In our discussions of uniqueness and comparability, I keep encountering a specific argumentative move that I do not have a precise name for. The pattern works like this. A speaker rejects any comparison between the Holocaust and another mass atrocity—whether colonial violence, other state killings, or modern genocides—on the ground that comparison necessarily relativizes the Holocaust's moral status or collapses its specific historical character into a generic category. Yet the same speaker then treats the Holocaust as an implicit comparative benchmark, describing a contemporary event as falling well short of the Holocaust's magnitude, or warning that a present policy risks producing an outcome structurally analogous to the Holocaust, thereby measuring other cases against it while forbidding others to do the same. I have seen this especially in debates over whether Bauman's thesis about modern rationalization can be applied beyond the Jewish case: critics reject analogies to other bureaucratic regimes, but then invoke the Holocaust's singular horror precisely to mark the outer boundary of what modern administration can become. Is there an established concept that captures this exact double structure—the explicit prohibition of comparison coupled with the covert reliance on the event as a moral yardstick? How should it be distinguished from related notions such as the singularization of the Holocaust, from charges of instrumentalization, and from ordinary false equivalence? If no precise term exists, what would be the correct way to delineate its scope, its necessary conditions, and its boundary cases? I am after the conceptual carving, not a bibliography or reading list.
