I'm taking an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar on self-knowledge, and we've been working through whether introspection is best understood as a direct, quasi-perceptual acquaintance with one's own mental states or rather as an interpretive, narrative-constructive process not essentially different from our understanding of other people. Rather than rehearse the standard textbook opposition, I want to compare these two frameworks specifically on how they account for cases of motivated reasoning and affective misattribution—situations in which a subject sincerely reports a reason or emotional state that seems causally disconnected from the actual drivers of her behavior. Could you set out the precise commitments of each view, particularly with respect to whether introspection delivers unmediated access to qualitative feel, to the causal structure of one's attitudes, or to neither? Then apply both to the following concrete case: a professional insists she objects to a promotion decision purely on grounds of procedural fairness, yet exhibits clear physiological and behavioral markers of anger toward the colleague who received the promotion, and she genuinely denies feeling any personal resentment. In your answer, please separate what is broadly settled in the relevant empirical and philosophical literature from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any single interpretation—whether a transparency thesis, a confabulation thesis, or a hybrid account—as if it were the consensus position. I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent intermediate view that grants introspection limited, defeasible authority over phenomenal character while denying it privileged access to the causal origins of one's own mental states, or whether such a middle ground inevitably collapses back into one of the two main models.
