I'm drafting a chapter on long-run predictive theories and want to stress-test an argument about Malthus that keeps returning to me. Here is the reconstructed chain.

(1) For a theory to be empirically meaningful rather than prophetic, there must exist some observation within a feasible temporal window that could in principle count against it.

(2) Malthus's principle holds that population tends to grow geometrically while subsistence grows arithmetically, so positive checks are inevitable.

(3) For more than two centuries those checks have not materialized globally; defenders respond that Malthus was correct in structure but premature in timing, with technological and institutional feedback placing the crisis in what I will call epistemic escrow.

(4) This move—call it chronological immunization—strips the principle of any temporal parameter: since the model specifies no date by which the checks must appear, every present absence of crisis is interpreted as delay rather than disconfirmation.

(5) If chronological immunization is legitimate here, it is legitimate for any predictive mechanism that lacks a built-in temporal index.

(6) But well-established long-range forecasts—say, the eventual exhaustion of stellar hydrogen fusion or certain cosmological decay timelines—similarly lack near-term temporal bounds, and we do not treat them as unfalsifiable when intermediate data show no immediate crisis.

(7) So either chronological immunization is illegitimate, which makes Malthus straightforwardly falsified and renders his defenders pseudoscientific, or it is legitimate and the distinction between empirical science and unfalsifiable prophecy collapses for all long-run theories.

The conclusion feels absurd—I do not want to say that cosmological prediction is on a par with astrology—but I also want to keep (1) and the intuition behind (3). Where does the argument go wrong? If I must reject one premise or step, which one costs me the least, and what does accepting that cost commit me to?
