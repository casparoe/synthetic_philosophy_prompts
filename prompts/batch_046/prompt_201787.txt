I am attempting to clarify my own view of the distinction between knowing how and knowing that by testing it against particular cases, and I should like to know whether a unified principle can capture my verdicts. A theoretical physicist who has memorised every equation governing fluid dynamics but has never touched water or observed a flow: knowing that, clearly. A chef who, after losing all taste and smell, continues to prepare meals by following precise temperature and timing protocols she devised herself: I judge that she still knows how to cook, though a vital dimension is missing. A chess grandmaster whose arthritis now prevents her from moving pieces, yet who can still teach every standard opening with complete fluency: the knowing how remains intact. By contrast, a computational system trained only on textual recipes that can generate a flawless soufflé procedure without ever having handled ingredients, adjusted heat by sight or smell, or corrected a failing oven: I am inclined to classify this as knowing that about cookery, not knowing how. A pilot who has forgotten all theoretical aerodynamics but retains flawless motor responses during an emergency landing: knowing how is plainly present. A mathematician who once derived an intricate proof, has since forgotten it entirely, but can reproduce it step by step only when given the preceding line: here I waver; it sometimes feels like a diminished knowing how, sometimes like knowing that the proof exists coupled with a mechanical reconstruction skill. I am similarly unsure about a deaf musician who has never heard sound and cannot physically play any instrument, but who reads notation fluently and describes harmonic progressions with perfect accuracy: is her relation to music a form of knowing that, or a strange, disembodied knowing how? Is there a coherent principle—perhaps built around embodied execution, direct causal interaction with the domain, or first-person accessibility to one's own competence—that accommodates all these judgements? If such a principle requires abandoning one of my verdicts, which should it be, and why? Please also apply your principle to a case I have not mentioned: a sleepwalker who reliably drives a familiar route home without awakening, but who, upon waking, denies all awareness of the journey and cannot describe any of the turns taken.
