I facilitate a philosophy workshop for advanced undergraduates — 16 participants, 75 minutes, no prior decision-theory background required. I want an in-class simulation that makes participants feel why aggregating imperceptible harms is not always a matter of linear summation, and specifically why a vast number of tiny harms can undermine a collective capacity in ways that do not map neatly onto the classic comparison of torturing one person versus placing dust specks in billions of eyes. To move away from that well-worn framing, build the exercise around structurally coupled micro-harms: each individual choice imposes a harm so small it is not subjectively registered, but each such harm marginally degrades a shared institutional or normative resource, and once that resource falls below a hidden threshold the system can no longer respond to any harm at all — including severe ones. Design the rules, roles, and sequential decision procedure so that participants discover this non-linear collapse through their own choices before the facilitator names it. Specify the group division and roles (for example, allocators, monitors, and affected parties), the exact timeline within the 75 minutes, a scoring or privilege scheme that does not use real money but creates genuine incentives, the debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual distinction between additive aggregation and coupled degradation, an honest assessment of which feature of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally, and a prediction of what will predictably fail in the first run together with instructions for handling it. The participants should leave having experienced the intuition that distributed, interdependent tiny harms can carry a moral weight that exceeds what simple head-counting would suggest.
