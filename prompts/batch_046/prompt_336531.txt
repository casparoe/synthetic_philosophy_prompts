As a retired physical chemist who has recently begun reading philosophy of technology, I witnessed a salon discussion between an AI researcher and a philosopher of science about whether recursive self-improvement will produce a smooth capability curve or discrete jumps separated by long plateaus. I proposed framing the debate through the analogy of autocatalytic reaction networks, distinguishing volume-limited from surface-limited regimes. I ask you to adjudicate each move on its logical and analogical merits, then render an overall judgment, and to avoid providing bibliographies, reading lists, or references to named works.

Dr. Lin, the researcher, opened by arguing that once a system can rewrite its own learning architecture, the dynamics become volume-limited autocatalysis: every improvement increases the rate of further improvement across the entire digital substrate, so we should expect a near-vertical takeoff with minimal latency.

Dr. Reeves, the philosopher, replied that this neglects surface limitation. In heterogeneous catalysis, reaction speed depends on available interfacial area, not merely on bulk reagent concentration. By analogy, algorithmic gains must still be verified against physical reality, integrated with legacy infrastructure, and subjected to engineering and regulatory review—processes that do not scale automatically with compute—so improvement should be staged: sharp discontinuities followed by extended plateaus while the catalytic surface expands.

Dr. Lin countered that in purely digital domains compute is itself the interface. If verification is internalized—if the system runs its own simulations and self-checks—then the surface scales with the volume, and the bottleneck dissolves.

Dr. Reeves then introduced a risk he called ontological consolidation: when verification is fully internalized, the system may reinforce its own representational framework without the perturbation that external reality provides. Rather than accelerating smoothly, it could enter prolonged plateaus of false confidence, escaping only through exogenous shock. Thus internalization does not eliminate plateaus; it transforms their nature from engineering delays into epistemic traps.

Finally, Dr. Lin argued that such plateaus are evolutionarily unstable. A system locked in false confidence is vulnerable to being outcompeted by parallel architectures that maintain external coupling, so selection pressure favors rapid transit through plateaus rather than dwelling in them. Hence the macro-dynamics still favor rapid takeoff, though punctuated rather than continuous.
