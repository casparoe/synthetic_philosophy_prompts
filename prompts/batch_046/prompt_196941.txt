I serve with a colleague on an independent technical review board; we share identical credentials, have access to the same engineering reports, and have previously calibrated our predictive accuracy on similar assessments with statistically indistinguishable results. After separate analyses of a proposed bridge design, I conclude that the risk of failure under an extreme load combination remains within regulatory margins, while she concludes it exceeds them, arguing that I am underweighting a subtle interaction between fatigue-cycle accumulation and thermal-stress covariance. We have reconstructed each other's models step by step; neither can locate an arithmetic error, yet each remains convinced the other has misassigned weight to one statistical parameter. She now insists that, because we are genuinely equal in expertise and judgment, rationality obliges us both to move our confidence substantially toward the middle—treating the risk as genuinely unresolved and recommending a costly redesign. I worry that weakening my judgment solely because an equal disagrees, when I still see exactly where I think her weighting is off, amounts to treating agreement as more important than the evidence I actually have. Our board must file a unified recommendation by Friday. Given that we are equally qualified and equally unable to persuade each other, what should my disagreement with her actually do to my belief, and how should that shape the recommendation I help sign?
