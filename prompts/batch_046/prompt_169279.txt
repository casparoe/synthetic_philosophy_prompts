A popular essay on the philosophy of physics summarized Newton's bucket argument like this: spin a bucket of water and the surface curves, but only when the water is genuinely rotating, not when it is merely turning relative to the bucket or the room around it. If motion were purely relational, as Leibniz held, then spinning the entire universe around a stationary bucket ought to produce the identical curvature, yet it never does; therefore there must be absolute space serving as a real frame of reference, making space itself a kind of invisible substance. The essay then claims that Einstein's general relativity confirms this picture, because spacetime curvature acts as a dynamical container capable of rotating independently of the matter within it, so modern physics treats substantivalism as the default position.

This sounds plausible, but I have also read that Einstein was influenced by Mach's view that inertia arises from distant matter, and that general relativity is sometimes read in a relationist spirit. Is the summary fair? Please separate what is accurate about the bucket argument, what misrepresents Leibniz's relationism or Mach's later contribution, whether the step from "the water curves" to "space is a substance" is justified, and whether the essay's claim about general relativity confuses dynamical geometry with old-fashioned absolute space. I am particularly unsure about the comparison between rotating the bucket and rotating the whole universe — is that part of Leibniz's own view, or a later addition?
