I've been in this philosophy of cognition seminar for like two semesters now and there's this one argument about embedded agency that just won't die. One camp — mostly people coming from rational choice theory — says that even if an agent is physically embedded in its environment, expected utility maximization is still a perfectly valid normative ideal. They say it's just coherence over actions and outcomes, and embedding only adds practical constraints like computation limits or self-reference loops, not a conceptual problem. The other camp — mostly people influenced by teh cybernetics and embodied cognition crowd — insists that once you reject the Cartesian boundry between agent and world, the very idea of a utility function over "world states" collapses, because the agent's reasoning process is part of the state being evaluated. They claim coherence constraints presuppose a stable boundary that embeddedness denies.

We've mapped out all the examples — Newcomb-style variants, self-modifying agents, prediction markets that include the predictor — and everyone agrees on the formal descriptions, but nobody changes there mind. Is this actually a disagreement about what counts as a "well-formed preference," or is it a hidden empirical claim about whether human cognition can be modeled without a clear agent-world cut? Or are they just using "embedded" in seperate senses? And given your diagnosis, is there any formal result or thought experiment that could break the deadlock, or should we accept it's a bedrock difference about where reasoning ends and the world begins?
