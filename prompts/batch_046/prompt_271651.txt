Here is an argument I put together for a philosophy of mind discussion, and now it strikes me as suspicious because if the steps hold, they seem to demolish far more than just phenomenal consciousness. (1) Illusionism claims that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion: introspection systematically misrepresents purely physical neural processes as possessing non-physical, subjective qualities. (2) The reason offered is that introspection is an unreliable representational mechanism — it presents its objects with properties like ineffability, immediacy, and intrinsicness that do not match anything in a physical description of brain states. (3) But introspection also represents propositional attitudes — beliefs, desires, and intentions — as having properties like directionality, normative force, and aboutness. (4) These properties are just as absent from purely physical descriptions of neural dynamics as phenomenal qualities are. (5) So if introspection's failure to align with physical reality is sufficient grounds for treating representations of phenomenal qualities as illusory, then it is equally sufficient grounds for treating representations of beliefs and desires as illusory. (6) Therefore, consistently applied, illusionism about phenomenal consciousness entails illusionism about all propositional attitudes. (7) But if propositional attitudes are illusory, then there is no genuine belief that illusionism is true, and no rational agent to be persuaded by the argument in the first place. (8) Hence the argument for illusionism undermines the very conditions required for its own assertion. (9) So either illusionism is false, or it must arbitrarily restrict its skeptical scope to phenomenal representations alone.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Is there a principled distinction between phenomenal representations and intentional ones that blocks the slide from (5) to (6), or must the illusionist bite the bullet and accept (7)? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (2) — that introspection can be systematically misleading — than accept (6), but I want to know precisely what rejecting (5) or (4) costs, and whether that cost is worse than the self-undermining result. Please explain which step you reject and why it only looked valid, and say how confident you are in your diagnosis; in particular, single out the point about which you are least sure.
