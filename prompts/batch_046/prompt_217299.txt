I am a PhD student in philosophy of science presenting next week at our formal-methods workshop. The audience is a group of philosophers of science and decision theorists fluent in Bayesian reasoning but hostile to importing epistemic-value results into normative ethics; they tend to treat moral uncertainty as a category mistake that reifies normative frameworks as empirical hypotheses, and they distrust any argument that analogizes resolving one's moral commitments to resolving a predictive belief.

My thesis applies Good's result on the value of information to deep moral uncertainty—cases where an agent assigns serious credence to deontological, consequentialist, and virtue-ethical frameworks—to show that cost-free, perfectly reliable information about which framework is correct can have strictly negative expected value. The argument sketch is that Good's non-negativity proof assumes a single, framework-independent utility function across worlds, but under moral uncertainty the utility function itself is world-dependent: resolving uncertainty does not just reveal which actions maximize value, it determines what counts as value. Consequently, acquiring information can lock the agent into a normative frame whose internal recommendations are globally worse than the recommendations the agent would have followed under continued uncertainty with appropriate hedging.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face: rank them by how likely and damaging they are, phrase each in the vocabulary this audience uses, and for each provide a concise reply together with a frank note on where that reply is weakest. Include what I should concede up front, identify the one reply that must land if the proposal is to survive, and tell me the objection I am most hoping nobody raises.
