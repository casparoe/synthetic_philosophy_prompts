I have been trying to think clearly about those decisions where you face two goods that seem to share no common measure — for instance, the choice between remaining in a place where you are deeply known, with all the obligations that entails, and leaving for work that offers material security but requires you to become, in some sense, a stranger to yourself. In conversations about such hard choices, people often reach for an analogy from physics: they say comparing these values is like comparing mass to electric charge. Both are real and precisely quantifiable, yet they belong to different dimensions, so asking "how many coulombs is a kilogram of belonging worth?" is not merely difficult but conceptually malformed. From there, they argue that when values conflict this way, rational deliberation is impossible and the only honest response is either tragic resignation or an unreasoned leap of faith.

Before I accept that conclusion, I want to audit the analogy carefully. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what in the value case plays the role of the physical quantity, the unit of measurement, the dimensional framework, the scientist who recognizes the category mistake, and the specific reason aggregation fails? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not: are values actually structured like physical dimensions, or does the analogy quietly import the idea that values are externally measurable properties rather than commitments that shape who one is? I am especially interested in whether the analogy makes incommensurability look like a logical error rather than a practical difficulty, and whether, once that framing is removed, there might still be room for rational comparison through narrative coherence or structural importance rather than through aggregation. If the analogy collapses in ways that undermine the conclusion, I would also like to know what a more accurate comparison might be — one that preserves the intuition that not everything reduces to a single scale without making the conflict seem irrational.
