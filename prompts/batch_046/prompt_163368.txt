After a career in condensed-matter physics, I have recently turned to metaphysics of time and find myself dissatisfied with the standard textbook illustrations involving temporal becoming or objects that vanish from existence. I want you to construct a carefully engineered thought experiment—realistic, no longer than a paragraph, with no appeal to science fiction, hypothetical predictors, or exotic physics—that forces presentism and eternalism to deliver opposite practical verdicts on a concrete decision. Here are the specifications. The scenario should involve a professional archivist or estate trustee who must choose whether to destroy a collection of personal letters at the explicit instruction of their now-deceased author. Presentism should imply that carrying out the destruction is morally permissible, because once the author and all contemporaneous correspondents have ceased to exist, there is no remaining subject of harm or bearer of interest; eternalism should imply that destruction is impermissible, because the author persists tenselessly at an earlier temporal location and retains a welfare interest in the integrity of her correspondence. You must then show the reasoning for each ontology, isolate the structural feature—specifically, whether moral standing requires present occurrence or can be sustained by tenseless existence—that causes the theories to peel apart, and explain whether neutralizing that feature collapses the case back into a generic problem of posthumous harm. You should also address how a committed presentist defender would attack the eternalist framing of the case without merely assuming presentism, and you should be explicit about whether your scenario genuinely derives the verdicts from the ontologies or merely stipulates them. The stakes must be ordinary—emotional or legal rather than cosmic—and the case should be suitable for a seminar of advanced undergraduates who have read only introductory material.
