I defend a modified Gödel-style modal ontological argument. Rather than adopting Gödel's original Axiom 1—that a property is positive if and only if its negation is not—I substitute a graded taxonomy in which properties can be positively valenced, negatively valenced, or modally neutral. I also weaken Axiom 2: closure under entailment holds only for properties entailed within a single S5-accessible world cluster, not across all possible worlds. From these qualified premises I still derive that a being with all positive properties exists necessarily, but I claim this avoids both the "all-properties-are-positive" collapse and Gaunilo-style parody by restricting the domain of perfect-making properties.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want the map of its exposure, not a generic survey of objections to ontological arguments. Specifically: which formal results in modal logic—for instance, about the behavior of property sets under S5 frame conditions or about the interaction of graded valuations with entailment—would undermine the restricted closure axiom? Which metaphysical commitments about the modal profile of properties (e.g., whether essential-origin properties are positively valenced) does the argument depend on, and what intuitions about perfection or possibility do those commitments trade on? Among the standard criticisms—modal collapse, the problem of negative properties, parody objections—which ones survive my qualifications, which ones are defanged, and which ones return in a stronger form precisely because of the graded taxonomy?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of this qualified argument should concede that it counts against them, or whether they can dismiss it by appealing to the graded-neutral distinction or to the world-cluster restriction. I also want an honest assessment: has this qualification strategy insulated the view into unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue (it protects a substantive metaphysical claim) or a vice (it renders the argument compatible with any modal intuition)? Finally, identify the weakest point in the argument's formal architecture—the premise or inference step that should be prepared to defend rather than hoped to be overlooked.
