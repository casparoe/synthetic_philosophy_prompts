Design a single 90-minute essay exam question for an upper-level undergraduate or first-year graduate seminar on philosophy of history and social explanation, focused on Fernand Braudel’s concept of the longue durée. The question must not be answerable by reciting Braudel’s familiar three-layer schema (geographical time, social-structural time, and the time of events). Instead, construct the prompt so that students must engage a specific conceptual tension: Braudel treats climatic and geographical structures as a nearly invariant base that constrains human action without being constrained by it, yet his own historical narratives describe economic and agrarian structures being gradually reshaped by sustained, decentralized practices. Force students to ask whether this introduces a “one-way dependency” problem—whether slow structures are genuinely inviolate with respect to events, or whether aggregated micro-actions can erode them over centuries.

Embed the following concrete hypothetical in the exam question itself: over four centuries, communities in a highland region practice decentralized terrace-building that slowly alters local hydrology, soil retention, and microclimatic patterns, eventually shifting agricultural possibilities and trade routes in ways that feed back into the regional economy. Require students to use this case to test whether Braudel’s temporal hierarchy collapses into a flat ontology of reciprocal causation, or whether he can preserve an asymmetrical relation of constraint without treating geography as metaphysically fixed. The question should ask them to take and defend a specific position.

Then provide:
1. The exam question, clearly formulated and self-contained, with the hypothetical integrated.
2. A model answer of approximately 800 words that advances a precise thesis (for instance, that the asymmetry is methodological—scaling of explanation—rather than ontological) and applies it rigorously to the terrace-building case.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, totaling 20 marks, with each point assigned a specific weight. For each point, include a one-line note describing the characteristic mistake that causes candidates to lose those marks (e.g., conflating temporal scale with causal immunity, or describing the hypothetical without using it to test the hierarchy).

Write the prompt in formal academic register, with no requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Ensure that every conceptual reference is either common knowledge within the field or defined within the prompt itself.
