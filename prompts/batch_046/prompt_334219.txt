You are a senior moral philosopher with particular expertise in Bernard Williams's critique of impartialist ethics and his argument about personal integrity—specifically the concern that certain moral frameworks demand an unnecessary mediating step of universal justification between an agent and their deepest commitments, a problem Williams illustrates through the idea that there can be one thought too many. Before constructing anything, please begin with brief conceptual brainstorming: clarify the difference between acting from a direct attachment that is partly constitutive of one's practical identity and acting only after translating that attachment into a reason acceptable to any impartial observer. Then design a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping conditions—that a clinical ethics consultant can run in about thirty minutes on any proposed care protocol. The consultant reviews roughly twelve protocols per quarter involving end-of-life and triage policies, with high stakes for both patient welfare and staff retention, and needs to determine whether the protocol forces family members or clinicians to justify special concern for particular individuals through detached, universalizing reasoning rather than allowing the concern itself to be the reason.

The checklist should contain, in order: (1) a screening question identifying whether the protocol requires justification of special treatment through an impartial metric; (2) a test for whether the attachment at issue is constitutive of practical identity or a contingent preference, including what counts as sufficient evidence for each; (3) a clear negative exit (protocol respects integrity—no further action needed) and a clear positive exit (protocol violates integrity—recommend revision); (4) an explicit suspension point where the procedure stops and hands the decision to human judgment because the constitutive versus contingent distinction is itself value-laden; and (5) a brief misfire warning noting where professional-role obligations might be wrongly classified as contingent preferences. Be explicit about which steps are genuinely procedural and which are judgment calls dressed in procedural language, and note the specific conditions under which the checklist is likely to fail.

Apply the checklist to three cases: (A) a triage rule requiring physicians to justify prioritizing their own child using a standardized quality-adjusted life-year score; (B) a palliative-care documentation rule prohibiting nurses from citing personal bonds with long-term patients as reasons for deviating from standard analgesic protocols, requiring instead purely autonomy-based justifications; and (C) a family-exemption clause in an organ-allocation algorithm that grants preferential matching to first-degree relatives without requiring demonstration of greater social utility. For each case, run every step in sequence, state the procedure's output, identify the specific step that drives the outcome, and flag any point where your own judgment conflicts with the procedure's verdict—especially in case C, where institutional intuition might resist the conclusion. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: which types of integrity conflicts it will systematically miss, and which non-integrity values such as fairness or transparency it deliberately does not weigh.
