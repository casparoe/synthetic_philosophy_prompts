I'm planning a 75-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students in a philosophy-of-mind module. They have just read an excerpt from Roger Penrose's popular argument that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem proves human mathematicians are not algorithms. They arrive convinced that Gödel discovered a permanent, mysterious gap between formal computation and human reason, and they are ready to invoke "truths that no machine can reach." I do not want to lecture them on the mathematics; I want a teaching plan for dismantling that inference while giving them a precise grasp of what the theorems do and do not establish outside mathematics.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, specify the dependency order: what conceptual layer must be in place before the next can land, with particular attention to the syntax/semantics distinction and the internal/external perspective distinction. Second, identify the single carrying example or constructed case that should run through the session: describe exactly what it must include—for instance, explicit syntactic rules, a mechanism of self-reference via encoding sentences as numbers, and a clear absence of internal semantic evaluation—and what it must deliberately exclude so that it does not mislead. Third, map the misconceptions they will bring or form in the order they are likely to arise—beginning with the conflation of unprovability and falsehood, moving through the Penrose/Lucas leap from "I see G is true" to "I am not formal," and ending with the scope error of applying the result to any consistent system regardless of strength or formalizability—and state at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off, with the specific move or question that does the work. Fourth, design one check-for-understanding task at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the internal/external boundary rather than merely memorized "Gödel means humans are special"; the task should involve applying the framework to a novel, non-mathematical system, such as a hypothetical rule-bound legal AI that meets Gödel's conditions. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and named as such—do not ask for readings, do not ask for a bibliography, and do not invoke technical conditions like ω-consistency except to name them as beyond scope; also explicitly refuse to settle whether human cognition is actually formalizable, marking that as an empirical question outside the session's reach. Finally, inject one pedagogical or framing device that is not standard in introductory Gödel explanations—something that forces the students to physically or conceptually track whether they are speaking from inside or outside the system being discussed, such as requiring them to switch notation or register whenever they shift from object-language derivation to meta-language commentary.
