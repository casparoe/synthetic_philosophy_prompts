As a physicist who spent thirty years working on statistical mechanics and collective phenomena in condensed matter, and who has only recently begun reading formal epistemology and philosophy of science, I find myself returning repeatedly to a question that sits at the boundary of my old discipline and my new interests: how do rational agents, each possessing private information, come to collectively suppress that information in favor of observed public behavior? The canonical answer, developed in 1990s economic theory, treats agents as sequential Bayesians who observe predecessors' actions but not their private signals; once enough actions align, subsequent agents rationally ignore their own evidence and a cascade locks in.

I want to compare this standard framework with an alternative I have been developing informally—let us call it the correlated-source herding model. In this variant, every agent knows that all predecessors drew private signals from distributions that share a hidden methodological parameter (for example, an unobserved calibration drift in a shared instrument), but no agent knows the correlation structure induced by that parameter. Consequently, an agent cannot treat her predecessors' actions as independent evidential updates; she must jointly infer the state of the world and the unknown correlation matrix governing the signal-generating process.

Please compare these two models across three dimensions: first, the formal conditions under which a cascade becomes irreversible; second, the normative evaluation of an agent who breaks the cascade by acting on her private signal despite overwhelming public opposition; and third, the empirical or conceptual applicability of each framework to episodes of scientific consensus formation, particularly in fields where experimental apparatus is widely shared.

In addition, identify which open problem in this area—whether it concerns tractability of inference under unknown correlation structures, the normative status of epistemic deference, or the historical identification of cascade dynamics in actual scientific communities—should be settled first if one's goal is to apply these models rigorously to collective rationality in science. Defend your prioritization by explaining which downstream questions depend on its resolution, which are tractable with current formal tools, and which you would deprioritize because their answer would not substantially alter practice or theory.
