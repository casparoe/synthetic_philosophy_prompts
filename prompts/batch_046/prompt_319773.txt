Please draft a final-exam essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, for an upper-level philosophy of mind or cognitive science seminar (final-year undergraduate or Master's level). The topic is conceptual slippage in a microdomain, drawing on the idea—familiar from microdomain analogy research and discussions of fluid concepts—that a concept's boundaries shift when it must serve structural, transformational, and relational roles simultaneously within a tightly constrained artificial domain.

To keep the assessment self-contained and independent of obscure memorised detail, construct a novel microdomain for the question: the 'hinge-array domain'. Define it precisely in the prompt you write for the students: four differently coloured tokens are mounted on a rigid two-armed hinge capable of 180-degree rotation about a central pivot. A legal move is either (a) a rotational pivot of the whole hinge, or (b) a local replacement of one token by another governed by an adjacency relation that is not visible in any single static state but becomes recoverable only by comparing arrays before and after transformation. Ask the students to examine how the concept of 'symmetry' slips between describing an invariant feature of the static array, constraining which pivots or replacements are permitted, and naming a relation that holds only between two arrays.

The essay question should require students to do three things: analyse the structural reason why this three-way slippage is necessary rather than accidental; evaluate whether it undermines conceptual stability or instead enables analogical mapping across the domain; and apply this analysis to a specific comparison they construct themselves (for example, comparing an array before and after a pivot-plus-replacement sequence).

Please provide:
1. The essay question itself, clearly framed so that a student can answer it in 90 minutes in approximately 900–1,100 words.
2. A model answer of about 700 words, written in British English, that demonstrates the specific analytical moves required.
3. A marking scheme with five must-hit points, allocating marks for each (total 25), together with a one-line note on the typical error that should lose marks for that point.

The question must be unanswerable by merely reciting a generic account of conceptual change or analogy; it should distinguish students who understand the dependency between transformation rules and concept boundaries from those who have only memorised terminology. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not quote verbatim from any published text; paraphrase arguments without quotation marks if necessary. Ensure the entire response is in British English.
