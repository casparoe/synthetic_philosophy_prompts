A recent magazine essay summarizes the work-and-leisure debate like this: Weber’s Protestant ethic made labor a secular calling (Beruf) whose value is intrinsic, not instrumental; modern campaigns for the 35-hour week and against karoshi therefore rest on a category error, treating leisure as a competing right rather than as recovery for further labor. The author adds that karoshi is best understood not as a systemic labor failure but as the logical endpoint of treating work as moral self-formation—so hour-limiting policies miss the point, since the real problem is the ethic itself, not its intensity. The essay also distinguishes leisure-as-recovery, which the ethic can accommodate, from leisure-as-autonomous-right, which it allegedly cannot, and claims that only the former is conceptually available within Weber’s framework. Something feels off: Weber was describing a psychological mechanism, not prescribing labor as a moral duty, and the jump from that description to “leisure is only recovery” looks like a sleight of hand. Could you audit this summary? Separate what Weber actually argued from where the essay turns description into prescription, check whether the critique of labor-rights policy follows from the corrected view, assess whether karoshi can be fairly framed as an “endpoint” of the ethic rather than of exploitative structures, and examine whether the recovery-versus-right distinction does the philosophical work the author needs or whether it collapses under pressure. I am especially unsure whether the claim that Weber’s framework leaves no room for leisure-as-right survives careful reading.
