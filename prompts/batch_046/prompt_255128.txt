I am a high school student preparing an independent study on classical accounts of character revelation. While reading a late-nineteenth-century English rendering of Plato's Laws (Book I, in the section where the Athenian Stranger discusses symposia and the civic purpose of wine), I came upon a passage that I find conceptually difficult. The passage argues that the lawgiver permits supervised drinking not for pleasure but as a form of civic examination: wine strips away the artificial composure of sober self-presentation and exposes the underlying temperament and constitution of the soul, allowing observers to see whether a person is genuinely courageous or cowardly, moderate or intemperate, without the interference of conscious disguise. The text further suggests that this exposure is valuable precisely because intoxication makes deliberate pretense hard to maintain. I am trying to determine whether Plato, as rendered here, advances a dispositional view—according to which alcohol merely reveals stable traits that exist unchanged beneath sober behavior—or a transformative view—according to which alcohol temporarily restructures the soul so that the revealed conduct belongs to a genuinely different psychological state. This distinction is central to my argument about whether Plato treats drunkenness as epistemically transparent, simply displaying what was already present, or as ontologically productive, generating a temporary but real alteration in the drinker. Could you help me parse the passage's reasoning on this point? In particular, I would like to understand how the translator's coupling of temperament, which suggests an enduring disposition, with constitution, which suggests an arranged and possibly mutable structure, might guide the interpretation of whether the revealed self is the true self or merely a drink-altered self. I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am looking only for conceptual clarification that I can apply directly to the passage.
