I am drafting a term paper for a graduate seminar on modern political theory, and I am trying to apply Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power to the organization of remote software development, where productivity is monitored through keystroke dynamics and eye-tracking applications yet workers remain physically dispersed in private homes. During our discussion section, a colleague argued that Foucault's framework is essentially bound to enclosed architectures—the prison, the school, the factory—where bodies are arranged in space and subjected to continuous, visible observation. Because remote labor lacks this spatial enclosure, and because workers often internalize algorithmic metrics as self-optimization goals rather than experiencing them as external coercion, she claimed that disciplinary power is the wrong analytical tool altogether and should be replaced by Byung-Chul Han's concept of psychopolitics, in which the neoliberal subject exploits itself without any institutional panopticon.

I want to object, but I am uncertain whether Foucault's later work on governmentality and biopower can genuinely accommodate this kind of distributed, self-directed surveillance, or whether remote algorithmic labor represents a qualitative rupture that makes the disciplinary model obsolete. If normalization now operates through voluntary productivity dashboards and self-tracking apps rather than through institutional walls, is it still coherent to call this disciplinary power, or ought we to treat it as a post-disciplinary regime? Please respond in American English, and explain whether my colleague's architectural objection rests on a misunderstanding of how capillary power functions.
