I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on philosophy of action and experimental philosophy, and after we discussed the classic Knobe-style asymmetry in judgments about intentional action, my study group tried to extend the framework to more structurally complex scenarios. I'm trying to determine whether my own intuitions about these extensions form a coherent pattern or whether I'm implicitly relying on a principle I cannot articulate. Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases; in each I ask whether the highlighted outcome counts as intentional on the agent's part.

First, the baseline pair. A corporate vice president approves a cost-cutting program solely to increase profits, correctly foresees that it will harm the local ecosystem, and proceeds with complete indifference to that result. I judge that harming the ecosystem is intentional. If instead she foresees that the same program will help the ecosystem—say, by forcing adoption of cleaner logistics—and proceeds with the same indifference, I judge that helping the ecosystem is not intentional. So far my intuitions track the familiar asymmetry.

But they become less stable once I move away from direct physical causation. A historian publishes declassified archives purely for scholarly transparency; she correctly anticipates that this will collapse a founding national myth and trigger widespread social unrest, yet she is indifferent to that consequence. I still feel that collapsing the myth is intentional, even though the causal path runs through public interpretation rather than direct action. By contrast, a surgeon performs a routine appendectomy and, through an unknown immunological interaction, accidentally cures the patient's separate autoimmune disorder; she knew the interaction was remotely possible but had no reason to care about the cure. Here I strongly judge that the cure is not intentional, despite the beneficial outcome being foreseen, if only probabilistically.

The cases that genuinely trouble me involve indirect mediation through other agents' choices. A city council raises taxes solely for revenue, knowing that this will drive a specific slumlord to sell properties to developers, who will then displace long-term tenants. The council is indifferent to the displacement, which occurs only because of intermediate economic decisions. I feel inclined to say the displacement is intentional, though I am much less certain than in the direct-harm cases. Meanwhile, consider a climate activist who sabotages fossil-fuel infrastructure knowing it will cause a temporary shortage that, via market response, accelerates a renewable transition. She cares only about the sabotage, not the transition. Here I want to say the transition is not intentional—even though the causal structure seems parallel to the tax case, except that the mediated outcome is beneficial rather than harmful. So I appear to have an asymmetry across both normative valence and mediation, but I cannot tell whether I am tracking one factor, both, or neither.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that combines normative valence with a condition on directness, or one that appeals to the structure of the agent's practical reasoning rather than bare foresight—that delivers all six verdicts, including the uncertain ones? If not, which judgment should I treat as the outlier to be revised: my willingness to call the indirect displacement intentional, or my reluctance to call the indirect transition intentional? Please also state what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not listed: for instance, a software engineer who pushes a routine security patch that she knows might accidentally break a malicious botnet, where the breaking harms the botnet's operators but benefits the public. Finally, please say how confident you are in the account you offer and single out the point you are least sure of—especially whether any condition on indirect mediation can be made precise without collapsing back into a purely normative asymmetry.
