This has become personally urgent since I was asked to advise a local heritage board in a post-industrial town that lost its dominant employer within a generation; they wish to frame their history through analogies to ancient “collapses,” and I have come to suspect the analogy misleads. I want to calibrate my own view through yours.

Consider the precise proposition: that none of the three canonical cases—the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui (Easter Island)—should be classified as a societal collapse, provided we define collapse strictly as the co-occurrence of institutional decoupling (the dissolution of formal governance, economic integration, and administrative record-keeping) and ontological rupture (the collapse of the symbolic and categorical framework through which the population understands collective life, so that social existence becomes unintelligible in prior terms). On this definition, each case exhibits decoupling without rupture, and is therefore a transformation rather than a collapse.

Give me your actual credence in this proposition, decomposed explicitly into these terms:

1. The defensibility of requiring both conditions jointly rather than permitting either alone to count as collapse. Rough probability?

2. The likelihood that the Late Bronze Age involved genuine ontological rupture for local populations—beyond the well-documented loss of palace economies and writing systems—rather than network reconfiguration with cultural and religious continuity.

3. The likelihood that the Classic Maya case involved not merely political fragmentation and demographic dispersal, but a rupture in the intelligibility of divine kingship, calendrical order, and monumental practice that would justify the collapse label.

4. The likelihood that the Easter Island narrative of ecological self-destruction and demographic breakdown is accurate enough to ground a collapse classification, as opposed to adaptive restructuring in which later European contact and introduced disease account for the primary demographic catastrophe.

Combine these approximately—without false precision—to reach your overall number. Then identify which term you are least confident in, and specify what archaeological, ethnohistoric, or comparative finding would most shift your credence. Finally, state what pattern of evidence across these three cases would resolve the matter: what would convince you that “collapse” is a coherent, non-normative category rather than a post-hoc judgment that complexity has undesirably decreased?
