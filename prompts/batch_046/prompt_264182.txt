As a doctoral student in formal epistemology, I am trying to work through a conceptual tension in how we apply the principle of indifference to continuous spaces, using Bertrand's paradox as my central case. On the one hand, there is what I will call the symmetry-fixing resolution: the claim that we must apply indifference not to all possible descriptions of a chord's position, but only to descriptions invariant under the physical symmetries of the circle, which yields a unique probability distribution over chord lengths. On the other hand, there is what I will call the representational-level resolution: the claim that the paradox reveals that applying indifference requires first specifying an epistemic state of total ignorance at a particular representational level—whether we are ignorant about endpoint pairs, about radial distances, or about midpoint locations—and that different levels are not inter-translatable without smuggling in substantive ontological commitments.

Please compare these two resolutions conceptually. Specifically, I want to know whether the symmetry-fixing approach actually eliminates the arbitrariness problem or merely relocates it into the choice of state-space parametrization. If selecting an invariant measure presupposes that we have already fixed a particular geometric ontology, then does that selection face the same charge of unprincipled privilege that the representational-level view directs against naive applications of indifference? Second, I am interested in whether this comparison changes when we move from two-dimensional geometric toy problems to high-dimensional parameter spaces—such as those in statistical learning—where symmetry groups may be non-compact and invariant measures may not exist. In such settings, does the representational-level resolution provide resources for constraining inductive priors that the symmetry-based approach lacks? Please do not provide a bibliography; I am asking for your direct conceptual analysis of which framework better survives the transition from classical geometric paradoxes to contemporary underdetermined inference problems.
