I've been in this distributive justice seminar all semester and we're honestly going in circles on the separateness of persons thing. Like, everybody understands Rawls's basic point that utilitarianism treats people like containers for utility, and we can all talk about the redistribution case where taking from one to give to many is wrong even if total welfare goes up. But then the discussion just dies. Half my classmates keep saying the whole objection collapses once you realize we can't do precise interpersonal comparisons of well-being anyway, so utilitarianism was already dead in the water for practical reasons. The other half insists that's totally missing the point — they say it's not about measurement, it's about the metaphysical fact that there's no "super-person" whose well-being is being summed. We've been at this since October and nobody's moved.

So I'm trying to figure out what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it secretly about whether welfare economics is coherent? Or are we running two different questions — one normative, one about the ontology of persons — under the same heading? I also noticed something weird: the people who seem most convinced by the metaphysical version all happen to have taken that philosophy of mind course where they talk about narrative identity vs. the Humean bundle view. Could there be a hidden dependency here, where your view on whether the self is a continuous substance makes you more or less likely to see person-boundaries as morally fundamental? And if that's driving the stall, is there any kind of argument or distinction that could actually move it, or are we just at bedrock intuitions?
