I have recently been discussing Simone Weil's political writings with a colleague who encountered a popular account of her position on parties. The account summarises her view in roughly the following terms: that political parties are structurally incapable of pursuing justice because membership necessarily demands the suspension of individual moral attention, requiring one to substitute collective loyalty for personal discernment, which she regards as a form of spiritual gravity—pesanteur—that crushes the soul's capacity for truth. On this basis, the account continues, she argues that all parties ought to be abolished outright, and that authentic democracy can only operate if citizens vote directly upon legislation without any mediating organisations, since any collective body inevitably corrupts judgment and prevents grace from acting upon the individual conscience. From these premises the author concludes that Weil's politics are essentially anarchic and incompatible with every form of representative government.

I suspect that this captures her well-known hostility to parties but perhaps overstates both her institutional prescriptions and the scope of her argument. In particular, I wonder whether the summary correctly integrates her critique with her broader metaphysics of attention and grace, or whether it mistakenly presents her reasoning as a purely sociological observation about group dynamics rather than a theological account of how collective passions obstruct spiritual discernment. Could you help me to audit this characterisation? I should like to know which elements are faithful to Weil's actual reasoning, which are subtle exaggerations or conflations—especially regarding her alleged advocacy of direct democracy without mediation and her claim that moral corruption is inevitable in any collective body—and whether the conclusion that she is an outright anarchist survives once these distortions are corrected. I am not seeking a reading list, but rather a conceptual clarification that might assist me in judging whether her critique is directed specifically at parties as she knew them, or extends necessarily to every form of political association.
