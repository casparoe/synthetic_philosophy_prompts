For a quantitative-genetics reading group I have been asked to write down my philosophical profile on heritability so that others can probe it. Here are my commitments:

(a) Heritability (h²) is strictly a variance-partitioning device defined relative to a specific reference population; it carries no causal implication for any individual and does not indicate whether a trait is “genetic” in any deeper sense.

(b) I accept the conventional twin-study estimates for behavioural traits in affluent populations—e.g., personality dimensions around h² ≈ 0.4–0.5 and cognitive ability around h² ≈ 0.7—on the condition that the equal-environments assumption holds approximately.

(c) I treat gene-environment correlation (rGE) and gene-environment interaction (GxE) as constitutive rather than corrective: heritability is a property of the trait-in-environment system, not of the trait alone, and identical genotypes can yield markedly different phenotypes across environments.

(d) For policy purposes I operate with a distinction between variance-partitioning heritability (h²vp) and predictive heritability (h²pred), where the latter measures the genetic contribution to phenotypic differences that would remain after a specified, uniform environmental intervention. I maintain that only h²pred is policy-relevant, and I generally avoid invoking h²vp in policy arguments.

(e) I hold that evolutionary explanations of behavioural traits require that those traits were heritable in the relevant ancestral environments, but I deny that modern h²vp figures provide any evidence about whether those traits are currently adaptive or maladaptive.

(f) I think that if a society achieved perfect equality of relevant environmental inputs, the observed h²vp for achievement-related traits would approach 1.0; I interpret this increase not as growing genetic determinism but as logical evidence that environmental inequality has been eliminated.

(g) For categorical outcomes—e.g., schizophrenia or autism—I regard the liability-threshold model as conceptually superior to direct heritability estimates, yet in practice I cite twin-study heritability figures (around 0.8) for these conditions as though they referred to the same kind of quantity as continuous-trait heritabilities.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the real tensions by severity, specify for each what has to give, and identify the single revision that would resolve the largest number of conflicts. I want to know whether I am helping myself to an unstated assumption—particularly in the move from (b) to (c), or in the relation between (c) and (d)—without signalling which commitment I am most reluctant to drop. Write your answer in British English.
