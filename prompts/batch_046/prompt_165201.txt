I'm working on a feature about how logistics firms contract for autonomous drone delivery networks, and I've noticed a recurring clause in the agreements: every contract requires a licensed human pilot to retain "meaningful control," yet the document defines that control purely through technical parameters—maximum signal latency, physical presence within a geofenced radius, and the ability to trigger an abort sequence—without any reference to comprehension, intention, or situational understanding. At the same time, the firms file regulatory paperwork classifying these drones as "tool AI," stressing that the system lacks goals or autonomy of its own, even though the operational architecture lets the drone reroute, prioritize deliveries, and negotiate airspace without real-time human input on each individual decision.

What philosophical commitments about agency, instrumentality, and causal responsibility does this contractual and regulatory practice presuppose? Does treating "meaningful control" as a matter of latency, proximity, and button-access implicitly endorse a view on which agency reduces to discrete causal intervention points, and can that view sit comfortably with the simultaneous claim that the system is purely a non-agentic tool? Reconstruct the implicit theory of human-machine agency that makes both classifications coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether rival views—such as one that demands understanding for genuine control, or one that distributes agency across the human-drone network—could license the same contracts. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the contracting framework and in regulatory filing language if we replaced the underlying view with an account that treats control as relational and distributed rather than located in a single human operator.
