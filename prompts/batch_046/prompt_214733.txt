I am leading a forty-five minute session for my high school philosophy club—mostly juniors with no prior exposure to Wittgenstein—on his view that religious belief is not a matter of weighing evidence but of living within a form of life that makes certain judgments possible. I want to avoid the standard chess analogy and instead build the session around a concrete scenario in which a person treats a deep personal commitment—such as the framework through which they grieve or judge moral failure—as though it were a scientific hypothesis open to empirical disconfirmation, so that the students can feel the category error before they name it. Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual distinction must be in place before they can understand why evidence is the wrong category for religious belief; what properties the carrying example must have and lack so that it illuminates the point without reducing religion to mere preference; the predictable misconceptions they will form in order—likely that Wittgenstein is calling religion irrational, that he makes all beliefs equally valid, or that he is covertly advancing an atheist reduction of faith to social convention—and the exact point in the sequence to head off each one; a single check for understanding that reveals whether they have grasped the conceptual shift rather than just memorized a slogan; and which parts of Wittgenstein's actual position, such as his remarks on miracles or the Last Judgment, should be explicitly left out at this level with a clear statement of why.
