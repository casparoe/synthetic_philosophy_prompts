After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether my decision to stop eating meat is morally pointless if the market simply replaces my portion, I started noticing something odd about the institutions that actually govern climate mitigation. Carbon-offset registries and certification bodies operate on a very specific logic: they validate aggregate, counterfactual emission reductions at the project level—say, a reforestation initiative that avoids a calculated number of tons—but they deliberately do not trace any particular ton back to an individual purchaser or abstainer, and they systematically exclude individual consumption choices like diet from the accounting framework altogether. I want to understand what philosophical view of collective harm and individual contribution this institutional architecture quietly presupposes.

Does the practice implicitly commit to the idea that moral responsibility requires counterfactual efficacy at the individual level? Or does it rest on a statistical, threshold-based conception of harm that renders individual actions invisible by definition? Can it be reconciled with the common intuition that driving a highly inefficient vehicle is wrong even when the marginal atmospheric effect is imperceptible? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in these protocols, distinguish carefully what they strictly entail from what they merely accommodate, and explain whether a policy genuinely built on tracking individual causal contributions—one that linked every avoided ton to a specific personal choice—could retain the counterfactual framework or would have to replace it with something like a direct causal-chain or participatory account. In your answer, please separate what is broadly settled in philosophical discussions of collective action and the inefficacy objection from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any single framework—whether purely consequentialist, contractualist, or virtue-ethical—as if it were the undisputed consensus.
