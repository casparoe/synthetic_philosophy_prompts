I am preparing a seminar presentation for next week's interdisciplinary philosophy of mind and metaphysics reading group, and I would like a speaker's Q&A prep sheet tailored to the objections I will actually face rather than generic counterarguments. I will defend a somewhat unconventional thesis: that canonical thought experiments in metaphysics and philosophy of mind do not persuade through logical entailment but through what I call framing lock-in—a mechanism by which the construction of a scenario tacitly compels the audience to accept background conditions that are precisely what is at issue. To make the claim concrete, I will compare Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument and Frank Jackson's Mary argument, contending that both suppress the social and embodied context of the concepts they invoke, yet they do so with an inverse directional asymmetry: Thomson forces acceptance of bodily autonomy by analogizing dependency to a non-parasitic case, while Jackson forces rejection of physicalism by analogizing qualitative experience to scientific knowledge. I do not claim the arguments are worthless, but that philosophers systematically underestimate how much persuasive force derives from the framing contract rather than from intuition about the case itself, and that recognizing this reveals a structural feature—directional asymmetry—that is rarely acknowledged.

The audience is specific and heterogeneous. There are two senior philosophy-of-mind faculty who are committed physicalists and deeply skeptical of intuition-based arguments; three graduate students in ethics and metaphysics who are sympathetic to thought experiments and likely to defend both Thomson and Jackson; one visiting cognitive scientist for the term who studies conceptual framing and priming but lacks philosophical training; and a philosopher of science who regards these debates as empirically empty unless tied to actual scientific practice. They care about whether thought experiments yield genuine knowledge, about the boundary between heuristic reasoning and philosophical argument, and about whether interdisciplinary borrowings illuminate or merely redescribe familiar moves. I need the objections ordered by how likely and damaging they are, phrased in the terms each subgroup would use—especially the physicalists and the graduate students—each accompanied by what I should concede, what I should hold, a brief reply suitable for live discussion, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. I also need the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, with a defensive strategy should it surface.

Please structure the prep sheet around the following anticipated objections: first, the charge from the physicalist faculty that framing lock-in is merely a familiar selection-effect bias rather than a distinctive philosophical problem; second, the graduate student's defense of Thomson that I am mischaracterizing rights-based reasoning as mere framing; third, the cognitive scientist's reduction of my claim to standard psychological priming coupled with a demand for a normative upshot; fourth, the philosopher of science's reversal—that framing success is not a defect but the very feature explaining why Mary persuaded physicalists; and fifth, the hidden objection asking whether my own analysis presupposes a neutral baseline framing that I have not justified. For each, identify the likely speaker, rank its severity, state the concession and the hold, draft a two-to-three-minute reply, and flag the soft point in that reply. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prep should be usable without access to external sources.
