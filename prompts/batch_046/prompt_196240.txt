I run a first-year philosophy seminar for students with no formal logic background, and I want to teach the surprise exam paradox not as a familiar "gotcha" about Friday, but as a structural problem in collective reasoning. Plan the session's explanatory arc: the idea that must be firmly in place before the paradox can even be stated correctly—specifically, the distinction between the exam occurring on a given day and the students collectively knowing that it will occur—followed by the one concrete carrying scenario and the properties it must have (a bounded window, a public announcement rule, and a condition that makes "surprise" depend on shared reasoning rather than private feeling) and what it must lack (no ambiguity about the teacher's honesty, no probabilistic reasoning, no hidden information asymmetry). Then lay out the misconceptions students will bring, in the order they typically arise—"the teacher is lying," "the students just aren't smart enough," "this is like the liar paradox," and "surprise is merely subjective"—with the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Include a check for understanding that demonstrates they have grasped the structural failure of the induction rather than merely repeating that "it can't happen on Friday." Finally, state what should be explicitly left out at this level—formal epistemic logic, game-theoretic common-knowledge notation, and the technical distinction between surprise and doxastic revision—and explain why omitting each does not break the explanation. Inject one niche framing: present the paradox as arising not from self-reference but from a hidden transparency assumption about collective reasoning, so that the induction collapses even if every individual student is fully rational.
