I'm on the hospital ethics commitee and we just recieved a proposal for a "Craving-Cessation Palliative Protocol" for terminal patients. The PI wants to use a specific drug combo not just for pain relief but to suppress all goal-directed desire, leaving patients in a state of pure absence of craving and suffering. The defense is openly tranquilist: craving and suffering are the only intrinsic bads, so eliminating them is the sole moral aim, even if the patient stops recognizing family or caring about unfinished projects. Half the board sees this as aggressive but legitimate palliative care; the other half says it treats autonomy and personal identity as just more cravings to be erased, which dissolves the very subject we're supposed to be helping.

Draft our decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning the researchers could actually respond to. Keep it in the weeds philosophically: address whether tranquilism can consistently value the absence of craving when the mechanism also wipes out the capacity to crave, and whether there's a real distinction between suppressing suffering and suppressing the subject who suffers. Give the strongest argument for the losing side an honest treatment, not a strawman.
