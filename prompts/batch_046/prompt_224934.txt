I'm in an undergraduate historiography seminar—sixteen students, one ninety-minute session—working through whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution mark real discontinuities or are later inventions that organize the past for us. I want a runnable in-class game, not a lecture, that makes us feel the pressure of what I am calling anachronistic coherence: the demand that every past practice be read as either fully expressing one period-spirit or as misdated noise, so that hybrid practices must be suppressed to keep boundaries sharp. The rules and incentives should make us perform that suppression ourselves before anyone names it.

Design the activity with explicit phases and a minute-by-minute timeline. Use tangible materials—perhaps envelopes with practice-cards describing real intellectual activities spanning roughly 1300 to 1700, with explicit dates removed, plus three labeled bins and a discard tray. Split us into four groups of four. Give each group identical sets of cards and require them to sort every card into exactly one of the three bins; include a hidden scoring rule that rewards internal consistency (no hybrid cards allowed inside a bin) with symbolic points, and penalizes groups that keep ambiguous cards. After twenty minutes of sorting, introduce a coherence audit: each group must defend its bins, and any card that does not clearly match the bin's assigned spirit must be moved to the discard tray. Only then reveal that every discarded card describes an actual practice of a well-known figure, and that all cards genuinely belong to the sixteenth or seventeenth centuries. Follow with a debrief that maps the audit onto historiographical practice—how the triad functions as a normative filter rather than a descriptive box—and include an honest note on which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce, likely the long archival work that actually reveals hybridity, which I will supply in discussion. Specify the payoff scheme precisely, predict what will go wrong the first time—probably groups inventing hidden sub-periods or refusing to discard cards—and tell me how to handle it without breaking the experience. The activity should not depend on anyone searching for sources during the session, and it should avoid asking us to memorize specific theorists.
