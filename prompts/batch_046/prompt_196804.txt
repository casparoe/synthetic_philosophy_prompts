I'm preparing a working paper for a philosophy of war seminar, and I've become stuck on the moral equality of combatants. Every time I think I've grasped the standard position, someone introduces a case—autonomous targeting, mercenary status, or coerced conscription—that seems to split the concept in two. Before I read any more commentary, I want the full specification: what must an adequate theory of moral equality actually be able to do?

Write the desiderata that such a theory has to meet. For each requirement, state the phenomenon it must cover or the distinction it must preserve—such as the difference between moral equality and legal symmetry, between liability to be killed and permission to kill, and between responsibility for ad bellum judgment and responsibility for in bello conduct—together with the motivating case behind it. Flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable (for instance, consistency with the principle of discrimination) and which are camp-specific preferences (such as requiring a symmetrical permission structure versus allowing asymmetrical rights). Then introduce a constructed scenario that I want you to treat as a stress test: on one side, combatants execute lethal targeting decisions through predictive algorithms they do not understand but are legally bound to obey, while on the other side combatants make structurally identical decisions through direct deliberation. Specify which desiderata this scenario puts under pressure, and whether it forces a revision of moral equality itself or only of its scope conditions. Briefly score how the conventionalist account associated with Walzer, the revisionist challenge associated with McMahan, and a plausible middle position fare against your list, noting where each fails. If any subset of your desiderata proves jointly unsatisfiable given standard commitments about moral agency, say so explicitly—identifying that tension is precisely the point of the exercise.
