I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to understand a long-running disagreement in the philosophy of mind about whether introspection is a genuinely reliable source of knowledge about one’s own mental states, or whether it is essentially a form of confabulatory narrative construction. On one side are those who maintain that, in ordinary conditions, introspection provides direct epistemic access to the contents and character of one’s experiences and attitudes; they tend to treat well-known cases of split-brain confabulation, post-decisional rationalisation, and predictive-processing accounts of self-modelling not as undermining introspection generally, but as revealing specific failure modes that occur when normal conditions are disrupted. On the other side are theorists who argue that these very cases expose the underlying mechanism: introspection is not a privileged window onto mental reality but an inferential, theory-laden process that generates plausible explanations for behaviour after the fact, much as a debugging log might reconstruct the state of a running process without ever revealing the source code. Despite decades of discussion, the two sides seem to talk past one another; they agree on the empirical literature—the same studies on hemispheric disconnection and predictive mechanisms are cited by both—but neither camp has revised its core commitment.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily empirical, but conceptual: the disagreement turns on what should count as “direct access” to a mental state, with one side demanding phenomenological transparency and the other demanding causal-explanatory fidelity, rather like the distinction in computing between inspecting readable source and observing the outputs of a compiled binary whose internal representations are opaque. If that is right, then the sides may be answering different questions—one about the subjective quality of self-knowledge, the other about its epistemic architecture—under the single heading of “the reliability of introspection.” I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement: is it verbal, substantive but hidden behind shared vocabulary, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about the architecture of mind that neither side can independently justify? And given that diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as genuine progress? Would a sharper conceptual distinction between transparency and warrant resolve it, would a novel experimental design that isolates predictive mechanisms from report mechanisms break the deadlock, or is the dispute essentially ill-posed in a way that suggests we should reformulate the question entirely?
