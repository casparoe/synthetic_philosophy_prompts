I'm putting together a six-month fellowhip proposal in political philosphy and need help sharpening the framing. The idea: everyone compares Halden prison in Norway to American mass incarceration through recidivism or human rights metrics, but I think both sides miss a deeper conceptual knot. Halden's architecture and daily rhythms treat inmates as future neighbors who will return to shared spaces, while American carceral logic treats them as permanently excluded risks — what some call civil death. My question is whether rehabilitation is only morally required when we already intend social reintergration, which means the justification for humane conditions secretly depends on an implicit theory of membership rather than on intrinsic dignity alone. If that's right, then debates about prison reform are really debates about who counts as part of the polity, and the Norway/US contrast isn't just about penal philosophy but about boundary-drawing.

Why it's open: the empirical literature measures outcomes, and the normative literature usually appeals to either deterrence or dignity, but nobody I can find asks whether dignity-based arguments for rehabilitation smuggle in a membership condition without defending it.

Approach: I'm going to do conceptual analysis, not comparative fieldwork. I'll distinguish rehabilitation-as-restoration from rehabilitation-as-risk-reduction and show that the first collapses into membership theory while the second doesn't need Halden-style conditions at all. The obvious alternative is an empirical study of Norwegian outcomes, but that wouldn't settle the conceptual dependency I'm after. Another alternative is a standard egalitarian argument, but that ignores the temporal dimension of belonging.

What counts as success: a clear framework showing that the moral case for Halden-style conditions either stands without membership premises or falls back on them. Failure would be finding that rehabilitation can be fully justified by harm-minimization regardless of reintegration prospects.

Scope: six months, about 8,000 words, no data collection. Realstic if I stay disciplined and don't drift into immigration law.
