I serve on the peer-review board of an interdisciplinary journal that receives comparative-historical submissions arguing that institutions, geographic endowments, or cultural norms explain cross-national differences in prosperity. Over time I have noticed that authors routinely treat these three factors as competing hypotheses without recognizing that they are structurally entangled: geographic conditions influence institutional design, institutions reshape how geography is exploited, and cultural norms both stabilize and adapt to both. I want to operationalize this epistemic problem into a reusable referee protocol—a step-by-step evaluation flow I can apply in about thirty minutes—so that I can distinguish submissions that genuinely address causal identification from those that merely construct plausible narratives.

The protocol should treat every empirical historical claim as facing a trilemma: either geography is exogenous and institutions adapt, institutions are exogenous and geography is mediated, or both are driven by an unobserved cultural vector. Design a sequence of explicit tests—pass, fail, or indeterminate—that forces an author to commit to one horn and then defend it. The steps should cover: (1) specification of an exogenous source of variation, whether a natural experiment, an instrumental variable, or a clearly bounded discontinuity, with explicit failure conditions for narrative counterfactuals that lack such a source; (2) distinction between institutional persistence—the claim that institutions remain stable and drive outcomes—and institutional selection—the claim that prior conditions filter which institutions survive—since observational equivalence between the two undermines most comparative case studies; (3) explicit treatment of reverse causation, particularly whether prosperity itself reshapes institutions or geographic exploitation patterns; (4) evaluation of culture not as a residual category but as a measurable mediator with its own identification strategy, including whether cultural traits are instrumented or merely inferred from outcomes; and (5) explicit marking of judgment calls that cannot be proceduralized, such as weighing the plausibility of a counterfactual when no instrument exists but the narrative is highly coherent.

The deliverable should include three worked applications to hypothetical submissions: one in which the protocol endorses an institutional claim supported by a credible exogenous shock to institutional design; one in which it rejects a geographic-determinist argument because the author treats climate and soil quality as fixed constraints without modeling institutional adaptation; and one that yields an answer I will find personally unwelcome—specifically, a culturally-mediated explanation that survives the identification checks but conflicts with my prior belief that culture is largely endogenous—showing precisely where the protocol requires a subjective judgment call that I must flag rather than disguise.

Finally, the protocol should state its own limits: it cannot generate primary historical data, it should not be applied to explicitly theoretical or normative papers that make no empirical causal claim, and it will systematically misfire when authors use comparative methods that rely on process tracing rather than variation-based identification. Write this as a practical checklist with hard cases embedded, from the perspective of someone who has read extensively in rationalist discussions of causal inference and epistemic humility but needs something that can survive a thirty-minute deadline.
