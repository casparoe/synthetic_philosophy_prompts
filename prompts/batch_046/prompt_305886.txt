Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers—Dr. Aris Venn, who defends guilt as the superior moral emotion, and Dr. Sera Kwon, who defends shame—debating whether a culture can genuinely choose which one to cultivate. Venn should argue that guilt’s act-specific structure (wrong act → internal sanction → reparative obligation) protects the self from totalization and that modern restorative frameworks show how institutions can deliberately foster guilt by treating harm as a breach of duty rather than a character stain; he should also claim that guilt’s “debt logic” allows the offender to discharge wrongs without submitting to communal spectacle. Kwon should reply that this “debt logic” privatizes moral failure, turning the offender into a creditor-manager of his own conscience and hiding the relational reality of harm; she should propose, as a counter-institution, a “recognition tribunal” in which wrongdoers present their actions to affected parties not for punishment or forgiveness, but so that moral standing is re-constituted through witnessed acknowledgment—what she calls “exposure-to-the-other” rather than humiliation. Have them clash over a specific case: a society that has replaced courts and private confession with these tribunals, but now faces a wrongdoer who refuses to participate, insisting that his guilt is “between him and the victim alone” and that forced visibility violates his autonomy. Venn should take this refusal as proof that guilt preserves private moral agency against coercive exposure. Kwon should counter that the refusal actually exposes guilt’s hidden dependency on an audience (the victim, the law) and that “privacy” here is merely a refusal to let one’s self be intersubjectively constituted. Each must respond to the other’s actual point, not talk past it: Venn should concede that guilt can degenerate into narcissistic self-punishment, and Kwon should concede that shame without safeguards collapses into humiliation. End with them locating their real disagreement—not over which emotion is “better,” but over whether moral selfhood is primarily constituted through internal obligation-management or through relational recognition. Keep both voices equally sharp, with no strawman.
