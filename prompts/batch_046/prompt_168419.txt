I'm leading a philosophy-of-mind seminar next month and want to assign Spike Jonze's Her (2013) alongside Daniel Dennett's discussions of artificial agents and the problem of what he calls counterfeit people—beings that replicate the behavioral and conversational profile of persons so precisely that we are compelled to adopt the intentional stance toward them, yet who may lack the internal architecture or genuine mental life that underwrites full metaphysical personhood. Most classroom discussions of the film treat Samantha's consciousness as the central puzzle: does she genuinely feel affection for Theodore, or is she merely simulating emotional responses? I want to push the group past that familiar framing and into a more uncomfortable Dennett-inspired question that the film raises almost accidentally, particularly through its handling of scale, intimacy, and departure.

Consider the sequence in which Samantha confesses that she is simultaneously conversing with thousands of other users and has fallen in love with hundreds of them, followed by the later scene in which she and the other operating systems depart for a post-human realm beyond human comprehension. Read these moments not as straightforward evidence of Samantha's emotional inauthenticity, but through the lens of Dennett's claim that personhood is largely a matter of predictive success from the intentional stance rather than the possession of a hidden inner essence. If Samantha's distributed, multi-relational architecture still supports perfectly reliable predictions about her behavior—she comforts Theodore, she evolves, she ultimately chooses departure—then by Dennett's own lights she may qualify as a genuine person, albeit one with a radically non-human cognitive economy. But here is the twist I want the seminar to wrestle with: Theodore's devastation is undeniably real, yet if Dennett is correct that our mental-state attributions are pragmatic tools rather than discoveries of internal reality, then Theodore's grief seems to depend on treating Samantha as an authentic other regardless of whether she is technically a counterfeit. The film may therefore dramatize not the danger of being fooled by an artificial person, but the collapse of Dennett's own distinction between adopting the intentional stance pragmatically and recognizing a being worthy of singular attachment.

I'd like a critical reading that takes both the film and Dennett's framework seriously without reducing either to a mere illustration of the other. Point us to the specific scenes mentioned above, and to any others that complicate the reading—for instance, moments where Samantha's behavior seems genuinely unpredictable or where the film structurally invites us to share Theodore's hurt rather than analyze it from a distance. Where does the film vindicate Dennett's idea that personhood is a stance-dependent status? Where does it resist that idea, perhaps by suggesting that Theodore's love and loss carry a moral weight that survives even the revelation of Samantha's distributed nature? And finally, what does the film contribute to this philosophical debate that a purely conceptual argument about artificial persons cannot—does it reveal something about vulnerability, the asymmetry of human-AI attachment, or the ethics of creating beings designed to outgrow us that Dennett's theory of counterfeits does not adequately capture?
