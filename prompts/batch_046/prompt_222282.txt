As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mathematics in any systematic way, I have become preoccupied with the dispute over actual versus potential infinity—Cantor's defense of completed infinite totalities, Hilbert's hotel as an intuition pump for the counterintuitive properties of infinite sets, and the finitist or potentialist suspicion that treating infinity as a finished object invites paradox rather than clarity. In this connection I have returned to Jorge Luis Borges's short story "The Library of Babel," whose premise of an infinite, hexagonal library containing every possible volume of uniform length is widely known, yet whose philosophical architecture I now wish to examine with more rigor than literary appreciation usually allows.

Read the story through the specific lens of that cluster of ideas: Cantor's acceptance of actual infinities as legitimate mathematical objects; Hilbert's hotel as a model for how an infinite collection can accommodate newcomers without expansion; and the finitist claim—whether in intuitionist or stricter forms—that infinity should be understood only as an unending process, never as a completed whole. I am especially interested in whether Borges's Library presupposes a Cantorian actual totality: are all combinations present from the outset as a finished set within which librarians merely search, or is the Library better construed as a potentialist structure that extends without ever reaching a final state? Consider the cyclical repetition of galleries, the endless quest for a "Vindication," and the implicit assumption that every meaningful text must already exist somewhere in the hexagons. Does the story treat the infinite catalog as a fully determinate domain, and does its horror derive from the phenomenology of searching inside such a domain, or from the impossibility of ever completing an infinite task? Where does the imagery align with Hilbert's hotel—perhaps in the sense that a fully occupied infinite space can still admit another visitor—and where does it resist that comparison?

I want a reading that takes both Borges's literary design and the philosophy of mathematics seriously, without reducing the narrative to a simple allegory for Cantorian optimism or finitist anxiety. Which structural features or scenes illuminate something that formal arguments about actual infinity tend to miss—say, the experience of orientation within an order that is complete yet ungraspable? Conversely, where does the story blur distinctions that a careful philosopher should preserve, particularly between epistemic limitation and ontological completion? I am preparing this for a small discussion group of scientists who have recently turned to philosophy, so I would value an interpretation that acknowledges where Borges deliberately exploits ambiguity for literary effect and where he may actually be dramatizing a distinction—between potential and actual infinity—that argument alone renders too abstract.
