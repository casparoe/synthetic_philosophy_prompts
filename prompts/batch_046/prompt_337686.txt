Since taking up my post as liaison between our Amsterdam studio and our Osaka distribution partners six months ago, I have become preoccupied with a tension that now feels personally unavoidable. On one side, my Dutch colleagues practise what they call bespreekbaarheid: a norm that anything may be said plainly, with the understanding that directness is respect rather than rudeness. They rarely offer the softened refusal or exaggerated praise that I grew up treating as ordinary politeness. On the other side, our Japanese partners operate through honne and tatemae, and through kuuki wo yomu—reading the air—so that refusal is rarely spoken but always understood, and harmony is preserved through strategic omission rather than explicit agreement. I have come to think of the Dutch style as a culture that has eliminated white lies altogether, substituting transparency for deception, and of the Japanese style as one that has refined the white lie into a social art. If both frameworks sustain high-trust cooperation—the Dutch are often cited for exceptionally direct interpersonal transparency, the Japanese for durable relational harmony—then does the moral status of a white lie depend on which cooperative framework one inhabits? Or does the Dutch example show that white lies are never genuinely necessary, being merely a failure of courage masked as cultural sensitivity, with Japanese indirectness representing a collective departure from the Gricean maxim of Quality? I want to understand whether there is a universal wrong here, or only a culturally indexed one. In your answer, please distinguish what is settled in moral philosophy and pragmatics from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view—such as the claim that indirectness is essentially deceptive, or that directness eliminates all face-saving omission—as if it were the consensus.
