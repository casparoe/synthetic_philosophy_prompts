I am a senior policy adviser to a regional transport authority. We have inherited a by-law, enacted in 1972, that prohibits the installation of wireless signalling equipment within 200 metres of any listed bridge. The original parliamentary debate records were lost in a flood, and the engineers who drafted it have died. A contractor now wants to modernise a line that crosses three such bridges and proposes not only repealing the by-law but substituting it with a proprietary algorithmic risk-assessment model that calculates bridge stability in real time; the model's training data and weightings are commercially confidential. Our legal team says the old rule is a dead letter because no modern safety case references it, and adopting the algorithm would save eighteen months and several million pounds. The bridge-preservation society objects that we should not discard an inherited constraint whose purpose we have forgotten, especially when the proposed replacement is itself a black box whose reasoning we cannot inspect. Our chair wants to know whether the society's objection expresses a coherent philosophical principle about institutional memory and inherited practice, or whether it is merely sentimental resistance to technological progress. Can you advise whether there is a serious defence—grounded in thinking about prescription, social knowledge distributed across time, and the epistemic status of rules whose justification has been lost—of retaining the spatial restriction at least until the original rationale is reconstructed? Please answer in British English, applying the conceptual framework to the specifics of this case rather than offering generic guidance.
