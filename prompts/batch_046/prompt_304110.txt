As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and rational choice, I have been trying to articulate my own intuitions about preference under risk in situations structurally analogous to the classic Allais problem, but embedded in scientific or physical contexts. I want to know whether there is a single coherent principle that captures all of my judgments, or whether consistency forces me to abandon at least one. Here are my verdicts on several concrete scenarios.

First, consider two research-funding allocations. Option A guarantees one million dollars regardless of experimental outcome. Option B offers an 89 percent chance of one million, a 10 percent chance of five million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing. I prefer A to B. Now consider Option C: an 11 percent chance of one million and an 89 percent chance of nothing; and Option D: a 10 percent chance of five million and a 90 percent chance of nothing. Here I prefer D to C. I hold both preferences firmly.

Second, suppose the probabilities are not given a priori but must be read from a noisy physical instrument with a known, irreducible error distribution. If I can reduce the noise at negligible cost, I feel I should do so before deciding; yet if the noise is irreducible, my preference pattern remains identical to the first case. This suggests to me that my judgments depend partly on my epistemic situation, not solely on the objective gamble.

Third, in a sequential formulation, I am offered B now, with the assurance that if I reject it I will certainly be offered D later. I want to reject B now and then accept D later, which strikes me as dynamically inconsistent with my first two verdicts, though I still feel the pull of both.

Fourth, imagine every outcome in both pairs is augmented by a certain baseline grant of fifty thousand dollars. Under this shift, my intuition reverses: I now prefer B over A, while still preferring D over C. This baseline-dependence seems hard to square with any stable utility representation.

Fifth, scale the stakes downward to pennies or upward by a factor of one thousand. At the penny scale I feel almost risk-neutral and the independence axiom is satisfied; at the massive scale the Allais pattern is strongest.

Is there a principled account—perhaps involving nonlinear risk attitudes, regret, or some form of epistemic weighting—that can accommodate all five verdicts without collapsing into ad hoc adjustments? Or must I drop the sequential inconsistency in the third case, or the reversal in the fourth? In particular, can any account preserve both the scale-sensitivity in the fifth case and the baseline reversal in the fourth? Please separate what is well-established in formal decision theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who study these paradoxes, and do not present any particular resolution—such as rank-dependent utility, prospect theory, or ambiguity aversion—as though it were the consensus view.
