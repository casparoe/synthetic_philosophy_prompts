I have recently retired from a career in molecular biology and, in attempting to read philosophy of science with some rigour, have constructed an argument concerning biological function that now strikes me as suspiciously self-defeating. I should be grateful if you could help me locate the error.

Consider the following reasoning, which I present with numbered premises.

(1) On the etiological account, a trait T in an organism O has the function F only if T was selected for F in O's evolutionary lineage.
(2) Consider a synthetic pacemaker fashioned from genetically modified cardiac tissue, implanted in a patient, which regulates heartbeat through a feedback loop identical to that of the natural sinoatrial node, but which was designed de novo in a laboratory and possesses no evolutionary history of selection for cardiac regulation.
(3) By (1), this synthetic tissue lacks the function of regulating heartbeat, despite performing that causal role with perfect fidelity.
(4) Yet clinical medicine treats it as functioning correctly when it regulates the rhythm and as malfunctioning when it fails; this is not mere metaphor, but determines surgical revision, pharmacological adjustment, and insurance classification.
(5) Therefore, either medicine is systematically mistaken in its functional attributions, or the etiological account is inadequate for biomedical contexts.
(6) To avoid this, one might adopt a causal-role account, according to which a trait's function is fixed by its present contribution to the capacities of the containing system.
(7) But consider an oncogenic mutation that accelerates cell division, thereby contributing causally to the tumour's growth and, consequently, to the organism's premature mortality. On the causal-role view, accelerating division is a function of the mutation within the tumour-system.
(8) However, we do not regard cancerous proliferation as a proper function of the mutation; rather, we classify it as dysfunction or pathological misbehaviour, not as the realisation of a legitimate functional role.
(9) Thus, if we apply the causal-role account consistently, we must attribute proper functions to pathological processes, which collapses the normative distinction between health and disease that underpins diagnosis.
(10) Consequently, whichever account we adopt, we are forced either to deny obvious functional attributions in synthetic biology or to erase the normative boundary between function and dysfunction.

I feel that something has slipped between steps (3) and (4), or perhaps between (7) and (8), yet I cannot name the equivocation or the hidden premise. Which step ought I to reject? And if I reject the etiological account to preserve biomedical practice, what cost does that exact for evolutionary explanations of natural traits? If I reject the causal-role account to preserve the normative distinction, how do I avoid the conclusion that synthetic organs are functionally vacuous?

Please diagnose the flaw, if there is one, and tell me what each rejection costs.
