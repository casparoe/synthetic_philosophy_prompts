(1) for kierkegaard the ethical is the universal -- if an act is right, its right for anyone in the same situation.
(2) abraham suspends the ethical (he's about to kill isaac) because he stands in an absolute relation to the absolute.
(3) but "standing in an absolute relation" is still a kind of property, so if abraham has it then others could in principle have it too.
(4) so the justification for suspending ethics is actually universalizable: anyone with that property should do the same.
(5) that means the suspension isnt a true singular exception, its just a higher-level rule ("god commands > ethics").
(6) but kierkegaard insists the knight of faith is totally isolated, outside all mediation, with no rule that applies to anyone else.
(7) so to keep abrahams act singular, kierkegaard has to deny that "absolute relation" is a shareable property at all.
(8) but then theres no public criterion to distinguish abraham from a fanatic who just claims the same private relation.
(9) therefore either the suspension collapses into hegelian universalism, or it becomes arbitrary and loses all normative weight.
