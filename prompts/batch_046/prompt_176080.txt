I'm a doctoral student in economic sociology working on development-policy evaluation, and I've encountered a design pattern in entrepreneurship programs run by international NGOs across parts of sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia that puzzles me philosophically. These programs routinely begin with intensive values-based modules—structured workshops on future orientation, individual initiative, and risk acceptance—before participants receive any capital access, market linkages, or technical training. Success is evaluated partly through attitudinal surveys measuring shifts in these dispositions, rather than solely through business-formation or income metrics. This design treats entrepreneurial culture as an independent causal input that can be engineered in isolation from material conditions.

Yet the comparative-historical literature with which I'm engaging—particularly the critical reception of Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic—suggests that macro-level capitalist development occurred across Confucian, Catholic, Hindu, and secular institutional contexts without requiring the specific ascetic ethos Weber isolated, and that cultural dispositions often appear endogenous to property rights, credit structures, and labor-market incentives rather than as autonomous drivers.

The tension seems to be this: practitioners behave as if culture is a manipulable lever, while the academic field treats values either as epiphenomenal to institutions or as post-hoc rationalizations of material interests. I want to understand what philosophical commitments license the NGO practice. What implicit ontology of values and implicit theory of causation does this program architecture presuppose? Does it commit to a form of methodological individualism about culture, or to a multi-level causal model in which values operate with independence from institutional feedback? Reconstruct the implicit theory rigorously, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it. Assess whether that implicit theory is coherent with the comparative-historical evidence, and identify what specific changes to evaluation metrics or sequencing of interventions would be required if we replaced it with a view that treats entrepreneurial values as strictly endogenous to incentive structures.
