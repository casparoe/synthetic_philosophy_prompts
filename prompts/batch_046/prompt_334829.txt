I'm applying for a one-semester graduate seminar paper of roughly 5,000 words, and I want to turn a half-formed idea into a concrete proposal. The standard literature on Gödel's modal ontological argument tends to cluster around two questions: whether S5 modal logic is appropriate, and whether the premise that existence is a perfection can be defended. I want to pursue a different tension that these debates largely treat as settled—namely, that Gödel's central notion of a "positive property" operates simultaneously in an axiological register (properties that contribute to perfection or goodness) and in a modal-formal register (properties that are not self-contradictory and are compatible with instantiation in some possible world). My precise question is this: if we rigorously separate these two registers—defining purely axiological P-properties and purely modal C-properties, then reconstructing Gödel's proof using each in isolation—does the inference from possible existence to necessary existence survive in either version, or does it depend on an implicit and undefended bridge principle linking perfection to modal consistency? I think existing answers leave this open precisely because critics and defenders alike treat "positive property" as univocal; they assess the modal axioms or the possibility premise without asking whether the argument's validity requires the coincidence of normative and logical registers.

My approach will be conceptual reconstruction rather than historical scholarship. Over the semester, I plan to do three things in sequence: first, an informal but precise reconstruction of Gödel's original argument tracking exactly where the hybrid notion does work; second, the construction of two purified variants, one substituting P-properties and one substituting C-properties, with explicit notation of which inference steps—especially the move from "it is possible that a being has all such properties" to "it exists in every world"—fail or hold; third, a philosophical evaluation of whether any bridge principle connecting P to C can be defended without assuming that axiological perfection entails modal accessibility, which would risk begging the question. I am not proposing formal symbolic logic beyond what can be presented clearly in prose; the deliverable should be readable by philosophers without specialized training in modal semantics. The obvious alternative—historical analysis of Gödel's notebooks—would miss the conceptual point, while a purely formal logic project would exceed the scope of a philosophy seminar.

A successful result would be one of three outcomes: the hybrid argument succeeds only when both registers coincide, revealing a hidden ambiguity that either weakens the argument (if the coincidence is implausible) or strengthens it (if a defensible bridge principle can be articulated); one purified version succeeds, showing whether the argument is really about goodness or about modal consistency in disguise; or both purified versions fail independently, demonstrating that the original argument is invalid as formulated but repairable in one direction only. The project fails if the distinction between P and C turns out to be purely verbal—if every axiologically perfect property just is modally consistent by definition, leaving the reconstruction unchanged. Given one semester, I will limit myself to this reconstruction and to the evaluation of a single bridge principle, not to a full theological defense.
