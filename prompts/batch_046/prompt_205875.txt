I am a journalist who covers philosophy of science and ethics, and I sit on the ethics advisory board of a regional natural-history museum. Our board must vote at the meeting three weeks from Thursday on whether to authorize a proposed permanent gallery titled Moral Status: From Rational Agency to Sentient Life. The curatorial plan centers on Immanuel Kant’s claim that direct moral obligations extend only to beings capable of rational self-legislation, which places non-human animals outside the sphere of direct duty and makes our treatment of them a matter of indirect obligation grounded in human self-cultivation and the cultivation of sympathy. A staff-and-community coalition has objected that presenting this framework as the historical baseline—without sufficient critical framing—functionally licenses instrumental use of animals and violates our institutional commitment to respectful representation of all sentient life. The board’s policy permits curatorial amendments for ethical alignment but does not define when an amendment is sufficient versus when rejection is warranted.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course—approval, approval with specified mandatory amendments and a six-month review checkpoint, or rejection with conditions for future resubmission—together with an explicit timeline for implementation. The reasoning should present the Kantian distinction between direct and indirect moral status with philosophical precision, avoiding both straw-man caricature and uncritical endorsement; it should also engage seriously with the coalition’s strongest objection, namely that indirect-duty frameworks structurally subordinate animal welfare to human moral psychology and therefore cannot provide stable protection against instrumentalization. The memo should be accessible to board members without advanced philosophical training, yet rigorous enough that a philosopher would not find it superficial. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; reference specific conceptual distinctions rather than particular editions or scholars, and avoid verbatim quotations from historical texts.
