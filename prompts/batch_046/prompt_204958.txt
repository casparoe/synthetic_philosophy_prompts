i run a grad seminar on historiography, about 10 students, 90 minutes. design a classroom activty or game that makes them feel the pull of history-from-below and microhistory versus the history of elites and states — not just talk about it. split them into two groups, each gets a different "archive" about the same 18th-century grain riot. group A gets the magistrate's report: rich, dated, names officials, frames the crowd as criminal, everything in calendar months. group B gets three fragments of oral testimony collected sixty years later by a folklorist — but here's the injected twist: these fragments don't just offer "the people's view"; they narrate events in seasonal/agricultural time (planting, harvest, frost) rather than calendar months, and they conflate the riot with a prior famine year, so the two groups aren't just seeing different perspectives but working with incommensurable temporal frameworks. rules and payoffs: they have 35 minutes to reconstruct "what happened and why" and present to each other, but they must assign causation (weather, market manipulation, local grievance, state policy) and defend it using only thier archive. then the class votes on the best explanation, but each student can only vote after citing at least one source from each archive — and they'll notice the causation categories themselves come from group A's framework. then a debrief that maps each stage onto the conceptual tension: whose time counts, whose causation is legible, what gets erased in translation. be frank about what the game can't reproduce (actual archival silence vs reconstructed absence) and predict what will go wrong (group B will probably feel gaslit by group A's precision, and the vote will feel rigged). write it casual, maybe include a typo or two.
