hey, i'm working on a story about how conflict-data NGOs frame "global peace" and i'm stuck on something that feels philosophically weird. every year the big reports — Uppsala, the UN homicide briefings, the Human Security Report crowd — announce that violence is at historic lows, but they always, *always* normailze by population. Per capita death rates only. At the same time they define violence super narrowly: direct, intentional physical harm by one agent to another. Famine deaths from a blockade? Not violence. And here's the kicker: they treat "counterfactual" deaths — ones that would have happened but were prevented by a peacekeeping deployment — as totally off the books. Too speculative, they say. But they don't treat population growth as speculative; they just scale it away like it's morally neutral. So you get this asymmetry: adding more people dilutes the badness of each killing, but imagining a world where a war was stopped is fantasy.

What philosophical commitments does that whole bundle presuppose? Does the per-capita preference commit them to harm being an average rather than an aggregate? Does the narrow definition presuppose violence is an intrinsic property of an act, not relational or structural? And what's doing the work in the asymmetry — is there an implicit view that there's a "fact of the matter" about global violence trends that only direct, actually-occuring killings can track, while counterfactual baselines are just made up? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say what's strictly required vs. what just fits well, and tell me if the package is coherent or if different parts pull in opposite directions. Then: what would these reports have to change if they replaced that framework with one that treated absolute numbers and counterfactual baselines — like "potential killings prevented" — as equally real? And is there any consistent defense of the asymmetry, or is it just begging the question?
