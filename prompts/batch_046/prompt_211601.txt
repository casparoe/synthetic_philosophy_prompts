Question: Explain the difference between productive causation and counterfactual dependence, then apply both to Case A (a certified lifeguard sees a swimmer in distress and chooses not to enter; the swimmer drowns) and Case B (a legislature with authority never passes a carbon-tax over ten years; a specific coastal flood then occurs). Is the legislative failure an omission that causes the flood? Then address the preemption problem for omissions: two bystanders both fail to call emergency services for a heart-attack victim, and either call alone would have saved him. Is either omission a cause of the death?

My roommate's answer: Omissions can't be productive causes since there's no physical event—just an absence. But they can still be causes via counterfactual dependence: if the omission hadn't occured, the result wouldn't have either. In Case A the lifeguard's failure is definatly a counterfactual cause; if he'd jumped in, the swimmer lives. It's not productive because he didn't physically affect the swimmer. Case B is different: a legislature isn't one agent, and no specific vote was missed, so there's no discrete omission-event. Without an agent-level event, there's nothing to carry counterfactual dependence, so the legislative failure is just background conditions, not a cause of the flood. The emissions caused it.

For preemption: both bystanders failed. If either had called, he lives. But with omissions there's no positive event to overdetermine. You can't split causation between two non-events, so actually neither omission is a cause—the death results from the joint absence, but assigning either one violates the rule that omissions lack productive reality. If you said both were causes, you'd be treating absences like strikes, which collapses the distinction.
