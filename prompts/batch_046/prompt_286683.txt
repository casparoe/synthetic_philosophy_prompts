Design a decision-tree taxonomy of positions on I.J. Good's result that free information never lowers a rational agent's expected utility. Start from a root commitment: does the agent accept the Bayesian framework with stable preferences, perfect recall, and non-strategic isolation? From there, branch by which premise is rejected: preference stability upon learning (e.g., learning one's future suffering changes present valuations), perfect recall or costless processing, single-agent non-strategic framing, or the reduction of epistemic value to expected utility. At each node, formulate the split as a concrete diagnostic question—such as whether an agent who learns a treatment will fail should retain her prior preference for attempting it—so that a respondent can answer without knowing the philosophical label. Every leaf should state a named position or 'unnamed,' summarize its claim in one sentence, and identify its most awkward commitment, for example having to endorse paying for ignorance or treating coherence as purely instrumental. Include branches that generate unoccupied combinations in the logical space and briefly diagnose each gap: incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate, such as strategic ignorance in multi-agent settings versus preference-instability in single-agent settings. Target the instrument at philosophy of science graduate students who need to locate themselves in the space rather than memorize names. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; every position must be fully described within the tree itself.
