A colleague who studies collective moral responsibility recently summarized the Fischer controversy for me in these terms. He claimed that Fritz Fischer's archival work—especially his interpretation of the September Program and related wartime documents—demonstrated that Germany's leadership had formulated detailed plans for continental domination years before 1914, thereby proving that the Kaiserreich alone bore both causal and moral responsibility for the war's outbreak. He further argued that since Fischer's evidence is now largely undisputed among historians, modern Germany's reluctance to embrace a form of national guilt represents a collective failure of historical accountability, and that holding a nation responsible is essentially equivalent to assigning moral culpability to each present-day citizen for actions taken by the state. I find this reasoning questionable, particularly because I understand Fischer's actual thesis to concern the risk-calculating behavior of specific elites in 1914 rather than a long-premeditated master plan, and because the inference from archival findings about state actors to collective moral obligations seems to require philosophical premises that historical scholarship alone cannot establish. Could you audit this summary? I want to know which parts accurately capture Fischer's argument and the subsequent historiographical debate, which parts conflate elite strategic intention with national causation or smuggle in unstated premises about collective guilt, and whether the normative conclusion about modern responsibility survives once those distinctions are made explicit.
