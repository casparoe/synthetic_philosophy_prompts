I work as a software engineer, and I have been trying to understand judicial review by treating democratic authority like layered system architecture—specifically, the rule that an overriding layer should be accountable to the authority that commissioned the layer it overrides. Here is the argument I derived, which now seems to collapse into contradiction. (1) If one institutional layer can permanently override outputs produced by the democratically authorized layer, then that overriding layer must be accountable to the same democratic authority, or the lower layer is not genuinely authoritative. (2) Constitutional courts can permanently strike down statutes enacted by legislatures. (3) Constitutional judges are not accountable to the current electorate. (4) Therefore judicial review makes the legislature non-authoritative in a way that violates democratic legitimacy. (5) The same reasoning applies to any unremovable veto point over democratic outputs: independent central banks, administrative agencies with binding rule-making power, and constitutional amendment rules that require supermajorities. (6) But a democracy that lacks any protected rules—rules that a temporary majority cannot repeal—is unstable and can vote itself out of existence. (7) So democratic legitimacy requires both that every veto layer be electorally accountable and that at least some veto layers not be. (8) Because (7) is contradictory, the fault must lie in (1). (9) However, if I abandon (1), I also lose the intuition that judicial review is different from central banking—courts apply a pre-committed constitutional text, whereas banks exercise discretionary judgment—because both are unaccountable veto points. Where exactly does the argument slip? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me in terms of preserving that distinction?
