I have been trying to think through a distinction that keeps coming up in conversations with friends about music made by algorithms. There is now a program that generates original-sounding compositions which many listeners find genuinely moving, but it was built by scraping copyrighted recordings without the musicians' consent. The company behind it argues that since no single song was copied exactly, no particular artist was harmed; my friends who enjoy the music say we ought to judge the sound itself rather than the process behind it. I find myself stuck on a middle case: the harm feels real, yet it is distributed across thousands of people in pieces so small that no individual can point to a specific injury, while collectively it still resembles a kind of theft. This is not quite the familiar problem of separating a bad person from their good art, because here there is no single artist with a biography—only a statistical system that absorbed other people's labor in an impersonal way.

I wonder how a thinker like Leo Tolstoy, who held strong moral convictions about art and believed it should serve truth rather than exploit people for pleasure, might have responded if he encountered this kind of distributed, machine-driven extraction. Would he have said that the emotional power of the music proves its moral legitimacy regardless of how it was made, or would he have argued that the very absence of a personal wrongdoer makes the corruption harder to see and therefore more dangerous? I am not asking for a scholarly reconstruction of his writings, just an honest attempt to imagine his reasoning applied to this specific situation where the "artist" is a corporate model and the wrong, if there is one, is statistical rather than personal.
