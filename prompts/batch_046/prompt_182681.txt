I'm writing a paper about institutional design and I keep thinking about this weird pattern in grad school admissions and funding. A lot of top programs now require applicants to submit not just writing samples but also evidence of having done unpaid research assistant work or long-term volunteer fieldwork — like, you have to show you've already borne a big cost before they'll even consider funding you. In fields like marine biology or archival history its even worse, cause the "cost" is often years of self-funded travel to remote sites or archives with no stipend. The idea seems to be that if you really want it you'll suffer for it first, which feels like it maps onto that handicap principle thing from biology where only the fit can afford the expensive display. But I'm not sure what philosophical commitments are baked into treating suffering and endurance as a credential, or wether we're just smuggling in an idea about honest signals being necessarily wasteful.

So here's my ask: take that practice — using prior unpaid labor and sustained personal cost as an explicit or implicit filter for who gets funded — and tell me what theory of value and rational communication it presupposes. Does it only make sense if we think honest signals have to be wasteful? Could it be compatible with a view that the cost is just practical screening, not a signal? And if we replaced the handicap logic with something like pure merit or need-based access, what parts of the institution would have to change? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, but also where it's underdetermined — like maybe several theories license the same practice, and some of them dont even need the biology metaphor at all.
