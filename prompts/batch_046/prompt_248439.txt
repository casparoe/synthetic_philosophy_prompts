I spent three decades in experimental physics before retiring, and for the past two years I have approached philosophy with the same patience I once reserved for calibrating instruments. I have been auditing a lecture series by a strategic-studies scholar — I will call her Dr. Voss — on the ethics of nuclear deterrence, and I have recorded her judgments on five conceptual cases. I now wish to apply her framework to a scenario I have constructed, but I find her underlying rule ambiguous.

Her verdicts so far are these.

First, she endorsed the 1962 naval quarantine and its implicit risk of escalation over Soviet missiles in Cuba, on the grounds that the threatened interest was vital, that decision authority remained centralized in a single deliberative body, and that the measure was fully reversible.

Second, she rejected any posture in which automated sensors trigger pre-programmed retaliation without human intervention, regardless of how vital the threatened interest might be.

Third, she approved a secret, non-treaty assurance to defend an ally with nuclear means, but only when that ally's security was genuinely vital and only because the assurance could be withdrawn unilaterally; she rejected the identical assurance when applied to a peripheral ally.

Fourth, she rejected a formal, legally binding treaty that automatically committed a state to nuclear retaliation on behalf of a non-nuclear ally, irrespective of that ally's strategic importance.

Fifth, she judged unjustified the actual execution of a limited tactical nuclear demonstration in an uninhabited region, even when intended solely to signal resolve during a conventional conflict over vital resources, on the ground that crossing the nuclear threshold eliminates the reversibility that makes brinkmanship rational rather than suicidal.

The new case is this. A democratic state faces an attack that simultaneously threatens its own existential survival and the border of a peripheral ally. In response, it activates a protocol that pre-delegates manual — not automated — launch authority to regional commanders, but only if central command is confirmed destroyed. At the same time, it issues a public ultimatum that is explicitly conditional: retaliation is promised only if both conditions, the destruction of central command and the attack on the ally, are satisfied. No nuclear weapon is fired; forces are positioned and messages are transmitted, but the nuclear threshold remains uncrossed.

What should we predict Dr. Voss will say about this case? Identify which of her five earlier judgments is the closest analog. Then, treating her record as a body of precedents that ought to cohere, recommend what she should say for consistency, and state honestly whether her record genuinely underdetermines the answer. If there is more than one plausible rule that fits all five verdicts equally well, articulate at least two such rules, say what each predicts about this new case, indicate which reconstruction you regard as best with your reasoning, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two rules.
