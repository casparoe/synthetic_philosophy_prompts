Write a dialogue between two grad students in a lounge with bad coffee: one is a republican-liberty person (freedom as non-domination) and the other is defending this veto-node version of negative liberty. Give them a concrete case to chew on — a citizen lives under an old statute that lets the state seize her property for "public interest" without judicial review, but it hasn't been used in decades and everyone knows the current government won't touch it. The republican should say she's still unfree because the power remains arbitrary regardless of exercise; the other should say she's free, since there's no actual interference and the dormant procedural check removes arbitrariness in practice. Make sure they actually respond to each other's points: the republican should press that domination tracks a structural relation, not a prediction about use, and the negative-liberty person should push back that treating a never-activated pathway as a real constraint smuggles in a counterfactual that's too speculative. Don't let either be a strawman — both should know the standard distinctions but be willing to bend them. Keep it casual; maybe one accidentally writes "dominization" or misspells "arbitrary" as "arbitary" in the flow of talk. No citations or reading lists needed — just the back-and-forth.
