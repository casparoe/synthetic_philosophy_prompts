I am preparing a feature on whether secular societies can sustain moral trust without religious scaffolding, and I have built an argument that seems to prove secular ethics is either impossible or secretly theistic. I present it in numbered steps.

(1) For cooperation to persist in low-observability domains—anonymous charity, tax compliance without audit risk, whistleblowing without legal protection—individuals need a credible reason to bear costs that no human audience will ever witness.
(2) The only psychologically stable source of such a reason is belief in what I will call an extra-social auditor: an omniscient, morally responsive observer who records hidden actions and eventually balances them outside the transparency gradient that governs ordinary reputation.
(3) Therefore, in a population where this belief is genuinely absent—not institutionally marginalized, but psychologically absent—cooperation in these domains should decay toward the baseline predicted by standard rational-choice models.
(4) Highly secular societies, such as the Nordic social democracies, display robust cooperation in precisely these low-observability domains.
(5) So either these societies are not genuinely secular—meaning they retain hidden religious substrates—or premise (2) is false.
(6) One might reply that internalized secular norms or reputation mechanisms substitute for divine oversight. But apply the same logic consistently: reputation requires an audience, and in conditions of perfect anonymity the reputation mechanism is inoperative.
(7) Experimental evidence from anonymous dictator-game variants shows that secular subjects still cooperate at rates well above the rational-choice baseline.
(8) Hence the reputation-substitute fails to explain the data without smuggling back some form of non-observable accountability—functionally equivalent to the extra-social auditor.
(9) It follows that genuinely secular moral motivation in hidden-action domains is either impossible or parasitic on an unrecognized theological framework.
(10) This conclusion is absurd: it implies that self-identified secular people are either self-deceived about their own motivations or sustained by cultural residues they do not acknowledge.

The chain feels suspicious because it proves too much—seemingly erasing autonomous secular ethics—yet each link looks defensible. Where does it go wrong? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly reluctant to surrender (1), since the problem of hidden-cost motivation seems real, or (4), since the empirical fact of secular cooperation is well documented. If I must choose, I would rather preserve the possibility of secular morality than accept the hidden-theism conclusion, but I want to know exactly which step breaks and whether repairing it requires abandoning a deep intuition about moral psychology.
