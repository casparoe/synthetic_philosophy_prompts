I work in distributed systems and have no formal philosophy training, but I am trying to understand a conceptual issue that keeps arising when people analogize software architectures to minds. The Buddhist doctrine of anatta denies a persisting self, which is often compared in philosophy to Western bundle theories of personal identity. But there is a more specific framing—call it pattern-identity—where a person is not an enduring substance but a replicable functional structure, like a protocol that can run on different hardware, with psychological continuity consisting only in the faithful replication of relational patterns across mental states. The conceptual problem is this: anatta seems to deny not merely substrate-identity but any stable, re-identifiable pattern across mental moments, since each moment is causally dependent on its predecessor yet ontologically distinct. If that is correct, then even saying that the same pattern recurs might smuggle in a hidden substrate. Please explain whether anatta should be read as rejecting only substance-based persistence, or whether it also undermines pattern-identity, and whether the Buddhist notion of causal continuity (pratityasamutpada) can do the work of explaining moral responsibility across lives without implicitly reintroducing either a substrate or a replicable pattern. I am not asking for citations or a reading list—just a clear mapping of the conceptual fault lines, particularly if we treat mental states as nodes in a distributed graph with no central controller.
