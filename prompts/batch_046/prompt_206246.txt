(2) Ideas are non-rivalrous goods: one person's grasp of an idea does not diminish another's capacity to grasp it.
(3) Intellectual property law addresses this non-rivalry by constructing artificial scarcity around specific expression-tokens—texts, blueprints, code files, or design objects—that serve as proxies for the underlying idea.
(4) Any given token is causally and semantically dependent on the idea it expresses; destroying every extant token does not erase the idea from the space of possible thoughts.
(5) Consequently, if the right-holder's exclusionary power extends only to tokens, there is no principled basis for preventing an independent agent from reconstructing the idea.
(6) To prevent such reconstruction, the right must extend to what I call the "reconstructibility conditions" of an idea—the particular constellation of background concepts, inferential rules, and observational access that render the idea available to a mind.
(7) Controlling those conditions requires controlling the cognitive environment, educational pathways, and inferential resources available to others.
(8) Such control amounts to a monopoly over mental resources that is incompatible with the autonomy of thought.
(9) Therefore, any IP regime that genuinely protects ideas (as opposed to merely protecting tokens) collapses into cognitive monopoly.
