Write a sestina in which the speaker has begun quantifying every leisure activity—reading, walking, conversation—through productivity metrics, using the six end-words “goal”, “metric”, “play”, “means”, “rest”, and “youth” in the form’s prescribed rotation. The poem should enact Schlick’s distinction between work (activity directed at an external end) and play (activity valued for its own sake, which he took to be the true locus of life’s meaning) by showing how each achieved “goal” is immediately converted into a new “means”, trapping the speaker in an infinite regress of instrumental purpose. Let the obsessive circular repetition of the end-words embody this cyclical frustration. In the envoi, break the cycle by rearranging the terms so that “play” and “youth” are liberated from subordination to “goal” and “metric”, thereby performing Schlick’s claim that meaning arises only when an activity is freed from purpose-driven labour. The poem should be formally precise—someone familiar with Schlick’s philosophy of life should recognise the work/play distinction being staged structurally—while standing as poetry without footnotes, citations, or references to specific editions.
