I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science and decision theory in any systematic way. In my research career I treated uncertainty as something to be quantified—probability distributions over parameters within a model whose form was fixed by physical law. Now I am trying to understand whether the Ellsberg paradox exposes a genuine normative failure in that habit, or merely a psychological quirk. To make the issue concrete for myself, I have been thinking about a distinction that seems rarely pressed in the philosophical literature: ambiguity about the values of parameters inside a given model (what I will call parameter ambiguity) versus ambiguity about which model class is appropriate at all—say, deterministic dynamics versus stochastic dynamics, or linear versus nonlinear response—what I will call structural ambiguity. The standard urn example seems closer to parameter ambiguity, since the urn is a single model with an unknown mixture; but in science we often face the deeper problem that we do not even know what state space to use.

Please write a polished, technically serious dialogue between two philosophers of science, Dr. Chen and Dr. Alvarez, both equally competent, debating whether structural ambiguity can be rationally assimilated to the Bayesian framework or whether it marks an irreducible limit. Dr. Chen should defend the assimilationist view: all ambiguity, structural included, should be folded into a single hierarchical subjective prior over models and parameters, and any reluctance to do so is an emotional aversion—an epistemic failure of discipline masked as caution. She should argue that hierarchical Bayesian methods with hyperpriors already do exactly this work in statistical practice, and that holding back is no more rational than refusing to assign a prior because one fears being wrong.

Dr. Alvarez should resist. He should argue that structural ambiguity is categorically different from parameter ambiguity because comparing incommensurable model classes—frameworks with different ontologies, different variables, different notions of explanation—requires a prior over structures that cannot be justified by the same evidential considerations used within any one structure. He should press that assigning exchangeability across such frameworks in a single probability space is a category error, and that in actual scientific reasoning we hold models provisionally, as working instruments, rather than betting on them with precise credences. When Dr. Chen replies that hyperpriors only push ambiguity up one level—what prior do we place over hyperparameters?—Dr. Alvarez should embrace the regress and claim it reveals that structural ambiguity is not relocated but irreducible.

The dialogue must be real back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other’s specific points, not talk past them. Dr. Chen should challenge whether Alvarez’s “provisional stance” is distinguishable from simple indecision, and whether it can guide action without collapsing into arbitrary preference. Dr. Alvarez should reply that guidance comes from robustness and predictive success within a framework, not from a comparative betting quotient across frameworks.

Keep the discussion grounded in conceptual logic rather than in citations, bibliographies, or appeals to specific authors and texts; I am not asking for a literature review, and I have no access to search for one. At the end, please include a brief self-critical note identifying where the dialogue takes liberties with working Bayesian epistemology or with how scientists actually reason about model choice, and flag any step that depends on a contested reading.

Finally, please invite direct pushback: if my framing of structural ambiguity versus parameter ambiguity rests on a confused premise—perhaps conflating an ontic feature of the world with an epistemic limitation of the agent, or assuming that model classes can be cleanly separated in the way I suggest—say so plainly rather than accommodating the distinction politely. If the task as posed cannot be done well without that confusion being resolved, explain that instead.
