As a novelist constructing characters who believe they have engineered their own contentment, I hold that Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness—where sustained intellectual labor is tempered by calculated illusion and moderated sensual pleasure—depends for its coherence on an aristocratic ecology of private study, patronage insulation, and gendered exemption from reproductive labor, and that once this ecology is stripped away the view either collapses into unacknowledged privilege or demands a universalized stoicism that contradicts her own embodied emphasis. What would count decisively against this reading? I want the arguments, textual moves, and structural reconstructions that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: for instance, whether she addresses readers lacking such resources; whether her psychology of happiness can be rebuilt in non-aristocratic terms without losing specificity; and whether defenders concede historical embeddedness or defend a transhistorical claim. I am especially interested in whether an implicit formal calculus—perhaps a quantitative balance between study and pleasure—survives the removal of its social substrate, or whether that substrate is doing irreducible philosophical work that the calculus alone cannot carry.
