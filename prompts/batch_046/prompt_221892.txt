I'm trying to figure out what I actually mean when I call a decision "satisficing" versus just "being rational with limits." I was talking about this with my roommate after skimming Herbert Simon's work for a course on bounded rationality, and we kept running into cases where our gut feelings don't line up neat.

Here are my verdicts. A job hunter sets a salary floor and max commute, finds one that clears both, and stops searching - that's satisficing, clearly. A chef picks the first produce vendor that meets her freshness and price thresholds and doesn't audit every farm - also yes. But a chess player in a blitz match who plays the first tactically sound move she sees: I want to say that's not satisficing, it's just optimization under a hard clock, though I can't say why the clock makes it different. Then there's a pair that bugs me. My cousin set a "good enough" bar for an apartment (two bedrooms, under $2000, near transit) and signed the first lease that hit all three. That feels like satisficing. But she also kept a spreadsheet of every nicer place she saw afterwards "just to check," and when one came up six months later she broke her lease to move. I still call the first decision satisficing; my roommate says if your tracking superior alternatives you never actually satisfied your aspiration level, you just paused searching. That sounds like a distinction without a difference to me, but I can't defend it well.

There's also this niche case I'm not sure has a name. A product team copies every feature of the leading competitor and calls the product "done" once feature parity is reached, not because users asked for them and not because they've hit an internal quality bar, but because the competitor's feature list acts as an external aspiration scaffold. I think that's still satisficing - the aspiration level is borrowed - but maybe it's a different mechanism altogether? And one more that flips for me: a physician picks a standard treatment with an 85% success rate because she hasn't researched whether a newer protocol hits 88%, knowing she lacks time. That feels like satisficing. But if she did the research, found the 88% option, and still picked the 85% one because she trusts it more, is that still satisficing or now just weighing trade-offs? So is there a coherent principle behind all of this, especially one that saves my chess-clock and spreadsheet intuitions without being ad hoc? If not, which of my gut feelings should I drop, and what does the best principle predict for cases I haven't listed? Please push back hard if I'm conflating satisficing with something else and the task doesn't make sence as posed - I don't need a bibliography or citations, just straight reasoning with an honest note on where your account breaks down.
