In a graduate seminar on epistemic value, two students—Mara and Julian—got into a sustained disagreement over Plato’s Meno problem, specifically whether the stability of knowledge explains why it is more valuable than mere true belief. Mara argued that true belief and knowledge guide action equally well in the moment, but knowledge carries a distinct form of diachronic security: because knowledge involves an account or justification that binds the belief to the fact, it is far less vulnerable to being overturned by counter-evidence or by shifts in one’s evidential situation. She insisted this resilience is not merely instrumental—not just that we happen to prefer beliefs that last longer—but that there is an intrinsic epistemic good in being rationally anchored to reality in a way that accidental true belief is not. Julian countered that this moves the goalposts: if the only difference is stability over time, then knowledge is just true belief plus a reliable maintenance mechanism, and its added value is purely instrumental, like installing a backup generator on a machine that already works. He pressed that in practical contexts—say, a navigator using a correct chart she does not understand versus one who does—the outcomes are identical, so attributing intrinsic value to the “tether” confuses the value of a cognitive achievement with the value of the state itself. Mara replied that Julian treats belief as isolated mental furniture, ignoring that epistemic agents are temporally extended and that part of what makes a belief valuable to an agent is its integration into a coherent, defensible web of understanding; without that integration, even a correct belief remains epistemically precarious in a way that diminishes its standing for the agent. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point—assessing Mara’s claim about diachronic security, Julian’s charge of instrumentalism, and the deeper issue of whether epistemic value should be assessed synchronically or diachronically—while separating what is broadly settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
