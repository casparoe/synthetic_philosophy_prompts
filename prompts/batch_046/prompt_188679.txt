Notes for an essay tentatively titled Autonomous Design Patterns: Re-examining Dennett's Stances in Directed Evolutionary Engineering

Dennett's well-known triad—the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance—offers three levels at which we can predict and explain the behaviour of systems. Typically, the design stance is treated as a pragmatic intermediate: we predict that a clock will chime because it was designed to do so, without computing its physics or attributing mental states. I want to explore whether this intermediate status holds in cases of directed evolutionary engineering, such as the development of adversarially robust gene drives or self-optimising metabolic pathways.

In these cases, human engineers do not specify component-level blueprints; instead, they define high-level fitness landscapes and selection pressures. The resulting biological structures exhibit robust, predictable patterns—failure modes, stability thresholds, and functional outputs—that are genuinely projectible. Yet these patterns are not derivable from the physical stance alone, because the evolutionary trajectory is computationally intractable at the molecular level. At the same time, they are not accessible through the intentional stance, since no agent within the system possesses beliefs or desires about the design. The predictive success therefore seems to belong specifically to the design stance, but in a form that is not merely heuristic: the design here is an emergent property of the selection regime rather than a mental representation held by any designer.

My central question is whether this extends Dennett's notion of real patterns in a meaningful way, or whether it collapses back into a complex physical description. Specifically, does the design stance acquire independent ontological weight when the design process itself is decoupled from intentional agency, or is it still just a convenient abstraction?

Please comment on the coherence of this framing, on whether the example of directed evolutionary engineering genuinely introduces a distinct predictive level, and on any points where the argument risks misrepresenting the relationship between physical reduction and design-level explanation. I would particularly value observations on whether the distinction between an emergent selection regime and a traditional blueprint-based design undermines or enriches the triad.
