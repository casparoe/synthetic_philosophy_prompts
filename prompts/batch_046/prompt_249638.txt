In my philosophy of social practice seminar, two classmates have been deadlocked since our unit on conventional norms, and the disagreement has become personally acute for me because I am leaving next month for a year in Berlin and then Tokyo. The first classmate, who just returned from Japan, argues that importing American tipping practices into Japanese service culture is not merely unnecessary but a form of moral imperialism—tipping can cause offense and disrupt a non-monetary honor system—while strict adherence to local queuing norms or jaywalking rules, by contrast, involves cooperative goods (fair turn-taking, pedestrian safety) that visitors actually benefit from. She therefore distinguishes three categories: conventions that encode genuine reciprocity obligations (queuing), those that are purely local etiquette with no cooperative function (tipping in Japan), and those that are legal or safety-related (jaywalking in Berlin). The second classmate rejects this taxonomy entirely. He claims that any convention sustaining a stable cooperative scheme generates a reciprocity obligation for anyone who participates in or benefits from that scheme, regardless of whether the convention feels "moral" to outsiders; refusing to tip in the United States or queue properly in Britain is therefore free-riding, not cultural independence. He insists that the "when in Rome" principle is not parochialism but a minimal condition of not exploiting local coordination, and that distinguishing tipping from queuing is arbitrary because both stabilize expectations that reduce transaction costs. She replies that this collapses morality into sociology—if every stable convention becomes obligatory, then morality loses critical distance from culture, and visitors become bound by rules they never consented to and that may conflict with deeper moral commitments. He counters that reciprocity does not require consent but only benefit, and that critical distance is preserved by asking whether the scheme is genuinely cooperative rather than oppressive, not by asking whether it feels familiar. They have asked me to adjudicate before I depart. Can you assess, point by point, where each party is correct and where each overreaches—specifically, whether there is a defensible middle position between treating conventions as purely optional and treating them as fully moral obligations, and whether the reciprocity argument genuinely distinguishes tipping, queuing, and jaywalking in the nuanced way the first classmate proposes? In giving your verdict, please state explicitly what argument or empirical finding about convention-formation or moral psychology would cause you to revise your position, and flag the step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
