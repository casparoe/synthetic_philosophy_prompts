Consider the thesis that responsibility gaps for autonomous systems are best understood not as missing subjects of blame, but as misallocated portfolios of moral obligation correctable through structured "accountability trading" — arrangements in which designers, operators, and autonomous agents exchange partial obligations analogously to risk-pooling, but with normative rather than financial capital. On this account, autonomous harm reveals not an absence of responsibility but an improperly distributed one; the gap closes once obligations are traded into their appropriate nodes. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Provide the evidence and arguments that should move a defender of it, sorted by decisiveness. Specifically: which actual or conceivable cases of autonomous harm resist redescription as portfolio misallocation? Which conceptual results — about the non-fungibility of moral blame, the conditions for agency, or the structure of collective responsibility — show that obligation cannot be pooled or traded without losing its normative character? Has the view been insulated by qualification — for example, by treating every harm as evidence of a poorly structured trading zone — such that it risks unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a normative framework of this kind, or an appropriate feature? Include what proponents of this account would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say, and distinguish evidence that would be fatal from evidence that would be merely awkward.
