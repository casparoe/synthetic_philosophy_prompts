I was recently reading an economics blog that tried to explain why rational people still disagree about climate science, and it summarized Aumann’s agreement theorem roughly as follows: “Aumann proved that if two agents are fully rational and share the same prior beliefs, then once they exchange all of their private evidence they must converge on exactly the same posterior probability for every empirical claim. This means that any persistent disagreement—say, between climate modelers about equilibrium climate sensitivity—demonstrates that at least one party is being irrational, suppressing data, or failing to update correctly. The result requires both agents to begin with literally identical belief distributions over every possible state of the world, which is so unrealistic that the theorem is essentially a mathematical curiosity with no purchase on actual scientific practice. And if we extend the logic to two large language models that share the same base architecture but have been fine-tuned on different corpora, the theorem would imply they can never genuinely disagree about factual outputs, which is obviously false, so the whole framework collapses when applied to artificial reasoners.”

I also want to know whether the conclusion—that real scientific disagreement is thereby shown to be irrational—follows from anything true in the summary, and whether the extension to fine-tuned language models is a legitimate analog or whether it quietly violates one of the theorem’s conditions in a way that makes the counterexample irrelevant.
