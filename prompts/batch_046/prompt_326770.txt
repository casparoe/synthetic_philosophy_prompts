(2) I think the correct response is not to deny the arithmetic but to reject binary moral patienthood; instead, I treat patienthood as a continuous, graded function of integrated information processing, with insects falling well below the threshold where obligations become action-guiding. (3) At the same time, I hold a broadly consequentialist framework in which expected moral value should, in principle, drive resource allocation. (4) I also think evolutionary continuity gives us some reason to think insect nervous systems share functional analogues with pain pathways, so assigning exactly zero prior is unjustified. (5) Finally, I maintain that moral theories must be action-guiding in a practical sense: if a view implies we should radically restructure agriculture, pest control, and conservation priorities in ways that are collectively impossible to implement, that is a serious cost against the view, though not necessarily a refutation.

Do these hang together? Meanwhile, (4) seems to push the prior upward, which makes (2)'s cutoff harder to defend, while (5) looks like an ad hoc rescue of human-scale ethics that (1) should undermine. Are there other hidden tensions—perhaps between (1)'s quantitative framing and (2)'s qualitative threshold? Please separate what is settled in the philosophical and scientific literature on insect sentience and scale arguments from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the claim that integrated information metrics provide the right framework for graded patienthood—as if it were the consensus view.
