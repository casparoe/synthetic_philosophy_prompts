I serve on the Medical-Legal Continuity Panel at the Meridian Research Hospital, and we face a binding decision that must be delivered before the end of the quarter. Our charge concerns Patient 734, who over the past eighteen months has completed the institution’s experimental gradual substrate-replacement protocol for advanced cortical degeneration. In this procedure, each biological neuron in the patient’s associative and prefrontal cortices was sequentially substituted with a synthetic analog engineered to preserve its precise synaptic weights, connectivity map, and electrophysiological firing dynamics; by the final stage, no original neural tissue remained, yet the patient reports an unbroken stream of consciousness, retains complete autobiographical memory with appropriate emotional coloring, and scores within the normal range on all personality and cognitive continuity metrics. The clinical team considers the intervention successful, but the patient’s spouse and adult children have jointly petitioned the panel to rule that legal personhood—including marital property rights, inheritance standing, and liability for obligations incurred prior to the procedure—did not survive the transition, on the grounds that physical substrate discontinuity creates a numerically distinct individual, regardless of psychological resemblance.

The family has offered to accept a transitional “bridged identity” arrangement with a defined sunset period rather than an abrupt discontinuity, but they reject full continuity recognition. Our panel’s mandate is not to reassess the therapy’s clinical approval, which falls under a separate review board, but specifically to recommend whether institutional recognition of continuous legal identity should be granted, modified, or denied, with explicit conditions if we choose a middle path. I am asking you to draft the formal advisory memo that I will circulate before Thursday’s vote: it should present a clear recommendation—full continuity, bridged transitional status, or discontinuity—supported by reasoning that takes seriously both the view that what matters in survival is the preservation of mental life and the view that legal and moral identity tracks material or bodily continuity. The memo must include an honest acknowledgment of the strongest argument for the position you reject, and it should be structured point by point so that the clinical staff, the family, and hospital administration can respond to specific claims without needing external philosophical references. Please avoid bibliographic citations or appeals to named theoretical frameworks; the reasoning should stand on its own conceptual merits.
