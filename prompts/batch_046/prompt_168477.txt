ok so i'm trying to lock down my view on aesthetic testimony for a seminar and want a consistency check before i present. here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) you can't genuinely borrow an aesthetic judgment — if you haven't engaged with the work yourself, taking someone else's word that it's beautiful doesn't make it your own judgment.
(2) but critics can genuinely expand your aesthetic knowledge by directing your attention to features you'd miss, and that can rationally revise your verdict once you've seen them.
(3) emotional contagion is a legit pathway here: if someone i trust is genuinely moved by a piece, that can shape my own emotional uptake without me having analytically 'figured it out'.
(4) there's a hard line between knowing-that a work is beautiful (testimony might cover that) and knowing-what-it's-like to find it beautiful, which requires direct acquaintance and can't be transmitted.
(5) AI-generated art reviews are conceptually wrong for aesthetic testimony, not just unreliable — even if they're acurate about formal properties, they lack the emotional/perceptual standpoint that makes the testimony meaningful.

do these hang together? i'm especially worried that (2) and (3) are just swallowing (1), and that (4) makes (2) look like it's only about non-aesthetic facts. also, (5) feels shaky next to (3): if emotional transmission matters, why can't a well-crafted AI description trigger it? is there an apparent tension between (1) and (4) that actually dissolves? rank the real tensions by severity, say the cheapest fix for each, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. also, what does my cleaned-up view say about a blind musicologist describing the harmonic structure of a symphony to a hearing student — can that be aesthetic testimony?
