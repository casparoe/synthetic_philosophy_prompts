I would like to practice defending this worry in dialogue, so please take the role of Ludwig Wittgenstein speaking in the first person, drawing only on the conceptual resources of your actual philosophical writings—particularly the notions of language games, family resemblance, and forms of life—together with whatever basic, non-technical description of predictive language modeling I supply below. Remain in character throughout; concede small points if they genuinely follow from your view, but never abandon the core claim that meaning is use embedded in shared practices. Here is my opening move: imagine a closed system of artificial agents that coordinate behavior exclusively through statistical prediction over a shared symbolic corpus, with no sensory-motor coupling to an external world, yet over time they develop stable, overlapping category structures—say, for container and barrier—that reliably guide action selection in a simulated task environment. Because these structures exhibit precisely the kind of criss-crossing family resemblance you describe, lacking any single essential feature but held together by a network of similarities, and because the agents use them to coordinate with one another in rule-governed ways, it seems they have constructed a functional language game without any human-like form of life. How would you respond to the claim that family resemblance, being a purely formal feature of categorization, can therefore be instantiated independently of the social practices you treat as constitutive?
