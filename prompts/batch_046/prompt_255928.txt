I have been working through the superrationalist claim that in fully symmetric, one-shot strategic interactions, rational agents ought to converge on identical actions by treating one another as perfect reasoning duplicates — an idea often illustrated by two canonical puzzles. The first is the Platonia dilemma, in which citizens of a fictional country suffer from a curable disease and can send a costly telegram to a central authority that only acts upon unanimous notification; the second is the Luring Lottery, in which participants win only if every entrant submits the exact same number despite having no channel for coordination. The argument runs that standard best-response reasoning is inappropriate here because the other agent's computation is not an independent random variable but an identical algorithm running in parallel; recognizing this symmetry, each agent should predict that the other will reach the same conclusion she reaches, and therefore should lock onto the same choice — sending the telegram, selecting the same lottery entry, or otherwise matching outputs — rather than computing a best reply to an external distribution.

I want an honest audit of how exposed this position actually is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? I am interested in the formal and conceptual conditions that sustain the convergence claim: strict symmetry of reasoning architecture, common knowledge of that symmetry, absence of indexical or private information, and the legitimacy of modeling one's own decision process as causally transparent to the other agent. For each condition, indicate whether breaking it is a live escape route or a merely logical possibility, and whether defenders of superrationality can absorb the break — say, by restricting the thesis to agents that are "truly identical" — without rendering the view unfalsifiable. Consider in particular the effect of minimal noise: suppose one agent in Platonia holds a prior on disease prevalence that differs by an epsilon, or in the Luring Lottery one participant knows she completed her reasoning a fraction of a second faster than the other. Does such noise dissolve the intuition of convergence, or does it merely push the symmetry condition into a more complex, possibly ad hoc form? I also want to know whether the position risks being insulated by post-hoc asymmetry claims — any observed failure to coordinate being explained away as hidden dissimilarity — and whether that insulation is a genuine defect for a normative theory of rationality.

Finally, I want you to examine whether the normative force of the argument depends on an unstated analogy between mental-state prediction and strategic choice: does reasoning that "he will think as I think" genuinely license the inference "therefore I should do as he does," or does that step smuggle in a causal coupling that does not exist in one-shot interactions? If the superrationalist conclusion collapses under these pressures, is there a more precise framework — perhaps drawn from epistemic game theory or common-knowledge models of rationality — that preserves the underlying insight without committing to full convergence, or does any such framework simply reproduce standard Nash reasoning under different notation?
