I'm giving a short toast at my old friend Elena Voss's retirment dinner next thursday. she's leaving the philosophy department after four decades working on Aquinas, specifically that uncomfortable tightrope he walks about tyrannicide — the real version, not the cartoon where he blesses assassination. she always insisted on the split between a ruler who never had legitimate authority and one who turned bad later, and that private citizens aren't the ones to judge, some public body is. half the room are medievalists who will wince if I turn her into a rebel icon; the other half are family and writers who barely know Aquinas from a type of cheese. I'm a novelist, so I want to frame her work through narrative unreliability — like she treated every claim about who holds power as a statement from an unreliable narrator, and asked who has the authoriy to declare a ruler illegitimate. the speech should be about three minutes, casual but not sloppy, and it needs to actually use that distinction to shape what I praise: not that she taught people to overthrow, but that she taught people to read authority without trusting the narrator. don't cite anyone or list books. please end with one line beginning 'Bottom line:' that gives the heart of the toast in a single sentence.
