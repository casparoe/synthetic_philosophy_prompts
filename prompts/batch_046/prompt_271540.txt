I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on moral philosophy focused on T.M. Scanlon's contractualist framework, particularly his treatment of the aggregation problem—the question of whether saving or benefiting a large number of people can justify imposing serious harm on a small number or on a single individual. Outside of coursework, I assist with a university research-ethics advisory panel that reviews study proposals involving exactly this kind of distributive risk: experiments or interventions that expose a few participants or subjects to significant burdens in order to generate benefits for a much larger population. The panel has no formal protocol for these cases, and members often default to intuitive cost-benefit reasoning that seems incompatible with the contractualist idea that individual complaints are not aggregable across persons. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates Scanlon's principle—that an action is wrong if it is permitted by a principle that some affected individual could reasonably reject—into an operational audit my panel can run on any proposal.

The procedure must explicitly incorporate what I am calling a non-cumulative rejection protocol: rather than weighing summed benefits against summed burdens, the auditor must isolate each distinct burden-type in the proposal, formulate the specific principle that permits that burden to be imposed on those who bear it, and ask whether a hypothetical representative of that burden-bearing role could reasonably reject that principle, holding constant that others are unaffected. Only if every burden-type survives this isolated test may the proposal advance; aggregate welfare figures must never enter as a counterweight. The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist with ordered questions, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, clear stopping points where the answer is "do not approve," points where the answer is "approve," and explicit flags where judgment cannot be eliminated and a human decision-maker must sign off. It should also specify what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether it can adjudicate conflicts between different reasonable rejections, or whether it applies only to risks imposed without consent.

Please apply this procedure to three hypothetical proposals: first, a clinical trial requiring 200 healthy volunteers to endure 48 hours of severe but temporary pain from an experimental vaccine, projected to prevent a debilitating epidemic among 20,000 people; second, a study requiring one healthy control subject to undergo an invasive brain biopsy with a 10% risk of permanent neurological damage, in order to validate a non-invasive diagnostic that could help thousands with a degenerative condition; and third, a proposal to expose one healthy volunteer to a 30% risk of fatal harm in order to generate data that would save 100,000 lives from an otherwise fatal disease. In this third case, I expect the procedure to yield a result that my panel will strongly resist—please explain exactly why the contractualist audit blocks approval despite the enormous aggregate benefit, and note where the procedure reaches its limits and a value judgment takes over. Be precise about which steps are mechanistic and which depend on contested judgments about what counts as "reasonable."
