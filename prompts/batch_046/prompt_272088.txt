Two members of a philosophy of perception reading group got into a sustained disagreement over how to interpret Molyneux's problem in light of contemporary debates about mental representation. The first, Alex, defended the standard Lockean answer—no immediate recognition—arguing that it demonstrates perceptual concepts are modality-specific: the idea of a cube acquired through touch is formatted for tactile exploration, and applying it to visual experience requires associative learning that takes time. The second, Blake, agreed that immediate recognition would fail, but denied this shows modality-specificity of concepts. Instead, Blake argued that what fails is the sensorimotor coupling between the concept and the new sensory channel: the blind subject possesses an amodal spatial concept of cubeness, but vision demands a specific calibration between that concept and retinal input that tactile experience cannot provide.

Their exchange went as follows.

(1) Alex said that if the concept of cubeness were truly amodal, then once the subject grasped the geometric properties through touch, sight should immediately deliver the same object under that concept—otherwise we are smuggling in an unnecessary intermediate layer of calibration that just renames the associative process Locke described.

(2) Blake replied that calibration is not renaming association but explaining its mechanism: the subject does not need to learn what a cube is, but must learn how visual edges, shading, and perspective map onto an already-possessed spatial structure. This is analogous to learning to drive on the left after years on the right—the skill is transferred, but the specific execution requires adjustment.

(3) Alex countered that Blake's analogy undermines his own point: a driver who has only driven on the right does not need to relearn what a car is or what a left turn means conceptually, yet still makes mistakes at first. By Blake's reasoning, this should count as evidence that driving concepts are modality-specific (right-hand versus left-hand), which is absurd.

(4) Blake said Alex was missing the distinction between conceptual content and procedural implementation: driving on the left involves the same conceptual knowledge but different procedural parameters, just as seeing a cube involves the same geometric concept but different perceptual parameters. The failure of immediate recognition is therefore at the level of implementation, not concept possession.

(5) Alex then pressed Blake on a modified case: suppose a blind subject has used a tactile-visual substitution device for years, receiving spatial information about cubes through patterns of vibration mapped onto the back, so that the spatial mapping is already coupled to a non-tactile sensory stream. If sight is then restored, Blake's view predicts faster recognition than in the classic case, because part of the cross-modal calibration is already done. But if recognition is still not immediate, Blake owes an explanation of what remains uncalibrated.

(6) Blake conceded that recognition might be faster but insisted it still would not be immediate, because substitution patterns and retinal images involve different computational transformations of spatial information; the remaining gap is precisely the specific visual format. He added that if recognition were immediate in this modified case, that would actually support his view—showing that prior cross-modal calibration is what matters, not innate visual ideas.

(7) Alex concluded that Blake had turned Molyneux's problem into a general claim about sensory-motor learning that applies to any novel modality, thereby dissolving the specific philosophical interest of the problem: if every new sensory channel requires calibration, then the blind man's failure tells us nothing distinctive about vision, touch, or the nature of spatial concepts.
