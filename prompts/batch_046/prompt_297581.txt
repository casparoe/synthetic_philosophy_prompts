I serve as the student representative on my university's Commission on Historical Accountability, and I am currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on collective moral responsibility that engages Karl Jaspers's well-known taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. Our commission must issue a formal recommendation by the end of next month on whether the university should adopt an institutional statement acknowledging collective moral guilt for its sustained historical entanglement with colonial resource extraction in the region, and if so, whether that acknowledgment should remain purely symbolic or be paired with concrete reparative commitments—specifically, an endowed scholarship fund for descendants of affected communities and a publicly accessible archival project—implemented within a defined two-year timeline. The commissioners are sharply divided. One group argues that because the university retains continuous legal identity, it can bear Jaspersian political guilt but not moral guilt, since moral guilt requires direct participatory wrongdoing by present members, which is absent; they further warn that attributing mental states to a corporate body is conceptually incoherent and that symbolic gestures risk substituting for real justice. Another group invokes a distinction developed in our seminar between participatory guilt and structural inheritance: they hold that present members are not personally culpable, but that institutional continuity creates a non-transferable yet action-guiding responsibility grounded in sustained benefit from unjust structures, which justifies material reparations even without inherited culpability. A smaller faction is concerned primarily with practical backlash, fearing that alumni and donor relations will suffer regardless of philosophical merit.

Please draft the commission's decision memo for circulation before our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the acknowledgment with material commitments, approve it as purely symbolic, reject it outright, or approve it with specific conditions—together with an explicit implementation time frame. The reasoning should treat Jaspers's four categories with precision, take the participatory-guilt versus structural-inheritance distinction seriously rather than dismissing it, and give full honest weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side, particularly the objection that institutional continuity alone cannot ground moral guilt or that symbolic acknowledgment may displace rather than advance justice. Write it as a polished, formal document that members who ultimately disagree can still recognize as engaging their position fairly and with philosophical care.
