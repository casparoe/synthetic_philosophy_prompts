I'm taking a seminar in philosophy of biology, and after class a friend who advises on pandemic-preparedness policy asked me whether there is any hidden metaphysics in the way agencies draw taxonomic lines. I mentioned that in microbial ecology researchers have long used a 97 percent sequence-identity cutoff in 16S rRNA genes to separate bacterial species, and that her field is now proposing to import an analogous fixed genetic threshold into viral taxonomy: any variant falling below a specified similarity level relative to a known pathogen would be automatically classified as a novel species and subjected to stricter containment protocols. The policy is defended as purely operational, yet it is enforced as though the number tracks a real division in nature rather than a convenient convention.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of species does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology—whether essentialist, cluster-based, or nominalist—that makes the cutoff seem like more than an arbitrary rule. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and assess whether that implicit view is coherent with evolutionary population thinking that treats species as dynamic, interbreeding populations without sharp boundaries. Finally, explain what concrete changes to regulatory procedure would follow if we replaced the presupposed view with a purely relational or process-based conception of biological classification that denies there are definite species lines to be discovered.
