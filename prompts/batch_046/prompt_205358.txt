I am defending my dissertation chapter next Thursday in a philosophy of logic seminar on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. Because I inherited this project from my recently deceased advisor and must now present his unfinished framework, the stakes feel personal and immediate: my thesis is that the result undermines not the existence of conditional probabilities but Stalnaker's identification of them with conditional belief, since indicative conditionals actually govern suppositional revision over restricted epistemic domains rather than standard Bayesian conditionalization. The audience comprises three philosophers of language committed to Stalnaker's thesis and familiar with imaging arguments, two probabilists concerned with coherence and Dutch-book vulnerability, and one visiting decision theorist who will recognize any hand-waving about belief revision. Give me exactly five items, numbered, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion: ordered by potential damage, each should present the objection exactly as that kind of interlocutor would phrase it, indicate whether I should concede or hold firm, provide the two-minute reply, and note its weakest point; include the question I am praying they do not ask.
