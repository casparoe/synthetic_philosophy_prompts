I've been trying to work out what a genuinely adequate framework for public commemoration—statues, street names, monuments—would actually have to accomplish, and I keep running into the same problem: people argue about whether a specific figure should stay or go without first specifying what the theory itself is supposed to deliver. So I'd like the full specification of desiderata first, with the motivating case behind each.

What must any normative account of commemorative markers explain or preserve? I see several categories, and I want each treated as a formal requirement.

First, the mnemonic versus honorific distinction: a framework must not collapse remembering that a person existed and shaped events into praising their moral character. The motivating case is a monument to a political leader who achieved structural reforms through brutal methods—if the theory can't separate acknowledgment from endorsement, it either forces us to erase complex history or forces us to celebrate cruelty.

Second, pluralist recognition without veto: it must accommodate that different communities living in the same city assign radically different moral weights to the same figure, without granting either group unilateral power to decide presence or absence.

Third, temporal indexing: the framework must account for the fact that a marker installed in one moral epoch persists into another with different values. The case is a street named in 1910 according to norms that were mainstream then but are widely rejected now—the theory needs to specify whether the relevant evaluation concerns the values of the namers, the conduct of the named person, or the present audience's relationship to the symbol, and it can't simply assume these three perspectives align.

Fourth, there's a distinction I'm not sure is real but want included explicitly: between retroactive moral contamination, where learning new bad facts about a figure should alter how we view an existing monument, and contextual evaluation, where we judge the monument by the intentions and norms of its creators rather than by present-day knowledge. A good theory must clarify whether these are genuinely different criteria and, if so, which one governs.

Fifth, an asymmetry problem: putting up a commemorative marker is an active, resource-committing act of collective attention, whereas taking one down is often framed as moral correction. The theory must determine whether this asymmetry is a genuine moral feature—meaning removal requires stronger justification than installation originally did—or merely a status-quo bias presented as principle.

Sixth, consistency with democratic change: if the framework permits present majorities to alter commemorative landscapes, it must also include protections against mere majoritarian whim, since a narrow present preference shouldn't automatically dissolve a symbol that has structured public space for generations.

Please lay out the complete list of requirements—those above plus any others you think are genuinely necessary—indicate for each whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (historical-preservationist, social-justice-oriented, liberal-neutralist, and so on), and identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the most common approaches—likely those that reduce commemoration either to present-day social utility or to fixed historical record-keeping. I don't need named theories scored in detail; I want to see where the criteria themselves conflict or jointly demand something that simpler frameworks can't provide, and whether the asymmetry and temporal-indexing requirements together create an unsatisfiable subset.
