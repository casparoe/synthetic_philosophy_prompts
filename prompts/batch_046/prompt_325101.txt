My philosophy-of-history professor has ruled on whether specific “why X and not Y?” comparisons are well-posed: (1) Britain versus France on steam-engine dominance is legitimate, since both possessed coal reserves, capital markets, and engineering cultures; (2) asking why “modern science” crystallized in seventeenth-century Europe rather than in the Islamic Golden Age is illegitimate, because the outcome is defined retrospectively and the two contexts lack institutional homology; (3) Japan versus Qing China after 1860 is legitimate, since both were East Asian agrarian bureaucracies facing Western pressure with comparable state capacities; (4) asking why Rome became an empire rather than remaining like the Achaean League is illegitimate, because geopolitical scale and military structures differ categorically; (5) Reformation Germany versus Catholic Spain on mass literacy is legitimate, since both are early-modern European states with printing technology, making divergence traceable to religious and educational institutions. Next week she must evaluate this proposal: “Why did Britain develop sustained fossil-fuel-based per-capita growth in the eighteenth century, and not the Lower Yangzi Delta, given that both regions exhibited comparable levels of market integration, proto-industrial textile output, and agricultural productivity around 1700, and both had coal deposits within a few hundred miles?” Predict whether she will judge it well-posed or ill-posed; identify at least two distinct rules that consistently explain her first five verdicts; state what each rule predicts about the Britain/Yangzi case; give your best-guess verdict with reasoning; and name the single further comparison that would best distinguish those two rules.
