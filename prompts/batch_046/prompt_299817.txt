I am drafting a satirical interlude for my novel—approximately 600 words, formatted as the deadpan FAQ page of a fictional luxury retreat called The Institute for Quantified Eudaimonia—where guests submit every experience for conversion into a single hedonic currency. The institutional voice should be that of an overly earnest concierge answering questions without ever breaking character. The conceptual target is the classic heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the assumption that all pleasures share one uniform qualitative nature—often called a distinctive feel—such that they differ only in intensity and duration and can therefore be summed, traded, and optimized interchangeably.

Rather than stating the philosophical objection, the satire should emerge from the questions themselves: guests ask whether the pleasure-token earned from solving a chess problem can be redeemed for the token from a sunset, whether the melancholy satisfaction of recognizing one's own hypocrisy carries the same exchange rate as physical warmth, and whether Dr. Elara Voss's 1982 taxonomy of seven irreducible hedonic modalities—reproduced in the brochure's sidebar—has been updated now that guests keep reporting experiences that fall between the categories. The standard objections should land implicitly—that pleasure cannot be isolated from its intentional object, that cardinal comparison fails across qualitative kinds, and that whoever writes the audit form determines what counts as valuable—while at least one invented institutional detail, such as a national holiday celebrating the audit's founder or a weighted exchange rate posted in the dining hall, makes the world concrete. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to real philosophical texts; the piece should be legible to a specialist as fair satire, yet never break its deadpan institutional voice to lecture, so that by the final entry the reader feels the pressure of the heterogeneity objection without it being named.
