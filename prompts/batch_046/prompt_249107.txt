I serve on the appeals panel of a municipal research-ethics board tasked with reviewing sanctions against investigators. We must issue a written opinion in the appeal of Dr. Elena Vasquez, a junior epidemiologist who led a rapid-response survey during an acute water-contamination crisis. To meet a federal reporting deadline, she used an abbreviated consent protocol that omitted one risk disclosure; the omission was permissible under emergency guidelines but technically breached the board's standing rule requiring full disclosure in all non-exempt studies. Unbeknownst to Vasquez, one respondent—who had an undiagnosed anxiety disorder—experienced severe psychological distress upon later encountering a partial explanation of the contaminant in a news report. The distress was triggered by a specific phrasing in Vasquez's survey instrument, a translation she selected because it was the only one available within the crisis window, produced by a different agency with no epidemiological oversight. The board initially suspended her research privileges for twenty-four months, applying strict liability for consent violations regardless of mental state or downstream harm. Vasquez appeals, contending that she suffers both resultant moral luck—the unforeseeable psychological injury—and constitutive moral luck—her junior rank and restricted access to alternative translations shaped her practical options. The board's chair has raised a further, more structural worry: if we reduce sanctions whenever investigators face unforeseeable bad outcomes, we generate what might be called institutional moral luck, in which the board's own authority becomes stochastically distributed—some investigators face ruinous sanctions for identical procedural choices while others do not—thereby eroding the rule's legitimacy and predictive value. Please draft the panel's decision memo recommending whether to uphold, reduce, or overturn the suspension, specifying a concrete course of action and an implementation timeline (for example, immediate reinstatement with supervised conditions, or a phased six-month probationary return). The memo must take a committed position: do not present a neutral taxonomy of philosophical considerations, but defend one outcome, explain why the strongest opposing argument—whether grounded in strict liability, regulatory consistency, or the danger of institutional moral luck—should be overruled in this particular case, and state explicitly whether your recommendation requires a permanent amendment to the board's rules or applies solely as an exceptional remedy.
