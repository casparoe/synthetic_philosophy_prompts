The essay begins by outlining the familiar scenario: a scientist, Mary, has lived her entire life in a black-and-white environment and has mastered every physical and functional fact about colour vision. Upon her release and first encounter with a red object, she appears to learn something she did not previously know—namely, what it is like to see red. The standard physicalist rejoinder, often associated with the ability hypothesis, contends that Mary acquires only new abilities or recognitional capacities, not new propositional facts.

The essay's central contribution is to propose that even if Mary acquires genuine propositional knowledge, it is best understood not as access to a non-physical phenomenal property, but as a form of de se, counterfactual self-knowledge. Specifically, Mary updates her internal self-model to include a direct mapping between a particular neural state and a qualitative description—something she could not do without the relevant causal interaction with her environment.

To test this claim, the essay introduces a modified case: Mary remains in her monochrome room, but receives precisely calibrated stimulation via a brain-computer interface that replicates the exact causal pattern in her visual cortex that viewing a red object would produce. She reports that she now knows what seeing red is like. The essay argues that if her epistemic state is genuinely equivalent to that of the released Mary, then the knowledge argument does not demonstrate an ontological gap; rather, it highlights that phenomenal knowledge requires a specific causal pathway—direct environmental interaction—to update one's indexical self-model. The mystery, on this view, is relocated from metaphysics to the conditions under which a self-model can be causally anchored.

The essay then anticipates an objection: the BCI case may lack the intentional, world-directed character of genuine perception, meaning Mary still lacks the full phenomenal concept associated with seeing an actual red object. The essay considers whether this objection concedes too much to the knowledge argument, or whether it can be accommodated within the causal-indexical framework by distinguishing representational content from causal origin.
