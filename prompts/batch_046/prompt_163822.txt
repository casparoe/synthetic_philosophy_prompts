I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and lately I've been digging into how moral norms actually collapse — slavery, duelling, that sort of thing. I keep seeing the same pattern, and I wonder if there's a proper name for it.

Take duelling. In the 19th century it didn't disappear because philosophers suddenly won an argument that honour was irrationel. It faded because aristocrats started getting mocked for it, and the legal risk made it unfashonable. Once it's gone, though, everyone tells the story as if people always knew it was barbaric and just needed the courage to say so. The real drivers — status anxiety, shifting interests — get erased, and the collapse gets rewritten as a victory for reason.

Same shape with other norms: economic incentives shift, elites stop gaining face from the practice, and then afterwards we give it a retrospective moral death certificate, as if the argument was always there waiting. So I'm asking about the narrative move, not the mechanism of change itself. Is there an established concept for this specific distortion — the retroactive assignment of a rational, argument-based death to a norm that actually died through honour or interest?

I know there's Whig history in the neighbourhood, but that's about progress generally, not specifically about moral norms being assigned fake rational obituaries. And writers like Bernard Williams and Kwame Appiah discuss whether norms end through argument, honour, or interest, but they seem focused on the cause, not the post-hoc storytelling.

Does this carving have a name? If not, can you coin one with a tight definition, boundary cases, and a clear distinction from ordinary historical revisonism or from the genuine possibility that moral arguments do eventually catch up? I don't want just a label — I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges sit.
