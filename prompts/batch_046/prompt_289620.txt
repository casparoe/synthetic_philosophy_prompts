I've been taking this course on sociology of religion and I've landed on a kinda specific read of Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis that I haven't seen defended exactly like this. Most people treat it like culture is an efficient cause—protestant beliefs made people work harder and that built capitalism. But I think that's wrong, and the better version is that protestantism created a 'legitimation archtecture' (sorry, spelling's off today) where rationalized economic practices could become sticky and self-reinforcing. The value-rational orientation toward a calling—used loosely here, not quoting anyone exactly—acted less like a push and more like a selection filter: it made calculative practices feel morally obligatory, so once they appeared they didn't get abandoned when they conflicted with other social norms. Culture doesn't cause capitalism in the direct sense; it builds the normative environment where certain economic forms survive and lock in.

What would count against this specific framing? I don't want the usual objections—correlation isn't causation, or capitalism in Catholic Italy. I want to know what'd actually break the 'selection filter' idea. Would it be historical cases where rational capitalism emerged without any analogous normative scaffolding? Or cases where the theological shifts clearly came after the economic practices, reversing the filter direction? Or is there a conceptual problem—maybe treating culture as a filter just smuggles in functionalism, and if every outcome can be explained by some substitute filter, the view is unfalsifiable? Sort these defeaters by how fatal they'd be, including ones that have maybe already shown up in historical work. And be honest: has this framing been qualified into something that can't really be wrong? If so, is that a problem for a claim that's supposed to be about actual historical causation? Don't need a bibliography or anything—just the logic and what we'd need to see in the world for this to be wrong.
