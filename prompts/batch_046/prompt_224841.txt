I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently preparing a long-form feature on adversarial collaborations in formal philosophy and welfare economics. After sitting in on a heated but cordial seminar at a university workshop—where one speaker insisted that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem normatively mandates a linear social welfare function and another countered that the result is mathematically correct but ethically empty—I decided to ask an expert to formalize the standoff. You should approach this as a professional philosopher of economics with deep familiarity in social choice theory. Before drafting anything, please think carefully about the axioms underlying Harsanyi's result, particularly the independence conditions at both the individual and social levels, and about why critics believe these conditions fail to capture distributive or relational concerns in real-world aggregation.

Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps—the Cardinal Aggregators, who treat the weighted-sum representation as carrying genuine normative authority, and the Ordinal Pluralists, who accept the mathematics but deny that the weights can represent moral importance—would both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration. The document should begin with a numbered section listing the claims both sides accept about the theorem's formal premises, its scope, and what it does not establish. Follow this with a disagreements table that sets out, in neutral wording each camp accepts for its own view, the precise points of divergence: whether von Neumann-Morgenstern utility indices permit meaningful interpersonal aggregation once risk attitudes differ, and whether the social independence axiom remains defensible when goods are relational rather than purely individual. Then identify the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—including whether a veil-of-ignorance framing can justify the aggregation weights without presupposing a prior distributive theory, and whether the uniqueness of the affine transformation in cardinal utility allows cross-personal comparison in principle. For each crux, describe a concrete conceptual case or thought experiment both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case will demonstrate. Conclude with an assessment of whether any portion of their disagreement is merely verbal—perhaps over the term social rationality—specifying which portion and why.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for an ideas magazine, do not include requests for citations or reading lists, and structure the output so that it stands entirely on its own reasoning. I am particularly interested in whether the dispute over the weights is substantive or partly a matter of how each side defines rational social choice, so please be explicit about that distinction in your conclusion.
