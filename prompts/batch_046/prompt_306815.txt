I run a philosophy-of-science seminar for 14 postgraduate students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game built around Bongard-style concept induction that makes participants feel the force of underdetermination in sparse-example learning — specifically, that any explicit rule extracted from a few left/right figure boards is always vulnerable to an adversarial counter-example that respects their formulation but violates the hidden concept. Structure it in three phases with clear roles and a token economy (no real money): (1) paired induction from two boards of geometric shapes, where pairs must write down a verbal rule; (2) an adversarial round in which designated 'testers' propose new figures that fit the verbalised rule but are engineered to break it according to a hidden rule held by the facilitator; (3) a 'projectibility wager' in which pairs bet limited tokens on which feature — convexity, rotational symmetry, or number of enclosed regions — will remain stable across an unseen board, then discover that arbitrary disjunctions can always be constructed post hoc. Include rules, a timeline, the debrief mapping each phase onto the philosophical problem (the gap between data and explicit rule, the role of background commitments in fixing relevance, and the impossibility of pure pattern-matching without prior feature-weighting), and an honest note on what will predictably fail the first time (e.g., students overfitting to colour rather than shape, adversarial examples feeling arbitrary rather than illuminating) and how to handle it. State frankly which aspect of global theoretical underdetermination this activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the thesis that entire theories can be empirically equivalent — and that the facilitator must supply that framing verbally. In your answer, separate what is settled about Bongard problems and human concept induction (for example, that people often outperform current algorithms on highly abstract relational rules) from what remains genuinely contested (for example, whether human success depends on innate conceptual primitives or on domain-general analogical reasoning), and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write in British English.
