I'm in a seminar on Bernard Williams's moral psychology, focusing on his integrity objection and the problem of reasoning that involves one thought too many. Our professor has ruled on four cases, and I want to anticipate her view on a fifth before next week's discussion.

Her verdicts so far:
1. The classic drowning-spouse scenario: Saving one's wife because she is one's wife is correct; adding that this maximizes expected welfare is exactly the kind of reasoning Williams warns against.
2. The overworked parent: Declining a promotion that requires travel, reasoning that being present for one's child is a ground project, and refusing to weigh the charitable donations the extra income could fund. Verdict: Correct; introducing the charitable calculus would be one thought too many.
3. The musician: Withdrawing a composition from a sponsored concert because the sponsor's politics conflict with her values, and refusing to consider the exposure's benefit to her career or audience. Verdict: Not protected by integrity — weighing artistic reach against personal discomfort is appropriate, so resisting solely on the grounds that this conflicts with her identity is not a valid shield.
4. The soldier: Refusing to abandon a wounded comrade to complete a strategic objective, reasoning that he is a brother-in-arms. Verdict: The tactical calculus is one thought too many; the direct bond takes precedence.

The new case: A senior editor at a nonprofit scientific journal discovers that a high-profile article has a subtle methodological flaw. Publishing it — perhaps with a minor correction — would generate massive funding for rigorous research; retracting it protects her lifelong commitment to epistemic rigor but loses the funding. She considers publishing, reasoning: The net lives saved by the funding justify the compromise, even though it feels like a betrayal of my vocation.

Predict her verdict on whether this reasoning involves the problem Williams identifies. Say which rule she seems to be running — for instance, one that restricts the objection to intimate personal bonds, one that applies it to any identity-constituting commitment, or one that targets specifically the substitution of quantitative welfare comparisons for qualitative commitments — and identify at least one other rule that fits her earlier verdicts equally well. Then say which case among the musician, the soldier, or a hypothetical case you specify would best distinguish these rules. Please separate what is settled in the literature on Williams's view from what philosophers genuinely contest, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
