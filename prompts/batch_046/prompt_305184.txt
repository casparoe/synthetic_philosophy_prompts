I'm running a postgraduate philosophy workshop for about twelve participants, with ninety minutes available, on whether honour, dignity, or victimhood cultures are morally superior. Rather than asking for an abstract comparison, I want the group to inhabit the internal logic of each framework through a structured simulation so that the incommensurability emerges from the rules they must follow, not from anything I say afterwards. Please design a complete classroom activity — including assigned roles, explicit rules of interaction, materials, a precise timeline, and a debrief — built around a single ambiguous slight in a shared academic workspace: one member's offhand comment is interpreted as an insult by another, but the content is genuinely ambiguous and the power differential between the two is not visible to outsiders.

Divide the group into three "moral economies" — honour, dignity, and victimhood — each with its own currency of moral status and permitted transactions. In the honour economy, status is relational and zero-sum: an unanswered slight depletes one's stock, retaliation risks escalation unless mediated by a public ritual, and third-party observers must take sides or forfeit their own standing. In the dignity economy, status is intrinsic and inalienable: the slight is an error about the victim's worth, resolvable only through private clarification or formal institutional redress, and any public spectacle — including ritual apology — is itself a further degradation. In the victimhood economy, status accrues through recognised suffering validated by a collective third party; the slight generates moral capital if formally acknowledged, but there is a hidden saturation threshold — if claims exceed a certain rate, the currency inflates and loses value, so participants must strategically choose which grievances to formalise and which to suppress.

Give each economy identical resource cards representing reputation points, but with different exchange rates and transaction rules between economies. The middle phase should be a cross-economy negotiation: each group must propose a resolution to the shared conflict using only its own currency and rules, with a hidden collective payoff — if all three reach unanimous settlement within twenty minutes, every participant receives a bonus point, but the conditions for unanimous consent are structurally incompatible by design. Follow this with a silent individual ballot in which each participant writes which economy they would personally want to live under, then reveal the aggregate. Include a timeline (opening framing, role assignment, two simulation rounds, negotiation, ballot, debrief); the specific materials needed; what will predictably go wrong the first time (for example, dignity-group members attempting to "rescue" honour-group members by imposing private resolution, or victimhood-group members gaming the saturation threshold by flooding the system) and how the facilitator should handle it without breaking the frame; and a debrief that maps each round onto the conceptual distinctions. Be explicit about which feature of the moral comparison — particularly the asymmetry in how each culture handles invisible power differentials that the rules deliberately conceal — the activity cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Write your response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
