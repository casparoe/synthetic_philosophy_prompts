I need you, in the role of an expert metaphysician with a specialism in the philosophy of time, to help me construct a realistic example. Before you begin drafting, please spend a moment outlining how the philosophical constraints will structure the rhetoric and what you will need to avoid saying.

Please write the opening remarks (around 800 words, formal but warm) for the retirement symposium of Dr. Lena Whitmore, a philosopher who spent forty years defending presentism against eternalist critics. The audience is split: on one side are professional metaphysicians and physicists familiar with the temporary-intrinsics problem; on the other are family, former students from other disciplines, and university administrators who have no background in ontology.

The philosophical idea—presentism versus eternalism—must do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. Because Dr. Whitmore rejects the view that past and future events exist tenselessly alongside the present, the speaker must not rely on formulations that presuppose eternalism, such as suggesting that her achievements will always stand in an eternal present or that she lives on in a shared past that remains equally real. Instead, the remarks should turn on the temporary-intrinsics objection—the puzzle about how an object can genuinely change if every temporal stage exists with equal ontological status—and show how her defence of presentism offered a way to take change seriously without treating time as an illusion.

Please ensure the speech remains accessible to the non-philosophers without misrepresenting the technical stakes. After the speech, provide a brief, clearly marked note that separates what is philosophically settled (for instance, that presentism and eternalism remain live positions in contemporary metaphysics) from what is genuinely contested among specialists (such as whether presentism can resolve temporary intrinsics without ad hoc manoeuvres, or whether the interpretation of special relativity genuinely undermines presentist commitments). In that same note, flag any point where the speech takes liberties with technical details or leans on a contested reading of presentism.

Write in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the audience to consult specific texts.
