I have been redesigning my senior seminar on virtue ethics and moral psychology—twelve advanced undergraduates, one seventy-five-minute session in a room configured for triads—and I find myself dissatisfied with the usual lecture-based presentation of the situationist critique. Last week, in conversation with a colleague who works on intellectual virtue, I became convinced that students need to experience the instability of character from within, particularly in a domain they do not expect to be situationally fragile. I would like you to design a fully runnable in-class activity—part structured role-play, part forced-choice exercise with small, symbolic stakes—that makes participants discover, through their own actions, that a trait they take themselves to possess can be eroded or activated by cues they do not initially notice.

The domain should be hermeneutic patience—the intellectual virtue of reconstructing an ambiguous philosophical argument in its strongest form before offering criticism—rather than the more familiar moral virtues of honesty or compassion. Provide an intentionally ambiguous, flawed passage and describe its features without reproducing copyrighted material, then pair it with a hidden manipulation of framing conditions: for example, whether the evaluator believes her assessment will be shared anonymously with peers or attributed to her by name, and whether she works under a visible countdown versus an open-ended prompt. The rules should ensure that the text itself never changes; only these apparently incidental social and temporal cues vary. Include a payoff scheme using course-relevant but non-monetary rewards—say, accumulated points that determine seating choice for the following session or exemption from a minor administrative task—and specify roles, materials, and a precise timeline for the exercise.

The debrief must map specific moments in the activity onto the theoretical distinction between global trait explanations and local situation explanations, and it should predict what will reliably go wrong the first time the activity is run—perhaps students will rationalize their own shifts as responses to textual differences rather than framing effects, or they will deny that the cue was genuinely incidental. Be explicit about which aspect of the full situationist challenge—such as the long-term cross-situational consistency of traits over months or years—the classroom simulation necessarily cannot capture, and tell me exactly how I, as the facilitator, must supply that missing element verbally without undermining the experiential force of the exercise. Please keep the design formal enough that I could hand the instructions to a teaching assistant, and avoid any request for bibliographies, citation lists, or specific page references from canonical texts.
