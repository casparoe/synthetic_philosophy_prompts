My philosophy-of-mind professor has ruled on whether particular phenomena are genuine cases of cognitive penetration of perceptual experience. Her verdicts: yes for expert radiologists seeing suspicious patterns in noisy scans that novices miss; yes for Russian speakers showing faster discrimination at the language-defined blue-green boundary; yes for subjects primed with the word “sail” reporting the ambiguous “sail/sale” auditory figure as a boat; no for a tired hiker judging an identical hill as steeper; no for visual-search attentional guidance—finding a red cup faster among green distractors—being penetration rather than selection; no for synesthetic color experiences triggered by numerals counting as cognitive penetration. The new case is a subject warned that an upcoming neutral sound will be painfully loud who then reports that the identical sound, when presented, subjectively seems louder and more piercing than the same sound heard by an unwarned control. Predict her verdict, identify the closest analog among her earlier rulings, and lay out at least two rules that accommodate all six prior verdicts but diverge on this one—say, one that restricts penetration to conceptual or expertise-based top-down influence and one that permits expectation-driven modulation of phenomenal character. State which rule she likely runs, what it predicts here, and name the single further case that would best distinguish the rules. Finally, say how confident you are in this reading and single out the point about which you are least certain.
