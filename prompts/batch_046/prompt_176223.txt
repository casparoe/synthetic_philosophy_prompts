Design a 75-minute classroom activity for 10–12 upper-level philosophy undergraduates that makes them feel the force of Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, without opening with a lecture or naming “gavagai.” Split the group into two “field linguist” teams (4–5 members each) and one rotating “native informant.” Give the informant a hidden rulebook defining a constructed micro-language, Lex-7, used only to respond to geometric shapes that appear and vanish on a screen; the language contains exactly three observable utterances tied to behavioral conditions (pointing, nodding, withdrawal). Both teams receive identical behavioral datasets and must construct translation manuals assigning English nouns and verbs. Secretly instruct Team B to adopt a “mereological analytical hypothesis” that maps every utterance not to enduring shapes but to temporal parts or stages of shapes. In the second phase, present both teams with a novel shape-combination never previously observed and require each to predict the informant’s reaction using only its manual; arrange the rules so that both predictions are satisfied, yet they describe ontologically incompatible events. Only then should the facilitator reveal that the manuals are empirically equivalent. Include a 15-minute debrief that maps: the behavioral data onto stimulus meaning; the noun/verb choice onto analytical hypotheses; the novel-case failure onto reference underdetermination; and the inability to adjudicate without circular appeal to one’s own language onto Quine’s thesis. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the holistic interdependence of sentences across a full theoretical framework—and about the predictable failure mode: students will attempt to resolve ambiguity by asking the informant meta-linguistic clarifying questions, which must be forbidden by the rules to preserve the simulation. Specify materials (screens or cards, hidden rulebook, prediction worksheets) and a timeline.
