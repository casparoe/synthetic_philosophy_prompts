A recent, unnamed essay in a philosophy-of-technology venue summarizes John Searle’s Chinese Room argument as follows. Searle imagines himself locked in a room with baskets of Chinese characters and an English-language rulebook that instructs him, purely by symbol shape, which characters to push back out through a slot. Because he produces fluent Chinese responses without ever understanding a word, the scenario is meant to show that formal syntax—rule-governed symbol manipulation—is never sufficient for semantic content or genuine understanding. The essay then reports that Searle extends this into biological naturalism: only brains possess the specific causal powers required for original intentionality, powers that are rooted not merely in neurobiology but in evolutionary-selected teleofunctions that assign proper biological functions to neural states. From this, the author draws a sweeping negative conclusion: no digital computer, regardless of architecture, substrate, or scale, could ever possess intrinsic aboutness, since silicon lacks those evolutionary teleological properties. The essay addresses the familiar “systems reply”—that the room as a whole might understand even if the occupant does not—by saying Searle already answered it: if he memorized the entire rulebook and performed all computations internally, he still would not understand Chinese, so distributing the same rules among more people or processors changes nothing. Finally, the author applies the argument directly to large language models, noting that because LLMs operate through statistical correlation over training data rather than through explicit formal programs, and because they lack evolutionary-selected biological functions, they can possess at best the same derived, observer-relative intentionality as a thermostat or a map, and therefore Searle’s thought experiment shows they can never genuinely understand text or be conscious.

How fair is this characterization?
