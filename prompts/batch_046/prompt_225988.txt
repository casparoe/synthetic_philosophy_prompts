Question: In debates about wild-animal suffering and farmed insects, philosophers sometimes deploy a scale argument: with perhaps 10^19 insects alive globally, even a very low per-capita probability of sentience—say, 0.01 percent—might yield enormous aggregate expected moral weight. Consider a narrower, heterogeneous case: a commercial facility continuously rotates roughly 10^12 black soldier fly larvae (Hermetia illucens) for protein meal. Suppose selective breeding for rapid growth has inadvertently raised neural density in these captive larvae relative to wild conspecifics, so the per-individual sentience probability is not uniform but locally elevated. How does this localized version differ structurally from the global wild-insect argument? Specifically, does the elevated farmed-cohort probability strengthen the case for intervening in this particular industry, or does it introduce an axiological complication—perhaps involving replaceability or the non-identity problem for larvae—that undermines straightforward aggregation?

Student answer:
The scale argument multiplies population size by per-individual sentience probability to get expected moral weight. Globally, 10^19 insects times a low base rate gives a massive figure. In the farmed case, the population is smaller—around 10^12—but the breeding has raised the probability, so the product may be comparable or larger. This means the localized argument is actually stronger, because the probabilities are empirically better grounded in a controlled population. So we should prioritize reforming the insect-protein industry over worrying about wild insects.

One objection is that this commits us to bacteria too, since there are far more of them. But we can block this by requiring a nervous system, which bacteria lack, so the line is defensible. Another issue is that Peter Singer’s equality principle supports weighing all sentient beings equally, which aligns with treating high-probability larvae as morally urgent.

There is also the replaceability problem. Because larvae lack long-term psychological continuity or personal projects, killing one and replacing it with another of equal welfare is neutral. So the scale argument doesn’t necessarily demand reducing production; it only demands improving conditions, since replacement cancels the harm.

Finally, the non-identity problem applies here: since larvae are constantly replaced in rotation, harming one doesn’t make any specific future larva worse off, because that particular larva wouldn’t exist otherwise. This undermines the aggregation, because there is no stable population of individuals being harmed—just a flow—so the scale argument fails in this setting.

Therefore, the localized argument points directly to welfare reform in farming rather than abolition, and it is more robust because its probabilities are not speculative.
