Restate the long-running exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on punishment and so-called just deserts. Dennett, as I understand him, treats moral responsibility as compatible with determinism, rejects the idea that anyone metaphysically “deserves” suffering in some deep libertarian sense, and defends forward-looking, socially functional responses to wrongdoing—while insisting that our reactive attitudes and practices of holding people responsible survive this reconstruction. Caruso, by contrast, treats determinism (and the absence of libertarian control) as undermining basic desert altogether, advocates replacing retributive punishment with a public-health quarantine model, and argues that Dennett’s compatibilism merely redefines “free will” rather than addressing the moral core of the issue. Despite extensive back-and-forth, the literature largely repeats the same moves: Dennett charges Caruso with changing the subject, Caruso charges Dennett with saving words at the expense of justice.

Diagnose why this debate has stalled. Is it primarily verbal—two sides using “free will,” “responsibility,” and “desert” for different phenomena? Is it substantive but hidden—perhaps Dennett is doing conceptual analysis of our practices while Caruso is doing normative institutional design, so they answer different questions under one heading? Is there an empirical claim about human psychology, neuroscience, or the social effects of punishment that one side assumes and the other denies? Or is the impasse a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether suffering ever requires a moral justification beyond social utility?

To make the diagnosis concrete, apply it to this case: imagine an algorithmic risk-assessment system that assigns preventive restrictions to individuals based solely on behavioral risk profiles, with no attribution of blame, no retributive intent, and full transparency about the statistical model—functionally a technological version of Caruso’s quarantine model, but embedded in an institution that still uses Dennett-style language about “holding people accountable” for their risk profiles. Would Dennett and Caruso converge on its legitimacy, and if not, what does their divergence reveal about whether they disagree about metaphysics, about institutional design, or about the semantics of moral language?
