I'm trying to clarify my own view about well-being by testing intuitions on variants of the experience-machine idea, rather than on the classic formulation alone. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether a coherent principle lies behind them.

Case one: a person spends an entire life inside a machine that delivers continuous pleasure and the experience of achievement, but nothing they do has any real-world effect, and they never learn this. I think this life is seriously deficient in well-being—not because pleasure is missing, but because actual accomplishment and real connection to others are absent.

Case two: the transparent version. The person knows from the outset that every experience is simulated, understands that no real achievements occur, and chooses to remain indefinitely. My verdict is the same—deficient—and I feel it may even be worse, since they are knowingly abandoning reality.

Case three: the evening module. A person lives an ordinary real life by day, and each evening spends several hours in a shared simulation with real friends who are also in modules; everyone knows it is simulated, yet they genuinely interact, share stories, and support one another. I think this genuinely adds to well-being; it is not hollow.

Case four: the solitary module. Same schedule, but the people they interact with are purely algorithmic constructs with no corresponding real people; the user knows this, yet forms deep emotional attachments. I am genuinely torn. Part of me treats it like enjoying characters in a novel; part of me thinks it is a real emotional life that counts for something.

Case five: the achievement drug. A person lives an unremarkable real life, but takes a substance that induces the feeling of having accomplished great things, while knowing the feeling is chemically induced and no real work was done. I think this adds nothing—it is counterfeit.

Case six: the memory-transplant life. A person lives forty real years of moderate happiness, then enters a machine that replaces their memories with forty simulated years of extraordinary happiness, and lives out those years believing them real. I think the first forty years still contributed, but the overall life is fractured, and the second half lacks value.

The tension I keep returning to is between case three and case four. Both involve knowing simulation and emotional engagement, but in one the others are real people in pods and in the other they are algorithms. Is there a principle that distinguishes them without being ad hoc? And can any principle save my verdict on case two—transparent total immersion is deficient—while also accommodating my verdict on case three—partial shared simulation is enriching?

Please do the following. First, propose a precise principle, or a small family of candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible; for each clause, cite the case that motivates it. Second, state what the principle predicts for two cases I have not mentioned: one in which a person spends half their waking life in a solitary module and half in reality, and one in which two people in a transparent total-immersion machine genuinely care for each other inside the simulation. Third, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate—especially whether it forces me to overturn my judgment on the solitary module or on the transparent total-immersion case—and explain why that verdict might have to go.
