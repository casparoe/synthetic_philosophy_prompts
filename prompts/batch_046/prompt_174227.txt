Draft the joint statement that a proponent of the institutional-dependency thesis and a proponent of the explanatory-autonomy thesis in the epistemology of conspiracy theories could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration. The institutional-dependency thesis maintains that belief in what is socially classified as a conspiracy theory is epistemically defective primarily because it severs the believer's warranted reliance on large-scale epistemic institutions—scientific, journalistic, and bureaucratic—whose authority is practically unavoidable in ordinary domains, and that this dependency is rational rather than merely habitual. The explanatory-autonomy thesis maintains that explanations invoking secret coordination among powerful actors are subject to the same explanatory virtues—consistency, predictive scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness—as any other historical or scientific explanation, and that treating them as a distinct epistemic kind is itself an ideological maneuver that masks legitimate dissent.

Produce a document with the following parts, in this order:

1. Numbered agreed claims: propositions both sides accept. Include formal criteria that distinguish a conspiracy explanation from a non-conspiratorial one without assuming either thesis, the shared recognition that secrecy and asymmetric power are present in both legitimate and illegitimate explanations, and a neutral account of what would count as decisive evidence that an epistemic institution had systematically failed in a particular domain.

2. A disagreements table: the precise residual disagreements, each formulated in wording that its own side would endorse. One disagreement must concern whether institutional dependency is a rational default or a contingent social formation that can be overridden by direct explanatory success; another must concern whether explanatory autonomy collapses into unrestricted skepticism once institutional filters are suspended.

3. The cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. One likely crux is whether there exists a non-circular criterion for identifying institutional failure that does not already presuppose the autonomy thesis; another is whether the social labeling of a belief as a conspiracy theory tracks an independent epistemic defect or only a relation of power.

4. For each crux, specify one concrete case or line of evidence both sides agree is relevant, with each side's explicit prediction about what the case shows. Use the public disclosure of the COINTELPRO program in the 1970s as the shared reference point: state exactly what each side predicts about how a rational agent with prior high institutional trust should have updated upon learning of partial document releases, and whether that update pattern supports or undermines the respective thesis.

Conclude with a brief joint assessment of whether any portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, specifying which portion and why. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for publication in a philosophy journal's adversarial-collaboration section. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
