Analyze the 1997 chess match between Deep Blue and Garry Kasparov as a case study in Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian minds. Deep Blue was not an evolutionary product selected for chess ability (Darwinian), nor an agent that learned primarily through live trial-and-error reinforcement (Skinnerian); rather, it used brute-force tree search guided by a hand-crafted evaluation function to reject poor moves before execution — a mechanical analog of Popperian pre-selection, in which bad hypotheses are discarded internally rather than destroying the organism. Kasparov represents the Gregorian extreme: his expertise depended on centuries of culturally transmitted opening theory, pattern libraries, and linguistic pedagogy. The standard narrative frames the match as artificial computation defeating human intuition, but apply the taxonomy with discipline. First, classify precisely what kind of mind Deep Blue instantiated, noting where brute-force search with static evaluation differs from genuine Popperian internal modeling. Second, identify which of Kasparov's Gregorian resources — shared databases, narrative framing of positions, social transmission of chess culture — were neutralized during play and which had already reshaped his cognition during preparation. Third, examine the claim of a Gregorian bottleneck: that human expertise is constrained less by raw computation than by the bandwidth of cultural inheritance. Does Deep Blue's victory break that bottleneck, or does it reveal that Gregorian tools (computer databases, engines used in training) had already restructured Kasparov's mind before the first move? Finally, say explicitly where Dennett's four categories are silent or strained — for instance, whether Deep Blue's hybrid architecture (symbolic evaluation plus massively parallel search) amounts to a genuinely new kind of mind that the taxonomy does not anticipate — and avoid forcing the framework where it does not apply. The deliverable is a two-directional analysis: the taxonomy illuminates the match, and the match tests the taxonomy.
