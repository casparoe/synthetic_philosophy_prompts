Consider the track record of verdicts my colleague Dr. Aris has offered on material-constitution puzzles. On the bronze statue and the lump of bronze, she judges them to be distinct objects capable of different fates. On the Ship of Theseus with gradual plank replacement, she holds that the ship survives but the original plank-assembly does not, so they are not identical. On a cloud and the water-vapor mass that currently composes it, she says they are distinct, since the cloud can dissipate while the same vapor remains dispersed in the atmosphere. On a human organism and the aggregate of cells that presently constitute it, she again asserts non-identity, because the organism persists through complete cellular turnover. Yet on a sand sculpture and the grains of sand arranged in that shape, she insists they are one and the same thing—there is no further object over and above the arranged grains.

Now consider a new case: a self-maintaining ceramic lattice sustained by a continuous flow of ions through a fixed geometric scaffold. Every ion is replaced within seconds, and the lattice can survive the complete removal and replacement of the scaffold provided the ion-flow pattern is preserved. Would Dr. Aris say the lattice is identical to the ion-flow pattern, to the scaffold, to neither, or to both?

Please do three things. First, articulate at least two competing principles or rules that fit her first four verdicts equally well but yield different predictions for this lattice case. Second, predict her actual verdict, explaining which rule you take her to be running and why. Third, propose one further hypothetical case—preferably one that introduces a different kind of structural or material continuity—that would best discriminate between those competing rules.
