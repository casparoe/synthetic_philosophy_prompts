I've been trying to develop an essay on what I call the inversion problem shared by Darwin's account of natural selection and Turing's account of mechanical computation: in both cases, a system produces outputs that appear to require comprehension—adaptive design in one case, intelligent behavior in the other—without any internal agent that possesses that comprehension. Philosophers have long treated these as structurally parallel demonstrations that competence can exist without comprehension, yet the debate over what they jointly prove feels curiously stalled. One camp reads them as jointly demolishing any necessary link between intelligent or designed output and an understanding subject; the other camp insists the analogy collapses because "competence" is being used equivocally across biological and computational domains, and that in neither case have we shown comprehension is genuinely absent rather than distributed, implicit, or simply misdescribed. After working through the standard literature, I still cannot tell whether we are disagreeing about metaphysics, about the norms of attribution, or about nothing substantive at all—perhaps just answering different questions under a single heading.

Is it verbal, turning on unstated differences in how "competence," "understanding," and "agent" are being regimented? Or are there two distinct questions running together—one about causal explanation (how the output is produced) and another about normative assessment (whether attributing competence to a system requires treating it as if it understood, regardless of mechanism)? Given your diagnosis, what kind of conceptual clarification, formal distinction, or targeted thought experiment would count as genuine progress here, and what would it mean for the debate to be bedrock rather than resolvable?
