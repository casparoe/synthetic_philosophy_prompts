A friend and I have been arguing about whether the simulation argument should weaken our concern for the far future. She believes that if advanced civilizations run many ancestor simulations, then statistically we are likely in one, which means the "real" timeline might be cut short at any moment by the simulators, or compressed in ways we cannot detect. From this she concludes that extreme long-termism—worrying about humanity millions of years hence—is irrational, like a video-game character worrying about the game's sequel. I replied that even simulated beings experience real suffering and flourishing, so our moral obligations to the future remain intact regardless of our ontological status. She then raised a further point I found harder to dismiss: if simulations can be nested inside one another, then the distant future we are planning for might belong to a higher-level reality rather than to our own layer. Our efforts, she said, might therefore be directed at the wrong ontological target. I countered that we can only act within the world we inhabit, and that metaphysical nesting does not change the practical urgency of preventing catastrophe in the timeline we actually experience. We remain divided on whether the simulation argument genuinely undermines fanatical far-future concern, or whether it is merely a distracting speculation. Could you work through our exchange point by point, say where each of us is right or wrong, and tell me whether the nesting consideration actually changes the moral calculus?
