I am drafting the opening remarks for a workshop convened around a recently declassified archive of testimony gathered under an amnesty-for-truth mechanism of the kind associated with transitional justice processes in South Africa and Argentina. The audience is intentionally mixed: survivors who testified under grant of immunity, constitutional lawyers who helped design the amnesty provisions, archivists who now govern access protocols, and graduate students in moral and political philosophy. The remarks should run about ten to twelve minutes, and they must do more than establish a cordial atmosphere. I want them structured around a conceptual tension I will call the bifurcation of performative force. Within the amnesty framework, a survivor's testimony acquires a dual juridical status: it is admissible for the historical record and for the granting of immunity, yet it is permanently shielded from use in criminal prosecution. The result is a peculiar moral economy in which the same utterance operates as disclosure in one register and as enforced silence in another; the speaker is simultaneously revealed and protected, named and rendered unnameable in the juridical domain. The remarks should take this bifurcation not as a legal technicality but as an architectural constraint on speech itself. They need to honor the testimony present in the room without converting it into accusation, to acknowledge the structural silence that amnesty demands without treating that silence as mere absence, and to address the lawyers and the survivors differently without collapsing their respective standpoints into a single narrative of reconciliation. The students should be reminded—implicitly, not didactically—that the archive is not raw material for philosophical abstraction but a site where speech has been permanently altered by the conditions of its production. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific scholarly texts; the philosophical idea must do the structural work, determining what can be said, what must remain unsaid, and how the different parts of the room are addressed. Write the remarks in the first person, as though I am delivering them, maintaining a tone that is formal, precise, and respectful of the asymmetry between the philosopher's position and the lived experience of those who spoke under the grant.
