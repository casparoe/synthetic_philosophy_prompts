I'm writing my diss on gender, care work, and welfare-state design, and I've been staring at this particular policy architechture in Nordic-style parental-leave systems. The setup is: parents get, say, 480 days of paid leave to split however they want, but there's a non-transferable "daddy quota" of 90 days—if the father doesn't take it, the family just loses that chunk. Meanwhile, public childcare slots don't become available until the child is about 12 months old, and the subsidy rate is calculated based on both parents working full-time or being in education. So you've got this institutional arrangement where the state gives families a long, flexible cash-for-time entitlement but only fills the care gap with direct services after the first year, and it uses the non-transferable bonus to push fathers into taking leave without actually restructuring who does the care work long-term.

What philosphical commitments does that specific design presuppose? On one reading, it treats care in the first year as fundamentally private—something families should handle with state income support rather than socialized care—while treating care after age one as a collective good that should be decommodified. But then there's the gender-equality mechanism: the non-transferable quota assumes that equality is achieved by redistributing time within the nuclear family rather than by reducing the total care burden or socializing it. Does the practice presuppose a liberal-feminist view of equality—symmetrical autonomy between partners—or can it also sit with a care-ethical framework? And is it consistent with itself? The leave is framed in gender-neutral, individual-rights language, which sounds like it presupposes parents are interchangeable rational agents, but the "use it or lose it" structure seems to presuppose that without coercion, gendered specialization is the default. So which is it?

I'm also wondering how this compares to the same states' elder-care policies, where they often provide direct home-help services rather than just giving families cash to buy time off. If the design for infants is philosphically defensible, it probably needs some theory of dependency—maybe that infant care requires parental attachment in a way elder care doesn't? Or is the difference just path dependency and political feasibility?
