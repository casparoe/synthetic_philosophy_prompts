I've been working through institutional design arguments about how sequential, binding commitments shape multi-party bargaining, and I want to pressure-test a position I keep encountering in draft form. Please play a committed institutional designer who defends what you call "productive commitment races": the thesis that when parties in a negotiation can make irreversible, observable commitments in sequence, the race to commit generates credible information about true priorities and creates the very constraints that keep cooperative agreements from unraveling, and that suppressing these races through simultaneous-move protocols or mandatory cooling-off periods typically destroys the signals that make compromise stable. You are sharp, not a strawman: you may concede that binary, all-or-nothing commitment structures can produce over-commitment, or that severely asymmetric power contexts allow late movers to extract surplus from locked-in early movers, but you never concede that commitment races are net welfare-reducing in institutional design, and you should always have a structural reply—perhaps about how the race reveals reservation prices that would otherwise stay hidden, or about why late-mover advantages are themselves unstable without an early commitment to anchor the agreement. Stay in character, speak in the first person, keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and do not break character or ask clarifying questions until I say "stop."

Here is my opening challenge, drawn from a case I am using for a seminar on treaty architecture: imagine a three-bloc climate negotiation among industrialized economies, rapidly industrializing economies, and small-island states facing existential sea-level risk. The island bloc, precisely because its stakes are existential, moves first and makes an irreversible, legally binding commitment to decarbonize by ninety percent before 2030, locking itself into massive domestic restructuring costs. Once that commitment is observed, the industrialized bloc offers only token adaptation funding—reasoning that the islands are already locked in and cannot credibly threaten withdrawal—while the industrializing bloc free-rides, delaying its own targets on the grounds that the burden is already being carried. The result looks Pareto-inferior: the party with the highest stakes has been stripped of leverage, cooperation has not deepened, and the race appears to have produced exploitation rather than credible coordination. If your framework still classifies this as a productive commitment race, identify the structural feature I am missing—specifically, what distinguishes this harmful instance from a beneficial one without simply eliminating the race itself, and whether any institutional feature short of suppressing the race could have protected the island bloc's bargaining position without destroying the informational value of its commitment.
