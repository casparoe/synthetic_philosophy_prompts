I've been arguing with my flatmate about whether Buddhist ethics and consequentialism are actually incompatible, or if people just talk past each other. I tried to formalise the clash and now I realise I'm worried the argument shows that *any* ethics that cares about mental states and outcomes together is incoherent, which feels wrong. Here are the steps I wrote out over coffee, probably with a mistake or two in the logic:

(1) In Buddhist ethics, cetana — the volitional quality behind an act — determines its karmic weight; the actual outcome doesn't alter that. (2) Consequentialism holds that only the actual outcome determines moral weight; mental states are just instruments. (3) So if a surgeon with pure compassion makes a mistake and kills a patient, Buddhism calls the act pure and consequentialism calls it wrong — they genuinely diverge. (4) A popular reconcilation says: use consequentialist reasoning to choose your act, but keep your cetana pure througout. (5) But consequentialist reasoning sometimes picks acts that can only be performed with greed, hatred, or delusion — for instance, deceiving a dictator to prevent genocide. (6) Buddhist ethics treats cultivating greed, hatred, or delusion as intrinsically wrong, regardless of the result. (7) So the reconcilation forces a choice: either you form the bad mental state and violate Buddhist ethics, or you refuse the best-expected act and violate consequentialism. (8) Hence no agent can consistently follow both. (9) But this same structure applies to virtue ethics, common-sense morality, or any view that gives both intention and outcome independant weight. (10) So either all pluralist moral theories are incoherent, or one of my premises is doing hidden work it shouldn't.

Which premise should I drop, and what does dropping it cost? I'd favour not losing (1) or (3), but I'm open to losing the reconcilation in (4). Please answer in American English — and don't worry about my sloppy spelling of cetanā, I just mean the Pali concept.
