As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I am preparing to present at an interdisciplinary seminar on computation and physical reality, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the defense. The thesis I will defend is that the standard Church-Turing thesis conflates two claims with radically different epistemic statuses: claim (A), the mathematical equivalence of formal models of computation (lambda calculus, general recursive functions, and Turing machines), which is demonstrable; and claim (B), the physical Church-Turing thesis that no physically realizable process can compute a function that is not Turing-computable, which is an empirical conjecture about nature, not a theorem, and which is often treated in computer science as settled background despite lacking direct empirical verification. I argue that collapsing (B) into (A) is a category error with practical consequences for how we assess claims about quantum speedup, analog hypercomputation, and the theoretical limits of computation.

The audience is precisely composed of three subgroups I can identify in advance: (1) philosophers of mathematics and logic who treat the thesis as conceptually true by virtue of the equivalence between the intuitive notion of "effective calculability" and formal Turing-machine models; (2) theoretical computer scientists and complexity theorists for whom the thesis functions as uncontroversial background underwriting asymptotic analysis and the Church-Turing-Deutsch principle; and (3) a smaller contingent of researchers in unconventional computation, including quantum and continuous analog models, who treat potential violations of (B) as live scientific possibilities rather than logical absurdities.

Please provide the objections I will actually face, ordered by the combined likelihood they arise and the damage they would do to my argument. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary and framing of the subgroup most likely to raise it. Indicate what I should concede immediately, what I must hold, and provide a concise reply appropriate to the time available in a seminar Q&A. Identify the single reply that must land for my proposal to survive, flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope goes unnoticed, and specify the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Crucially, please distinguish throughout between what is genuinely settled among specialists and what remains actively contested, and do not present any interpretive stance—whether my own, that of the philosophers, or that of the computer scientists—as if it were the undisputed consensus.
