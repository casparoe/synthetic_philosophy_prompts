I’m writing this for my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, where we’ve been examining how evolutionary biology and computability theory each seem to invert our intuitive picture of design and intelligence. The question I want to press is whether Darwin’s inversion—functionally competent adaptation arising through natural selection without any agent’s comprehension of the problem being solved—and Turing’s inversion—behavior that appears intelligently responsive arising from fixed syntactic rules without semantic understanding—are genuinely structurally parallel, or whether treating them as twin “cranes” collapses a distinction that matters for how we understand human cognition. Some philosophers argue that both cases license a greedy reductionism: if eyes can be built without anyone understanding optics, and if dialogue can be simulated without anyone grasping meaning, then human reasoning and linguistic competence are just further layers of crane-built mechanisms with no residual “skyhook” of genuine understanding. Others object that the analogy overreaches, because evolutionary processes are embedded in continuous world-directed feedback loops that are not analogous to the detached, rule-governed syntax of a Turing machine, so extending the Darwin-Turing parallel to human language risks smuggling in a hidden skyhook—specifically, the assumption that representational content can be fully reconstructed from statistical or functional patterns without intentional grounding.

Write an essay on this controversy. Weigh the case for treating the two inversions as jointly supportive of a comprehension-free, reductionist account of mind, against the case that they differ in kind—particularly regarding whether evolutionary adaptation carries a form of environmental “world-directedness” that formal computation lacks, and whether recognizing this difference preserves a non-reductionist conception of understanding rather than merely postponing reduction. You should reach and defend a specific conclusion: does careful attention to the Darwin-Turing analogy actually strengthen greedy reductionism about human cognition, or does identifying where the analogy breaks down show that at least one of these domains still requires something like a skyhook? In developing your answer, you may draw on Dennett’s distinction between cranes and skyhooks and his discussion of competence without comprehension, but do not treat the verdict as obvious—justify whether human linguistic competence is ultimately a crane-built skill, or whether modeling it on evolutionary or computational inversion necessarily presupposes the very comprehension it claims to have eliminated.
