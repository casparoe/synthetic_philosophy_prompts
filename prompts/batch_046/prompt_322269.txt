I am delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "The Visibility of State Punishment," co-hosted by a constitutional-law center and a school of theology. The audience is evenly divided between appellate lawyers who have litigated capital cases and prison chaplains who have witnessed executions. I need a ten-minute address that uses Albert Camus's argument about modern execution — specifically his claim that hiding the act behind prison walls and bureaucratic ritual allows the society that authorizes it to avoid owning the reality of state killing — as the organizing framework for the entire speech. The idea must shape what can and cannot be said about transparency, democratic accountability, and the presence of witnesses; it should not serve as a decorative opening. I must avoid sounding like an abolitionist sermon, and I must avoid reducing Camus to a sentimental critic of cruelty. There is a further structural constraint: one of the organizers has recently proposed restoring public visibility not by returning executions to town squares, but by broadcasting them in real-time immersive virtual reality to citizens who opt in, arguing that this answers Camus's concern about concealed violence. The remarks need to engage this proposal directly — showing whether Camus's logic undermines it, complicates it, or unexpectedly supports it — without resolving the policy debate and without taking a direct legislative stance. The speech should not quote any author, name any case, or cite any text; it should refer to "the essayist's critique of concealed execution" rather than to specific works. The tone must be formal enough for lawyers, restrained enough for clergy, and appropriate to an occasion that is neither a rally nor a funeral. Please write the remarks.
