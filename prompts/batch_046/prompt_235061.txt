"Okay so agency here doesn't mean the volcano had a plan. It means it had what Bruno Latour calls 'actant' status — it enters networks and changes outcomes in ways that can't be reduced to human choices. Latour says actants don't need minds, just the capacity to make a difference. So Tambora wasn't 'trying' to cause famine, but its aerosol release altered global temperature patterns, which then disrupted rice harvests in Asia and led to migration patterns that reshaped labor markets. The human response — like British grain tariffs — mediated the effect, but the eruption still acted.

Some people think this is determinism because if the climate does X, history has to go Y. But that's wrong. Determinism means outcomes are fixed regardless of human response. Here, human institutions like trade policy actually shaped how bad the famine got, so the outcome wasn't fixed. The eruption was a necessary condition but not sufficient.

I think Jane Bennett's idea of 'vibrant matter' supports this too. She says non-human things have a kind of vitality or thing-power that lets them influence events. So microbes like Yersinia pestis aren't just passive background; they actively restructure feudal economies by killing laborers. That's not metaphor — it's literal causal power. The only mistake is thinking agency requires consciousness.

One objection is that this is just metaphorical language. But if we accept Latour's symmetry principle, there's no reason to reserve 'action' for humans. The language tracks real causal differences, not just poetic ones. So yes, germs and volcanoes are historical actors, but that doesn't mean humans don't matter."
