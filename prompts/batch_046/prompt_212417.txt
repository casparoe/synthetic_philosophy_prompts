I am a doctoral student in political philosophy, currently completing my dissertation on democratic legitimacy and institutional design. Last week, in a working-group discussion with colleagues who study deliberative democracy, I became convinced that the standard literature on the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—offers elegant abstract criteria but almost no operational guidance for practitioners who must apply them repeatedly under administrative constraints. I have since been asked to advise a mid-sized city’s participatory-budgeting office, which is convening a deliberative body to allocate infrastructure funds across neighborhoods, including districts with large seasonal agricultural-worker populations and remote digital consultants who pay local property taxes but maintain primary residence elsewhere. The office needs a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off philosophical verdict, because they will face structurally similar membership questions for every future body they establish.

Please turn the tension between the all-subjected-to-coercive-authority principle and the all-affected-interests principle into an operational procedure my colleagues can run without my presence. Specify, in strict order: the questions to ask about any proposed membership claim; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the precise conditions under which the procedure returns a definite inclusion, a definite exclusion, or a mandatory hand-off to a human board on the ground that the conflict is irreducibly one of values rather than facts. You must preserve the conceptual distinction between being subjected to coercive authority and being merely affected by a decision’s externalities, though you may drop finer distinctions that do not change procedural outcomes. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: a seasonal agricultural worker present for six months; a remote tax-paying consultant with no physical presence; and an ambiguous case—say, a graduate student registered in the district but with no long-term residential or economic stake—where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with the office’s intuitive preference. Throughout, take a committed position: if the two dominant presumptions conflict irreducibly in a particular case for this kind of infrastructure-allocation body, state clearly which should carry decisive weight and defend that choice with reasons rather than leaving the tension suspended.

Verdict: [state in one sentence which principle dominates in irreducible conflict and what that implies for the ambiguous graduate-student case]
