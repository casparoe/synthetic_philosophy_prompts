Here's an argument I constructed for a workshop on collective rationality and now find suspicious because it seems to show that democratic deliberation is conceptually impossible. (1) In judgement aggregation, the discursive dilemma arises when a group agrees pairwise on premises but, because of aggregation rules, ends up with a conclusion that a majority rejects. (2) To avoid this, a group can adopt premise-based aggregation: collect judgements on premises and derive the collective conclusion. (3) But premise-based aggregation requires that every member's premises be consistent; if they are not, the group must either exclude some members' judgements or impose consistency externally. (4) If we exclude inconsistent members, we violate anonymity—the democratic requirement that every judgement count equally. (5) If we impose consistency externally, we violate universal domain—the requirement that the aggregation rule work for any profile of judgements. (6) So premise-based aggregation is incompatible with democratic equality. (7) Now apply the same structure to an individual over time: my beliefs at t1 and t2 form a profile of judgements across temporal stages. (8) To remain rational, I must aggregate my past and present judgements using either premise-based or conclusion-based rules. (9) If I use premise-based rules across time, I must either reject some of my past beliefs (violating temporal anonymity) or have my present beliefs dictated by an external consistency constraint (violating autonomy over belief formation). (10) Therefore, maintaining rational consistency over time requires me to treat my past self either as disenfranchised or as subject to dictatorship by logical rules. (11) But this applies to every rational agent. (12) Hence rational agency itself requires a form of internal dictatorship, and democratic equality is impossible not only for groups but for any being that reasons across time.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I want to preserve the insight that premise-based aggregation has real costs, but the conclusion in (12) feels like it proves too much—surely rational belief revision does not require internal dictatorship. Which step fails, and what is the price of rejecting it?
