I am trying to articulate the principle behind my judgments about when a liberal state may require cultural assimilation and when it must accommodate visible difference, particularly in the tension between French-style laïcité—which suppresses conspicuous religious expression in public institutions to secure neutrality—and Anglo-American multiculturalism, which tends to accommodate such expression through exemptions and recognition. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases; they seem partly consistent and partly not.

First, a public primary school prohibits students and staff from wearing conspicuous religious symbols—headscarves, turbans, large crosses—citing the need to protect minors from religious pressure and to preserve an egalitarian environment. I judge this acceptable, but only if the prohibition applies evenly across traditions and is confined to the school setting rather than extending to public space generally.

Second, the same school prohibits religious symbols but permits students to display secular ideological symbols—climate-action badges, political party pins, or pride flags—on the grounds that these are not religious. I judge this unacceptable; the asymmetry reveals that neutrality has been replaced by a substantive secularism that privileges non-religious worldviews.

Third, a municipal ordinance bans amplified calls to prayer from mosques, citing noise pollution, while continuing to permit church bells of comparable volume and duration. I judge the ban unacceptable; comparable secular and religious sound events must be regulated alike, and selective targeting undermines neutrality.

Fourth, a public employer requires all customer-facing staff to wear a standardized uniform that, due to its cut and material, makes wearing a turban or hijab physically impossible without violating safety codes that are genuinely necessary. The employer offers no alternative position. I judge this unacceptable; a genuinely neutral functional requirement that happens to exclude should trigger reasonable accommodation—such as a modified uniform—rather than forcing assimilation into a single visible norm.

Fifth, a state-funded university provides prayer and meditation spaces for religious student groups but denies an identical request from an explicitly secular humanist association, arguing that humanism is not a religion and therefore lacks a comparable need. I judge the denial unacceptable; the relevant criterion should be the functional need for contemplative space, not theological classification.

Sixth, the state makes funding for private religious schools conditional on teaching the full national curriculum, including a mandatory module on gender and sexual equality that conflicts with one recipient school’s doctrine. When the school refuses to teach the module, funding is withdrawn. I judge this acceptable; conditional public subsidy may legitimately require adherence to core civic norms, provided the conditions apply equally to all funded institutions and do not single out particular traditions.

Seventh—the case I keep revisiting—a public hospital requires all surgical staff to wear short sleeves for infection control. The policy prevents an observant Muslim nurse from covering her arms according to her understanding of modesty, and the hospital refuses to allow fitted, washable sleeve covers that meet hygiene standards, even though such covers exist and are used in comparable institutions. Here I am genuinely torn: hygiene is a compelling state interest and uniform rules prevent arbitrariness, yet refusing a demonstrably equivalent accommodation looks like assimilation for its own sake rather than for health.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes suppressing visible difference as such from imposing genuinely functional requirements with minimal accommodation—that delivers all seven verdicts? Or must I abandon one? Specifically, can my acceptance of the school symbol ban in the first case be reconciled with my resistance to the hospital sleeve refusal in the seventh, given that both involve state institutions imposing visible uniformity for purportedly neutral reasons? If they cannot be reconciled, which verdict should I revise?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely, with the clause motivated by each case above, its predictions for situations I have not listed (for instance, a ban on face coverings in all public spaces versus a targeted prohibition only in courtrooms), and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without distortion. In doing so, separate what is settled—such as the requirement that neutral rules apply symmetrically across religious and secular analogues—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of liberal neutrality, particularly whether republican neutrality ever justifies the suppression of visible religious difference in public institutions, and do not present any contested interpretation of laïcité or multicultural accommodation as the consensus view.
