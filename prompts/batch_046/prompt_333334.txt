For a piece I am researching on translation theory, I have been examining the institutional norm in formalist poetry workshops—of the kind debated in discussions of verse preservation, such as the constraints applied to poets like Clément Marot—where a translator is judged to have failed if the rhyme scheme, syllable count, and stanzaic architecture are altered, even when every propositional claim in the original is retained with perfect semantic accuracy. In this framework, imagery, diction, and emotional register are treated as negotiable variables; form is treated as inviolable. By contrast, legal and scientific translation operate under the opposite hierarchy: semantic identity is absolute, and formal structure is entirely disposable. What philosophical theory of textual identity and artistic value does the formalist practice presuppose? Must it hold that form is constitutive of the poem itself—that altering the rhyme produces not a translation but a different work—or can it be reconciled with the view that meaning is independent of its linguistic vehicle and that formal fidelity is merely an aesthetic preference? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and describe what a translation institution committed to a purely semantic theory of textual identity would be forced to change about its evaluation criteria.
