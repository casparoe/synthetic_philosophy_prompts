I've been trying to map the conceptual architecture of the frontier AI open-source debate beyond the standard safety-versus-democratization framing, and it seems to me that most substantive disagreement hides in a few upstream questions: whether released weights remain an excludable good in any economically meaningful sense once they circulate, how distributed epistemic responsibility should work when capabilities are opaque to both the original distributor and downstream users, and whether partial-disclosure regimes—selective access, staged release, or capability-capped weights—can be stable in equilibrium or inevitably collapse into full release or full closure. There's also a crux I haven't seen adequately addressed, which I'm provisionally calling the compositional opacity problem: once open weights are fine-tuned on private data or composed into agentic loops with external tools and retrieval systems, the risk profile is no longer determined by the original release, which makes the binary "release or don't" framing conceptually inadequate. If you were advising a small conceptual research group—philosophers and theorists, not ML engineers—with roughly three years of funding and no training cluster, which of these lines should take priority, and in what order? For each, defend its place: why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is without empirical runs, and what kind of conceptual result or empirical finding would cause you to reorder the ranking. I want a defended agenda rather than a survey—specifically, which questions are overrated distractions that consume attention without shifting policy, which depend on resolving the compositional opacity problem first, and what evidence over the next few years would actually move your confidence in the ranking. I'm clarifying my own research priorities, so I care about the reasoning structure, not a bibliography or reading list.
