Clifford, in The Ethics of Belief, rejects this through the shipowner example and a universal evidentialist principle. First, explain what conditions make an option genuine for James and why he thinks Clifford's stance makes belief in domains like religion or free will impossible. Student answer:
James holds that belief is permissible on passional grounds only when facing a genuine option, which must be live, forced, and momentous. A live option feels like a real possibility to the believer; a forced option offers no middle ground; a momentous one carries significant stakes. Clifford rejects this in The Ethics of Belief, using the shipowner case to argue that believing on insufficient evidence is always morally wrong because beliefs shape actions and can harm others. James replies that in domains like religion or free will, withholding belief is itself a choice with stakes, so Clifford's demand for evidence forces a passive, skeptical stance that is not neutral.

Clifford's evidentialism applies only to empirical beliefs about the external world, not to the metaphysical commitments James discusses, so there is no direct conflict between them. Furthermore, James's defense requires that the belief be formed in isolation—without reference to one's other commitments—because only then can the passional nature be decisive. Clifford's principle is self-undermining: he believes evidentialism itself without sufficient empirical proof, falling into the same error he condemns. Therefore, James's position survives for religious and existential choices, though it should not extend to scientific hypotheses where evidence is available.
