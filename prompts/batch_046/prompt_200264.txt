You are a professional political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and collective decision-making. Before answering, take a moment to map the conceptual terrain: distinguish the general boundary problem, democratic founding paradoxes, and strategic exclusion from one another. I keep noticing a specific circular pattern in arguments about who belongs to the demos, and I want to know whether it has its own name. Here are two concrete instances. First, a polity restricts voting to long-term residents, justifying this by appeal to a shared long-term fate that is said to be demonstrable only through sustained participation in democratic life—yet the democratic practices that would demonstrate that fate are closed to the excluded residents by the very residency rule being justified. Second, a local government defines stake in the community for voting eligibility by reference to policies enacted by prior electorates, but the people who will be most affected by future policies are precisely those currently barred from being part of the electorate that defines the stake. The circularity is not merely that the demos decides its own boundaries; it is that the normative criterion used to justify the boundary makes essential reference to democratic outputs or participatory histories that are themselves products of the boundary being justified. Is there an established concept in democratic theory for this specific bootstrap structure—distinct from the standard boundary problem, from the paradox of democratic founding, and from gerrymandering? If the literature has a precise term, explain how it applies and where its edges lie. If not, propose a name and a rigorous definition that clearly separates this pattern from plain circular reasoning, from epistemic arguments about voter competence, and from temporary transitional exclusions. Be precise about what makes it conceptually distinctive rather than just another form of democratic closure.
