I'm a software engineer working on system architecture and interpretability, with no formal training in philosophy of mind, though I've recently been trying to understand the idea of an "intentional stance"—the notion that we might predict or explain a system's behavior by treating it as if it had beliefs and desires—after a colleague mentioned it in a discussion about explainable AI. A senior researcher in my group, who has published in embodied robotics and human-computer interaction, has offered a series of judgments about when it is appropriate to attribute beliefs to artificial systems. She maintains that a conventional chess engine should not be described as having beliefs about board positions, even though its evaluation function processes complex state representations, because those internal weights do not support counterfactual predictions about how the engine would behave if specific positions were different; she does, however, attribute a rudimentary belief-state to an autonomous vacuum-cleaning robot that maintains and updates an occupancy grid through direct sensorimotor interaction, on the grounds that describing it as believing "the chair is at location L" yields better predictive and explanatory leverage than a purely reflex-level account. She denies that a large language model trained solely on next-token prediction should be said to hold beliefs about the world, even when its outputs are factually accurate, arguing that its distributed representations lack the causal modularity required for belief-like counterfactuals. She also allows that a reinforcement-learning agent may be instrumentally characterized as having desires tied to its reward function, though not literal propositional attitudes. Notably, she rejects attributing beliefs to a classical symbolic expert system with an explicit knowledge base of if-then rules, insisting that static logical storage without active perceptual updating and adaptive error-correction amounts to computation rather than cognition.

Our laboratory has recently developed a system I will call MUSE: a multimodal agent mounted on a robotic platform with continuous visual and tactile input, which maintains a persistent, dynamically updated world-model encoded entirely as distributed vector embeddings within a graph neural network. MUSE employs counterfactual simulation—rolling out imagined trajectories in its embedding space—to plan physical actions, and it incorporates a meta-cognitive monitoring layer that detects its own prediction errors and adjusts its reasoning strategy in response. Nevertheless, it possesses no explicit symbolic rules, its internal representations are not locally interpretable, and it was trained end-to-end via gradient descent on interaction data rather than engineered by hand. Given my colleague's track record, should I predict that she will attribute beliefs about the environment to MUSE? I am less interested in a flat yes or no than in identifying the competing general rules that could consistently accommodate her five earlier verdicts, specifying what each rule predicts about MUSE, and determining which of her precedents is the closest analog. If her judgments genuinely underdetermine the case, I want to understand what principle she would be adopting by extending attribution to MUSE, what principle she would be adopting by withholding it, and what further hypothetical system—defined precisely by its architecture and mode of interaction—would best distinguish between those two rules.
