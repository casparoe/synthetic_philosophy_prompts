I'm taking a course on international law and we got into a weird argument about whether the genocide label is being overextended. My friend Maya said that in Lemkin's original 1944 book he defined genocide way broader than the 1948 UN Convention — it covered destroying cultural institutions, languages, and the economic base of a group, not just physical killing. She thinks that if we stick to the narrow legal definition we miss what she calls "slow genocide" where a group is destroyed socially over generations, like through forced assimilation programs or destroying the land they depend on. My other friend Jordan said thats exactly why we need the narrow version: the word carries moral and legal weight, and if we expand it to cover every harmful state policy that eventually erases a culture, it just becomes a synonym for oppression and loses its power to trigger international action. He also said Lemkin was partly motivated by his own family being killed in the Holocaust, so his broad definition was emotionally driven, and the 1948 drafters wisely trimmed it for legal usability.

Maya came back saying that bringing up his family is an ad hominem and that the 1948 narrowing was actually political — Soviet pressure to exclude political groups, plus Western states wanting to protect colonial policies. Jordan replied that even if the trimming was political, it created a workable standard based on specific intent, and without that you cant prosecute anyone because every policy has diffuse cultural effects. Maya then said there's something she calls "jurisdictional capture," where states fight over the definition not for accuracy but because controlling the label decides whether an international court can override state sovereignty. She said Jordan's "workable standard" argument is just a cover for states wanting to stay safe from cultural-destruction charges. Jordan said her concept is clever but untestable — it's just attributing hidden motives to anyone who wants precision. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldnt.
