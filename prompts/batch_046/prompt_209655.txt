In philosophical discussions of Daniel Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three predictive stances—physical, design, and intentional—a recurring analogy compares stance-shifting to changing cartographic scales: adopting the physical stance is like consulting a geological survey, the design stance like reading an engineering blueprint, and the intentional stance like using a tourist guidebook, all mapping the same region. The analogy is deployed to argue for a form of pattern pluralism: mental-state attributions are real in the same derivative but legitimate way that bus routes are real features of a street grid, and adopting the intentional stance carries no more ontological weight than choosing the guidebook over the survey. Audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what corresponds to the territory, the cartographer, the user, the patterns invariant across scales, and the patterns that are scale-relative? Second, identify where the mapping fractures. Does the analogy smuggle in an external observer or a representational relation that Dennett's stances do not require? Does it mischaracterize the predictive success of the intentional stance as merely pragmatic navigation rather than genuine explanation? Third, press a less obvious disanalogy: in ordinary cartography the territory is causally and ontologically prior, and the maps are representations of it, whereas in Dennett's account the patterns picked out by the design and intentional stances are not representations of physical-state patterns but autonomous predictive regularities with their own dynamics. Does this undermine the pluralist conclusion, or can the analogy be repaired—for instance, by treating the stances as different compression algorithms rather than different representations? Fourth, assess whether the intended conclusion—that intentional-state realism is ontologically lightweight, comparable to accepting bus routes—actually survives once these fractures are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying all the argumentative work by importing representational neutrality where none exists. If the cartographic comparison collapses under this pressure, propose a more accurate analogy that preserves the pluralist intuition without the representational baggage.
