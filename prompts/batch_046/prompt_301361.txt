You are an expert philosopher of mind and an experienced university exam setter. Before you begin, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm the central conceptual tension: on standard epiphenomenalism, phenomenal properties have no causal powers, yet we appear to form authoritative first-person judgments about them. Your task is to construct one final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme.

The question must not be answerable by simply reciting the standard statement of the problem. To push students into genuinely analytical work, build the exam around the following niche conceptual distinction—introduced in the prompt itself—between causal access (a judgment is physically caused by the phenomenal state it concerns) and non-causal tracking (a judgment is reliably correlated with a phenomenal state through an intermediate physical mechanism that receives no causal input from the phenomenal property). Ask students whether adopting a tracking-based model of introspective judgment allows the epiphenomenalist to resolve the paradox of phenomenal judgment, or whether it creates a deeper justification problem.

Constraints and specifications:
- Level: upper-level seminar final; students may use course knowledge but have no access to texts or the internet.
- Format: single essay question, designed for about 45 minutes, expecting roughly 600–800 words.
- Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, specific citations, or named authors of niche papers.
- Avoid relying on obscure positions, precise publication dates, or technical apparatus that a well-prepared student could not reasonably be expected to recall.
- The model answer should be approximately 600 words, clearly structured, and must contain these specific moves: (1) a precise formulation of the paradox of phenomenal judgment under causal closure; (2) an explanation of why non-causal tracking is proposed as an alternative; (3) an argument about whether tracking confers epistemic justification without causal interaction; (4) a reasoned conclusion on whether the paradox is dissolved, relocated, or intensified.
- The marking scheme should separate an A-grade answer from a B-grade answer by listing the specific argumentative moves required for each (e.g., distinguishing correlation from causal efficacy in the tracking relation; identifying the justification gap that tracking leaves open; explaining why physical causation of the judgment mechanism does not entail phenomenal access). It should also identify specific mistakes that should cost marks (e.g., conflating epiphenomenalism with eliminativism about consciousness; assuming that reliability automatically yields authoritative first-person knowledge; ignoring the asymmetry between third-person tracking and first-person judgment).

Please begin with a brief outline of the essay structure, then present the exam question in full, followed by the model answer, and conclude with the marking scheme broken into grade bands.
