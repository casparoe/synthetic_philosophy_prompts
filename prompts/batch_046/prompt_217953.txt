I have just concluded a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend who is a diplomatic historian. She maintains that treating germs, climate anomalies, or volcanic eruptions as “historical actors” is either a metaphor that obscures human responsibility or a slide into environmental determinism, and that any middle position is conceptually incoherent. I want to run a fifty-minute seminar for second-year history undergraduates who, like her, are deeply committed to narrative agency and view non-human causes as mere background conditions at best.

Please design the teaching plan—not the lecture text itself. I need the dependency order of the ideas: which distinction must be secured before the students can even entertain the question, and why the standard “background conditions versus primary causes” framing actually blocks rather than opens the concept. Identify the one concrete historical scenario that should carry the session; it must involve an interaction between a volcanic or climatic forcing event and an epidemiological event, it must be specific enough that causal pathways are traceable but not so overdetermined that human choices disappear, and you should specify exactly what the scenario must include and what it must exclude to serve this pedagogical purpose. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will arise—beginning with “this is just determinism in scientific clothing,” then likely moving to an intentionalist fallacy about microbial or climatic “purposes,” and perhaps ending with the anxiety that granting causal weight to nature diminishes human moral significance. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the conceptual move that defuses it without merely asserting the middle position. Include a final check—one question or task—that distinguishes students who have genuinely grasped the distinction between causal significance, agency without intentionality, and moral responsibility from those who have only been made uncomfortable. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether specific theoretical frameworks, particular historiographical debates, or technical climate-science mechanisms—and acknowledge them as omissions rather than as settled exclusions. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions.
