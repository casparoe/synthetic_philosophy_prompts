Assume the role of a professional philosopher specializing in comparative metaphysics and political ontology. Before responding, briefly brainstorm the different structural ways philosophical debates stall—verbal ambiguity, hidden empirical premises, mismatched disciplinary questions, or bedrock intuitions that resist argument.

Then diagnose the following long-running impasse. For decades, scholars comparing East Asian and Western conceptions of personhood have argued over whether relational accounts (in which the self is constituted by social roles, reciprocal obligations, and harmony) and liberal individualist accounts (in which the self possesses pre-social rights and moral status prior to community) represent genuinely incompatible ontologies, or whether they merely stress different features of a more complex relational-individual structure. Both camps cite familiar sources—Confucian role-ethics emphasizing interdependence, and Kantian and Lockean traditions emphasizing autonomous agency—yet the dispute never advances. Some claim the conflict is verbal: "individualism" is read as "atomism" by critics but as "non-reducible moral status" by defenders. Others treat it as substantive, insisting that either obligations are grounded in socially constituted duties or in pre-political rights, with no middle ground. A further hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions—one descriptive (how is the self constituted?), one normative (what justifies political authority?)—while using the same vocabulary.

Analyze the actual structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of disciplines talking past one another? Refer specifically to what each side says about moral agency and social constitution. What, if anything, would resolve it—a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a reframing? And if it is not resolvable, explain precisely why not.
