I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop on post-conflict reconstruction next thursday — about half the room are diplomats who've actually negotiated ceasefires in the Balkans and the Sahel, and the other half are grad students in politcal theory. The organizer asked me to frame the day around Hegel's view, specifically from the Philosophy of Right, that war isn't just an evil to be eliminated but has a kind of ethical function: prolonged peace lets particular interests rot the state from within, and war forces the universal substance back into view.

I want the remarks to actually use that idea structurally, not just mention it — they should tell the practitioners why building stable peace might accidentally create the stagnation Hegel describes, and they should tell the students why this isn't just an apologia for violence but a claim about what keeps a community from collapsing into self-interest. Don't lay out both sides and leave me to choose: take a real stance on whether Hegel's claim that war reveals the state's ethical substance should guide how we think about reconstruction, or whether it's a dangerous rationalization that needs to be rejected outright. The speech has to work for people who know Hegel and for people who just know what it feels like when a negotiation stalls, so don't dumb it down but don't use jargon as a shield either. No citations or bibliography, please — just the text — and I'm serious about the philosophical stakes, so don't soften them to sound nice.
