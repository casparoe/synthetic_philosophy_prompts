Construct a thought experiment in the philosophy of biology that engineers a borderline case isolating evolutionary dynamics from organizational closure. Design a physically realizable, non-digital chemical system—explicitly not a virus, prion, computer simulation, or conventional cell—in which simple replicating polymers undergo sustained heredity, variation, and selection for improved catalytic efficiency within a shared open bath, yet possess no enclosing membrane, no internal metabolic network regulating its own boundary, and no unitary self-maintaining organization; the “individuals” are transient polymer strands that use ambient energy passively. Present the scenario in one paragraph, with no exotic physics, so that evolutionary dynamics are present and organizational closure is entirely absent, with no other confounding feature. Then show the reasoning: explain why an account that defines life through heredity-with-variation-and-selection must classify the system as living (or as a living process), and why an account requiring organizational closure—internally maintained boundary conditions that regulate the system’s own production—must deny it life. Identify the exact structural feature of your design that forces this divergence. Next, perform the removal test: describe what happens to both verdicts if each replicator is encapsulated in a lipid membrane that maintains internal chemistry but does not alter the evolutionary dynamics, and say whether the case collapses back into a familiar example. Finally, state how a defender of the organizational-closure view would attack your scenario—whether by denying that evolutionary dynamics alone are ever sufficient, or by arguing that your open bath secretly exhibits a distributed form of closure—and note whether that attack succeeds only by redefining closure so broadly that it loses its distinctive explanatory role.
