You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm: what makes a research proposal in this area both philosophically sharp and executable in a single semester?

Write a short research proposal—around two pages, suitable for a graduate workshop or small internal grant—on the following half-formed idea. The Principal Principle holds that rational credence should align with known objective chance, except where other admissible evidence is present. Standard debates treat admissibility as a binary filter on outcome-related evidence, but they rarely address a distinct tension: when an agent knows that the chance-generating process is deterministic at a lower level (for example, a specific biochemical pathway) and also knows the aggregate statistical chance assigned to a broad reference class (for example, a clinical-trial prognosis), the agent possesses mechanistic information about which sub-process is actually running. Your proposal should ask whether we can distinguish statistical admissibility—evidence about outcomes within the reference class—from mechanistic admissibility—evidence about the internal deterministic structure generating those outcomes—and whether recognizing this distinction forces a revision of how the Principal Principle applies in cases of heterogeneous aggregation. State the precise question, explain why current treatments of deference to objective chance leave this open, outline a conceptual and formal method (analysis of credence updates plus a model of partitioned chance structures, with no empirical data collection required), specify what a successful result would look like (for instance, a refined admissibility clause, or a proof that the distinction collapses back into standard formulations), and give an honest assessment of what could make the project too ambitious for a one-semester term paper. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; refer to positions and arguments by name only, and note that references will be added later. Keep the tone formal, the framing concrete, and the deliverable focused on a plan for work not yet done.
