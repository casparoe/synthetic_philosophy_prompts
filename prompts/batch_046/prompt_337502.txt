In a reading group on intertemporal democratic theory, a colleague keeps invoking the Ulysses contract as the master analogy for constitutional entrenchment: just as Ulysses binds himself to the mast and has his crew plug their ears so that future temptation cannot steer the ship onto the rocks, a polity writes rigid amendment rules and bills of rights to bind its future selves against the siren call of majoritarian passion. She uses this to license the claim that such precommitments are not anti-democratic but rather expressions of rational autonomy projected across time. I want to audit the analogy carefully rather than accept it by intuition. Can you lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the sirens, the mast, the ropes, and the command not to untie—and then identify precisely where the mapping fractures? I am especially interested in three pressure points: first, that in the original case the crew is present but deafened, whereas in constitutional politics the analogous "crew" (future citizens or representatives) are not yet born and have never consented to the binding; second, that Ulysses retains sovereign authority to command his own release, which raises the question of who holds that authority in a constitutional order where the restraining rules are meant to be self-binding rather than externally imposed; and third, whether the analogy quietly smuggles in a paternalistic temporal asymmetry—present rationality versus future irrationality—that democratic theory cannot consistently endorse. Once these disanalogies are fully on the table, does the normative conclusion—that entrenchment is a legitimate exercise of collective self-rule—survive, or was the analogy carrying the entire argumentative load? If a better analogy for democratic precommitment exists—perhaps drawn from institutional design rather than individual psychology—what would it be, and which of these specific fractures does it avoid?
