I am a graduate student currently enrolled in my department's seminar on moral responsibility and collective agency, and I am preparing my defense presentation for next week. I am defending a modified Jaspersian framework that I call archival institutional liability. The thesis is that a contemporary nation-state can bear a non-psychological, non-criminal form of structural culpability—distinct from Jaspers's four familiar categories of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—when three conditions are jointly satisfied: first, the state's administrative and legal apparatus exhibits diachronic continuity with the regime that committed the wrong; second, the state's institutional archives and bureaucratic procedures actively preserve the normative structure of that wrong rather than merely recording it as dead history; and third, the present state continues to derive concrete juridical, territorial, or economic benefits traceable to the prior regime's actions. On this view, present citizens do not inherit guilt in any moral or psychological sense, but the institutional entity they inhabit carries a reparative obligation tied specifically to bureaucratic memory rather than to cultural memory or collective consciousness. I apply this to the specific case of whether the contemporary Federal Republic bears a distinctive modality of responsibility for National Socialist crimes—one that differs both from the direct political guilt Jaspers assigned to German citizens in 1946 and from a purely metaphorical national shame.
