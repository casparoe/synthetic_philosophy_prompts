I have recently been appointed as a deputy for health and welfare decisions on behalf of an elderly relative whose cognitive capacities are now in steady decline, and the experience has made the distinction between internal and external reasons urgently personal rather than merely academic. I find myself unable to determine whether the obligations guiding my choices about her care, residence, and financial affairs arise genuinely from my own subjective motivational set — perhaps from long-standing familial commitments — or whether they apply to me with full normative force regardless of whether I happen to be moved by them at all. Before I examine particular theories in detail, I want the full specification of what any adequate account of practical reason must deliver if it is to distinguish internal reasons, which depend in some constitutive way upon an agent's existing psychology, from external reasons, which are said to bind agents independently of their contingent desires and dispositions.

Write the desiderata: the core phenomena the theory must explain, including cases where an agent is rationally required to act despite lacking any corresponding desire, as well as cases of what might be termed "motivational grafting" — where an agent's subjective set has been deliberately or structurally shaped by institutional roles, relational expectations, or therapeutic regimes she never chose — and whether reasons flowing from such grafted desires should count as internal; the distinctions it must preserve, particularly that between possessing a reason and being actually motivated by it, and between a reason's being merely contingently related to an agent's psychology versus necessarily so related; the constraints imposed by moral psychology and philosophy of action that it cannot contradict without abandoning explanatory adequacy; and which single requirement does the most damage to the major competing positions. Please flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable — I have in mind the tension between demanding that all authentic reasons be capable of motivating rational agents and demanding that some reasons retain authority over agents who are constitutionally alienated from them. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard of named theories, though I would welcome a brief indication of how Bernard Williams's internalist framework and a robust moral externalism respectively stand against the list. Please provide your answer in British English, in a formal and polished register.
