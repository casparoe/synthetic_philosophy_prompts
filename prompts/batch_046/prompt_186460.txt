At a recent workshop on intergenerational justice, two colleagues—Jordan and Alex—became locked in a sustained disagreement over whether inheritance taxes can be justified solely by appeal to fair equality of opportunity. I am working through my own position for a dissertation chapter on dynastic wealth and democratic equality, and I need a rigorous, move-by-move adjudication of their exchange rather than personal advice. Both are making genuinely decent points; neither is a strawman.

Here is the back-and-forth as it unfolded.

Jordan opened by arguing that inheritance taxes are structurally necessary to preserve fair equality of opportunity across generations, not merely to reduce income inequality. The core problem, she said, is not that heirs receive a lump sum, but that dynastic wealth creates a self-reinforcing cycle: inherited capital buys access to elite education, professional networks, and—crucially—political influence, which in turn reshapes institutional rules to protect that very wealth. Without taxation, the "starting gate" is not just uneven; the race itself is continuously redesigned by those who inherited the fastest shoes.

Alex replied that this conflates equality of opportunity with equality of outcome. The morally relevant baseline, he insisted, is the start of an individual’s adult life. Once an adult enters the market, differences arising from parental choices—including bequests—are not "morally arbitrary" in the same sense as natural talents or birth-class, because they result from legitimate autonomous choices by parents. To tax inheritance is therefore to penalize familial loyalty and to treat parents as mere custodians of state resources.

Jordan responded that Alex’s baseline is artificially static. She granted that parental autonomy matters, but argued that large inherited fortunes do not merely benefit heirs individually; they generate institutional feedback loops. Heirs and their families can lobby for tax loopholes, influence philanthropic governance of public goods, and engage in regulatory capture, so the harm is structural rather than distributive. The problem is not that one person starts ahead; it is that the rules of the game are continuously rewritten to keep them ahead.

Alex then pressed a consistency objection. Even granting the structural harm, inheritance taxes are incoherent if we do not also tax the transmission of "social capital"—parental education, linguistic codes, professional networks, residential sorting—that is equally determinative of life chances but legally inalienable. Taxing only liquid financial wealth arbitrarily targets one transmission mechanism while ignoring others that are often more decisive. If the goal is genuinely equal opportunity, we should either tax all transmission vectors or none; selecting money alone looks like class resentment dressed up as philosophy.

Jordan countered that the two mechanisms are not equivalent. Financial inheritance is fully fungible and directly convertible into political power and market advantage; social capital, while important, is non-rivalrous and diffuse. Moreover, society can mitigate social-capital transmission through universal public education and integration policies without violating familial intimacy, whereas unregulated financial bequests actively concentrate democratic influence in ways that public policy cannot easily neutralize. Taxing the liquid asset is therefore not arbitrary; it targets the specific vector that undermines democratic equality.

Alex’s final move was to argue that Jordan’s distinction collapses in practice. The fungibility of money is precisely why wealthy families use it to purchase social capital—elite schooling, internships, geographic mobility—so taxing the liquid asset without taxing its downstream uses is like taxing the symptom while ignoring the disease. Furthermore, if democratic equality is the genuine concern, the appropriate remedies are campaign-finance reform, antitrust enforcement, and transparency mandates—not an inheritance tax that penalizes saving, undermines the intergenerational social contract that motivates capital accumulation, and ultimately reduces the total pool of investment available to future generations.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing. In particular, I am interested in whether Jordan’s "institutional feedback loop" argument genuinely distinguishes financial inheritance from social-capital transmission, and whether Alex’s appeal to the "intergenerational social contract" succeeds in blocking the tax without also blocking other forms of redistribution. Do not conclude with a non-committal summary; reach and defend a verdict.
