At a writers' residency, two fellow novelists—Elena and Marcus—argued over how Nietzsche's three modes of relating to the past should govern literary practice. (1) Elena claimed the novelist must practice critical history: inherited narrative architectures—such as the three-act structure or omniscient narration—should be dismantled when they no longer serve present creative life, because to preserve them as an antiquarian does is to curate dead forms. (2) Marcus objected that critical destruction is self-defeating for a novelist, since our medium is language and form itself; dissolving inherited structures destroys the very material we work with, so we are necessarily antiquarian, preserving technique, with only limited room for monumental admiration of past masters. (3) Elena replied that Marcus confuses preservation with veneration: antiquarian history hoards everything indiscriminately, whereas selective destruction—discarding only what chokes life—leaves what is vital, without canonizing any replacement form. (4) Marcus countered that Elena's "selective destruction" is actually monumental history disguised: by elevating, for instance, modernist fragmentation over Victorian coherence, she venerates a hero-form that serves her present ambition, not true critical dissolution. (5) Elena answered that the risk is avoidable: one can write in fragments designed to resist canonization, maintaining critical force without creating a new monument. (6) Marcus concluded that even fragments become a recognizable style, and style inevitably monumentalizes; therefore the novelist should accept being primarily antiquarian, permitting critical impulses only at the margins.

Please adjudicate move by move—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—say who is more right overall, and indicate what both overlook about the interaction of these modes in artistic, rather than historiographical, practice.
