Write a two-page research proposal for a six-month philosophical-history project. The question: historians increasingly treat pathogens, climate anomalies, and volcanic eruptions as “actors” in political and social narratives—framing, for instance, the 1815 Tambora eruption or the 1918 influenza pandemic not merely as background conditions but as entities with causal agency that reshape state capacity, military logistics, or demographic politics. Is this attribution of agency a substantive metaphysical claim about non-human causation, or is it a heuristic narrative device? More precisely, can one develop a principled taxonomy that distinguishes (a) direct causal efficacy, (b) structural conditioning, and (c) narrative agency for entities like microbes, climate patterns, and volcanoes—such that invoking (c) for Tambora or the 1918 virus does not entail environmental determinism?

State the question so it has a determinate answer: what criteria must be satisfied for a non-human event to count as an “actor” rather than a condition, and do existing case studies in environmental history meet them without collapsing into determinism? Explain the approach: rather than surveying the entire field, select two paired cases—one microbial, one geophysical—where historians have explicitly used agency-language, and analyze whether that language tracks a distinct causal structure or only rhetorical emphasis. Say what the possible conclusions are: either the taxonomy reveals that “actor” talk is consistently reducible to complex causal conditioning (in which case the determinism worry is misplaced but the language is redundant), or it identifies a genuine intermediate category of “structural agency” that avoids determinism by being irreducibly relational. Give an honest assessment of failure: the project stalls if the distinction between causal efficacy and narrative framing turns out to be purely pragmatic with no historiographical bite, or if the paired cases are too heterogeneous to support generalization within six months.

In the response, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. Acknowledge that no serious scholar denies microbes and volcanoes cause historical change, but note that philosophers of history and metaphysicians of causation genuinely disagree about whether agency-language applies only to intentional beings, can extend to functional systems, or is entirely metaphorical. Do not present any one of these positions as the consensus.
