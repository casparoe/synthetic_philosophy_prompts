I am preparing the final assessment for an upper-level philosophy of mind and artificial-intelligence ethics seminar. Please draft one essay question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a detailed marking scheme. The essay question should present students with a hypothetical distributed computation: a large language model running across three geographically separated server clusters that share weights only through hourly batch synchronization, producing outputs that are behaviorally indistinguishable from a continuously running, causally integrated instance, yet lacking any real-time interaction between clusters during the intervals. The prompt must ask students to evaluate whether this system should be granted the same moral status—specifically regarding sentience attribution—as its continuously integrated counterpart, and to identify the precise philosophical error in answering solely by appeal to substrate continuity on the one hand or solely by appeal to behavioral equivalence on the other. The model answer should include: (1) a sharp formulation of the conflict between causal-integration criteria and functional-equivalence criteria for moral patienthood; (2) an analysis of why temporal discontinuity in computation threatens one criterion but not necessarily the other; (3) a diagnosis of the mirror-image mistakes—the substrate-continuity fallacy and the pure-behaviorist fallacy; (4) a reasoned judgment on the moral status of the hourly-synced system, with explicit conditions under which it should or should not count; and (5) a brief reflection on how this reasoning bears on biological cases such as sleep or general anesthesia. The marking scheme should allocate 25 points total, distinguish an A-range response (22–25) from a B-range response (16–21) by naming the specific argumentative moves required in each band, and identify the two most common student mistakes together with the point deductions they should incur. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should stand on conceptual argument alone.
