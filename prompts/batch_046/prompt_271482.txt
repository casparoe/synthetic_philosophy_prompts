As I construct a narrative about the fragility of historical evidence, I keep returning to the Vinland Map—the parchment chart that surfaced in the 1950s and was brought to scholarly attention shortly thereafter, purporting to depict Vinland in a hand ostensibly belonging to the fifteenth century. For decades the controversy turned on whether the document was a genuine medieval artifact or a modern fabrication executed with anachronistic geographic knowledge. Analyze this episode through the lens of source criticism, that discipline stretching from Herodotus, who weighed oral testimony against the physical traces left in landscapes and artifacts, to Thucydides, who insisted on separating immediate eyewitness account from later reconstruction, and onward to the modern practice of testing a document’s internal consistency, provenance, and material substrate against one another.

Treat source criticism not as a mechanical checklist but as an epistemic posture: a disciplined judgment about which anomalies are fatal, which silences are merely tolerable gaps, and when the absence of corroboration should outweigh the presence of a striking claim. Apply this posture to the Vinland Map. How would a Herodotean mode of inquiry—tracing chains of transmission, comparing the chart’s depiction of Greenland and Vinland against independent archaeological evidence of Norse presence—have assessed the document’s credibility? How would a Thucydidean standard—requiring contemporaneous corroboration and treating later testimony with suspicion—have evaluated the fact that no fifteenth-century source mentions such a map? Identify precisely what classical source criticism illuminates about the case—the suspiciously modern outline of the continent, the anachronistic projection of geographic knowledge unavailable in the medieval period, the broken chain of custody before the document’s emergence—and what it necessarily cannot address, particularly the chemical analysis of ink and the radiocarbon examination of parchment, methods that rest on scientific principles foreign to textual historiography.

Then reverse the direction: ask what the Vinland Map reveals about source criticism itself. Does the episode demonstrate that internal criticism must always defer to material science, or does it show that even forensic results require a hermeneutic frame—someone must still decide which chemical anomalies count as definitive disproof and which as tolerable noise?
