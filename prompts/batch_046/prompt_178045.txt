I have been asked, with almost no notice, to substitute for my sister in leading a single ninety-minute session of her community philosophy circle, a group of well-read adults with no formal metaphysical training; she had planned to introduce the problem of universals, and because she is unwell I have agreed to do it, which makes the topic suddenly and personally urgent for me since I want to honor her commitment without delivering a lecture I barely understand myself. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself: the precise order in which the ideas must be introduced and what logical dependency holds between them, so that the problem feels like a genuine puzzle rather than an exercise in labeling positions. For the carrying example, I want to use the idea of a boundary-condition—specifically, the shared feature of being a threshold in a physical doorway, the edge of a shadow cast on uneven ground, and the boundary of an abstract mathematical set—and I need you to say whether this example succeeds, meaning it applies to genuinely different particulars, cannot be reduced to the material parts that happen to exhibit it, and avoids being mistaken for a mental category, and also whether it carries a hidden flaw, such as smuggling in spatial intuition that does not transfer to non-physical cases, that I should correct or abandon. Then lay out the misconceptions these adults will bring or form in sequence: I expect they will first say universals are only words, then that they are private mental images, then that modern science has made the question obsolete—tell me exactly where in the arc to intercept each misconception, with a brief phrase or redirect that stops it without breaking the flow, and warn me if there is a fourth misconception specific to this boundary-example that I have missed. I also need one final check for understanding that demonstrates they have grasped the structure of the problem rather than just memorized the names realism and nominalism; design a single question that applies the distinction to a case involving a negative property, such as the absence of sound in two different silent rooms, which they will not have discussed during the session. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—for instance, whether I should avoid introducing immanent versus transcendent universals, or resemblance-based accounts, or any technical apparatus that requires prior familiarity with medieval logic—and state that refusal plainly. Please respond in American English, in continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, written as if in a single letter.
