Two philosophers, discussing reproductive ethics over coffee, fall into a disagreement about whether the standard procreation asymmetry survives a particular test case. Their exchange runs as follows.

(1) The first argues that the asymmetry is robust: we have a stringent reason not to bring into existence a person whose life would contain net suffering, but we have no parallel reason to bring into existence a person whose life would contain net happiness. The reason, she says, is not about aggregate welfare but about the direction of moral claims: suffering generates a direct, person-affecting grievance, whereas happiness generates only a conditional preference that lacks a bearer when the person does not exist.

(2) The second replies that this merely restates the baseline problem in fancier language. Every moral evaluation requires a baseline. Not creating a happy person leaves no one worse off relative to non-existence, and preventing suffering improves someone relative to that same baseline. The asymmetry is therefore not a deep feature of moral reasons but an artifact of comparing actual states against a counterfactual of non-being.

(3) The first concedes the baseline framing but insists on an asymmetry of modal weight. Negative reasons, she claims, carry greater moral urgency because they respond to a determinate harm that can be avoided, whereas positive reasons depend on the contingent instantiation of a welfare-bearing subject. To illustrate, she proposes a fixed-identity genetic editing scenario: we can edit the genome of an existing embryo to eliminate a severe disability, or edit it further to enhance cognitive capacities. Once the disability is removed and the life is clearly worth living, the asymmetry implies we have no further reason to pursue the enhancement, since there is no subject who is harmed by its absence.

(4) The second seizes on this implication. He argues that in fixed-identity cases the non-identity problem is sidestepped—the same subject exists in both outcomes—so the asymmetry cannot hide behind the absence of a victim. If we genuinely have no reason to enhance, then we face an implausible welfare ceiling: our obligations to the same person stop exactly at the threshold where life becomes worth living, and any further improvement is morally optional even at negligible cost. He concludes that either the asymmetry collapses in fixed-identity contexts, or it commits us to an arbitrary ceiling that no plausible person-affecting theory should accept.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four positions, rate its strength from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and provide a brief justification. Then identify the specific point—if any—on which both interlocutors are missing something important, and explain what a more adequate view would need to say about the relationship between the procreation asymmetry and obligations to determinately existing potential persons.
