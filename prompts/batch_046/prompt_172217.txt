I'm in a grad seminar on algebra and set theory and I keep noticing something weird about how Zorn's lemma gets used in practice. In human-written proofs people invoke it constantly — every vector space has a basis, every ring has a maximal ideal, all that — but they almost never mention it's literally equivalent to the Axiom of Choice. Meanwhile in formal proof assistants like Lean or Coq, if you want that result you have to import AC explicitly and it's flagged like a big deal. So there's this silent institutional norm in math culture where we treat Zorn as a harmless neutral tool even though it's carrying serious ontological baggage. What philosophical commitments does that practice preseuppose? Does it assume that existence proofs don't need to be constructive to count as knowledge? Or can a formalist about mathematics just treat the lemma as syntactic sugar and avoid any real commitment?

Also I was reading about this proposed curriculum reform — kind of like conflict-of-interest disclosure but for theorems — where every textbook proof using Zorn would have to carry a little warning label saying "this result depends on AC / is non-constructive". What view of mathematical knowledge or epistemic responsibility does that labeling practice presuppose? Would it actually change anything philosophically, or is it just bureaucratic hygene? And is the current silent practice even consistent with the same culture's obsession with rigor and explicit axioms everywhere else? I want to understand whether the implicit theory is coherent or if we're just being sloppy, and what a proof culture built on explicit constructivism would actually have to change.
