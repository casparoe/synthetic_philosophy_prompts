I'm writing a seminar presentation for my political philosophy course on Michael Dummett's immigration ethics, and I want to move past the standard summary of his open-borders argument in On Immigration and Refugees. Instead, I'm probing an underexplored tension: his anti-realist philosophy of language holds that meaning and understanding are constituted by shared communal practice, which might seem to generate obligations to preserve the linguistic and cultural conditions that make meaning possible—yet his normative conclusion demands borders open enough to rapidly transform those very practices. Among the open questions this raises, which matter most, and in what order? Should I prioritize (1) whether anti-realist semantics actually entail cultural-preservation obligations strong enough to constrain immigration rights; (2) whether Dummett's normative case can be reconstructed on grounds entirely independent of linguistic considerations; or (3) what institutional designs, if any, could reconcile open entry with the stability of the communal practices his philosophy of language requires? Rank these lines of inquiry and defend the ordering: which is conceptually upstream of the others, which is tractable with philosophical rather than empirical sociological tools, and which alleged policy implications—say, for language-policy conditions on residency—are genuine consequences versus overrated extensions of his view?
