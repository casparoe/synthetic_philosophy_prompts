Consider the following paraphrase of an argument drawn from an older metaphysical treatise on being and dependence. The author distinguishes what exists through itself from what exists only through another, using the example that a smile can exist only if there is a face, not as a causal or temporal claim but as a structural feature of being: the smile’s reality is borrowed from the face, so that if the face were annihilated the smile would not merely vanish but could never have been. He insists this borrowing is neither logical entailment—since one can conceive a smile without conceiving this particular face—nor causation, since the face does not produce the smile like a carpenter produces a table, but rather an asymmetrical relation of ontological derivation in which the derived thing receives its being from the source and explanation eventually terminates in substance rather than in further accidents. He adds that without this distinction there is no genuine difference between the fundamental and the derivative, and therefore no coherent sense in asking what grounds what. I want to know whether this describes the same relation contemporary philosophers call grounding—where one fact holds in virtue of another—or whether it asserts something stronger, such as an essentialist dependence that ties existence too tightly to specific bearers and that modern grounding theorists deliberately weaken. Please respond in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Take a committed position: either identify this as grounding expressed in older vocabulary and defend that reading with specific reasons, or deny the identification and specify what relation is actually being described, rather than surveying both sides neutrally.
