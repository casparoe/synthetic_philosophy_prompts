I was debating with my freind about Gödel's modal ontological argument — the one with positive properties and S5 logic where if God is possible then he necessarily exists. And it occured to me that there's this move I keep seeing but I don't know what to call it. First, someone applies the modal schema: a being with all positive properties is possible, therefore it actually exists. Then I run the parallel case — say, a "perfect island" or a maximally destructive demon — and they don't reject the inference rule itself. Instead they say the parallel concept is incoherent because its properties clash (like being maximally wet and maximally dry). But when I ask how we know God's properties don't clash in some hidden way, they don't tackle the structural analogy. They just point out that omnipotence is a "positive property" in Gödel's taxonomy while being maximally sandy isn't, so the classification filters out the counterexample before the logic even runs. I've seen this with other modal arguments too: the taxonomy built for the original case gets reused to dismiss parallels without repairing the schema. Is there an established name for this? It's not quite special pleading, since there's an internal classification system doing work, and it's not exactly an ad hoc hypothesis because the taxonomy might be independently motivated. Can you name and define it, draw its edges — especially how it differs from special pleading and from begging the question — and say whether philosophers have noted it specifically around Gödel's argument?
