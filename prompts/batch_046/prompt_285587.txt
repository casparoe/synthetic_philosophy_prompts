I work on distributed data systems, and I've recently tried to map metaphysical debates about persistence onto the consistency models I deal with every day. Specifically, I understand endurantism as a kind of mutable-record model: there is one entity—say, a person—whose primary identifier stays constant across time, and whose properties are updated in place, so that the object is literally "wholly present" at each timestamp even though its attributes change. Perdurantism, by contrast, looks like an event-sourced architecture: what we call a person is actually a four-dimensional sequence of discrete temporal stages, each bearing only instantaneous properties, with identity over time reconstructed from adjacency or causal links between slices.

My difficulty is that both models seem formally consistent, and in software we treat such choices as implementation details rather than discoveries about reality. So I assumed philosophers must be arguing about something deeper—perhaps about whether temporary intrinsics can be genuinely instantiated by one and the same subject. On that reading, endurantists need a mechanism (maybe relational properties indexed to times) that allows the same record to bear being 30 at t1 and being 40 at t2 without logical contradiction, whereas perdurantists avoid the contradiction by distributing the incompatible properties across distinct parts.

But I'm not sure this captures the actual dispute, because my framing may smuggle in false equivalences. For instance, I initially took "wholly present" to imply that no part of the object is missing at any time—like a complete snapshot—which made change seem impossible unless I allow in-place mutation, which then looks like a violation of strict identity. Meanwhile, the perdurantist stages seem to require some binding principle—causal continuity, psychological connection, or a primitive relation of continuity—to avoid collapsing into a mere aggregate of unrelated slices.

So my question is this: have I reconstructed the disagreement correctly as a conflict over whether a single subject can consistently instantiate temporary intrinsics, or have I merely translated two ontological positions into isomorphic database schemas—mutable records versus append-only logs—and thereby obscured a genuinely metaphysical question about what exists? If the latter, what is the substantive commitment—perhaps about the reality of temporal parts, or about the nature of property instantiation—that my engineering analogy necessarily hides?
