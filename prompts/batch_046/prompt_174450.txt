I am a retired theoretical physicist who, since leaving my department, has been attempting to read philosophy with the same rigor I once applied to statistical mechanics. Last week, in a small discussion circle I have formed with former colleagues, we attempted to work through Friedrich Nietzsche’s The Gay Science, and I found myself stuck on the well-known passage in which he introduces the idea of eternal recurrence, often referred to as the greatest weight. In this passage, Nietzsche asks the reader to imagine a demon who slips into one’s loneliest solitude and announces that this very life, with every pain and every pleasure, every small and great event, will be repeated in precisely the same sequence innumerable times, without any change or omission; the demon then asks whether one would curse him and gnash one’s teeth, or whether one would fall down and worship him for having spoken something so divine. I understand that Nietzsche frames this as a kind of test, but my training leads me to wonder whether he is proposing something like a cosmological thesis—perhaps analogous to the Poincaré recurrence theorem, which demonstrates that a bounded mechanical system with finite energy will eventually return arbitrarily close to its initial state—or whether he intends the scenario as purely hypothetical and ethical. I also notice that he describes the thought not as a comfort but as the greatest weight, which puzzles me: if the recurrence is literal, then acceptance might be a form of metaphysical resignation; if it is hypothetical, then affirming it seems to be an exercise in loving one’s fate entirely, including all that one would ordinarily wish to alter. Yet I cannot tell from the text alone whether Nietzsche requires a specific ontology of time—say, a cyclical rather than linear cosmology—to make the thought coherent, or whether he deliberately leaves the physical mechanism unspecified in order to isolate the ethical response.

Could you help me disentangle these threads? I would be grateful if you could explain whether the passage is best read as a cosmological claim, an ethical experiment, or some hybrid of the two, and whether my comparison to statistical-mechanical recurrence is entirely out of place. Please write your response as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, in the manner of a thoughtful letter; I find that such formatting distracts me from the argument itself.
