I've been working through some metaphysics problems for a seminar on identity and modality, and a friend walked me into an argument I can't find the exit from. The setup begins with Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles: necessarily, if two objects share every property, they are one and the same. Black's familiar two-sphere universe is usually taken to challenge this, since sphere A and sphere B share all intrinsic properties and differ only relationally—say, by being two miles apart. A natural defense is that relational properties still count, so A bears being two miles north of B while B bears being two miles south of A, and PII survives. So far I follow. But then my friend extends this to what he calls a periodic lattice world—an infinite, boundary-less array of qualitatively identical spheres arranged with perfect translational and rotational symmetry, lacking any external frame of reference. He argues as follows: (1) In this lattice, pick any sphere S. (2) For every relational property R that S bears to another sphere, there exists a different sphere S* that bears R to a corresponding partner. (3) Therefore no relational property distinguishes S from any other sphere. (4) Even allowing relations, PII entails that all spheres in the lattice are numerically identical—there is effectively only one sphere instantiated at every point. (5) Yet such a lattice is clearly metaphysically possible. (6) So PII, even in its relational form, rules out a possible world and is false. (7) Rejecting PII forces us to accept bare numerical difference: objects can be distinct with no qualitative ground whatsoever. (8) Once bare difference is permitted, we must allow duplicate worlds—worlds that are qualitatively identical in every respect but contain different individuals. (9) But duplicate worlds violate the principle of sufficient reason, since there could be no rational ground for actualizing one rather than its perfect twin. (10) Hence the attempt to save PII from Black's counterexample by appealing to relations ultimately collapses into a metaphysics that destroys the rational intelligibility of modal space.

I want to hold onto the idea that relations save PII in Black's original case, and I also want to preserve the intuition that a perfectly symmetric lattice world is genuinely possible. But I feel sure something has slipped between the claim that every relational feature of S is shared by some other sphere and the conclusion that S lacks any distinguishing relational feature altogether. I also worry there may be a hidden modal slide between PII being false in this particular world and its being false as a necessary principle. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I block the move from symmetric indistinguishability to bare numerical difference, do I have to deny that duplicate worlds are possible, or only that they are metaphysically innocent?
