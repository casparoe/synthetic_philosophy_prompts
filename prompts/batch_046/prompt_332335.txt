hey, i'm trying to figure out what i actually think about laws of history, in the Popperian sense - can there be unconditional ones, or only trends, or nothing at all?

firtst, Marx's stage theory (feudalism -> capitalism -> socialism) feels like a fake law. it made big predictions, but whenever capitalism survived a crisis people just said the contradictions hadn't matured yet. so unfalsifiable, not a law.

but the demographic transition - industrial societies eventually crash their birth rates - feels real. it held for britain, japan, brazil, korea. it even predicted south korea's collapse faster than expected, and wasn't just tuned after the fact.

Spengler's cyclical life-cycles of civilizations? clearly not a law. pure analogy, fitted post-hoc.

then there's cliometric counterfactuals, like the claim that without railroads US GDP in 1890 is about 25 percent lower. this uses economic mechanisms that seem law-like elsewhere, but applied to one path. is that a historical law or just borrowing from physics?

here's the tension: i think if child mortality hits X, fertility falls within two generations is okay as a conditional law. but rome was bound to fall because of fiscal overreach feels wrong, even though it's also conditional. what's the difference? maybe the conditions in the second are too vague?

and climate correlations, CO2 to temperature, operating inside history - do they count? sometimes i say yes, they're just science in the past. other times i think Popper's point is about open social systems, so natural laws don't count as laws of history.

so is there a coherent principle that sorts these? maybe something about counterfactual robustness, or about whether the conditions can be specified without using the outcome? please lay it out precisely: what clause handles each case, what it predicts for something i haven't listed (like the democratic-peace claim that democracies never fight each other, which looked solid until recent tensions), and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to throw out.
