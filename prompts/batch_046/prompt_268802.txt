I'm a phd student in philosphy of mind, working on introspection and self-knowledge. Lately I've been obsessed with how people misread their own emotional states in ways that aren't just mistakes but actually serve some practical purpose. I keep returning to Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day — Stevens's narration feels like a perfect case study in what I'd call motivated interpertive failure, especially around Miss Kenton. I want to read the novel through current debates about introspective authority and confabulation, but I want to inject a slightly more niche framing: emotional self-knowledge as predictive processing, where the brain actively suppresses interoceptive prediction errors that would threaten narrative identity. Not just 'Stevens is wrong about being sad,' but his system predicts sadness, then downweights the signal to maintain the self he needs to be.

Could you take the major scenes — the letter from Miss Kenton, the stop by the lake near the end of his journey, and the final meeting at the bus stop — and map them onto this? Where does Ishiguro treat Stevens as genuinely ignorant of some hidden emotional truth, and where does the novel resist that framework, suggesting there's no 'real' feeling underneath, just the constructed self? I'm especially curious whether the novel gets at something philosophical argument usually misses: that being wrong about yourself can be a kind of practical success rather than pure epistemic failure. I don't need citations or a reading list, just a serious reading that takes both the philosophy and the novel on their own terms.
