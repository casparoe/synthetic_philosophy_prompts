At a meetup last week Maya and Leo got stuck arguing about whether you can build a "low impact" AI without basically solving value alignment first. Maya said yes: you define impact structurally, like restricting the AI's causal influence to a bounded physical region or requiring thermodynamic reversability outside that box. She thinks this is like putting the AI in a sandbox with transparent walls -- you don't need to know what's valuable inside the world, just keep the AI from reaching out.

Leo said that's sneaking values in through the back door. The boundary of the "sandbox" has to be drawn somewhere, and picking that somewhere requires knowing what parts of the world matter. If the AI moves a boulder that happens to be a sacred site, the physical footprint is tiny but the "side effect" is huge. Without a full specification of value, you can't even say whether a given causal chain counts as a side effect or a main effect.

Maya came back with an objective reference class: the AI should avoid altering the trajectory of distant galaxies but can mess with local temperature. That doesn't require knowing what's good, just physics.

Leo replied that "distant galaxies" is already an anthropocentic bias we're smuggling in. If the AI was genuinely value-neutral about impact, it shouldn't privilege nearby over far away unless we tell it why.

Maya then said okay, maybe pure structural impact is impossible, but we can use "preservation of option value" -- the AI avoids reducing the number of possible future states available to humans. Leo said that just pushes the problem back: "available to humans" requires specifying human values, and "option value" itself is a contested economic concept that assumes risk-neutrality or something.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Then say who's closer overall and whether there's any coherent notion of "side effect" that doesn't collapse into full value alignment.
