I've been working through Bernard Williams's scattered reflections on opera—particularly his claim that Wagner's music dramas achieve a distinctive emotional and moral seriousness precisely by refusing to justify extreme feeling through rational narrative, so that the audience's proper response can become something like an uncontrollable surrender that reason neither sanctions nor explains away. I'm writing this for a philosophy-of-art seminar in which my peers keep reducing opera either to pure formalism or to disguised moral instruction, and I want to push back with a concrete imaginative case that takes Williams's anti-rationalist suggestion seriously rather than domesticating it.

Write a short story set in a near-future orbital conservatory, the last functioning opera house in a decaying station, where the repertoire has been restricted to works in which every dramatic choice—betrayal, ecstatic union, self-destruction—is presented without psychological backstory, causal explanation, or moral accounting. The protagonist should be the station's acoustic engineer, responsible for maintaining the resonant architecture that amplifies the singers' voices. Over the course of the narrative she discovers a growing structural distortion that makes the voices physically impossible, warping them into registers and harmonic overtones that no human throat could produce; she must then decide whether to repair the system and restore rational audibility or to preserve and even amplify the distortion, recognizing that the architecture's refusal of acoustic logic mirrors the opera's refusal of narrative logic.

Take a definite position in the ending: either she repairs the system and restores rational listening, or she locks the distortion into the structure and accepts that listening will become permanently irrational and physically overwhelming. Give the narrative reasons for her choice rather than leaving it ambiguous, and explain within the story's logic why that choice is not merely aesthetic preference but a commitment about what opera demands from its audience. After the story, include a brief but explicit note identifying where you take liberties with Williams's actual views—for instance, if you extend his Wagnerian example to all opera, invent the architectural metaphor of acoustic distortion, or attribute to him a stronger anti-rationalist thesis than he held—and state whether those liberties serve the conceptual point or distort it.
