You serve as the senior ethics and acquisitions advisor to the acquisitions committee of a regional university archive, and I am writing to you as a fellow committee member who must cast a vote next Thursday. We are deciding whether to accept a donation of approximately 300 handwritten patient-intake ledgers and 40 hours of analog cassette interviews drawn from an unlicensed volunteer health cooperative that operated from 1978 to 1993, providing care to populations systematically excluded from formal medical systems. The donor, a retired volunteer nurse, demands that all patient identifiers be permanently sealed—not merely redacted—and that the audio never be digitized or subjected to automated transcription, accessible only through in-person listening in a supervised reading room. At the same time, medical historians have petitioned for full open access, contending that accepting the materials only to seal them extends the historical silence these patients already endured and risks turning the archive into an instrument of concealment. One member has proposed adopting a framework she terms strategic illegibility: preserving the physical record intact but structuring access protocols so that state or commercial actors cannot readily parse the contents, while allowing community-based researchers meaningful entry.

Before delivering your formal recommendation, please briefly outline the three central tensions—preservation against protection, institutional legitimacy against solidarity with marginalized sources, and the archive’s complicity in either repairing or deepening historical silence. Then draft the committee memo: state a clear bottom-line recommendation in the opening paragraph (accept with a defined access framework, reject, or accept with modified conditions), include a concrete implementation timeline, and treat the historians’ open-access argument as the strongest opposing position, engaging it seriously rather than dismissing it. Keep the memo to no more than 450 words. Also state explicitly which argument or piece of evidence would change your recommendation, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail. Write in a formal, institutional tone suitable for circulation to the full board.
