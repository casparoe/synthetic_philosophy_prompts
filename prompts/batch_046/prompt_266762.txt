I was talking with my study group about logical induction and how people actually reason about mathematical claims they can't verify step by step, and we kept noticing this pattern that none of us could name. Imagine there's a hard theorem A with a really long proof, and then there's a simple corrolary B that follows in basically one line. People will often say they're like 85% on B, and when you ask why they point straight to A. But then if you poke a hole in A's proof — maybe there's a hidden assumption that fails in some weird edge case — they don't revise B downward by anything close to the same amount. Instead they retreat to "well, B was always independently plausible, I never really needed A for it." So the logical link is used to pump confidence upward, but it doesn't carry doubt back downward; it acts like a one-way valve.

I've seen versions of this in actual arguments, like people being very confident in some consequence of the Riemann Hypothesis while openly admitting they haven't checked RH itself, and then when RH starts looking shakier they just decouple the consequence and cite other intuition. Is there an established term for this exact asymmetry? How is it different from ordinary incoherence in the logical-induction framework, or from just being a sloppy Bayesian? And if there's no specific name, how would you carve it out — what's the boundary between rational decoupling because B really does have independent support, and just protecting a belief by retroactively weakening its premises? I care more about the edges and distinctions than about a bare label.
