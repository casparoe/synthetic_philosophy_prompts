After a long argument with a friend about whether deference to objective chance collapses once the chance concerns where you stand in a centered world, I want a seminar activity that makes the conflict visceral rather than abstract. We are running an upper-level metaphysics and philosophy-of-science seminar: twelve graduate students, one ninety-minute session. I want them to feel the pull of applying the Principal Principle to self-locating credences—specifically, to discover for themselves what happens when an authoritative stochastic oracle reports objective frequencies over a partition of physically identical situations, each containing a subject with first-person access to one local mental-state token, and the subjects must decide whether to defer to the oracle or to reason from their private access.

Please design a runnable in-class simulation. Use only simple materials—sealed envelopes, colored cards, a visible weighted die or spinner, score sheets, and point tokens that carry no monetary value but determine a collective outcome (e.g., whether the group "funds" a shared research proposal). Arrange the rules so participants run into the problem before it is named.

Structure:
1. Setup (10 minutes): Each student receives an envelope containing one of two type-identical mental-state descriptions—say, "Token-M" and "Token-N"—that differ only in their token identity, not in qualitative content. Unbeknownst to them, a physical randomizer (the weighted die rolled in full view) has already assigned objective frequencies across six identical "branch" slots: Branch A occurs with physical chance 2/3, Branch B with 1/3. Each student is privately assigned to one branch and one token. They know their own token and that the world contains only these two token-types distributed across branches, but they do not initially know the oracle's report.

2. First round—private reasoning (15 minutes): Students form a credence about "My token belongs to Branch A" using only their first-person access and a principle of self-indifference for centered worlds. They record this on a score sheet.

3. Oracle revelation (5 minutes): The facilitator reveals the objective physical chance report—2/3 A, 1/3 B—generated by the visible randomizer. This is presented not as statistical data about past rolls, but as an authoritative report about the current distribution of branches.

4. Betting round (20 minutes): Students must place point bets on the proposition "I am in Branch A" under two conditions: first using only their private reasoning, then revising in light of the oracle. The payoff scheme rewards calibration: points are awarded according to a proper scoring rule applied to each student's reported credence, but there is a collective bonus if the group's average credence tracks the oracle's frequency. Crucially, include one "forced-deference" round where the rules require them to adopt the oracle's 2/3 as their personal credence regardless of private token, and one "independence" round where they are penalized for ignoring their token.

5. Reveal and comparison (15 minutes): The envelopes are opened collectively. Students see that the token distribution does not match the oracle's frequency in the sample (by design, the assignment is fixed, not resampled), forcing them to confront whether their deference was rational or whether it required treating their own mental-state token as merely one draw from an urn about which they have no special access.

Debrief (25 minutes): Map each phase onto the formal idea—private reasoning as centered belief formation, the oracle report as the Principal Principle's demand for deference to objective chance, the forced-deference round as the tension with Self-Indifference, and the collective reveal as the problem of applying uncentered chance to indexical propositions. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the actual metaphysics of objective chance in a deterministic multiverse, the precise two-dimensional semantic treatment of centered worlds, and the formal distinction between de dicto and de se probability, all of which you will supply verbally. Also be frank about what will predictably go wrong: students will conflate the facilitator's randomizer with the metaphysical chance generator, and they will struggle to see why their private token is not straightforward evidence about branch membership—handle this by pausing after the first round to ask them explicitly whether they are reasoning about the world or about themselves, without resolving the ambiguity for them.

Inject a niche framing: treat the mental-state token not as qualitative experience but as a causally isolated, type-identical but token-distinct representational state, so the debate that emerges is whether the Principal Principle applies to the proposition "this very token is in the high-frequency branch," and whether deference in that case forces abandonment of rational self-indifference.
