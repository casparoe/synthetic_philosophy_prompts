I have been thinking, without any formal training, about whether I am literally the same person I was a decade ago or whether I am better understood as a kind of long chain of connected moments. I have encountered the philosophical debate about whether people and other objects are fully present at every instant of their existence — sometimes called endurantism — or whether they are spread through time like a four-dimensional "worm" made up of distinct temporal parts, which I understand is called perdurantism. Rather than just reading more arguments, I would like to see how a specific story might help me work through the idea.

Please read the film The Curious Case of Benjamin Button through that lens. Benjamin is born with the body of an elderly man and ages in reverse, eventually dying as an infant. If endurantism is correct, then at every point Benjamin is wholly present, yet he seems to carry genuinely contradictory intrinsic properties — being physically old and physically infantile — without ever being two people. Does the film present these as properties of one enduring self, or does it implicitly treat him as a sequence of separate stages linked only by memory and causation?

I am particularly struck by his relationship with Daisy. She loves him when he looks middle-aged, then when he looks young, and finally cares for him as he becomes an infant. Is her love directed at a single enduring person, or does the narrative invite us to see her as relating to different temporal parts? And does Benjamin's reverse aging make the philosophical problem of temporary intrinsics — how one thing can have incompatible properties at different times — especially vivid? Or does the film resolve the tension by suggesting that psychological continuity, rather than bodily form, is what binds the stages together?

I do not want the film reduced to a mere illustration of the theories. I want a reading that takes both the emotional logic of the story and the philosophical debate seriously: where the film illuminates something the arguments miss, where it risks distorting the view to fit the plot, and whether Benjamin's life ultimately favors the idea that we are four-dimensional worms or shows that an enduring self can survive even the strangest bodily transformation. What, if anything, does this particular story add to the debate that pure reasoning alone cannot?
