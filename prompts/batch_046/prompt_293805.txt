I am writing as a student in an upper-level philosophy-of-history seminar. During our unit on value-neutral explanation, a classmate defended adopting an explicitly normative historiographical framework by invoking the claim that the arc of history bends toward justice. She argued that this is not an ethical bias but a causal insight: the past is not a random walk but displays a long-range directional tendency toward moral improvement, so recognizing that tendency belongs to accurate explanation rather than to partisan interpretation. I want to come back to our next session with sharper conceptual tools, but I am unsure what precise commitments the slogan actually carries.

Could you lay out the distinct, precise theses this claim might express, treating each as a separate reading rather than a single ambiguous metaphor? In particular, I would like you to distinguish and evaluate:

1. A metaphysical teleological reading—history is driven by an immanent purpose or rational end-state that pulls events toward a specific conception of justice.
2. A probabilistic or institutional-convergence reading—certain social arrangements (e.g., liberal-democratic institutions) tend to outcompete alternatives over very long timescales, producing an observable statistical drift without any metaphysical directionality.
3. A normative or prescriptive reading—the claim functions not as a description but as a practical commitment: we ought to organize our institutions and historiography as if progress is inevitable, perhaps for motivational or coordination reasons.
4. A reflexive or performative reading—belief in the arc is itself a causal mechanism; the expectation of justice produces the behaviors that bring justice about, so the slogan describes a self-fulfilling dynamic rather than an independent historical law.

For each reading, please specify: what kind of “justice” is being tracked (distributive, procedural, recognitional, or something else); what temporal scale makes the claim substantive rather than trivial (centuries, millennia, evolutionary timescales); whether the reading permits local reversals, path-dependent dead ends, or prolonged regressive periods; and, crucially, whether that particular interpretation actually supports the move from “history tends toward X” to “historians should build X into their explanatory framework.” I am not asking for bibliographic citations, author attributions, or a reading list—just conceptual disambiguation with verdicts on plausibility and argumentative force. Please respond in American English, using a formal tone appropriate for an academic seminar discussion.
