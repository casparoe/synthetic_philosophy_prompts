As someone who spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics before retiring, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in metaphysics, partly to understand how conceptual analysis differs from the quantitative modeling I am accustomed to. Last week, in a session on classical theism, a fellow student—an undergraduate in mathematics—dismissed both Anselm's original argument and Gödel's modal reconstruction with the single sentence: "existence is not a predicate." The phrase was delivered with the confidence of a settled result, yet no one in the room paused to ask which of its many possible meanings was being invoked. I am skeptical that one slogan can simultaneously undermine Anselm's conceptual analysis, in which existence functions as a perfection belonging to the greatest conceivable being, and Gödel's formal derivation, in which necessary existence is treated as a positive property derivable within a second-order modal framework rather than appended as an ordinary first-order predicate.

I would like you to disambiguate the slogan precisely. Please present a menu of distinct readings—grammatical, logical, metaphysical, and epistemological—specifying for each whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or genuinely contested by specialists in philosophical logic. In particular, I want to know which readings target first-order predication in natural language, which concern the inclusion of being in a concept's intension, and which address the specific formal role that "necessary existence" plays in Gödel's axioms about positive properties and modal consistency. For each interpretation, indicate whether it actually undermines Anselm's claim that a non-existent greatest being is inconceivable, whether it survives Gödel's move to derive existence from the consistency of positive properties in modal logic, and whether it is the version the student likely intended. I intend to bring this taxonomy back to the seminar so that we can discuss the argument on its merits rather than by slogan.
