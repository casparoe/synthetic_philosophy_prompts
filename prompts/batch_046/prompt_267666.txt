You are advising a small interdisciplinary working group—philosophers of ethics, population ethicists, and a long-term-risk policy advisor—who have three years of dedicated funding to resolve conceptual bottlenecks at the intersection of antinatalism and Benatar's asymmetry. Their mandate is not to settle antinatalism's overall truth, but to produce a defended, ordered research agenda: which open conceptual questions deserve priority, why, and in what sequence should they be pursued?

Structure your recommendation around four genuinely interdependent problem clusters, treating them as candidates for prioritization rather than as independent topics: (1) the internal architecture of the asymmetry—whether its premises about the value of absence can be modified, restricted to beings with a welfare baseline, or generalized without collapse; (2) its interaction with person-affecting restrictions in population ethics, specifically whether resolving the asymmetry forces a commitment to totalist, average, or critical-level frameworks, and how it relates to the non-identity problem; (3) its extension to potential non-biological minds—whether artificial or synthetic descendants lacking biological sentience fall under the asymmetry's absence-of-pain clause, and whether that extension strengthens or undermines longtermist reasoning about future population creation; and (4) its meta-ethical interpretation—whether the asymmetry reveals a structural feature of harm (that harm requires an actual subject) or merely an asymmetry in comparative value weighting.

For each cluster, defend its place in the ranking using explicit criteria: upstream dependency (does resolving this unlock progress on others?), tractability for conceptual philosophers rather than empirical scientists or formal modelers, neglectedness relative to current literature, and the strength and directness of its practical implications for reproductive ethics or long-term evaluation of future populations. Identify the trade-offs: if resources are constrained, which cluster should be deprioritized—because it presupposes answers to another, because it requires empirical inputs the group cannot supply, or because its implications are more indirect than they initially appear?

Specifically address what evidence or conceptual breakthrough over the three-year window would cause you to reorder the clusters, and flag any cluster whose importance is likely overrated in current philosophical debate. Your output should be a ranked agenda with reasoning, not a survey, not a reading list, and not a call for empirical research. Keep all reasoning self-contained: do not request bibliographies, citations, or external verification.
