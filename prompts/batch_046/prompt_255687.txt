In my bioethics reading group we've been stuck for months on advance directives and whether the person who wrote them still has authority once they're severely demented. Everyone agrees that the patient now has diffrent preferences, everyone agrees suffering is bad, and we all reject the simple idea that it's not the same person. But half the group says the earlier self made a kind of binding promise to future caregivers, and the other half says authority dies with the capacity to revoke, like a power of attorney that lapses when the principal can't understand it. We've mapped every argument from the literature and nobody budges. Is this actually a hidden empirical disagreement about what demented patients exeperience? Or is it a bedrock clash about whether autonomy is a one-time allocation of rights or an ongoing releational status? Or are we just talking past each other—one side asking about moral authority and the other about legal enforceability—under a single heading? I want a diagnosis of the stall, not another round of pros and cons, and an honest take on whether some distinction—maybe around partial cognitive restoration or nested counter-directives issued during lucid intervals—could actually break the deadlock, or if we're just circling intuitions that can't be argued for.
