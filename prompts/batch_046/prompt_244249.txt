I'm trying to identify whether there is an established term for a specific historiographical pattern I've noticed in accounts of simultaneous discovery—particularly with calculus (Newton and Leibniz) and evolutionary theory (Darwin and Wallace). The pattern works like this: a commentator cites the near-simultaneous, independent emergence of two results as strong evidence that the discovery was inevitable, as though intellectual history were converging on a single peak that anyone with sufficient talent would eventually reach. Yet for that convergence to be visible, the commentator must treat the two results as essentially the same discovery, smoothing over deep structural divergences—Leibniz's differential notation and monadological metaphysics versus Newton's fluxions and commitment to absolute space; Wallace's divergence-driven mechanism versus Darwin's gradualist framework with sexual selection and deep-time accumulation. Once these differences are flattened, the 'identical' result can be invoked to prove inevitability; but if the divergences are taken seriously, there are not two climbers reaching one summit, but two distinct theoretical edifices erected in the same decade, which considerably weakens the inevitability argument. So there seems to be a circular smoothing at work: simultaneity is used to demonstrate convergence, but demonstrating convergence requires suppressing the incommensurability that makes the simultaneity ambiguous in the first place. Is there a recognised name for this precise double move—using parallel arrival to argue for inevitability while implicitly assimilating the arrivals so that they count as parallel? If not, how ought one to distinguish it from ordinary Whig history, or from the more modest claim that a shared intellectual environment made independent breakthroughs likely? Please answer in British English, and I would value clarity on where the edges of the concept lie—what it is not, as well as what it might be.
