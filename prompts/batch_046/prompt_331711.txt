Policy commentators frequently invoke the Munich Agreement of 1938 as an analogy for negotiating with an expansionist adversary: Britain and France conceded the Sudetenland in the belief that satisfying Hitler’s territorial demands would prevent wider war, only to see the concession followed by the dismantling of Czechoslovakia’s border fortifications and, eventually, the occupation of the Czech lands themselves. The analogy is typically deployed to license a hardline conclusion—that any strategic concession to a rising power signals weakness and invites further demands, just as appeasement did.

Audit this analogy with precision. First, map the correspondences explicitly: what in a modern target situation corresponds to the Sudetenland concession, to Hitler’s subsequent demands, to the Anglo-French strategic calculus, and specifically to the loss of fixed defensive works that made the 1938 concession structurally irreversible? Next, identify where the mapping fractures—particularly in cases where the asset being conceded is not geographic territory but a technological, economic, or institutional capability whose value is fungible rather than anchored to a map. Examine what psychological and strategic assumptions the analogy quietly imports about whether adversarial demands are genuinely satiable, and whether the strategic harm lies in the concession itself or in the removal of a specific geographic buffer. Finally, assess whether the hardline anti-concession conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether a different structural comparison—perhaps to a security dilemma in which both sides rationally arm in response to each other’s defenses—better captures the dynamics without smuggling in the Munich narrative of naive misjudgment.
