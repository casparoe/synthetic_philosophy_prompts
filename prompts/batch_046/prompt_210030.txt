I'm in a small reading group on Enlightenment political thought and we just got to Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the theater in Geneva — the one where he argues they shouldn't build one. There's a passage that's been driving me up the wall. He writes that the actor recieves wages to lie, that he puts himself on display for money, and that the more skillfully he deceives the audience the more they aplaud him. The spectator, meanwhile, forgets his own affairs, feels pleasure in seeing crimes and vices that ought to make him blush, and leaves the hall more corupt than he entered. But then he turns around and admits that Genevans already have other pasttimes — dancing, cards, music — that might be just as frivolus, yet he doesn't call for banning those.

I'm trying to understand this through the idea of 'mimetic capture,' where the problem isn't just that bad things are shown but that the theatrical form structurally forces the crowd to emotionally synchronize with whatever is performed, so applause becomes an automatic moral endorsement regardless of the play's explicit lesson. Does Rousseau think the corruption comes mainly from the content (the specific vices depicted) or from this formal structure of spectatorship? And how does he justify singling out theater when he concedes that other amusements produce a similar kind of collective distraction? I'd really like help untangling the passage itself, not a bibliography or reading list — I just can't tell whether he's making a formal argument about spectatorship or just complaining about the particular morals of the plays he knows.
