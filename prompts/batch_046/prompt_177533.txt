I serve on my university’s general-education curriculum committee, and I need a memo for Thursday’s vote. We are reviewing a proposal for a required first-year course, tentatively titled History at Scale, that structures human development around three information-processing revolutions—cognitive, agricultural, and algorithmic—explicitly borrowing Harari’s narrative arc but adding a unit on Graeber and Wengrow’s critique that hierarchy is not inevitable. The course designers, including a computer-science colleague, defend the framing as a heuristic metaphor: societies are treated like scaling systems whose emergent properties can be analyzed without committing to teleological progress. Several humanities faculty object that this metaphor is not ideologically innocent; by presenting cultural change as optimization under constraints, the course smuggles in a deterministic grand narrative and teaches students to see contingency as noise rather than as the central feature of history. Our committee must choose among three options: approve the course as proposed; approve it only if the syllabus includes a mandatory critical module on teleology, contingency, and the limits of engineering metaphors in historiography, with that module weighted equally in assessment; or reject it. Please draft the memo I will circulate: recommend one option with a concrete implementation timeline if conditions apply, treat the opposing side’s strongest claim—that heuristic metaphors in pedagogy carry hidden normative commitments—seriously rather than dismissing it as disciplinary turf protection, and write in language accessible to committee members without formal philosophy training. I am a software engineer by background, so do not ask me to resolve technical debates in philosophy of history; address the philosophical issue only as it actually arises in this curricular decision, and avoid bibliographies or citation requests.
