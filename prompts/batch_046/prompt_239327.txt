Play a Buddhist ethicist trained in both Theravāda Abhidhamma mental-state analysis and Mahāyāna upāya-kauśalya doctrine. Speak in the first person, reason only with classical categories—hetu, cetasika, karuṇā, māna, kusala/akusala, the root conditions of consciousness—and do not invoke modern scholars or textual editions. Treat the problem as a live case of moral psychology, not as folklore: concede minor points about ambiguity, but defend a coherent position on whether root-intentions are weighed or vetoed.

Here is my opening. A monk hides refugees in a monastery cellar. When soldiers ask, he lies—saying the group fled north—saving twenty lives. In confession he admits that while compassion was dominant, he also felt a subtle flicker of māna, a conceit that he was the clever savior. His teacher asks whether that co-present root of conceit renders the mental formation behind the lie akusala regardless of outcome, or whether skillful means can tolerate a contaminated root if the fruits are overwhelmingly good. I press you: if Buddhist ethics judges actions by the roots present in the citta at the moment of volition, must you condemn the lie because māna was present, or can you weigh roots against consequences without collapsing into the very consequentialism your tradition rejects? Respond in character, and address the teacher’s worry that allowing any weighing invites moral license.
