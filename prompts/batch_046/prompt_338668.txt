You serve as the lead review officer for a regional climate-adaptation board that evaluates competing district-scale socio-ecological redesign proposals—each proposal defining a complete, mutually exclusive configuration of land-use rules, governance structures, ecological targets, and population distributions for a coastal flood zone. The board receives such proposals quarterly, has three weeks per review, and must sign off on one, reject all, or return for revision. Your staff possesses technical expertise in engineering and ecology but no philosophical training, and you must defend recommendations to elected officials who are skeptical of abstract reasoning.

Your task is to translate Leibniz's principle that reality is the best of all possible worlds—interpreted here not as theological optimism but as an optimization framework in which perfection equals the greatest richness of phenomena ordered by the simplest, most compossible laws—into a reusable decision procedure that your staff can execute. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the sequence of questions to apply to each world-proposal, what counts as sufficient evidence or reasoning at each stage, the explicit stopping conditions that produce a reject or revision request, and the precise points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be escalated to you with a flagged justification.

In translating the theory, preserve the distinctions that must survive simplification: compossibility (whether desired goods can coexist without contradiction under the proposed institutional laws), the trade-off between simplicity of governing rules and richness of outcomes, and the treatment of unavoidable negative features—displacement, restricted liberty, ecological loss—not as simple demerits but as conditions that may be necessary for greater overall order. Indicate which distinctions staff can apply mechanically and which require interpretive judgment.

Apply the procedure to two concrete world-proposals for the same district. Proposal A institutes strict geometric zoning, minimal governance layers, and high population density protected by engineered flood barriers, yielding low institutional complexity but significant displacement of informal settlements and restricted waterfront access. Proposal B adopts adaptive multi-layered governance, dispersed settlement patterns, and extensive ecological restoration, yielding high institutional complexity and rich ecological diversity but requiring coordination mechanisms with significant failure risk. Work through each stage for both proposals, identifying where the procedure yields a clear ranking, where it stalls, and—critically—where it recommends the proposal that carries greater unavoidable harm to a vulnerable group because of superior compossibility and simpler laws. Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot determine—such as the moral weight of distributive injustice relative to metaphysical perfection—and where it must return the decision to human values rather than technical evaluation.

Present the result as a formal operational manual: role and stakes at the top, the procedure in ordered stages with decision rules and escalation flags, the two worked cases with annotated step-by-step reasoning, and a closing section on procedural limits and sign-off protocols.
