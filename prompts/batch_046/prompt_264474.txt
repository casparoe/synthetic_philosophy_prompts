I've been trying to extend Frankfurt-style counterexamples beyond moral action into the domain of epistemic responsibility, but I keep ending up in a reductio that makes rational autonomy disappear entirely. Here is the argument as I have it. (1) In canonical Frankfurt cases, an agent is morally responsible for performing an action A despite being unable to do otherwise, because the counterfactual mechanism that would have compelled A remains dormant and never actually interferes with the agent's own deliberative pathway. (2) The same structural situation can be constructed for doxastic states: imagine an agent S who, through her own reasoning, comes to believe that anthropogenic climate change is real; unbeknownst to her, a neuroscientific doxastic safeguard is in place that would have induced precisely this belief had her reasoning failed, but because her reasoning succeeds, the safeguard never activates. (3) By direct parity with (1), S remains epistemically responsible for her belief—she deserves credit or blame for holding it—despite lacking the ability to have believed otherwise in those circumstances. (4) Now observe that for any belief anyone actually holds, there is always some counterfactual causal pathway—different upbringing, propaganda exposure, neural intervention, or algorithmic training—that could have produced the same doxastic outcome. (5) Hence, for every actually held belief, there exists a dormant counterfactual mechanism of the kind described in (2); therefore, the conditions under which epistemic responsibility survives the absence of alternatives are universally satisfied. (6) It follows that doxastic alternatives are never required for epistemic responsibility: whenever a belief is actually formed through an agent's own cognition, some dormant intervention is available in principle, yet responsibility remains intact. (7) But if the presence or absence of doxastic alternatives is never normatively relevant, then there can be no principled distinction between an agent who revises her belief through rational reflection and an agent whose belief is implanted by an external manipulator; in both cases, the belief either runs through the agent's cognition or is replaced by an external pathway, and since alternatives do not matter, the two pathways are on a par. (8) The collapse of this distinction is intolerable: it eliminates the concept of rational autonomy and renders practices such as scientific peer review, democratic deliberation, and education conceptually indistinguishable from indoctrination. (9) Therefore, the Frankfurt-style reasoning that undermines the principle of alternative possibilities for action cannot be extended to belief without absurdity.

I want to preserve (1) and (8), and I am inclined to think the error lies somewhere between (4) and (7), but I cannot locate the precise step. Which premise or inference should I reject, and why did it look plausible? What does giving it up cost—does it protect rational autonomy only by reintroducing doxastic alternatives in a way that undermines the original Frankfurt case for action, or is there a more local repair?
