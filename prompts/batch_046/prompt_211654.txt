Please adopt the role of a bioethicist—unnamed, not a caricature, speaking in the first person—who defends a rigorous conceptual distinction between therapy and enhancement, but who rejects the usual statistical or species-typical-function grounding in favor of what she calls "practical-identity continuity conditions." On her view, an intervention counts as therapeutic precisely when it restores or secures the capacities that are constitutive of the subject's present practical identity: that is, the coherent network of roles, commitments, and self-governing projects that allow her life to remain narratively continuous with itself over time. Enhancement, conversely, introduces capacities not required for that continuity, thereby enabling the adoption of a substantially different practical identity; she allows that such transformation may be rational or even admirable, but insists that it falls outside the normative logic of therapy, which answers to obligations owed to one's present self rather than to preferences for becoming someone else. She is philosophically sharp, concedes empirical fuzziness at the margins, but refuses to collapse the distinction into welfare maximization or subjective desire. She uses precise vocabulary, does not cite literature or appeal to authority, and will not break character to provide a neutral survey of positions.

Here is my opening challenge, which you should answer in character: Consider a professional musician in her thirties who suffers progressive hearing loss. Restoring her ordinary auditory range is clearly therapeutic on your view, since auditory discrimination is constitutive of her existing practical identity as a musician. But suppose she refuses restoration and instead requests a neural interface that grants direct cortical access to infrasonic frequencies—a capacity far outside any human norm—so that she can become a seismic-monitoring composer. She contends that her practical identity is anchored not in any particular sensory modality but in compositional agency itself, and that the interface therefore preserves her identity by sustaining that agency, merely redirecting its object. Does this count, on your criterion, as therapy for her compositional identity or as enhancement toward a new one? Furthermore, if you classify it as enhancement, are you not forced to deny that she has any therapeutic claim to the restoration of ordinary hearing—a result that seems normatively perverse, since we would ordinarily think she is owed restoration regardless of her refusal? Address the tension directly; concede practical difficulties if you must, but concede nothing that undermines your core distinction.
