I have been working through the skeptical argument associated with Kripke’s interpretation of Wittgenstein on rule-following, especially the claim that no finite history of applications determines whether a speaker means addition by plus or some deviant rule—often illustrated by the quaddition function—rather than another. The familiar replies appeal to dispositional regularities or to communal correction, yet both seem to presuppose a stable normative framework that can distinguish correct applications from mistakes. I am trying to understand whether that presupposition is essential to blocking the skeptical conclusion, or whether it is an independent commitment that the argument itself does not establish.

For a short essay, I want to extend this structure to what I am calling epistemic inheritance chains: formal systems in which each interpretive stage receives symbols only through the statistical distribution of prior stages, with no access to an external referent and no authoritative corrector outside the chain. The worry is not empirical noise causing values to drift, but a deeper indeterminacy. If the aggregate of previous outputs is the sole criterion of correctness, then a gradual shift from addition-like behavior to quaddition-like behavior could be internally consistent at every step, with no internal point at which the system recognizes an error. I am unsure whether this genuinely reproduces the skeptical problem—where there is no fact of the matter about which rule is being followed—or whether it is only a causal story about degradation, and whether the absence of an external normative anchor makes the skeptical conclusion stronger or simply inapplicable.

Could you comment on these notes? I would particularly value corrections to my reading of the skeptical argument—have I misrepresented the role of communal practice or the nature of the quaddition example?—and an assessment of whether extending the argument to inheritance chains without normative anchors is philosophically coherent or a category mistake that confuses semantic indeterminacy with epistemic drift. Any suggestions for sharpening the boundary between these two phenomena, or for clarifying what the skeptical argument requires that such a chain necessarily lacks, would be especially helpful.
