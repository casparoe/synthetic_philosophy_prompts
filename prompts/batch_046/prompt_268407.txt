I'm presenting a workshop paper defending a revisionist reading of Augustine's prohibition on lying: the claim is that Augustine distinguishes sharply between falsifying one's own belief-state through false assertion (the lie proper) and arranging truthful speech so that a listener draws an erroneous inference without being told anything false—a practice I call Augustinian redirection. I argue that his well-known cases of the physician and the soldier are not embarrassing hard cases that expose the impracticality of his rule, but positive illustrations of its internal logic: truthful utterances that exploit inferential habits rather than corrupting the speaker's duty to belief-integrity. I further claim that recognizing this distinction collapses the standard framing of Augustine as a naive opponent of Gricean cooperation, since his target is specifically the betrayal of one's own mental state, not the strategic management of information. The audience is a workshop on early Christian ethics comprising four patristics scholars who work with Augustine's Latin texts—especially De mendacio and Contra mendacium—two analytic philosophers of language specializing in deception and implicature, and one historian of philosophy tracking Stoic rhetoric in early Christianity.
