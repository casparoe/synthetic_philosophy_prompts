I've been applying the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework not only to direct interventions but to what I would call framework-modifying projects—efforts that do not themselves reduce harm but alter the machinery by which causes are identified, ranked, and funded—and my verdicts across these cases feel patterned yet difficult to reconcile. Here are my judgments. A conventional malaria bed-net program scores high on importance and tractability but low on neglectedness; I rank it positively but unremarkably, in line with standard analysis. A speculative basic-science program investigating the neural correlates of severe suffering strikes me as low-tractability and ambiguous on direct importance, yet I judge it more worthy of marginal funding than an expansion of the bed-net program, apparently because I weight heavily its potential to generate entirely new intervention categories. An AI system optimized specifically to improve philanthropic allocation—essentially automating parts of the evaluation framework itself—feels high-tractability and potentially transformative, but I hesitate to score it highly because applying the framework to an intervention that reshapes the framework's own outputs seems circular in a way that corrupts the assessment. A campaign to shift donor norms away from emotional appeals toward evidence-based reasoning has virtually no direct beneficiaries, yet I think it dominates both the bed-net and the basic-science cases, which suggests I am implicitly treating structural changes to the evaluation environment as a separate dimension of value. Meanwhile, funding a think-tank that publishes cause-ranking reports seems to require different treatment depending on whether I treat those reports as outputs with low direct importance or as inputs that systematically reshape neglect and tractability scores for every other cause. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving levels of analysis, conditions for permissible circularity, or a rigorous distinction between direct impact and structural impact—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned for the sake of consistency? And what should the best principle predict about an intervention whose sole effect is to improve the accuracy of tractability assessments across all domains, such as the development and adoption of a rigorous new forecasting protocol?
