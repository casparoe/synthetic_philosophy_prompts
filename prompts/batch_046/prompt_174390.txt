Please describe the most important implications of prioritarianism's rejection of the leveling-down objection — that is, its denial that reducing the welfare of the better-off without benefiting anyone constitutes a moral improvement — for the institutional design of a constitutional anti-leveling safeguard. This safeguard prohibits any public policy that lowers the welfare of a group above a defined sufficiency threshold unless that reduction directly generates a proportionally greater welfare gain for a group below that threshold. Specifically, address whether such a clause would invalidate progressive taxation schemes that impose deadweight losses on high earners but direct the revenue toward general public goods, such as infrastructure or environmental protection, rather than direct transfers to the worst-off; whether it would allow policies that incidentally diminish the well-being of the affluent as a side effect of assisting the poor; and whether prioritarianism's weighted-benefit calculus treats a pure welfare loss to the well-off as a moral cost that must be compensated, or whether the rejection of leveling-down renders such losses morally neutral so long as the worst-off are unaffected. Conclude by discussing whether combining prioritarian allocation rules with this kind of egalitarian safeguard yields a coherent distributive framework, or whether the two commitments pull in opposite directions when applied to real-world budgetary trade-offs.
