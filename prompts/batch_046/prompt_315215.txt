You are a philosopher of mind with particular expertise in the intentional stance and the conditions under which mental-state attributions are warranted. Before constructing your answer, spend a few minutes brainstorming the logical space of positions on whether artificial systems—particularly large language models and embodied robotic architectures—can genuinely be said to hold beliefs and desires, as opposed to merely simulating the behavioural profiles associated with them.

Please write a taxonomy, structured as a branching tree, that sorts the positions on this question. Each branch should be labelled by the specific claim or concrete case that divides it from its sibling branch, so that a reader can navigate the tree by answering diagnostic questions without already knowing the philosophical labels.

Build the tree around three concrete cases that you should embed as the principal splitting nodes:

1. The adversarial representation case: an AI consistently outputs correct geographical answers, yet internal linear probes show no stable representational vector corresponding to the entities mentioned; adversarial perturbation of hidden-state dynamics eliminates the correct output while leaving surface-level statistical correlations intact. Does this failure of representational stability undermine belief-attribution?

2. The normative-accountability loop: an AI is integrated into an institutional framework in which its outputs are treated as testimonial, it receives feedback and updates its weights when penalised for error, yet it reports no phenomenal experience and its updates are driven purely by loss-gradient descent. Does this social embedding, without consciousness, ground genuine belief?

3. The substrate-duplicate scenario: a silicon-based system replicates, in precise functional and dynamical terms, the representational and counterfactual reasoning patterns of a human brain that believes water is H2O. Does the absence of biological origin or evolutionary history defeat the attribution?
