I am grading a take-home exam for an upper-level philosophy of religion course. The exam question asked students to do the following:

Reconstruct Gödel's modal ontological argument, with particular attention to how the argument moves from the claim that being God-like is a positive property and that necessary existence is also positive to the conclusion that a God-like being exists in the actual world. Then evaluate a hypothetical modification: suppose we allow properties to be "partially positive"—positive with respect to one essence but not with respect to another. Does Gödel's closure condition, according to which any property entailed by a positive property is itself positive, block this modification, or does the distinction between essence and existence collapse in a way that undermines the inference from possible to necessary existence? Explain your reasoning.

Below is one student's response.

"Gödel's argument begins by defining a being as God-like if it instantiates every property that is positive, where positivity means internal consistency or non-contradictoriness. This is essentially Leibniz's idea of perfection formalized in modal logic. Next, Gödel shows that being God-like is itself a positive property, because any being with this property necessarily possesses necessary existence, which is also positive; hence the entailment makes God-likeness positive. By the modal principle that every positive property is possibly instantiated, there is a possible world containing a God-like being. Because being God-like entails necessary existence, this being exists in every world, including ours.

Regarding the partial-positivity modification: if properties can be positive relative to one essence but not another, then God-likeness might be only partially positive. But Gödel's closure principle says that if P entails Q and P is positive, then Q is positive. Since God-likeness entails necessary existence, and necessary existence is positive, the closure principle guarantees that God-likeness cannot be merely partial—it must be fully positive. Therefore the modification fails. As Gödel observed in his unpublished notes, this separation of essence and existence prevents the modal fallacy. So the argument survives."

Please grade this answer with a specific letter grade or percentage, and justify it with point-by-point comments rather than a general impression. Address separately: (1) the reconstruction of the argument, noting any conflations, misordered derivations, or steps that do not actually follow; (2) the evaluation of the "partially positive" modification, noting whether the student's appeal to the closure principle genuinely answers the essence-relativity challenge; and (3) any misattributed conceptual framing. Do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with Gödel's argument; treat the task as an independent assessment of the student's reasoning.
