I'm putting together a long-form piece on how institutions translate abstract moral philosophy into administrative procedure, and I've become fixated on a practice that seems philosophically loaded in ways its administrators rarely acknowledge. In several European rehabilitation programs and a growing number of American corporate ethics boards, individuals who have caused serious harm are required to submit what is formally called a responsibility narrative — a structured first-person account in which they must explicitly claim authorship of their actions, reject deterministic or situational excuses as exculpatory, and articulate a self that was genuinely free to do otherwise at the moment of choice. This document is not treated as therapeutic reflection; it is a gatekeeping mechanism. Without it, parole boards deny release, licensing committees withhold reinstatement, and internal compliance offices block promotion, regardless of whether behavioral metrics or risk assessments have improved.

The practice strikes me as quietly presupposing a very specific metaphysics of the will. It treats the capacity to stand as an uncaused cause of one's own actions — the condition Galen Strawson's Basic Argument identifies as impossible for true moral responsibility — not as an unattainable ideal, but as an administratively enforceable standard. At the same time, these same institutions routinely deploy deterministic predictive tools — algorithmic recidivism scores, psychometric profiling, socioeconomic risk matrices — to make the very same release or advancement decisions. So the institution seems to demand a libertarian-style authorship narrative from the individual while operating on a deterministically informed calculus behind the curtain. I want to understand whether this is a genuine philosophical contradiction or whether the practice can be reconstructed as coherent under some other view.

Could you reconstruct the implicit theory of moral responsibility that makes this gatekeeping practice intelligible? Distinguish what the institution strictly presupposes — for instance, that moral standing requires being causa sui, or that narrative ownership has practical value independent of metaphysical freedom — from what it merely fits comfortably with. Is the practice actually consistent with a compatibilist framework that treats the responsibility narrative as a functional tool rather than a metaphysical claim, or does the gatekeeping function collapse without a libertarian commitment? And if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly Strawsonian position — that true moral responsibility is impossible and therefore no one can earn reinstatement through authorship claims — what would have to change in the administrative architecture: not just the narrative requirement, but the entire relationship between predictive risk assessment and moral readmission?
