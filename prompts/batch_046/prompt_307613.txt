My friend and I have just concluded a long and rather heated argument about what, if anything, we owe to real dead people when we dramatise them in historical fiction and biopics. She defends a position we might call fiduciary narrative humility: the claim that a dramatist stands in something like a trustee relation to the dead, obliged to avoid inventing decisive interior moments or counterfactual dialogue that would violate the subject’s documented self-conception, particularly when the work is presented as based on real events. I defend an opposing view—call it presentist aesthetic autonomy—which holds that art is addressed exclusively to the living, that the dead possess neither rights nor interests capable of being harmed, and that treating dramatisation as ethically answerable to its subjects confuses aesthetic construction with historical testimony. Despite our disagreement, we both think the dispute turns partly on whether there is a coherent category of narrative expropriation—the appropriation of a real life as artistic raw material in ways that disregard the subject’s own counterfactual testimonial consent, that is, whether they would endorse not merely being depicted but being assigned the specific narrative arc and interiority the work imposes, were they fully informed and capable of judgement. We wish to treat our disagreement as an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement we might both sign.

The document should follow a clear format. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—formulations about the formal structure of dramatisation (selection, compression, temporal rearrangement, invention of dialogue), about the distinction between representational harm to living relatives and allocative harm to cultural memory, and about the relevance but non-decisiveness of the public-versus-private-figure distinction—that both sides can accept without slant. Second, a disagreements table setting out, for each crux, the precise wording each camp accepts for its own position: specifically (a) whether the dead have interests that can be violated; (b) whether counterfactual testimonial consent carries normative weight in evaluating a dramatisation; (c) whether narrative expropriation is a distinct moral category or merely rhetorical dressing for ordinary misrepresentation; and (d) whether artistic autonomy can ever justify the deliberate invention of a documented-decision scene for dramatic effect. Third, a section identifying the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us—including whether audience confusion between invented interiority and documented biography undermines aesthetic autonomy, and whether imposing narrative constraints eliminates artistic value altogether. Fourth, a predictive-register section listing at least two concrete cases or arguments both sides agree would bear on the cruxes, with each side’s predicted outcome recorded in advance: for example, a biopic that invents a private reconciliation never attested in the record but consonant with the subject’s documented values, and a work that explicitly signals its fictional status yet uses the subject’s real name and biographical trajectory. Where the residual disagreement is partly verbal—particularly around whether respect for the dead denotes a genuine moral relation or an emotional projection onto non-sentient objects—say so explicitly and explain why. Write in British English, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
