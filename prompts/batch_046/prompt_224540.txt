I have been reading about the neurological cases in which a patient's corpus callosum is surgically divided to treat severe epilepsy, and I find myself stuck on a conceptual problem that popular explanations never quite address. The usual account suggests that after the split, each cerebral hemisphere can operate with its own perceptions, intentions, and even verbal responses, leading some thinkers to conclude that a single human body now houses two distinct streams of consciousness. Yet almost every discussion I encounter frames the separation as binary: either the hemispheres are fully integrated through intact neural pathways, or they are completely isolated. I keep wondering whether the philosophical conclusion depends on this sharp division. Specifically, I am trying to understand whether the argument for divided consciousness is primarily about the subjective unity of experience—what it feels like to be a single subject—or about the functional integration of decision-making and agency across the brain.

To make this concrete, imagine a hypothetical variation that I have not seen discussed: instead of a clean surgical cut, suppose the interhemispheric connection remains anatomically present but is severely degraded, allowing information to travel only slowly, unreliably, and through indirect mediation, so that one hemisphere must effectively compose a message for the other rather than sharing awareness directly. Would philosophers still say there are two minds in such a case, or does the presence of any communicative link, however imperfect, preserve a single consciousness? I would like you to explain the conceptual difference between the unity of consciousness and the unity of cognitive control, and to apply that distinction both to the classic split-brain experiments and to this kind of gradual-degradation scenario. I am not looking for medical details or a literature review—just a clear philosophical analysis of whether split-brain cases genuinely fracture the self, and on what grounds.
