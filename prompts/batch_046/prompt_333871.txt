Two members of my philosophy study group have spent the last week arguing over whether Charles Darwin's account of natural selection and Alan Turing's conception of computing represent the same kind of logical inversion, specifically the separation of functional competence from conscious comprehension. The first student maintains that Darwin demonstrated how a biological structure such as the eye can achieve extraordinary design-level precision—focusing light, adjusting to distance—through a process that possesses no understanding of optics, no intention to see, and no representation of the task being performed. She argues that this overturns the classical assumption that competent performance must be guided by comprehension of the goal, because the evolutionary explanation runs entirely downward from effect to blind mechanistic cause. She then claims that Turing executed an identical inversion: a digital computer can defeat human chess masters by executing formal rules, without grasping strategy, recognizing patterns in any meaningful sense, or understanding what chess is. In her view, both thinkers prove that the capacity to perform a complex task can be fully detached from the capacity to understand it, and that anyone who still insists that true intelligence requires internal comprehension is simply failing to absorb the lesson of modern science.

The second student objects that this framing conflates two very different phenomena. He grants that natural selection produces functional outcomes, but he insists that it is a category error to call evolutionary filtering itself competent, since selection involves no agent, no performance, and no goal-directed behavior—only differential survival and reproduction. For him, Darwin did not show that competence can exist without comprehension; he eliminated the need for any competent agent by replacing intentional design with statistical filtering. Turning to Turing, he argues that the computer's apparent competence is entirely derivative: every rule in the program, every logical gate in the hardware, was constructed by human engineers who fully understood the task. The machine does not borrow understanding in some partial way; it is literally an extension of human comprehension crystallized into silicon and syntax. Once the program is running, no human mind is present, but the causal structure of the machine's behavior remains saturated with prior human understanding. Therefore, he concludes, Turing did not demonstrate mindless competence at all; he demonstrated that human comprehension can be distributed across artifacts and time, with the machine serving as a prosthesis rather than an independent counterexample.

The first student responds that this misses the philosophical point of both cases. She agrees that selection has no mind and that programmers designed the machine, but she insists that the relevant inversion concerns explanation, not origin. Once the eye exists, its functional precision is maintained and reproduced by mechanisms that have no access to the concept of vision; once the chess program is running, its move selection is determined by algorithms that have no access to the concept of winning. The absence of comprehension in the operating system—whether evolutionary or computational—is precisely what makes both cases striking. She adds that if we accept the second student's reasoning, we would have to say that a printed book contains the author's comprehension and therefore the paper itself understands the text, which she takes to be absurd.

The second student then introduces a different challenge. He proposes that if we adopt the first student's broad definition—where any functional system that lacks mental states counts as competent without comprehension—then a river eroding a canyon is also performing competent geology without understanding rock mechanics. This, he says, dilutes the concept until it applies to every physical process, stripping Darwin and Turing of their special philosophical significance. He suggests that we should restrict the discussion to goal-directed performances that we typically associate with minds, such as seeing or playing chess. Even within that restriction, he argues, Darwin explains the origin of the eye's function without invoking comprehension, but the eye itself was never a candidate for having comprehension in the first place, so no inversion occurs there. Meanwhile, the computer performs a task—chess—that we normally do with understanding, which highlights the absence of understanding in the machine, but this absence is only philosophically interesting because we brought our mentalistic expectations with us. Thus, neither thinker actually proves that comprehension is dispensable in general; they only show that specific mind-associated tasks can be executed by mechanisms that lack mental properties.

At this point neither will yield. I am observing all of this as a fellow student in the group and I do not wish to take sides prematurely. Could you adjudicate the dispute point by point? I want to know whether Darwin and Turing genuinely share a common logical structure or whether the comparison collapses upon inspection; whether evolutionary processes should be described as competent in any meaningful sense or whether that language is purely metaphorical; whether a running computer program performs chess with genuinely borrowed comprehension or with none at all; and whether the river-eroding-canyon objection successfully undermines the analogy or merely shifts the boundary of the discussion. I also want you to identify what both students are neglecting—perhaps a distinction between explanatory inversion and ontological separation, or a hidden ambiguity in how the word comprehension is being used across biological and artificial contexts. Please write your answer entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, as one might write in a formal letter or a carefully composed email.
