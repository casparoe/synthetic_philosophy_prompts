I am a doctoral student in philosophy working at the intersection of ethics and philosophy of religion, and I am drafting a short essay for an advanced seminar on moral paradox and practical reason. The essay concerns Kierkegaard's account of Abraham in Fear and Trembling, specifically the problem of the teleological suspension of the ethical. Rather than rehearsing the familiar opposition between the knight of faith and the tragic hero, I want to explore a structural feature that standard readings tend to overlook: the possibility that the ethical universal does not merely suffer an external suspension by the religious, but contains within itself a reflexive mechanism that permits—indeed, demands—its own temporary suspension in the face of the singular.

The argument proceeds in three steps. First, I distinguish two modes of suspension. The conventional reading treats the suspension as hierarchical: the particular relation to the absolute (the divine command) overrides the universal (the ethical prohibition against killing). On this view, Abraham exits the ethical sphere and enters a purely religious register. I argue that this reading is unstable because it renders Abraham's continued ethical identity unintelligible; if the ethical is genuinely negated, there is no reason to describe Abraham as a father who loves Isaac, only as an instrument of an alien will.

Second, I propose an alternative model, which I call normative reflexivity. The ethical universal demands that every individual be treated as an end in itself. When this demand is directed at the singular individual—Isaac, or Abraham's own singular responsibility—without mediation through universalizable maxims, it produces a structural tension. The universal does not disappear; rather, it applies to itself by recognizing that its own highest commitment (to the singular) cannot be satisfied within the medium of universal disclosure and justification. The suspension is therefore not an external annulment but an internal modulation: the ethical generates the conditions of its own silence.

Third, I interpret Abraham's silence not as a withdrawal from the ethical community, but as the only form of ethical consistency available under conditions where disclosure would violate the singular value that the ethical itself mandates. His refusal to speak—to justify himself to Sarah, to Isaac, or to the community—is not irrational concealment, but the performative enactment of a practical reasoning that recognizes its own limit.

This framing avoids both the religious irrationalist interpretation, which treats Abraham's faith as a pure leap beyond reason, and the tragic hero comparison, which treats the suspension as a collision between two incommensurable goods. Instead, it locates the paradox within the architecture of practical reason itself.

I would be grateful for critical comments on the conceptual architecture, particularly regarding:

1. Whether the distinction between external suspension and internal self-suspension can be sustained within Kierkegaard's framework, or whether it illegitimately collapses the religious into the ethical.
2. Whether the notion of normative reflexivity captures something genuinely present in the text, or whether it imposes an anachronistic apparatus drawn from contemporary moral philosophy.
3. Whether the claim that universalizability can generate its own singular exception undermines the coherence of universal moral law, and if so, whether that undermines my reading or Kierkegaard's.
4. Any significant objections from standard interpretations—whether the knight of faith, the tragic hero, or the ethical universalist—that I have failed to anticipate or address.

Please focus on the argument's coherence and its fidelity to the philosophical problem; I am not seeking stylistic revisions or bibliographic recommendations.
