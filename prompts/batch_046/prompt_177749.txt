Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations.

Exam Question:
Galen Strawson's Basic Argument contends that true moral responsibility is impossible. (a) Reconstruct the argument's three core premises, emphasizing why Strawson holds that it succeeds whether or not determinism is true. (b) Distinguish Galen Strawson's conclusion from Peter Strawson's well-known position on reactive attitudes, and explain why conflating the father and son undermines an accurate reading of the argument. (c) One critic objects that if Strawson's demand for being causa sui were applied consistently, it would also undermine our entitlement to ordinary beliefs or knowledge, since we are not the ultimate source of our cognitive faculties either. Assess whether this objection succeeds.

Student Answer:
Strawson's Basic Argument proceeds in three steps. First, you act according to the way you are—your character, motives, and mental makeup. Second, to be truly responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are. Third, you cannot be responsible for the way you are: either your mental nature traces back in an infinite regress of prior causes, or you must be causa sui, which is impossible. Therefore true moral responsibility is impossible. The argument does not depend on determinism, because even in an indeterminist world with quantum randomness, you do not choose the random outcomes that shape your mental nature, so breaking causal chains does not restore ultimate responsibility.

For part (b), Galen Strawson accepts Peter Strawson's observation that reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude are deeply embedded in human life, but Galen uses this to show that we continue to hold people responsible even though, on his view, nobody is ultimately responsible. The common error is to think that Peter's defense of reactive attitudes saves free will, when in fact Galen deploys the same psychological point to highlight the gap between our practices and true desert.

For part (c), the objection fails. Strawson applies the causa sui requirement only to moral desert, not to epistemic justification. Ordinary knowledge only requires that our belief-forming processes be reliable, not that we have created our own minds ex nihilo. So there is no parallel that undermines the argument.
