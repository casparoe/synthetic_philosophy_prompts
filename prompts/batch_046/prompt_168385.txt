I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion and philosophical logic. I have come to defend a particular dissolution of the Damascus paradox: rather than treating the fugitive's flight as causally self-defeating, I hold that the paradox only arises if we assume future-tensed propositions possess determinate truth-values at the time of decision. By adopting a branching-time semantics with an indeterminist past together with a supervaluationist account of future truth, I argue that the agent's flight and death's presence in Damascus are compatible across branches. In one branch the agent never flees and death awaits there; in the actual branch the agent flees and death awaits here, but no single branch realizes both the flight and death's absence from Damascus. Foreknowledge is thereby preserved without causal determinism, and the agent's choice remains genuinely open. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position is. What formal results, ontological commitments, or intuitive judgments would count against it—ranked from fatal to merely awkward? In particular: (1) Would a demonstration that supervaluationism collapses into bivalence under any decision-relevant framing undermine the dissolution? (2) If branching-time models require an ontology of concrete worlds that conflicts with modal actualism, is that a fatal cost or an acceptable trade-off? (3) Which moral-responsibility intuitions in fatalist scenarios does this view fail to accommodate, and would its defenders concede those failures? (4) Has the view been insulated by qualification into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect for a position of this kind? Please distinguish evidence that a committed proponent would themselves grant as damaging from criticisms they would dismiss as misunderstandings, and assess whether the insulation—if present—is problematic.
