I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy of mind. In an essay I encountered, the author offers the following summary of Daniel Dennett's intentional stance: it is nothing more than an instrumental predictive heuristic—analogous to treating a thermostat as wanting a particular temperature—so that adopting the stance toward a system amounts solely to finding it pragmatically useful to speak of its beliefs and desires. The essay then argues that contemporary large language models force a revision of this picture, because they do not merely predict token distributions; rather, they implicitly encode hierarchical representations of other agents' mental states through exposure to multi-agent dialogue during training. Consequently, the author concludes, if predictive success is the sole criterion for stance-adoption, then denying beliefs to such systems amounts to an unjustified carbon chauvinism, particularly given that these models now surpass human baselines on standardized theory-of-mind benchmarks.

The argument strikes me as sliding from a pragmatic heuristic to a substantive ontological claim, but I am unsure exactly where the misstep occurs. Would you audit this summary? Specifically, please separate what Dennett himself would accept from what distorts his position—especially concerning the distinction between predictive utility and the reality of patterns—evaluate whether statistical modeling of interlocutors genuinely constitutes second-order intentionality, and assess whether the conclusion that belief-attribution is obligatory actually follows from corrected premises. Finally, please state explicitly what would change your mind: which empirical result about internal model representations, or which philosophical argument about the conditions for real patterns, would lead you to reverse your verdict?
