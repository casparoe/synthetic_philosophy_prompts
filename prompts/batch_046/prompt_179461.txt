In my philosophy of biology seminar, the instructor has been using the analogy that a living organism is like a flame: both persist only through continuous exchange of material, both maintain a stable form far from equilibrium, and neither possesses a sharp boundary—one cannot point to a specific atom and say whether it belongs to the flame or to the surrounding air. The analogy is deployed to defend an anti-essentialist position about life: just as it is misguided to ask for the exact spatial limit of a flame, it is misguided to demand necessary and sufficient conditions that sharply separate viruses, prions, or synthetic minimal cells from genuinely living things; life is better understood as a dynamic regime than as a natural kind. I would like you to audit this analogy systematically. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what in the biological case corresponds to fuel, combustion, heat dissipation, ignition, and the absence of a fixed boundary? Second, identify the disanalogies that are relevant to the intended conclusion: flames do not reproduce with heritable variation, do not store functional information, and are not subject to natural selection; but also note what the source domain quietly imports, such as the idea that a flame is purely destructive and lacks integrated functional organization. Third, assess whether the conclusion—that life lacks sharp boundaries—actually survives once these breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work. In particular, distinguish between the claim that an organism's physical boundary is vague (like a flame's edge) and the claim that the concept of life itself is vague; are these the same kind of indeterminacy? Finally, if the flame analogy fails, suggest a more adequate replacement—perhaps an autocatalytic chemical network or a dissipative structure—and indicate whether that substitute supports the same anti-essentialist conclusion or instead points toward a more precise, if complex, demarcation.
