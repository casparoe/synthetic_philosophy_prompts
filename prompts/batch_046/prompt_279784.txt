You are a professional historian of social science with expertise in Fernand Braudel's historiography and comparative political development. Before answering, take a moment to recall the distinctions among longue durée, conjuncture, and événement, and consider how they apply to comparative welfare-state formation.

I am a graduate student in comparative politics who recently began a research project on Nordic institutional divergence, and this debate has become personally urgent for framing my own work. At a departmental seminar last week, two senior colleagues had an exchange that left me genuinely unsure how to weigh structural versus contingent explanations. I want you to adjudicate their arguments move by move—rating each step rather than simply declaring a winner—and to say what both are missing.

Colleague A, a historical sociologist, argued that Sweden's welfare-state trajectory is best understood through Braudel's longue durée: access to iron ore, timber, and navigable waterways, combined with a temperate agricultural base, created a structural surplus that made centralized redistribution feasible centuries before the 1930s. Modern Swedish policies, she said, are largely surface phenomena—events that ride atop slow-moving geographic and climatic structures.

Colleague B, a comparative political scientist, countered with Finland. Finland shares nearly identical geographic and climatic conditions, yet after 1945 it pursued sharply different institutional paths—land-reform patterns, neutrality policies, and education structures diverged significantly. If structures are determinative, B asked, why do near-identical profiles produce divergent outcomes? He concluded that specific political choices and contingent leadership carry far more explanatory weight than Braudel allows.

A replied that Finland is not structurally identical: its soil composition, Baltic exposure, and geopolitical position relative to Russia constitute a different structural package. She warned that if every divergence is explained by inventing a hidden structural difference, the framework becomes unfalsifiable taxonomy rather than explanation.

B then pointed to Norway's 1969 oil discovery. Norway and Sweden had followed structurally parallel paths for centuries; a single contingent discovery—what Braudel might call an événement—reshaped Norway's political economy entirely. If one event can overturn centuries of similarity, B argued, then the long-term framework overstates inertia.

A responded that Braudel distinguishes événement from conjuncture: oil discovery is not a random event but a medium-term economic shift, and Norway's ability to institutionalize the revenue—through parliamentary consensus and a sovereign fund—reflects pre-existing structural capacities (bureaucratic stability, democratic continuity) that Sweden also possessed. The event activated structures; it did not replace them.

B's final move was that this distinction saves the theory only by expanding structure to include institutional responses themselves, collapsing the boundary between slow causes and rapid effects. Without a principled criterion for what belongs to the longue durée, A's framework absorbs every counterexample.

Please adjudicate each of these six moves: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and identify where both colleagues miss something important—particularly regarding how Braudel's own writing on the Mediterranean treated geographic determinism versus institutional agency. Think step by step, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need conceptual clarification, not references.
