Question:
State Arrow's impossibility theorem precisely, identifying all four conditions imposed on a social welfare function. Then explain, with a concrete three-candidate example involving at least three voters, how the Borda count rule violates the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) condition. Finally, consider a proposed resolution in which IIA is weakened so that it applies only to pairs (A, B) in profiles where the remaining alternative C is not ranked first or last by any voter. Assess whether this weakened condition, together with unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and non-dictatorship, still produces an impossibility result. In your assessment, address the structure of Arrow's proof and whether the pivotal profiles used in that proof fall within the scope of the weakened condition.

Student's answer:
Arrow's theorem demonstrates that when there are three or more alternatives and two or more voters, no social welfare function can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), and non-dictatorship all at once. IIA requires that the social ordering of A against B depend exclusively on each voter's pairwise comparison of A and B, without influence from the position of C. To see Borda violate this, consider three voters: v1 ranks A > B > C; v2 ranks B > C > A; and v3 ranks C > A > B. Assigning two points for first, one for second, and zero for third yields Borda totals of A = 3, B = 3, C = 3, a three-way tie. Now suppose v3 revises to A > C > B, leaving v3's A-versus-B preference unchanged (A still above B). The Borda totals become A = 4, B = 3, C = 2, so A now strictly dominates B. Because the individual A-B comparisons are identical in both profiles yet the social ranking shifts, IIA is violated.

As for the weakened condition: Arrow's proof identifies a pivotal voter who can alter the social A-B ordering by changing preferences over C alone. In every such pivotal profile, C necessarily occupies an intermediate position for that voter—if C were at an extreme, altering its rank could not produce the required pairwise pivot—so the restricted IIA applies exactly to these critical cases. Hence the proof's contradiction is fully preserved, and a dictator must still exist. It is true that Borda violates even this restricted version, as shown above where v3 moved C from the top rank to the middle; this confirms that Borda is properly excluded by the weakened conditions, leaving the impossibility intact.
