I have spent nearly a decade composing a novel of some formal ambition—an interwoven narrative of three generations of glassmakers in a region that no longer produces glass—only to discover, upon completing the final revision, that the publishing landscape has shifted so radically that its release would be little more than a private ritual witnessed by a few dozen strangers. Concurrently, a former colleague has invited me to direct a field office for an organization that constructs irrigation systems in arid highlands; the labor is unambiguous, measurable, and necessary. Yet the prospect of abandoning the manuscript to take up this work does not feel like moral clarity so much as an exchange of one arbitrary devotion for another. If the novel was destined to leave no trace, was my commitment to it a failure of judgment, or was the commitment itself sufficient to justify the years? And if I redirect my life toward work whose value is externally confirmed, do I escape that arbitrariness, or merely hide from it?

I am not suffering from melancholy; I am attempting to determine whether there exists any coherent framework by which one form of life can be judged more worthy than another, or whether the judgment must be made without such a framework and then lived with as if it were true. Given that both paths lie open, and that neither can be justified by reference to an outcome I can guarantee, how ought I to choose? Should I treat the absence of an external guarantee as a reason to privilege the work that helps others, or should I treat that absence as proof that the only value available is the one I confer through my own sustained attention, however obscure its object? I need not generic encouragement, but a way of thinking about the structure of this choice that does not simply assume the answer in advance.
