As a novelist who has spent the past year writing a work of fiction about a philosopher gradually losing her grip on whether she is a continuous self or merely a sequence of discrete mental events, I have been asked to design the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy—specifically on the epistemological and ontological architecture of Descartes's Meditations. I want the exam to reward students who understand the machinery of the argument rather than those who can only recite that Descartes moves from thinking to being; please draft a single essay question that a candidate could answer in ninety minutes, focused on what the cogito in Meditation II actually establishes. The question should force candidates to determine whether the argument proves the existence of a persisting substantial self, or only the occurrence of a present-tense act of thinking, and whether Descartes is entitled to infer from the certainty that thinking is occurring to the claim that there is an I that thinks—specifically confronting whether the argument relies on a suppressed premise about personal identity over time, and whether it avoids or commits the fallacy of converting a grammatical subject into an ontological substance.

Please provide a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (totalling twenty), and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each point. The difficulty should come from identifying the crux: a student should not be able to answer well by summarizing Meditation II, and the question should separate those who grasp the logical structure from those who have memorized the labels. Do not include any request for a bibliography, reading list, or citations—this is intended for a closed-book sitting.
