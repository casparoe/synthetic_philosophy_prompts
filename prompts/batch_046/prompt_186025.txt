The debate over whether moral expressivism can survive the Frege-Geach embedding problem has run for decades with remarkably little movement. The basic structure is well known: if moral sentences express non-cognitive attitudes rather than describe moral facts, then in unasserted contexts—such as the antecedent of a conditional or the scope of negation—the attitude seems to disappear, yet the sentence still contributes to the logical structure of the whole. Blackburn's commitment-based response holds that embedding expresses a second-order attitude toward the attitude that the embedded clause would express; Gibbard's norm-expressivist account treats moral sentences as expressing plans governed by consistency norms that apply even in embedded positions; and descriptivists reply that neither mechanism reproduces the straightforward compositionality of descriptive discourse, often arguing that the expressivist machinery either collapses into a disguised form of descriptivism or fails to license the inferences speakers actually make. Meanwhile, newer hybrid proposals attempt to split the difference by assigning both expressive and representational roles to moral vocabulary. Despite this, the literature feels circular: every refinement of attitudinal compositionality is met with the objection that it smuggles in truth-conditions, while descriptivists rarely address why ordinary moral discourse feels categorically different from empirical reporting. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not a single disagreement but several questions collapsed under one heading: a formal question about whether attitudes can be composed in logic-like structures; a semantic question about whether moral sentences must have truth-conditions to embed; and an empirical question about whether human cognition actually processes moral conditionals through compositional rules or through pragmatic, context-sensitive mechanisms. Diagnose this using the actual theoretical commitments of commitment-based expressivism, norm-expressivism, and standard descriptivist counterarguments. Is the disagreement verbal—turning on different uses of terms like content or meaning—or substantive but obscured by incompatible standards of theoretical adequacy? Could progress come from a formal result about the limits of non-cognitive composition, an empirical finding about moral reasoning in embedded contexts, or a conceptual distinction between pragmatic accommodation and semantic embedding? Or does the persistence of the deadlock suggest that the original question is ill-posed, presupposing a uniform model of linguistic meaning that moral language does not provide?
