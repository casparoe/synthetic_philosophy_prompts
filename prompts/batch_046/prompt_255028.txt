There's this metaethics debate about wrong-kind reasons that feels completely frozen, and I'm trying to figure out if it's actually one debate or three disguised as one. The classic setup is familiar - someone offers you money to do the right thing, and that gives you a reason but not the right kind. But lately my group has been stuck on cases where the reason is about the structure of your own practical reasoning rather than an external bribe. Say you have a choice: you can adopt a decision-rule that weights fairness heavily, and that rule will lead you to share resources, or you can be paid directly to share. One camp says the decision-rule reason is still wrong-kind relative to the fairness-reason itself, because it's one step removed from value. The other camp says that's nonsense - the rule is tracking value, so the reason is exactly the right kind, just mediated. We've mapped this out every which way and nobody budges. So is the stall happening because they're answering different questions - one about normative justification, one about psychological causation? Or is there a hidden disagreement about what counts as a reason at all? Or is the framework itself broken once you allow mediated reasoning? I want a diagnosis with actual engagement with these two positions, not a history of who said what. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress - maybe a formal distinction, maybe a thought experiment that forces a bullet-biting moment, or maybe an argument that this particular framing should just be abandoned. I also genuinly can't decide wether 'you should study because education makes you a more autonomous thinker' counts as wrong-kind if autonomy is itself valuable - what's the verdict there given your diagnosis?
