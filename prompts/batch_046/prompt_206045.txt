Since I have recently begun to examine Jain ethical frameworks in the context of a personal inquiry into non-violence and epistemic humility, I observed the following disagreement between two members of my study group. R maintains that anekāntavāda dissolves moral conflict entirely: by applying the sevenfold predication (saptabhangi) to the claim that consuming root vegetables violates ahiṃsā, one sees that it is true in one respect (it harms organisms with one sense organ) and false in another (it preserves soil ecosystems), so the appearance of contradiction is merely an absolutist error. S responds that syādvāda demands explicit conditionalisation—perhaps it is so in some respect—without which one does not state a Jain view but merely lists contradictions. S further argues that anekāntavāda is not moral relativism: the framework of ratnatraya requires right conduct, which obliges one to adopt a specific vow-bound standpoint (naya) for action; therefore the conflict is structured and navigated, not dissolved. R counters that S introduces an absolute meta-standpoint: if all first-order knowledge is partial, then S's insistence that we must specify the naya is itself only valid from S's own epistemic position, generating an infinite regress unless one accepts that every assertion, including S's, is simultaneously true and false in different respects. S replies that this is the familiar self-refutation objection and that it misses the distinction between first-order propositions about reality, to which anekāntavāda applies, and the regulative logic of conditionalisation, which governs how those propositions are expressed. S adds a practical test: if an ethical AI were designed to emit all seven predicates for every moral query, the result would be decision paralysis rather than wisdom, showing that anekāntavāda actually underscores the necessity of committed judgement. R asks whether that regulative logic is not simply the rejected absolutism relocated to the meta-level. Please adjudicate point by point: where is each party correct, where does the reasoning fail, and what are both overlooking about the relation between anekāntavāda and practical decision-making? Please answer in British English.
