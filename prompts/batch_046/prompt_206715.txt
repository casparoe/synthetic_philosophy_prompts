As a retired biologist who has lately begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have become preoccupied with the parable of Death in Damascus—the servant who, warned that Death awaits him in Damascus, flees instead to Samarra, only to meet Death there. The narrative raises questions about whether flight causes the fate it seeks to avoid, or whether the outcome was already fixed regardless of action.

I should like to read Sophocles' Oedipus Rex through this lens. The play turns on a prophecy that Oedipus will kill his father and marry his mother, and on his flight from Corinth to avoid that very outcome, which instead delivers him to Thebes where it is fulfilled. Could you analyse whether Sophocles treats this as a case of causal self-fulfilment—Oedipus running toward Damascus, as it were—or as an instance of logical fatalism, in which the prophecy's truth-value is fixed independently of any causal chain? I am particularly interested in whether the play distinguishes between epistemic uncertainty, which Oedipus certainly suffers, and metaphysical determinacy, and whether it ever deliberately collapses the two for dramatic effect.

Please take both the play and the philosophical distinction seriously: identify scenes or choices that illuminate one reading or the other, note where the text resists a neat classification, and consider what the drama adds to the debate about future contingents that a purely formal argument might miss. I should be grateful for a response in British English, with no bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
