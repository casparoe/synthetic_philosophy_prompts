In debates about Montaigne’s “Of Cannibals,” writers often frame his comparison of Tupinambá ritual cannibalism and European religious violence as two different operating systems running incompatible code: each culture has its own source logic, execution rules, and error conditions, and judging one by the other’s standards is like calling a Linux kernel buggy because it lacks a Windows registry. The analogy is used to cast Montaigne as a proto-relativist who denies any legitimate cross-cultural moral evaluation. Audit this comparison: give the explicit correspondence table (source code, execution, errors, patches, interoperability); identify exactly where the mapping collapses—especially regarding harms that are intended features rather than system errors in both cultures; examine whether the analogy smuggles in the assumption that cultures are closed, coherent, and version-controlled, ignoring Montaigne’s own hybrid European formation and the contested, ritual-specific context of Tupinambá practice; and determine whether Montaigne’s actual argumentative move—using the cannibals as a mirror to expose European “barbarism” rather than to license moral indifference—survives once the operating-system framing is dismantled. If the analogy fails, propose a better one for what Montaigne is actually doing.
