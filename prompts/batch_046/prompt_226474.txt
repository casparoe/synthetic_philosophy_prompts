In our high school philosophy club, two members argued over a classmate who erased all digital messages with a deceased mentor. Priya said this violates a duty to remember: those messages are shared past artifacts, and destroying them is like tearing pages from a communal archive. Tom replied that the duty concerns the person, not every trace, and that what he calls "attentional release"—deliberately letting some memories fade—is different from archival destruction; in fact, constantly revisiting old messages prevents the integration that real remembrance requires. Priya answered that without material anchors, memory becomes unreliable and self-serving, so attentional release easily slides into convenient amnesia. Tom countered that treating every digital trace as a sacred relic confuses data preservation with moral remembrance; the true duty is to carry forward the mentor's values, not to curate a perfect database. They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please explain who is right about each specific point—the nature of the duty, the distinction between destroying records and allowing recall to fade, and whether digital artifacts alter the moral calculus—and say who is more right overall.
