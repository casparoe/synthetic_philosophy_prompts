I'm giving the opening remarks at our workshop "Credit and Value in Early Modern Thought" — maybe fifteen people, half are Berkeley scholars who'll spot a sloppy reading of The Querist, half are ethics grad students who mostly know him as that immaterialist guy. Need about four minutes, casual like I'm talking over bad confernce coffee. The idea should do structural work, not just decorate: start from Berkeley's view that money isn't real wealth but a kind of ticket or counter that only functions if people agree it stands for goods. Use that to frame a live puzzle — can a representation of value become so abstract that it stops being a ticket and loses any moral anchor? — but don't name bitcoin or blockchain; let them feel the analogy without being told it. For the Berkeley people, paraphrase his position accurately around the heart of the Querist, no verbatim quotes, I'm not checking page numbers and please don't cite editions. For the ethics students, leave them holding one concrete question: does a purely symbolic obligation carry the same weight as one tied to labor or land? Maybe open with a joke about Berkeley blaming Ireland's poverty on people mistaking the counter for the thing being counted. Don't finish with a bibliography — the specialists are already writing them.
