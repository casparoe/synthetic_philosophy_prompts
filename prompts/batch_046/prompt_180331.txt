I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about whether the AI alignment problem is genuinely one problem or several, and I want to crystallize that disagreement into the final essay question for my graduate seminar on philosophy and artificial intelligence. My friend kept insisting that alignment is essentially a single problem of value specification—getting a system to do what we intend—whereas I tried to press a distinction I kept calling alignment-as-translation, the problem of mapping human intentions into formally specifiable objectives without losing normative content, against alignment-as-stewardship, the problem of maintaining institutional and epistemic answerability to human judgment as capabilities scale, regardless of whether the initial specification was correct. We could not agree on whether these are conceptually distinct problems with different failure modes or merely two phases of one underlying issue, and I want students to have to take a position.

Please draft one 90-minute essay question for graduate students that cannot be answered by reciting the standard taxonomy of outer and inner alignment. The question must require students to analyze the conceptual architecture of the alignment problem: they should have to defend or reject the claim that it is unitary, specifically engaging with the translation versus stewardship distinction I have sketched, and they must address whether treating alignment as plural creates normative or practical tensions—such as whether different solutions are required for each aspect, or whether pluralism about the problem undermines the urgency of addressing any single part. The question should also ask them to identify at least one point where the distinction collapses or where it genuinely diverges in its conceptual commitments.

Please provide a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with assigned marks totaling 20. For each point, include a one-line note on the specific mistake or omission that typically costs candidates marks. The scheme should clearly separate an A answer, which must show precisely why the translation/stewardship distinction is either philosophically robust or unstable and cannot be reduced to a single framework, from a B answer, which may accurately describe both concepts but fails to demonstrate their separation or collapse at the level of conceptual commitment. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should stand on conceptual argument alone.
