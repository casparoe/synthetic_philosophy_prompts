In a recent discussion about cross-cultural metaphysics of the self, I found myself defending an austere reductionist reading of the Buddhist anatta doctrine—one that treats personal identity not merely as lacking a permanent atman but as lacking any conventional, narrative, or psychologically continuous bearer whatsoever. On this interpretation, only momentary mental and physical events (dharmas) exist, linked by immediate causal contiguity; there is no substrate that owns experiences across time, no pragmatically useful fiction of a "person," and no justification for first-person pronouns beyond a strict error-theory. Moral responsibility, autobiographical memory, and the intelligibility of rebirth must all be reconstructed without any enduring entity, and any attempt to preserve a "weaker" conventional identity—whether narrative, relational, or functional—is rejected as a disguised instance of atma-graha, or self-grasping, that undermines the doctrine's purpose. I want a rigorous map of what would count against this position: which arguments in philosophy of mind or law, which phenomenological data, which formal results about persistence, or which conceptual distinctions genuinely threaten it, as opposed to merely offending pre-theoretical intuition. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of this view would concede versus dismiss as importing Western essentialist biases, and evaluate whether the position has qualified itself into unfalsifiability—say, by defining every rival account of identity as covert self-grasping—and whether that insulation is a strength or a methodological defect. Write your response in American English.
