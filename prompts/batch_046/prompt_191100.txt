I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of law and moral philosophy presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on collective agency and responsibility next Thursday. The audience comprises about fifteen scholars: primarily compatibilist philosophers of action well-versed in Frankfurt-style counterexamples and in Christian List and Philip Pettit's framework for group agency, alongside two legal theorists who defend corporate criminal liability and one moral psychologist who studies institutional blame practices. They are not hard determinists and generally regard moral responsibility as compatible with causation by prior mental states, but they take distributed and collective agency seriously.

My thesis applies Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui, which is impossible—to collective agents such as juries, parliamentary committees, and corporate boards. I grant that groups can exhibit intentional attitudes and decision procedures that no individual member possesses, yet I contend that the regress of conditions for self-origination is not halted at the collective level either: each member's contribution is causally determined by prior states, and the group's emergent properties supervene on these non-self-originating parts, so collective responsibility inherits the same impossibility. I am not asking for bibliographies or citation lists; I need a Q&A preparation sheet tailored specifically to this room. Please order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrase each objection in the vocabulary these particular interlocutors would use, indicate what I should concede upfront, provide a concise reply suitable for delivery in about two minutes, flag the weakest point in each reply, and identify the single question—likely from the legal theorists or the group-agency camp—that I am hoping nobody asks, together with a brief damage-control strategy should it arise.
