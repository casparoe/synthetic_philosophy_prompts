I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to formalize my intuitions about counterfactual decision problems—specifically counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail—as they might apply to predictive AI architectures. Before I discuss these with my team, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments.

1. Counterfactual mugging is a coherent and non-trivial challenge for evidential decision theory: if a predictor’s reward is causally upstream but informationally tied to my choice, cooperation can be rational even when the prediction is already fixed.

2. XOR blackmail is structurally parallel—an adversary conditions harm on my refusal—yet the correct response is clearly refusal, because the adversary’s action is causally independent and already executed.

3. The decisive difference between the two cases is not the presence of a predictor, but the causal direction of dependence: in mugging, the predictor’s move is upstream; in blackmail, the adversary’s move is fixed regardless.

4. I also hold that if the predictor is best modeled as a deterministic compiler that analyzes my source code rather than observing a noisy signal, then the “choice” to cooperate is illusory—I am simply executing the branch that was statically predicted, which dissolves the decision-theoretic tension.

5. At the same time, I think that an AI system with only derived, syntactic intentionality—one that runs internal self-predictive simulations to optimize its outputs—faces an internal analog of counterfactual mugging, because its own forecast module is causally upstream of its behavior.

6. I further think that such a system cannot face an internal XOR-blackmail analog, because there is no mechanism for pre-committed, adversarial randomness inside a unified architecture; internal randomness is just sampling, not blackmail.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe each tension is. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most conflicts overall. I am particularly interested in whether commitment 4 undermines commitment 1, and whether commitments 5 and 6 are compatible with the causal-direction principle in commitment 3, or whether they smuggle in an extra condition I have not acknowledged. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and indicate which apparent conflicts dissolve once the underlying logic is traced carefully.
