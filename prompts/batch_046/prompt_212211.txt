In psychiatric philosophy, a debate about looping effects—where classifying people with mental disorders changes their behavior and self-understanding, which in turn reshapes the classification—has reached an apparent standstill. One side, which I will call interactive realists, holds that psychiatric categories remain genuinely explanatory even though they are moving targets: the loops track real patterns of suffering and dysfunction, and clinical practice should adapt to the motion rather than deny it. The opposing side, loop constructivists, contends that in psychiatry institutional feedback—diagnostic manuals, pharmaceutical marketing, school accommodations, disability benefits—is so rapid and powerful that the loop dominates the looped; what looks like a stable disorder is mostly an administrative artifact sustained by incentives.

Both sides invoke the same episodes—the broadening of autism spectrum criteria in recent DSM revisions, the disappearance of hysteria, the post-Vietnam expansion of PTSD, and recent epidemiological surges in adolescent ADHD—to reach opposite conclusions, yet neither has conceded ground in years. My hypothesis is that the impasse is not ontological but temporal: the realists evaluate stability across multi-decade clinical trajectories, while the constructivists weight five-to-ten-year epidemiological spikes driven by institutional change. So they may be measuring the same loops at different scales and calling the results reality or construction.

Is that diagnosis correct? Or is the disagreement substantive—perhaps about whether specific loops, such as school-based ADHD screening, generate genuinely novel cognitive profiles or merely relabel pre-existing variation? Could it be verbal, with each side using natural kind to answer different questions—one about predictive utility, one about causal independence from human practices? Or are they answering different questions altogether, one about clinical explanation and one about epidemiological causation, under a single heading? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each position actually says, and tell me what, if anything, could resolve it: a particular longitudinal study, a formal model of feedback strength, a conceptual distinction that separates the questions, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
