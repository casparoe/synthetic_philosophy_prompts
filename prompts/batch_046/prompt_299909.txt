You serve as an external advisor to a hospital bioethics committee that has issued four prior rulings on conflicts between advance directives and later expressions of will. In Case 1, a patient with progressive Alzheimer’s disease had executed a refusal of artificial nutrition for the stage at which she could no longer recognize close family; when that stage arrived, she showed no distress and made no explicit request for nutrition, though she retained some emotional responsiveness to caregivers. The committee enforced the refusal. In Case 2, a patient with dissociative identity disorder had, in a stable “host” state, executed an advance refusal of blood transfusion; during a subsequent medical emergency, an alternate “protector” identity emerged, expressed different values, and requested transfusion. The committee enforced the original refusal. In Case 3, a patient suffered a traumatic brain injury that eliminated autobiographical memory and produced a radically different personality with new attachments and values; this patient revoked a prior refusal of life-sustaining ventilation. After psychiatric evaluation confirmed reasoning capacity, the committee honored the revocation. In Case 4, a patient undergoing long-term pharmacological treatment experienced a gradual, stable shift in risk preferences; after evaluation confirmed intact reasoning and the absence of delusion, the patient formally amended an advance directive to reject an experimental intervention previously requested, and the committee permitted the amendment.

The committee now faces a new proposal. A patient with treatment-resistant depression had, during a documented remission, executed an advance refusal of electroconvulsive therapy. The patient then enrolled in a novel, reversible neurostimulation protocol that suppresses affective responses but leaves cognition, memory, and reasoning intact. While in this “flattened” state—witnessed and documented—the patient formally revoked the refusal and requested ECT. Two weeks later, after the stimulation parameters were adjusted and affective range returned, the patient expressed horror at the revocation and insisted that the original refusal be honored.

What should the committee decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own track record? Identify the closest analog among Cases 1–4, explain which features make it analogous and which distinguish it, and then state the decision that best coheres with the committee’s precedents, with your reasoning. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome—so that at least two plausible governing rules are compatible with Cases 1–4 but diverge here—lay out those competing rules, say what each predicts about this case, give your best-guess recommendation with the reasons for preferring it, and specify one further hypothetical case that would force the committee to choose between those rules. Finally, name the principle the committee would be adopting if it followed your recommended path, and name the different principle it would be adopting if it went the other way.
