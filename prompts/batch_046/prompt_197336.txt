I am preparing an interview for a long-form feature in a science-ideas magazine with an unnamed geologist and historian of science who defends a deliberately unconventional framing of the Anthropocene. Rather than locating its onset in a physical stratigraphic signal—the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide spike, the 1610 Orbis marker associated with colonial ecological exchange, or earlier Holocene transitions—he argues that the epoch should be dated to the institutional moment around 2000 when Earth-system science first treated anthropogenic forcing as a formal stratigraphic agent, essentially holding that scientific self-awareness, not a sediment layer, establishes the boundary. I need eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of this view. I want at least one question that forces him to defend why institutional recognition should take priority over physical markers; one that asks whether this framing makes the Anthropocene a performative category in the philosophical sense—an epoch brought into being by the act of naming it—rather than a purely descriptive one; and one that probes who has legitimate authority to decide—geological commissions, climate-science consortia, or democratic bodies—when a geological epoch is formally declared, particularly when different regions contributed asymmetrically to any candidate signal. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give: for example, if he insists on institutional priority, press him on whether this collapses the distinction between describing a geological shift and legislating it; if he accepts the performative reading, ask what empirical finding could still falsify the epoch's existence rather than merely its naming; and if he appeals to expert bodies, ask how to weigh the voices of nations that generated the signals against those that suffer them but did not produce them. The remaining questions should address the implications for teaching deep-time chronology to students, how his view interacts with the Anthropocene Working Group's actual deliberative procedures without requiring citation of specific meeting minutes, and what he thinks is genuinely lost—conceptually, not just administratively—if the International Commission on Stratigraphy ultimately rejects a formal epoch designation altogether. Keep the tone formal, journalistic, and rigorous; avoid generic biographical openers and do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists.
