I was talking with some friends recently about those puzzles where someone completes an infinite number of actions before a clock strikes—like the lamp that gets flipped on and off faster and faster until a minute has passed, and then people ask whether it is lit or dark at the end. I am not in academia, but I have been trying to understand whether the weirdness is really about the lamp itself or about finishing an endless sequence of jobs in a finite span of time. To test that, I want to design my own version that avoids a simple on/off switch entirely. Could you construct a thought experiment that meets the specifications below?

The scenario must be a supertask: discrete actions occur at times approaching one minute—say at 1/2 minute, 3/4 minute, 7/8 minute, and so on—using only ordinary physical objects with no quantum effects, no perfect predictors, and no science-fiction devices. Replace the binary lamp with a single object that has a continuous state variable, such as a pointer sliding along a calibrated rail or a piston inside a transparent tube marked with volume levels. At each step n, the object shifts by a distance equal to half the previous shift, with the direction fixed by the parity of n (left for odd steps, right for even steps), but arrange the distances so that the sequence of positions approaches two different boundary values without ever converging to either. Crucially, inject one structural twist that Thomson's lamp lacks: the rule governing step n+1 must depend on the entire accumulated history of positions, not only on the current location—for instance, the mechanism maintains a physical record of whether the pointer has ever crossed a central midpoint, and that record alters the force or distance applied at the next shift. Describe the whole setup in about one paragraph.

Once you have presented the scenario, show precisely what three philosophical positions say about the object's state at the one-minute mark. First, a Thomson-style indeterminacy view: there is no determinate fact about the pointer's location, because an infinite alternating history that lacks a final entry does not settle a unique outcome. Second, a limit-state realist view: the object does occupy a definite position corresponding to the mathematical limit of the sequence, even though no discrete step physically placed it there. Third, an anti-supertask or potentialist view: the sequence of shifts is never actually completed, so asking where the object is after all steps have been done is a category error, comparable to asking for the largest integer. For each position, state the reasoning and identify which feature of the case it appeals to. Then specify the exact structural feature—the interaction between continuous state and history-dependent rules—that causes the limit-state realist view to come out worst, and explain what happens if you remove it: does the case collapse back into a standard binary-toggle version of Thomson's lamp? Finally, include the strongest reply available to a defender of that realist view, for example by arguing that physical continuity laws guarantee a well-defined limit regardless of discrete non-convergence, or that the history-dependence illegitimately assumes an infinite physical memory that no real mechanism could possess.
