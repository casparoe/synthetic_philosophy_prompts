I serve on my university's Joint Research Ethics and Disclosure Standards Panel, and we must issue a formal opinion before next week's vote on an appeal involving a principal investigator in a longitudinal neuroimaging study. The investigator applied an internal "stake-calibrated disclosure protocol"—a framework the lab designed that assigns different evidential thresholds for mandatory disclosure depending on whether a therapeutic pathway exists. Under this protocol, incidental findings in a pure-research context require 99% confidence of pathogenicity to trigger disclosure, whereas clinical contexts require only 75%. During the study, three participants were found to carry an uncharacterized structural variant in a non-coding regulatory region; the evidence stood at roughly 85% confidence of being benign, with no established intervention. The investigator withheld the results. One participant later learned of the variant through independent clinical screening, experienced severe anxiety, and filed a complaint alleging that the PI "knew" a risk was present and violated disclosure obligations by applying a stricter research-context standard.

The PI defends that, without meeting the 99% research-context threshold, there was no knowledge of actionable harm, and that importing clinical disclosure standards into research would generate harmful false positives where no treatment exists. The panel is split: some members hold that disclosure obligations must be invariant across contexts, treating "knowledge of risk" as governed by a single epistemic standard regardless of practical stakes; others argue that pragmatic encroachment legitimately permits institutional knowledge-standards to shift with stakes, and that contextual variation in evidentiary demands is not arbitrary but responsive to the consequences of error.

Please draft the panel's written opinion for circulation: recommend whether to uphold the PI's decision, reject the stake-calibrated protocol outright, or approve it subject to specific conditions; include a concrete recommendation about future disclosure practices with an enforceable time frame; and treat seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side—specifically, whether allowing stakes to calibrate epistemic standards for disclosure collapses into discretionary judgment that undermines uniform participant protection. The opinion should be actionable by the board and should address the philosophical issue without requiring citation to specific literature.
