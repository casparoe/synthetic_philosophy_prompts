everyone in my philopsophy reading group treats Keynes's essay about our grandkids and leisure like a retirement plan for civilization: you grind through growth now, compound capital like a 401k, and then once the economy hits maturity you cash out into 15-hour weeks and art. they use this to argue we should just push GDP now and worry about leisure later. i want you to audit how far the analogy actually holds.

give me the correspondance table — what maps onto contributions, compound interest, the retirement age, the withdrawal phase, and what plays the role of the retiree?

then where it breaks. Keynes made that weird distinction between absolute needs and relative needs, and he said the love of money was a morbid condition. the retirement model treats leisure as a passive payout, but there's this niche point that Keynes thought leisure had to be cultivated like a musical skill, not just received. does that wreck the mapping?

also check what the analogy quietly imports about value: retirement planning is individual and opt-in, but Keynes was talking about collective social transformation. and it assumes the endpoint is just absence of work, whereas Keynes seemed to think the good life required specific virtues.

lastly, does the conclusion grow now, relax later survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? if it's broken, what's a better analogy for what Keynes actually wanted?
