So I've been reading up on mental causation for a philosphy seminar and I'm kinda baffled why the exclusion argument debate hasn't moved in decades. You know the setup: mental properties seem to cause behavior, but if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause and the physical domain is causally closed, then mental properties look either causaly redundant or like they overdetermine things. Kim's argument makes this sharp. Then the non-reductive physicalists talk about realization, the emergentists talk about downward causation, and the reductive physicalists just identify mental with physical properties. But everyone just digs in deeper with the same moves, and it feels like we're running in place.

My hunch is that there's a hidden disagreement about what kind of causation we're even tracking -- mechanistic production versus difference-making -- and also about whether "closure" means physics is complete in the sense of having no gaps, or complete in the stronger sense of being ontologically exhaustive. But I'm also wondering if it's just bedrock: some people have the intuition that mental properties must be causally efficacious in their own right, others think that's mystical, and no argument bridges that gap.

Can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is this a verbal disagreement masked by shared vocabulary, a substantive disagreement about causation or closure that could be settled, or just two groups with incompatible starting intuitions? And specifically, has anyone actually shown that mechanistic causation is the right framework for mental causation, or is that being assumed without defense? If it's bedrock intuitions, is there anything -- a formal result about overdetermination, an empirical finding about neural mechanisms, a conceptual distinction -- that could actually shift the debate, or is this just one of those philosophical dead ends where we should stop expecting resolution and move on?
