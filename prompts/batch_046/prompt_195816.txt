A friend pressed me on institutional compromise and I cannot see the exit. (1) Define a safe Pareto improvement in normative space: a change that leaves no value system worse off by its own internal lights, while leaving at least one better off. (2) Take two groups with incommensurable commitments—say, one treating ecological integrity as non-commodifiable, the other treating resource efficiency as paramount—who share a watershed. (3) Design a dual-track institution: the first group sets non-negotiable preservation thresholds; the second allocates resources within those bounds, with exchange permitted only at the margin. (4) By each group’s own evaluation, this dominates uniform regulation that both reject; no core commitment is surrendered, only jurisdiction partitioned. Hence it is a safe Pareto improvement in normative space. (5) Generalize: any value pluralism can be resolved by assigning each system sovereign authority over its domain and permitting trade only at the boundaries. (6) Therefore there are never rational grounds to refuse institutional compromise; refusal is merely failure to recognize available jurisdictional gains. (7) But this proves too much: it dissolves all deep moral conflict—such as whether a particular practice should exist at all—into mere coordination problems, contradicting the phenomenology of genuinely contradictory values. (8) So either (4) misapplies ordinal reasoning to values that resist internal comparison, or (5) conceals a hidden moral cost—perhaps that granting the opposing system legitimate jurisdiction is itself a betrayal of one’s commitments. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the possibility of moral compromise?
