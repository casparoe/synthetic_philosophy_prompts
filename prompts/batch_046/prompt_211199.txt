I have been thinking about pain asymbolia, the rare condition in which people report feeling pain yet do not experience it as unpleasant or harmful. Some philosophical accounts I have encountered suggest that this dissociation proves pain is not intrinsically bad: it is merely a sensory signal that human beings have learned to dislike. I find this unconvincing. If a person burns their hand and feels no aversion whatsoever, it seems more plausible that their emotional-evaluative response is damaged, not that the injury lacked any intrinsic badness.

Yet I also keep returning to a hypothetical mirror-image: suppose there were an individual who experienced perfectly ordinary sensations—gentle warmth, mild pressure—as carrying the same overwhelming urgency and suffering that most people associate only with severe injury. If such a case were possible, would it not show that negative evaluation can be attached to any sensory content, and therefore that pain's badness is not built into its specific sensory character?

Please write a dialogue between two interlocutors—Mara and Jonah—debating whether pain is intrinsically bad in light of asymbolia and this mirror hypothetical. Mara should defend the view that asymbolia reveals pain to be essentially informational, with negative affect being a contingent, learned attachment; she should use the mirror case to argue that evaluation is separable from sensation in both directions. Jonah should defend the view that asymbolia represents a specific failure of affective integration, that pain is essentially harmful, and that the mirror case demonstrates only that our evaluative mechanisms can misfire, not that badness is freely detachable.

The exchange should be genuinely dialectical: each speaker must address the other's actual arguments, not merely deliver parallel monologues. Mara should answer Jonah's integration-failure claim directly; Jonah should answer Mara's appeal to the mirror case rather than dismissing it.

Finally, please take a committed stance. Do not conclude that both views are equally reasonable. Instead, have the dialogue reach a definite verdict—either that pain lacks intrinsic badness or that it possesses it—supported by explicit reasoning. Any residual weakness in the favored position may be noted, but the conclusion should be firm rather than agnostic.
