I'm delivering the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop I'm helping organize — the room will be split between stratigraphers and Quaternary geologists who followed the Anthropocene Working Group's decade-long study and its Crawford Lake golden-spike proposal, and philosophers of science together with some rationalist-adjacent forecasters who are less interested in plutonium isotopes than in how institutions draw authoritative lines through continuous processes. Most of them already know the standard chronology — Crutzen's 2000 intervention, the Great Acceleration graphs, the competing start-date candidates stretching from the Columbian Exchange to 1952 — so I don't need to lecture on background. Instead, I want an address of about seven minutes that treats the boundary question as structurally self-referential: the committee tasked with defining the epoch we currently inhabit is simultaneously inscribing its own decision criteria into the sedimentary record it claims merely to observe.

The philosophical idea should do real work, not decoration. I'm calling the framing stratigraphic self-reference — the problem that defining a geological epoch while embedded inside it collapses the observer-observed distinction in a way that makes who decides inseparable from when did it start. Because of this, the speech cannot simply champion one date over another; it must show why the failure of the Crawford Lake proposal to secure formal ICS ratification isn't merely bureaucratic delay but exposes something about temporal authority — specifically, that any golden spike selected now is also a prediction about which signals future geologists will treat as definitive. For the geologists in the room, the rhetoric should press on the GSSP procedure itself; for everyone else, it should clarify why boundary-drawing is never just empirical. A friend mentioned this is structurally analogous to resolving a prediction market on an outcome that includes the resolution mechanism itself, so if that analogy sharpens the point without turning the talk into a primer on forecasting, feel free to weave it in. Keep the tone formal and polished, appropriate for people who read both LessWrong threads and Nature Geoscience, and don't let the mixed audience collapse into either pure sociology or pure stratigraphy.
