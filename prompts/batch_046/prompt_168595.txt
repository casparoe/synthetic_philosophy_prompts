I'm preparing a workshop pitch for a philosophy of memory working group, and I'd like to develop a short conceptual proposal—something that could become a semester-length research paper—on the ethics of remembrance and forgetting. The existing literature I am familiar with largely frames the problem as a tension between Margalit's claim that shared memory binds moral communities together and Rieff's argument that therapeutic forgetting is sometimes a moral necessity. What both positions seem to treat as uniform, however, is the functional role that particular memories play within a collective narrative. To probe this, I want to introduce a distinction between what I will call architectural memory and ornamental memory: the former consists of those retained events or judgments whose presence is structurally necessary for the coherence of the moral framework that justifies other communal obligations, while the latter comprises memories retained for expressive, commemorative, or affective value but which do not bear that load-bearing weight. The precise question is whether the duty to remember applies with equal stringency to both categories, or whether there is an asymmetry—perhaps a stringent duty not to erase architectural memories coupled with only a weak, defeasible duty to preserve ornamental ones—and whether this distinction can clarify stalemates in post-conflict contexts where societies must decide which events to institutionalize in public monuments or curricula and which to allow to fade.

For the proposal itself, I need the research question stated with enough precision that it has a determinate answer, an explanation of why the standard debate between remembrance and forgetting does not already settle it, and a methodological defense of approaching the problem through conceptual analysis of collective narrative identity rather than through empirical case studies or historical sociology. I also want an account of what a useful deliverable would look like—likely a framework that distinguishes permissible from impermissible forms of collective forgetting—together with an honest assessment of what could make the project too large for one semester and what would count as failure rather than mere inconclusiveness. Since this is pitched to philosophers rather than to policymakers, the proposal should explain why a conceptual contribution matters for people actually designing memorial practices. Please separate what is well-established in the ethics-of-memory literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular reading of Margalit or Rieff as though it were the consensus view.
