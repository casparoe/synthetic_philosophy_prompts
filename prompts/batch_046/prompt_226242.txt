I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I've come to defend a position I call functional endurantism: I maintain that an object persists through time as strictly identical and wholly present at each moment—so there are no temporal parts—but that its persistence conditions are determined by the uninterrupted continuity of a functional architecture rather than by spatiotemporal or material continuity. I think this lets me say that a person survives gradual replacement of neural tissue by synthetic analogues, provided the functional organization is preserved without branching or merging, and that it avoids both the ontological cost of perdurantism and the arbitrary cutoff problems facing material-continuity endurantism. I also take it to capture the intuition in teletransportation cases that the reconstructed individual is the same enduring subject, not a new temporal slice. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the defeaters mapped out and ranked: formal arguments about identity conditions, thought experiments involving fission or fusion of functional architectures, empirical considerations about consciousness and self-modeling, and objections showing that the view either collapses into standard endurantism with an ad hoc supplement or into perdurantism disguised by different vocabulary. Which of these would a committed functional endurantist concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed? Does the view risk being insulated by allowing the functional architecture to be characterized post hoc to preserve identity, and if it is so insulated, is that a philosophical defect? Also, what does my view predict—about personal survival, about branching cases, about non-conscious objects—that its rivals do not, and what result or case would falsify that distinctive prediction?
