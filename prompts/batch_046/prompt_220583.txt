Take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in logic and the epistemology of reasoning. Before delivering your verdict, briefly outline the logical structure of Carroll's tortoise regress and list the main argumentative moves on both sides. Then adjudicate carefully, move by move.

Two members of a philosophy reading group, Sam and Jordan, have been arguing about Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise. They agree on the setup: the Tortoise keeps pressing Achilles to accept another hypothetical premise linking what he already accepts to the conclusion Z, generating an infinite regress that seems to block any deduction from ever being fully justified.

Sam argues that Carroll's regress genuinely undermines any attempt to justify deduction by adding logical rules as premises. On Sam's view, the only consistent response is to accept that deduction ultimately rests on primitive, unjustified commitments—rules we apply not because we've justified them, but because reasoning must start somewhere. Sam thinks this reveals that logical reasoning is either circular or grounded in arbitrary convention, not in rational necessity.

Jordan replies that Sam has missed the central lesson. Jordan says Carroll is not showing that deduction needs justification via premises; rather, he is exposing a category mistake. Logical rules like modus ponens are not premises that need backing; they are norms governing how premises are used. Jordan claims that once you see this distinction, the regress dissolves: you do not need to add "if A and B then Z" as a premise, because that conditional expresses the very structure of the inference you are performing. Jordan adds that treating rules as premises confuses the act of reasoning with a description of reasoning, and that Sam's "primitive commitment" view concedes too much by accepting the Tortoise's framing.

Sam answers back that Jordan's "category mistake" response is just a relabeling. Even if rules are norms rather than premises, the Tortoise can still ask what justifies treating them as norms. If Jordan appeals to the meaning of logical constants or to our cognitive competence, Sam says that is psychology or semantics, not logical justification—it explains why people apply the rules, not why they ought to. And if Jordan says the justification is exhibited in the practice itself, Sam objects that this makes justification circular: we justify deduction by pointing to people doing deduction.

Jordan's final move distinguishes internal from external justification. He grants that you cannot justify modus ponens from outside logic without circularity, but insists that is a feature, not a failure. The internal justification comes from the rule's role in constituting what counts as a valid inference within the system. Sam, Jordan says, is demanding an external certificate that logic by its nature cannot provide, and mistaking that absence for a defect.

They asked me to referee, and I am genuinely unsure. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate each central claim—Sam's claim that deduction ends in unjustified primitives; Jordan's claim that the category distinction dissolves the regress; Sam's charge that meaning/competence is a psychological dodge; Jordan's internal/external distinction and the claim that demanding an external certificate mistakes logic's nature—identifying which are clearly right, clearly wrong, or genuinely contested. Then say who is more right overall, and specifically identify what both parties are missing: is there a coherent middle position about how logical rules carry authority without being either bare primitives or externally certified? Work through this step by step and be precise about where the argument actually stands.
