I am preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of absence—specifically on how we reason from missing evidence when the missingness itself is the central phenomenon—and I want to test whether students can distinguish between treating silence as a temporary communicative failure and treating it as a structural datum that overturns our background expectations. Take the 2016 film Arrival, in which alien vessels appear and the protagonist works to decode their language; the plot implicitly frames the Fermi paradox not as a statistical puzzle about cosmic abundance but as a problem of translation that will be solved by direct encounter. I want the class to read the film through the lens of the Fermi paradox, but not through the familiar Drake-equation or Great Filter framework; instead, through a conceptual distinction I have been developing between an evidential gap, in which we lack data that our background model predicts should exist, and an explanatory inversion, in which the persistent absence overturns that very model and demands a revision of our priors about intelligence in the universe. Does Arrival dramatize the wrong kind of response—treating cosmic silence as a temporary failure of contact rather than as an inversion that should reshape our expectations? Where does the film’s emphasis on linguistic determinism and nonlinear temporality illuminate, or perhaps obscure, the philosophical problem of waiting in a universe that offers no reply? I am looking for a reading that takes both the film’s narrative architecture and the distinction seriously, that does not reduce the work to an illustration of probabilistic reasoning, and that ends by saying whether the film adds something to the debate—perhaps about the ethics or phenomenology of anticipation—that purely quantitative treatments of the paradox miss.
