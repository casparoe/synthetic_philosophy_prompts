I am enrolled in an upper-division seminar on comparative philosophy of mind, and after our session on personal identity a classmate sent me a summary she had drafted for a discussion blog. She writes: "The Buddhist doctrine of anatta holds that a human being is nothing but the five skandhas—material form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—temporarily arranged like the parts of a chariot. Because each skandha is impermanent and arises only in dependence on conditions, there is no enduring soul or self that survives death; rebirth is therefore not the migration of an entity but the transmission of karma across successive bundles, analogous to one flame lighting another. This means Buddhists reject even the conventional use of 'I': to say 'I feel pain' is strictly false, since only the skandha of feeling is occurring, with no owner behind it. From this it follows that Buddhist ethics lacks any coherent notion of moral responsibility, because without a persistent agent there is no one who can be said to deserve praise or blame for actions performed in past lives."

Before I circulate this to our study group, I want to audit its accuracy. How fair is her characterization? Please work through the summary claim by claim and indicate which parts a Theravada Abhidhammika or a Mahayana philosopher working within the two-truths framework would accept, which parts conflate conventional and ultimate descriptions, which overstate the doctrine into a metaphysical eliminativism about everyday self-reference, and whether the conclusion about the impossibility of moral responsibility survives once those corrections are made. In particular, I am curious whether her framing of the chariot and flame analogies captures the soteriological purpose of anatta—namely, to undermine attachment and clinging rather than to deny persons altogether—or whether she has turned a practical teaching about non-ownership into an ontological thesis that no conventional self exists at all.
