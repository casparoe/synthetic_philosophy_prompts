Construct a six-node decision tree for a value-theory seminar that sorts respondents by whether the absence of craving and suffering is sufficient, necessary, or irrelevant to the best life. Every internal node must present a concrete comparative case answerable without knowing philosophical labels—for example, ranking a permanently tranquil, affect-neutral mind against one that achieves profound joy through occasional longing. Branch first on sufficiency, then on necessity, then on whether absence can coexist with a specific invented positive condition. The tree must end in five labeled leaves: (1) pure tranquilism, where absence is both necessary and sufficient and all other mental goods are derivative or illusory; (2) "residual-content tranquilism," a position you invent here, in which absence is necessary but must be paired with a precisely defined non-affective state that is neither craving nor suffering; (3) positive hedonism; (4) a striving-based or eudaimonist view that denies absence is necessary; and (5) an explicitly empty branch in which absence is sufficient only when combined with intense positive affect, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why that combination is unstable. Map the exact yes/no path to each leaf, flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate given these cases, and keep all descriptions concrete enough that a respondent navigates by intuition alone.
