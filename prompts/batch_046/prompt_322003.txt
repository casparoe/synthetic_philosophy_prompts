As part of my dissertation research on decision-theoretic cooperation, I am developing a variant of the large-worlds argument that I call structural evidential cooperation. The standard formulation assumes that rational cooperation extends across causally disconnected agents who share terminal values but differ only in spatiotemporal location; I am testing whether the evidential link survives when we substitute architecture-alignment for value-alignment. Specifically, consider agents in causally disconnected Hubble volumes who share an identical Bayesian prior over physical constants and an identical decision-theoretic framework—expected-utility maximization with a shared likelihood function over observational outcomes—yet hold strictly orthogonal normative priors, for instance one weighting suffering-minimization and one weighting complexity-maximization. What is your actual credence that evidential cooperation between such agents remains rationally obligatory? Please decompose your reasoning across four dimensions: (i) the evidential strength generated by shared epistemic architecture alone, absent value convergence; (ii) whether orthogonal normative priors undermine the symmetry conditions required for mutual cooperation or merely redirect the cooperative equilibrium; (iii) the extent to which standard large-worlds reasoning tacitly presupposes value-alignment and therefore fails to generalize to architecture-alignment; and (iv) whether the concept of rational obligation retains determinate content across agents with incommensurable normative priors. Provide an overall probability, assign rough weights or sub-probabilities to each component, identify which component is most decisive for your final number, and specify what kind of conceptual or formal argument would shift that decisive component by at least 0.2 in either direction.
