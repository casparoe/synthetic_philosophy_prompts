Write a short story set in a democratic city-state that has abolished elections and fills every legislative seat by sortition—random draw from the adult citizenry, with no campaigns, no parties, and no re-selection. Center the narrative on a single council session debating whether to approve a river-damming project that would supply the city with water while flooding an upstream agricultural commune. Do not portray the council as collectively wise or collectively foolish; instead, dramatize the conceptual tension embedded in descriptive representation. Because the council is a statistical microcosm of the population, it reproduces the same distribution of expertise, ignorance, self-interest, and indifference found among the citizens at large. Show how this randomness simultaneously undermines the epistemic defense of sortition—that an unbiased sample should yield informed judgment—and strengthens its democratic defense, since no elite filter can distort the popular will. Have at least one council member recognize, in the middle of deliberation, that the institutional feature guaranteeing legitimacy—the complete absence of selection—also guarantees that no one present was chosen for understanding hydrology, communal land rights, or long-term ecological risk. Let the story’s resolution turn not on the vote itself, but on whether the citizens who were not drawn have any legitimate basis for accepting or rejecting the outcome, and explore whether consent to the process can survive once everyone acknowledges that the deciders were, in every relevant respect, interchangeable with those left outside the chamber.
