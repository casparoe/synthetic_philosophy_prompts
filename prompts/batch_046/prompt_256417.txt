Consider the regulatory practice, widespread in European and several other jurisdictions, that prohibits plant-derived products from being marketed under names reserved for animal-derived foods—so that beverages made from oats, almonds, or soy may not be labeled "milk," and plant-based spreads may not be called "butter" or "cheese"—while the same legal frameworks routinely permit terms like "peanut butter," "coconut milk," "mineral water," and "wood alcohol," none of which share the defining biological origin or chemical nature of the categories they invoke. What philosophical commitments about linguistic reference, natural kinds, and categorization does this asymmetrical labeling regime presuppose? Does it commit to a form of semantic externalism—of the kind tested by Twin Earth thought experiments—according to which the extension of a kind term is fixed by an underlying natural essence rather than by functional similarity, perceptible properties, or prevailing social usage? Can a descriptivist or internalist theory of meaning accommodate the prohibition without ad hoc adjustments, or does the practice quietly rule such views out? Examine whether the regime is philosophically consistent across its tolerated exceptions, or whether the permitted borrowings reveal that the practice is underdetermined and could just as well rest on a conventionalist, interest-based, or functional-role account of food nomenclature. Finally, if the presupposed externalist theory of natural-kind reference were replaced by a usage-based semantics, what specific changes would have to occur in labeling law, enforcement criteria, and the legal distinction between misdescription and permissible metaphor for the practice to remain coherent?
