Hey, I've been trying to get my head round the paradox of fiction and I think I might be missing something obvious. The classic problem is that we feel genuine sadness for Anna Karenina even though we know she isnt real, which looks irrational if emotions need beliefs with matching content. But hasnt psychology more or less killed the idea that emotion requires full-on belief? We've got aliefs, quasi-beliefs, make-believe — all these states that carry affect without ontological commitment. If thats the case, the original paradox just dissolves: theres no contradiction between feeling sad and not believing she exists, because the feeling never needed that belief in the first place. So why do philosphers like Walton or Radford still present the problem as live? Is there a stronger version that survives even once you reject the cognitive theory of emotion? Or am I just conflating the ontology problem with the rationality problem — like, maybe the puzzle isnt 'how can I feel this?' but 'why is it appropriate to feel this given I know its made up?' I want to know where my reasoning is shakiest and what argument would change my mind. Would it take showing that aliefs still smuggle in a belief-like commitment that clashes with knowing fiction is false? Or is there an independent normative paradox that doesnt depend on the cognitive theory at all? Cheers.
