A colleague discussing Black's spheres—two qualitatively identical spheres in an otherwise featureless, symmetric universe—dismissed my claim that they might still be numerically distinct by saying, "If symmetry doesn't break, they aren't two." I suspect that line is doing far more work than it ought to. Could you lay out the different precise theses it might express: a metaphysical claim about identity, an epistemic claim about our inability to individuate them, a semantic claim about the limits of reference, and a physical-descriptive claim about redundancy in formal representation? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive thesis, or plainly false, and indicate which ones actually licence dismissing the possibility of two spheres. Please answer in British English.
