Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two interpreters of Hegel's master-slave dialectic in the Phenomenology of Spirit, Self-Consciousness section: Camp A reads the dialectic as a normative-phenomenological account of mutual recognition (Anerkennung), holding that self-certainty requires being acknowledged as free by another free consciousness and that the slave's labor is the formative mediation through which such recognition is eventually achieved; Camp B reads it as a structural-materialist account of domination and praxis, holding that the life-risk reveals an asymmetry of power stabilized by the slave's transformation of the objective world, that recognition is at best an ideological superstructure masking continuing material subordination, and that the slave's independence arises from the objective skills and products of labor rather than from intersubjective acknowledgment.

The document should contain, first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the dialectic's architecture: the initial struggle for life and death, the asymmetry of risk and the master's position of pure consumption, the slave's formative labor upon the object (the Ding), the slave's fear of absolute lordship as a formative experience, and the subsequent transitions through stoicism, skepticism, and the unhappy consciousness. These claims should be textually specific yet interpretation-neutral, so that neither camp reads them as endorsing its own framework.

Second, present a disagreements table. Each row should formulate one precise disagreement in wording that the camp holding that view would accept for its own position. At minimum include: (i) whether self-certainty requires actual acknowledgment from another free consciousness or can be achieved through the slave's objective mastery of the formed thing; (ii) whether the slave's eventual independence is explained by intersubjective recognition eventually granted or by the structural irreversibility of labor's material transformation; (iii) whether the move to unhappy consciousness demonstrates the failure of recognition-based self-certainty or demonstrates the inherent instability of consciousness detached from material praxis.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete textual feature, conceptual argument, or comparative structural case that both camps agree bears on it. Crucially, treat as central (not peripheral) the niche question of whether object-formation in labor can functionally substitute for intersubjective recognition: both camps should address whether the slave's relation to the formed object provides a sufficient ground for self-certainty without a second consciousness granting acknowledgment. For each such item, record in advance what each camp predicts it demonstrates—the recognition camp should state what pattern would confirm the irreducibility of mutual acknowledgment, and the materialist camp should state what pattern would confirm that object-mediated praxis can substitute for it.

Fourth, include a brief concluding assessment in which both camps examine whether any portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically addressing whether disagreements about recognition dissolve once the term is disambiguated between normative acknowledgment and functional social validation—and identify exactly which parts, if any, are resolved by that disambiguation.

Write in formal, polished prose appropriate for a philosophical journal's supplementary materials. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Avoid verbatim quotations from Hegel's text unless verified against a fetched source; otherwise paraphrase conceptually. Ensure that the framing about the substitutability of object-formation for intersubjective recognition is treated as a core crux, not an aside.
