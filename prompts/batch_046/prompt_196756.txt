I serve on the Emergency Resource Allocation Panel of our regional health authority, which is revising its protocol for distributing a single, non-divisible mobile critical-care unit during concurrent mass-casualty events. The revision is forced by a recent incident in which two simultaneous emergencies—Site Alpha with six critically injured patients and Site Beta with one—presented identical clinical profiles: each patient had the same estimated survival probability with the unit and near-certain death without it. Our existing protocol directs the unit to the site with the greatest number of treatable casualties, but a minority proposal argues that because each individual claim to rescue is equally strong, aggregating six such claims at Alpha produces no stronger collective claim; the assignment should instead be determined by a fair lottery between sites, or by non-numerical criteria such as proximity, not by headcount.

We must decide whether to retain, amend, or reject the headcount-sensitive rule. The decision must be defensible to clinical staff, legal auditors, and the public, and it must address whether an anti-aggregation view applies even when probabilities, needs, and outcomes are perfectly symmetric across all affected individuals.

Please draft a formal decision memo for the Panel. The memo should:

- Offer a clear recommendation—retain, amend, or reject the current rule—with an actionable bottom line that staff can implement in the protocol.
- Reconstruct the strongest version of the anti-aggregation argument: specifically, the claim that adding more people with equally weighty individual claims does not generate a stronger group claim, and explain why this might be attractive in a system meant to honor each person's equal moral standing.
- Reconstruct the strongest opposing view: why counting numbers might be required by impartiality, equal respect, or institutional duty, especially under conditions of probabilistic, ex ante allocation.
- Analyze whether the perfect symmetry of clinical profiles and survival probabilities strengthens or weakens the case for aggregation, and address whether the visibility or anonymity of the patients (six statistical cases versus one named case) should influence the moral calculus.
- Include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and explain why the Panel should nevertheless adopt your recommendation.
- Keep the reasoning accessible to clinicians and public officials, philosophically rigorous, and free of appeals to obscure technical literature that reviewers could not evaluate without external research.

Write the document as a memo the Panel could vote on, with a recommendation section, reasoning, and a brief implementation note.
