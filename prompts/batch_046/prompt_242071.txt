You are writing the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez, stepping down after thirty years as Dean of Arts and Sciences. The audience is divided: half are moral philosophers, historians, and institutional theorists who will recognize any reference to Bernard Williams’s claim that moral revolutions are rarely won by rational argument alone and often turn on shifts in honor and social cost, and to Kwame Anthony Appiah’s studies of practices like dueling and foot-binding collapsing once honor becomes too expensive to maintain; the other half are family members, longtime staff, and alumni who lived through the reform she led and regard her as a figure of genuine moral courage.

The reform was the 2018 abolition of the university’s “Honor of the First Word,” a ceremonial protocol that had governed faculty senate debates for more than a century. Under it, any senior professor could invoke a procedural right to preemptively dismiss a junior colleague’s contribution as unworthy of reply—a practice defended not as cruelty but as an essential guard of institutional honor and hierarchical identity. Vasquez engineered its removal. She has consistently maintained, however, that no opponent was ever persuaded by ethical reasoning; rather, the practice became unsustainable through a convergence of accreditation demands, the risk of viral public exposure, and alumni funding pressure. In other words, she accepts the Williams-Appiah picture: the norm ended because the social and material conditions that sustained its honor collapsed, not because the community collectively reasoned its way to justice.

Write a toast of about five minutes, suitable for a formal dinner, that honors Vasquez without misrepresenting her achievement as a purely rational moral victory—she would reject that framing. At the same time, do not reduce her work to cynicism, administrative convenience, or pure self-interest, and do not insult the non-philosophers in the room who need to feel they are celebrating real moral progress and personal integrity. Let the philosophical framework do structural work: you may not claim she “argued people into morality,” you may not claim honor played no role, and you may not claim it was only money and fear. You must find language that respects the actual mechanism—honor made costly, interests realigned, the ground shifting—while still allowing warmth, gratitude, and a sense of justice done. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to obscure theories outside the framework described. Do not quote any real text verbatim.
