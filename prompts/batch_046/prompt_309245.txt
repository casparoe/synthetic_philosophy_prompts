I'm taking an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind and personal identity, and we've been discussing mind uploading through gradual neuronal replacement. Most standard treatments frame the issue as a binary question: if biological neurons are replaced one by one with functionally equivalent artificial substrates, does the original subject of consciousness persist across the transition? I think this framing misses a more intricate problem involving both the temporal structure of replacement and the biochemical dimensions of neural integration.

Consider a scenario in which replacement is not uniform across the brain. Different cortical and subcortical regions are replaced at different rates, so for a substantial period the brain exists as a hybrid system—part biological tissue, part synthetic substrate—communicating through an adaptive interface that translates biochemical signaling into electrical protocols and back again. Some philosophers argue that this staggered hybridization actually supports continuity of identity, because there is never a discrete break in psychological functioning; the subject is always fully present in some mixture of old and new tissue. Others contend that the hybrid intermediate is precisely where identity is dissolved: the biological tissue is gradually decoupled from its native metabolic and homeostatic environment, while the synthetic modules, however accurate their electrical dynamics, never participate in the organism's biochemical regulation. On this view, the subject is not suddenly destroyed but slowly diluted, and the hybrid phase masks a gradual extinction rather than a preservation.

There is also a deeper disagreement about what properties the replacement modules must replicate. One position holds that preserving local synaptic weights, connectivity patterns, and electrical firing dynamics is sufficient for consciousness and personal survival. A rival position insists that consciousness depends on global biochemical gradients—neurotransmitter diffusion fields, glial signaling networks, and metabolic coupling—that are not captured by purely electrical replication. If the synthetic substrates reproduce action potentials and synaptic transmission with perfect fidelity but fail to maintain these broader biochemical dimensions, then even a flawlessly executed gradual replacement might substitute a functional duplicate for the original subject.

Write an essay that examines this controversy in detail. Weigh the arguments about whether the spatiotemporal profile of replacement—uniform versus staggered hybridization—and the required level of biochemical fidelity determine whether the original person survives the uploading process. Reach and justify a specific conclusion: for example, defend the claim that gradual replacement preserves identity only if biochemical signaling remains integrated throughout the hybrid phase, or defend the opposing view that electrical-functional continuity is sufficient regardless of intermediate hybrid states or biochemical replication. Do not end on an agnostic or purely descriptive note; your essay should argue for one of these positions and explain why the alternative fails.
