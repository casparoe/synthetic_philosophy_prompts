I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been reading about how moral norms such as chattel slavery and duelling eventually collapse. I understand Kwame Anthony Appiah argues that these changes are often driven not by rational argument but by shifts in the codes of honour that sustained the practices, and that Bernard Williams expresses a related scepticism about whether pure moral reasoning alone can dissolve long-standing social norms. I may be simplifying both views.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet in British English that renders this three-way tension — argument, honour, and material interest — and makes the poetic form do philosophical work. Use the octave (rhymes ABBA ABBA) to present the rationalist story: abolitionist reasoning, logical exposure of duelling's futility, the sense that argument forms a closed, self-confirming loop. At the volta, turn sharply to the historical fact that these arguments circulated for decades without dissolving the practices. In the sestet, let honour and argument pair into resolving rhymes (CDECDE), but assign material interest the single unrhymed line — the C rhyme that appears once and is left structurally dangling — so that one explanation remains unresolved. The poem should not declare a winner; it should leave the tension open. It must stand as genuine poetry, and someone familiar with Appiah's thesis should recognise the turn at the volta.
