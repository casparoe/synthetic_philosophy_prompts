I'm running an 80-minute philosophy-of-mind seminar with 14 upper-level students. Design an in-class simulation using timed card reveals and assigned roles that makes participants run into the color-phi problem from the inside: they must report what they "experienced" at specific millisecond marks, but the cards that determine the correct sequence are only revealed after the fact, analogizing a ~150 ms binding window. Divide them into Perceptual Observers, Time-Stamp Loggers, and Synthesis Adjudicators; give the Adjudicators a small-stakes payoff scheme (group points redeemable for a seminar privilege) that forces them to vote on whether the Observers misremembered an original intermediate color (Orwellian revision of a prior draft) or whether there was never an intermediate experience at all and the report was constructed wholesale (Stalinesque revision with no prior record). The rules should be arranged so participants first feel certain they saw a mid-path change, then discover that information necessary for that judgment arrived later, and only then learn the names of the two interpretations. Include a precise timeline, card rules, and role instructions—especially an instruction to the Adjudicators that asking "what did you experience at t=200 ms?" may be a category error if experience has no intermediate draft. In the debrief, map each round onto either editing an original record or generating a finalized narrative; predict exactly where groups will conflate first-person certainty with archival accuracy and how to handle it; and be explicit about which feature of real neural postdiction—most likely the total absence of any accessible "uncorrected original" for comparison—the exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. Keep the framing in the weeds: treat the Stalinesque option not as memory failure but as the denial that there is anything to fail to remember, and build that denial into the adjudication rules.
