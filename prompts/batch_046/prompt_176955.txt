Consider Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, standardly introduced through the gavagai scenario in which a field linguist cannot empirically fix whether a native term refers to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit-stages. Now extend this to a constructed case: a translator produces two manuals for an alien language that are fully behaviorally adequate and preserve all observed syntactic regularities. Manual A parses every apparently singular noun phrase as referring to a four-dimensional spatiotemporal worm, while Manual B parses the same surface expressions as referring to instantaneous three-dimensional stages linked by a counterpart relation. The native speakers have no explicit metaphysical vocabulary, and their observable behavior gives no purchase on the distinction. Crucially, however, the manuals diverge not only in ontology but in grammatical structure: what Manual A treats as a singular term with continuous reference, Manual B treats as a disguised plural expression carrying a hidden distributive operator, thereby altering the logical form of quantified sentences throughout the language.

Argue that this scenario destabilizes Quine’s own separation between translation indeterminacy and ontological relativity. Defend the claim that the ambiguity revealed here is not merely about what exists, but about semantic-structural parsing itself—how grammatical categories map onto logical form—so that there is no fact of the matter independent of the manual adopted. In your argument, explain why the familiar gavagai example, which focuses on reference to rabbits versus rabbit-parts or stages, actually obscures the deeper point: that indeterminacy can penetrate the syntax-semantics interface in ways that make the choice between manuals irreducible to either pure translation or pure ontology. Address whether this stronger claim reinforces Quine’s anti-realism about meaning, or whether it introduces a form of structural underdetermination that his framework cannot consistently accommodate.
