I work as an arbitration officer for the Meridian Strait Treaty Organisation, a private regulatory body that oversees vessel transit through a disputed maritime corridor. Our charter binds me to apply identical inspection, delay, and detention protocols to all flagged vessels, regardless of national origin. It is widely acknowledged that State Alpha seized control of the corridor through unprovoked aggression, with no recognised legal claim, whereas State Beta is defending territory that the international community recognises as rightfully its own. Despite this, my mandate strictly forbids me from weighing the justice of either cause; I must maintain perfect procedural symmetry.

Recently, I was required to detain a humanitarian supply vessel registered to State Beta. I was fully aware that delaying this shipment would weaken Beta's defensive capacity and facilitate Alpha's consolidation of an illegal occupation. I executed the order because my institutional role demands strict neutrality. I am now troubled by whether my compliance was morally justified. Specifically, I want to understand whether there is a meaningful moral distinction between assessing the justice of a conflict's origins and enforcing rules within it. If an agent applies neutral procedures with full knowledge that one party is an aggressor and the other a defender, does that agent remain blameless when the impartial application of rules predictably aids injustice? Or does such knowledge dissolve the excuse of neutrality, making procedural fairness into a form of complicity?

Please answer in British English, and advise me on whether my concern is philosophically well-founded, particularly with regard to the relationship between an enforcer's procedural duties and the moral asymmetry of the parties involved.
