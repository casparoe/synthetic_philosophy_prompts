I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am designing a 50-minute seminar exercise for a mixed group of about eighteen technical professionals. I want them to experience, through interaction rather than lecture, the asymmetry that Douglas Hofstadter exposes in his pronoun-reversal satire on gendered language: the way an unmarked category functions as invisible infrastructure while the marked category carries unexpected cognitive load. To keep the framing niche and away from standard gender-studies vocabulary, please treat the pronoun system as an API with a default parameter that can be patched—the Hofstadter reversal—so that participants experience protocol drift when the default is flipped.

Design a runnable in-class game with explicit rules, materials, and a non-monetary payoff or scoring mechanism. Split the group into two teams of nine, each receiving an identical system-specification document that describes eight professional roles—engineer, nurse, pilot, manager, surgeon, secretary, soldier, caregiver—paired with brief scenario cards. The only difference between the two specs is the pronoun protocol: Team Alpha uses he as the unmarked generic and she for specifically female roles; Team Beta uses she as the unmarked generic and he for specifically male roles, the reversal. Both teams then play a rapid matching and verification round: they draw scenario cards describing actions in ambiguous contexts and must map each card to the correct role in their spec, defending the choice aloud using only the pronouns available in their protocol. Points are awarded for speed and for consistency across rounds, but the hidden mechanism is that Team Beta’s reversed default introduces a predictable processing lag and coordination failure—members hesitate, laugh, or misattribute ambiguity to the scenario content rather than to syntax.

The rules should be arranged so that Team Beta runs into the friction before anyone names it as gender bias. In the debrief, map specific moments—the hesitation on the first he card, the collective relief when she is read as generic, the misidentification of the marked role—onto Hofstadter’s idea of markedness and invisible defaults. Be explicit about what will go wrong the first time I run this: likely resistance to the reversed protocol, treating the difficulty as a content error rather than a linguistic one, and the risk that technically oriented participants try to debug the language rather than feel it. Also be frank about which feature of sexist language this simulation cannot reproduce—probably the historical and institutional weight behind the male default—and state that I will need to supply that context verbally after the activity.

Please do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Provide the deliverable as a concrete protocol: setting, group split, time allocation, materials list, rules, payoff structure, debrief script outline, and the failure forecast.
