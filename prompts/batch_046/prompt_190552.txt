I am preparing a position paper for an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I find myself stuck between two unsatisfactory extremes in the current discussion of artificial intelligence: on one side, commentators who treat large language models as literal believers because they produce coherent propositional outputs, and on the other, those who dismiss all belief-attribution to such systems as a mere metaphor. What I need before I can adjudicate between them is a rigorous specification of what an adequate theory of belief-attribution—specifically, the application of the intentional stance to transformer-based architectures—would actually have to get right. Rather than a survey of existing views, I want the desiderata first.

Please specify the full list of requirements that any adequate theory in this area must meet. The list should cover the phenomena it must explain—such as predictive accuracy in reasoning tasks, the absence of persistent internal representations between inference calls, in-context learning that temporarily shifts behavior without altering weights, and the capacity for what I will call epistemic partitioning, where a single model generates high-confidence, logically incompatible outputs across different framing conditions without any global consistency mechanism. It should also respect distinctions the theory cannot collapse: between predictive attribution and normative attribution (where the system is treated as answerable to epistemic norms), between functional role and causal origin, and between temporary contextual activation and stable doxastic commitment. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or architectural consideration behind it, and indicate which ones you take to be non-negotiable versus which might be relaxed if doing so resolves a tension. In particular, flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—especially whether normative answerability can survive recognition of epistemic partitioning—and identify which single requirement eliminates the largest number of existing positions.

Finally, apply the list briefly to three broad contenders: a strict biological or architectural eliminativism that denies belief-states to any system lacking human-like doxastic storage; a literalist interpretation that identifies calibrated output probabilities with genuine beliefs; and an instrumentalist view that permits attribution only for predictive or explanatory convenience. Note where each fails, which failure is fatal, and whether dropping one negotiable desideratum would allow any contender to survive intact. Please answer in American English, and organize the requirements in a table if that clarifies the structure.
