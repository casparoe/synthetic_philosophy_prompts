In a philosophy-of-religion seminar I have run for two years, the discussion of divine hiddenness has reached a strange equilibrium. The evidentialist camp—roughly in the line of Schellenberg—insists that a perfectly loving, omnipotent being would make its existence unambiguously available to all sincere seekers, yet we observe pervasive, reasonable non-belief and profound ambiguity about transcendence; they treat this distribution of belief as strong abductive evidence against theism. The relationalist camp—drawing on free-will defenses and soul-making theodicies—responds that hiddenness is not an absence but a structural condition: a direct, overwhelming manifestation would undermine the kind of free, trust-based response that a personal God seeks, and the ambiguity is therefore compatible with, even required by, perfect love. Both sides accept the same phenomenological data (the global patchwork of belief and unbelief, the experience of divine absence reported even by committed seekers, the lack of public, replicable signs), and both agree that God is not epistemically present in the manner of a middle-sized physical object. Yet after dozens of sessions no position has shifted by even a degree.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about God, freedom, or love at all, but about a tacit disagreement over the appropriate standard of epistemic closure for theological claims: evidentialists implicitly apply a closure condition requiring direct perceptual or propositional access for justified belief, while relationalists apply a closure condition appropriate to interpersonal relationships, where absence of direct evidence can be consistent with presence. But I am unsure whether this is the real fracture, or whether there is a deeper, unacknowledged formal question—for instance, about the modal robustness of belief: whether a counterfactual agent who received direct theistic evidence would retain the same doxastic structure, and whether that stability is a virtue or a cognitive bias—that both sides answer differently without recognizing it. There is also the possibility that 'hiddenness' is being used indexically (hidden relative to a particular agent's epistemic position) by one side and absolutely (hidden simpliciter, despite omnipresence) by the other, making the debate partly verbal.

Diagnose the disagreement precisely: is it verbal, substantive but masked, a clash over standards of evidence, a hidden empirical or formal question about belief formation, or bedrock intuitions about love and knowledge that neither side can justify? Support your diagnosis by reference to what each camp actually commits to, not just the standard labels.
