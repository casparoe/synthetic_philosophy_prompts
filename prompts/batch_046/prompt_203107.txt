Having studied the frame problem in the context of representational architectures, I am convinced that it is a difficulty unique to artificial systems: human cognition evidently solves it by sustaining an exhaustive, actively updated representation of every feature of the environment that remains unchanged across an action, effectively maintaining a complete world-model that eliminates any need for selective relevance filtering. If this exhaustive-state strategy is indeed the biological norm, then the failure of AI to replicate it should be attributable solely to computational limits of scale rather than to any principled architectural difference. Yet I wonder whether I am mischaracterizing human cognition here—does the mind actually employ a different mechanism, such as default reasoning or heuristic framing, that avoids the problem without representing the full inertial background? And if so, what specifically prevents artificial systems from implementing that same selective mechanism?
