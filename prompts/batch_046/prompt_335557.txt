I have recently learned, through genetic counseling for a planned pregnancy, that my partner and I carry a pleiotropic mutation that confers exceptional pattern-memory and auditory synthesis—the very cognitive architecture that has shaped my life as a composer—but also guarantees progressive neuropathy in any child who inherits it. Germline editing could remove the mutation, yet the counselor noted that the edited embryo would develop a sufficiently different neural architecture that, on some accounts, it would not be the same person I might otherwise have had. This is not abstract for me: I must decide whether to edit, and I am trying to understand the nonidentity problem in its narrow, person-affecting form rather than as a general puzzle about future generations.

Write a short story—no more than about a thousand words—narrated in the first person by someone in an analogous position. The story should dramatize the nonidentity problem rather than merely alluding to it: the protagonist should confront the possibility that preventing suffering also prevents the existence of the very child who would have borne that suffering, and should do so in a concrete, sensory way (perhaps through music, through the visualization of two diverging neural pathways, or through a dialogue with a future self who does not exist). Take a definite philosophical position through the narrative arc—either that editing is impermissible because it annihilates a specific identity-constituting life, or that it is obligatory despite the nonidentity objection, but do not remain agnostic. The plot should hinge on this commitment, not on external circumstances.

After the story, include a brief author’s note of roughly one hundred words explaining exactly where the narrative takes liberties with the standard philosophical formulation (for instance, whether it treats genetic editing as identity-preserving selection between embryos or as transformation within a continuous life) and stating which interpretation of the nonidentity problem—person-affecting, impersonal, or another—the story leans on and why. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or references to further reading; the note should stand on its own reasoning. Keep the tone formal and polished throughout, and avoid resolving the emotional tension with a sentimental conclusion: the philosophical stakes should remain visible in the final image.
