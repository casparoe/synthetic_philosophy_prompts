Exam question:
In the Groundwork, Kant distinguishes contradiction in conception from contradiction in will and links these failures to perfect and imperfect duties, respectively. He also insists that maxims must be formulated with sufficient specificity—the agent’s end, circumstances, and action must all be explicit. Consider this maxim: “Whenever I exercise discretionary control over resources belonging to others, I will appropriate them for my own benefit, but only in cases where institutional oversight is opaque.” First, apply the contradiction-in-conception test specifically to the maxim’s trigger clause (the opacity condition), not merely to the action-type in isolation. Second, apply the contradiction-in-will test, taking care to distinguish it from a hypothetical-consent or golden-rule analysis. Third, determine whether any failure here would be a perfect or imperfect duty, and explain whether that classification turns on the form of the maxim or on the magnitude of harm produced. Finally, assess whether the specification strategy associated with Onora O’Neill—which requires that maxims explicitly state the agent’s circumstances and ends—resolves any ambiguity in applying these tests to the present maxim.

Student answer:
The maxim can be specified as “Appropriate resources that belong to others when institutional oversight lacks transparency.” The trigger clause is the opacity condition, and the scope covers anyone with discretionary control. For contradiction in conception: if this were universal law, then everyone in such positions would appropriate resources whenever oversight was opaque. This would mean oversight mechanisms are constantly undermined, so opacity becomes universal and permanent. Because the trigger clause is then always satisfied, the maxim applies constantly rather than self-destructing; there is no logical impossibility in conceiving such a world, only an undesirable one. So contradiction in conception does not fail, and we must look to contradiction in will. For that test, a rational agent could not consistently will this maxim because they might find themselves among the vulnerable population and would not want to be exploited. This shows the maxim violates the Formula of Humanity by treating people as mere means, which makes it a perfect duty failure. The duty is perfect because the harm is direct and intentional, not because of the maxim’s logical form. As for O’Neill: she argues that maxims must specify the agent’s social role and temporal location, but she does not require that contingent trigger conditions be made explicit in the maxim formulation. Therefore her specification strategy does not help here, since the ambiguity lies in the action itself rather than in an underspecified agent or circumstance.
