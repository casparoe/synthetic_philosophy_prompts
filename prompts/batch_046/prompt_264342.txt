I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students who understand determinism as the uniqueness of an evolutionary law and have encountered Laplace's demon, but who have no training in dynamical systems and are prone to conflate long-term unpredictability with ontological indeterminism. I want them to see why deterministic Hamiltonian chaos—specifically, exponential divergence of nearby trajectories—does not undermine determinism, and I want to build the session around a carrying case drawn from structural instability and the shadowing property: the fact that numerically integrated orbits diverge from true ones, yet over finite intervals remain arbitrarily close to actual trajectories of the identical system, so that computational error is epistemic rather than physical. Plan the pedagogical architecture, not the lecture itself. Specify the exact order in which the components must be introduced so that shadowing operates as a resolution rather than a technical footnote; identify what the carrying example must exhibit (sensitive dependence, boundedness, deterministic law) and what it must suppress (stochastic forcing, quantum effects, open-system interaction); map the predictable misconceptions—e.g., that divergence proves randomness, that Poincaré recurrence cancels practical unpredictability, that numerical error equals physical openness—and state precisely where in the sequence each should be defused; provide one check question that reveals whether a student distinguishes determinism-as-law from determinism-as-predictability; and list what must be explicitly omitted at this level—such as ergodic measure theory, the KAM theorem, or quantum decoherence—with a brief justification for each omission. Do not provide readings, citations, or bibliographies.
