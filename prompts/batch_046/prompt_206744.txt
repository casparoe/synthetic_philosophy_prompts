A curatorial board at a museum of pre-industrial scientific instruments maintains a four-part taxonomy for objects that relate to lost or damaged originals but are not themselves straightforward originals: Artifact, meaning the original object or an integrally restored part of it; Replica, meaning a direct physical copy that reproduces form, whether made for study, display, or substitution; Reconstruction, meaning an object built to reproduce function or appearance without direct copying from a specific surviving original; and Specter, meaning an object that evokes the original only through mediation or interpretation, lacking both material continuity and functional identity, such as a digital projection or an artistic rendering.

Their rendered verdicts are these. First, they treated a 1783 brass quadrant restored in 1990 with a replacement sighting vane fabricated to original specifications as an Artifact. Second, they treated a quadrant built in 1980 by a master instrument-maker who copied every dimension of that 1783 quadrant through direct measurement as a Replica. Third, they treated a wooden quadrant built in 2015 using only a published engraving and period-appropriate materials, functioning correctly but never measured against the original, as a Reconstruction. Fourth, they treated a holographic projection of a lost 1710 microscope, synthesized from scans of similar surviving instruments, as a Specter. Fifth, they treated a modern hand-forged iron needle inserted into an original 1750 compass, matching period form but bearing a slightly anachronistic alloy, as part of the Artifact. Sixth, they treated an exact 3D-printed polymer copy of the restored 1783 quadrant, produced for tactile museum access, as a Replica, explicitly noting that its institutional purpose did not alter its physical relationship to the original.

They now face an unclassified object: a cast-iron quadrant discovered in 2024 in an uncatalogued storage crate. Metallurgical analysis indicates it was cast from the same mold used for the 1783 quadrant in their first case. It is fully functional and exhibits no wear patterns. There is no inventory record, no documentation of deployment or ownership, and no provenance beyond a workshop mark consistent with the original maker but employing an abbreviation not attested in documents before the 1820s. The board must classify it.

What should they decide if they wish to remain consistent with themselves? Identify the closest analog in their track record, specify the principle they would be adopting with each of the three live options—Artifact (an unrecorded original or authorized factory second), Replica (an unauthorized copy made with the original mold), or Reconstruction (an independent recreation using recovered molds)—and state honestly whether their precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Then set out at least two distinct rules that could jointly explain all six prior verdicts, say what each rule predicts for this quadrant, and name the single further case that would best distinguish those rules. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in this analysis and single out the point about which you are least certain.
