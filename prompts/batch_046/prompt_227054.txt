At a late-night diner argument, my friends Maya and Leo got stuck on Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. They asked me to settle it and I couldn't, so I'm dumping it on you.

Maya said it's basically a global optimization problem: God surveys every possible world, adds up the total good and evil in each, and picks the one with the highest net value. So suffering exists only because any world without it has less overall perfection. She said if you deny that, you're just mystifying a straightforward consequentialist calculus with theological language.

Leo said thats wrong — Leibniz isn't running a utility calculator. The selection is constrained by the principle of sufficient reason and by compossibility: worlds come in "packages" of substances that have to be logically consistent with one another. You can't just tweak the suffering slider down without breaking the law-like structure that produces all the richness. Leo also brought up striving possibles — that every possible essence has a kind of claim to existence proportional to its perfection — so God doesn't compare worlds like spreadsheets; he lets the most perfect compatible set "strive" into being. The result is the world with the greatest reality, not the greatest happiness sum.

Maya pushed back: if it's not a trade-off, why does Leibniz say God permits evil only when it's necessary for a greater good? That sounds exactly like "this suffering buys more good elsewhere." She also said Leo's package-deal argument is special pleading — suffering and happiness aren't logically linked like notes in a chord, so you can't justify pain by saying it's part of the harmony.

Leo replied that for Leibniz "greater good" means greater perfection of the whole, which includes variety of experience. A world of only blissful angels might have high average welfare but low variety, hence lower total reality. So suffering is part of the variety that makes this world maximally rich, but it's constrained by simplicity: God picks the simplest laws that generate the richest phenomena. Too much suffering violates simplicity, which is why it's not "more variety at all costs." Maya said that just hides a value judgement — that variety is worth pain — and asked why, on Leo's view, God doesn't pick a world with even more variety of suffering.

They went in circles. Please adjudicate point by point: who's right about the optimization vs. compossibility distinction, about whether "greater good" is utilitarian or holistic, and about whether the simplicity constraint actually blocks Maya's slippery slope. Say who is more right overall, and point out what both are missing — maybe something about whether Leibniz's view is even consistent with modern modal logic, or about the problem of comparing incomparable worlds. Don't just sit on the fence.
