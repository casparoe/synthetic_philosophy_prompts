I am preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for advanced philosophy undergraduates who have encountered the standard paradox of self-deception—how one agent can simultaneously know and not know the same proposition—only in passing and without any formal background in clinical psychology or psychoanalytic theory. Rather than treating the problem through the familiar Freudian or Sartrean frames, I want to approach the possibility question through a structural idea I will call normative compartmentalization: self-deception becomes coherent when an agent does not split her beliefs into contradictory sets, but instead constructs a local, self-imposed permission structure that licenses selective inattention to specific counter-evidence while leaving intact the global belief that the evidence is epistemically relevant. The session must show why this is not mere weakness of will, not clinical delusion, and not a literal division of the mind.

Plan the explanatory arc as a teaching sequence, not as the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual piece must be in place before normative compartmentalization can be introduced, why the unitary-model paradox must be presented before the students see any proposed solution, and at what point the distinction between global belief and local licensing norms should enter. Second, identify the single carrying example that should carry the entire session: it must feature an agent who genuinely holds a global belief, actively constructs a local norm that permits her to bracket disconfirming data in a specific domain, and then acts on the bracketed view without experiencing herself as lying or suppressing. Specify exactly what this example needs to possess—what kind of evidence must be bracketed, what form the local norm takes, and why the agent retains access to the global belief—and what it must lack. In particular, explain why the conventional spousal-infidelity scenario should be avoided, and why examples involving clinical delusion or straightforward ignorance are also unsuitable as anchors.

Third, map the misconceptions the students will predictably form or bring, in the approximate order they will arise: likely the conflation of self-deception with mere weakness of will, the assumption that compartmentalization requires a literally divided or sub-personal mind, and the error of treating the local licensing norm as an explicit second-order belief that the agent can readily report. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence it should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose one concrete check—whether a brief written exercise or a targeted verbal question—that would reveal whether the students have grasped the structural point about normative compartmentalization rather than merely feeling the emotional pull of the example or repeating the vocabulary. Finally, state clearly what must be left out of this session and openly acknowledged as beyond its scope—for instance, empirical literature on motivated reasoning, clinical taxonomies of dissociation, or metaphysical theories of divided consciousness—and explain why excluding each protects the conceptual clarity of the argument you have built.
