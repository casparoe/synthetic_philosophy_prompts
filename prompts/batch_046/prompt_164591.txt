I have come to accept that the only variety of free will worth wanting is what I call narrative sovereignty: the capacity of an agent to author and reflectively stand behind the overarching explanatory narrative of her life, irrespective of whether her choices are causally determined or open in any metaphysically robust sense. Moral responsibility, on this view, attaches not to the causal origin of an action but to whether the agent can genuinely endorse the narrative arc that renders the action intelligible, even if that endorsement is itself determined. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want more than standard Frankfurt-style manipulation objections. Which specific intuitions—perhaps those triggered by engineered endorsement, by agents who construct coherent but false self-narratives, or by cases where narrative integration is achieved through cognitive distortion—would genuinely threaten it? Are there findings in narrative psychology or confabulation research that show reflective endorsement tracks nothing normatively significant? Which rival accounts—hierarchical coherence, reasons-responsiveness, or libertarian openness—does narrative sovereignty risk collapsing into if its qualifications are pushed too far? Rank the defeaters: which are fatal (rendering the view incoherent or empty), which merely awkward (requiring an ad hoc rescue), and which a committed defender could concede without damage? Finally, say whether the view is well insulated by its qualifications, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
