I'm finalizing the exam for my upper-level metaphysics seminar this term, and I'm frustrated that the standard essay prompts on composition always produce the same taxonomy—students name universalism, nihilism, and restricted composition without actually working through why any criterion of unity succeeds or fails. I want a 75-minute final-exam question, aimed at third- and fourth-year philosophy majors, that introduces an artificial but conceptually serious case they won't have seen in the standard literature: a "distributed functional system" (DFS) whose parts are spatially dispersed, never co-located, linked only by a continuous information-flow protocol, and collectively maintaining a stable causal profile over time despite complete turnover of individual nodes. The prompt should ask students to determine whether the DFS is one object, and—crucially—whether resolving that question forces them to choose among contact-based restriction, causal-unity restriction, or a functionally reinterpreted universalism, with explicit attention to overgeneration and undergeneration worries.

Please draft the exam question together with a model answer and a 25-mark marking scheme. The model answer should identify three specific conceptual moves a top response must make—something like diagnosing the failure of spatial-contact criteria, assessing whether causal integration can do the work without collapsing into universalism, and showing awareness of the "trap" answer that appeals to functional unity but misses the mereological crux. For the marking scheme, allocate marks to each move, specify the misconception that typically costs marks at each stage (e.g., conflating causal integration with spatiotemporal continuity, or treating functional unity as sufficient without addressing the special composition question itself), and flag one plausible-sounding but partially correct answer that should earn limited credit. The question must be answerable without searching for obscure texts or citing specific page numbers; students should reason from the conceptual framework of the course alone. Keep the framing formal and polished, as if addressed to a colleague helping design the exam.
