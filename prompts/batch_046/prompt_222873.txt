I'm part of an interdisciplinary seminar that meets to discuss metaphysical puzzles in the context of modern computational physics, and last week we got stuck on a variant of Black's sphere-world instantiated inside two parallel, bit-for-bit identical virtual simulations running in separate memory spaces. A colleague kept repeating the principle that 'indiscernibles are identical' as if it automatically dissolved the problem: if the two simulations contain spheres with exactly the same relational properties and no intrinsic differences, then, she argued, there are not really two worlds but one world redundantly represented. I want to understand what precise claims could be hiding inside that slogan, because her move felt like it was smuggling in assumptions about absolute space, about epistemic indistinguishability, about identity conditions for worlds rather than for objects, or about the semantics of counterfactual descriptions. Could you separate out the distinct theses—logical, epistemic, metaphysical, and perhaps semantic—that the phrase might be expressing, marking which ones are trivial tautologies, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which actually depend on rejecting the kind of relationalist framework that makes Black's example interesting in the first place? For each reading, please say whether it genuinely applies to symmetric digital replicas that share every relational feature but occupy different computational substrates, and whether it supports her conclusion that the parallel universes collapse into one. I need this as a disambiguated menu I can bring back to the seminar, with clear markers for where judgment about interpretation or values takes over from strict argument, and with explicit notice of which readings require an ontology of absolute positions that Black's scenario was designed to undermine.
