I'm taking a course in philosphy of law and my roomate just got into a car accident caused by a drunk driver, so suddenly the whole do people really deserve punishment thing isn't abstract anymore. I've been reading Dennett's idea that free will is like the center of gravity — useful fiction — and Caruso's argument that we should replace retributivism with a public-health quarantine model. But I think both miss something: that desert isn't a metaphysical property of agents but a relational status that only makes sense inside a practice of mutal accountability, kind of like how being in debt only exists inside an economy. So I hold that even if determinism is true and neuroscience explains every choice, retributive attitudes aren't errors to eliminate — they're the background conditions that make moral address possible at all.

What would actually count against this? I don't want a bibliography or citations, just the arguments, intuitions, or empirical findings that should move me. Like, is there a culture without mutal accountability practices? Would that show the relational view collapses? Or is there a formal result showing that any relational-desert view ends up being indistinguishable from Caruso's quarantine model once you cash out risk to the practice? Also, has this kind of position been qualified so much that it's unfalsifiable — and if so, is that a problem for a view that's supposed to be normative rather than empirical? I want the decisive stuff separated from the merely awkward, and I want to know what defenders of this kind of relational view themselves admit would hurt it.
