I have been observing a particular argumentative move in debates about whether machines or complex artifacts can possess genuine aboutness, and I would like to know whether philosophers have already given it a specific name. The pattern is this: first, one concedes that a particular artifact—for example, a printed sentence or the output of a language model—lacks intrinsic meaning and possesses only a borrowed kind of aboutness, derived entirely from the intentions of its designers or users. Next, one notices that this artifact is embedded in a larger loop in which its outputs become inputs to other artifacts, which in turn influence the environment or feed back into further processes, with no single human mind tracking each intermediate state. From this observation, one infers that at the collective level the aboutness is no longer borrowed but has become autonomous or original, because the chain of derivation is too long, too distributed, or too opaque to trace back to any mind. I have seen this move made with autonomous vehicles whose navigation systems interpret traffic signs that were designed by humans, and with large language models embedded in agentic loops that modify their own prompts; in both cases, defenders seem to treat the opacity and distribution of the derivation chain as if it were a kind of alchemical transformation that washes away the borrowed status of the original meanings. This feels different from a straightforward claim that the system is conscious, and also different from a legitimate argument about emergent properties in complex dynamics; it seems specifically to be an inference about the metaphysical status of intentionality under conditions of distributed opacity. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this move—the claim that derived aboutness, once sufficiently distributed and hidden from view, transforms into something indistinguishable from original aboutness—or is it an unrecognized variant of a familiar fallacy? If the literature lacks a precise term, could you propose one, together with a definition that clearly distinguishes it both from valid emergentist reasoning and from the simple mistake of attributing mental states to tools, and could you specify its boundaries—for instance, whether it requires that the derivation chain actually be untraceable, or whether mere practical inconvenience suffices?
