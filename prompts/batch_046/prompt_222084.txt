Please argue that the evidentialist prohibition associated with Clifford—according to which it is always wrong to form a belief without sufficient evidence—is self-defeating when applied to the very frameworks we use to assess what counts as sufficient evidence. Focus specifically on what we might call meta-evidential deadlock: a situation in which two competing epistemic frameworks, such as a permissive and a restrictive standard for inductive inference, are equally compatible with all available first-order data, so that adopting either framework requires a prior commitment that cannot itself be justified by the evidence internal to that framework. Show that in such deadlock cases, the demand for universal suspension actually presupposes a hidden commitment to one framework over the other—specifically, the framework that treats suspension as the default doxastic attitude—rather than being a neutral, framework-independent rule. In doing so, distinguish this objection from the more familiar Jamesian claim that some belief-options are live, forced, and momentous, and explain why the self-defeat problem arises even for framework-level commitments that are not momentous in that sense. Conclude by stating whether this argument, if successful, collapses the evidentialist view into a form of permissivism or merely establishes a narrower exception for commitments about how to interpret evidence. Throughout, be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable—identify the specific premise or distinction that, if rejected, would cause the argument to fail, and say what kind of consideration would lead you to abandon the conclusion.
