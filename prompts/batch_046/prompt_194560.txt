Trying to work out whether there's a principaled line between okay and not-okay generalizations about peoples. Here are my gut calls, and I want to know if one rule covers them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

1. A travel blogger writes "Italians are loud and affectionate" after a week in Rome. Feels like a lazy stereotype — not okay.

2. An economist publishes peer-reviewed data showing that citizens of Country A report higher stranger-trust than citizens of Country B, and uses that to explain welfare-state differences. I think that's okay, even though she talks about "the people of A".

3. A manager refuses to hire someone cause "people from Region C are unreliable workers," based only on three past employees. Obviously not okay.

4. A diplomat reads ethnographies describing a culture's emphasis on collective harmony, then assumes the specific negotiator across the table will avoid direct disagreement. I'm torn — it's grounded in real patterns, but applying it to one person feels like it erases them. Is this different from 2?

5. A friend argues that "national character" is incoherent cause nations aren't agents and can't have mental properties, so "the French are rational" is literally meaningless. I think he's wrong — groups can have traits — but I'm not sure why exactly.

There's also this idea I ran across, maybe statistical essentialism: treating a population average like a hidden essence shared by every member. That seems to capture what's wrong with 1 and 3, but does it also condemn 4? And does 2 avoid it cause it's probabilistic?

So what's the principle? Maybe statistical grounding plus non-determinism? Or descriptive vs predictive use? I feel 2 and 4 are close, but one feels okay and one risky. Can you lay out a principle (or a few candidates) that saves as many of my verdicts as possible, say which ones it forces me to drop, and predict for a case I haven't listed: a doctor using ancestry-based genetic risk averages for a specific patient?
