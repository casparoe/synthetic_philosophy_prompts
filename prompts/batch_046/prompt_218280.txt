I am a junior philosophy major serving as the student representative on our university’s Faculty Senate Appeals Panel, and I am currently enrolled in a seminar on speech acts and institutional language. The panel must circulate a short written opinion before Friday’s vote recommending a concrete course of action in the case below, with reasoning that the faculty can actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.

The case: During a formal faculty senate debate on research-integrity policy, Professor Elena Vasquez was accused—she insists falsely—of misrepresenting data in a recent grant proposal. In direct response, she said: “Given what has just been said about my integrity, I am finished here; I vacate my chair, effective immediately.” The Provost, present in the chamber, treated this as a performative resignation, initiated replacement hiring, and accepted her office keys. Professor Vasquez now appeals, arguing that her utterance was an emotional outburst lacking serious performative intention, that she never filed the written notice required by the faculty handbook, and that treating her words as binding constitutes an institutional misfire—an abuse of conventional procedure—because the appropriate mental conditions were absent. The administration responds that the statement occurred in a recognized institutional setting, was witnessed by the authority competent to receive it, employed explicit conventional wording (“I vacate”), and satisfied audience-uptake conditions; they further warn that allowing a speaker to retroactively void an institutional utterance by pleading emotional distress would destabilize every formal declaration made under pressure.

The panel must recommend whether to uphold the administration’s interpretation, reverse it entirely, or approve it with conditions—such as imposing a brief cooling-off period followed by a required written confirmation or formal revocation process—and must specify a clear time frame for whatever action it proposes. In drafting the memo, please apply the distinctions between procedural failure (misfire), intentional absence (abuse), and audience uptake, but do not treat any one philosophical account of these conditions as settled consensus where scholars genuinely disagree. Specifically, separate what is widely accepted in speech-act theory—for example, that performatives depend on both conventional procedures and some form of speaker commitment, and that institutional context shapes but does not wholly determine validity—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether a speaker’s subsequent denial of intent can retroactively nullify an utterance that already satisfied procedural and uptake conditions, and exactly how much weight subjective mental states should carry against witnessed institutional form. Please do not present a contested view as if it were the unanimous position of those who work on these issues. The memo should engage Professor Vasquez’s concern seriously rather than merely restating administrative authority, and it should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the panel needs actionable reasoning rather than scholarly apparatus.
