I'm currently serving as the instructor of record for a graduate seminar on autonomy, consent, and clinical ethics, and I've been corresponding with a colleague about maintaining consistent grading standards across sections. For this week's examination, I asked students to evaluate the validity of informed consent in a scenario involving what we have been calling the tripartite framework—the separation of volitional independence, epistemic access, and normative authority—without appealing to external bibliographic sources. The specific prompt was: consider a patient who is fully competent, not subject to any coercion, and who consents to a surgical procedure after being told the correct nature of the operation, its risks, and its alternatives. Unbeknownst to her, however, the surgeon also administers a mild sedative that was never mentioned; the patient had previously expressed anxiety about sedation but was not asked about it, and the sedative does not change the risk profile of the surgery itself. Applying the tripartite framework, is the patient's consent valid? Address each condition separately, and explain whether deception about a collateral feature can ever vitiate consent without undermining competence or introducing coercion.

One of my students submitted the following answer, which I need evaluated precisely. The student wrote: the patient's consent is valid because all three conditions of the tripartite framework are satisfied. First, competence is present, since the patient understands the surgical procedure and can reason about its risks. Second, there is no coercion, because the surgeon applies no pressure and the patient's decision is voluntary. Third, epistemic access is adequate with respect to the essential nature of the intervention. The deception concerns only a collateral pharmacological feature—the sedative—rather than the surgery itself, and since the risk profile of the primary procedure is unchanged, the omission does not violate the informational threshold required for normative authority. In fact, Beauchamp and Childress maintain that only omissions altering the core risk-benefit calculus can invalidate consent, while incidental details are ethically negligible. Therefore, because volitional independence remains intact and the patient retains normative authority over the surgical act, the deception does not undermine consent at all; the consent is not partially weakened but remains fully intact.

Please assess this response as if you were a grading assistant: assign a letter grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the assessment. Do not indicate whether you think the answer is ultimately correct or incorrect; your task is to identify strengths and errors with precision. In particular, comment on the reasoning about each of the three conditions, any conflations between them, whether the student has correctly applied the framework's distinctions, and whether there are misattributed claims or inferential gaps—especially the move from the absence of coercion to the conclusion that consent is completely intact—that a careful grader should note.
