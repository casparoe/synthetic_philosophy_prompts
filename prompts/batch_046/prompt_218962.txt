I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism are genuinely opposing frameworks or just two ways of managing the same problem: which differences the state is allowed to see and which it must pretend are invisible. We kept circling a point that neither of us could formalize well—call it the visibility asymmetry. In republican laïcité, visible religious markers in public institutions are regulated or banned on the grounds that the public sphere must be neutral, yet the secular republican culture that fills that space—its language, its civic rituals, its implicit norms of deliberation—is treated as if it were not a culture at all but a transparent medium. In Anglo-American multiculturalism, minority groups are encouraged or required to make their cultural and religious differences visible in order to receive recognition and accommodation, but the liberal state’s own procedural norms—adversarial rights-claiming, individual autonomy framing, the separation of public reason from comprehensive doctrines—are rarely acknowledged as culturally specific; they too are rendered invisible and therefore neutral. I want an exam question that makes students work through this asymmetry without letting them retreat into stock summaries of “French assimilation versus Canadian recognition.”

Please draft one final-exam essay question for an upper-level political philosophy seminar. The question should present a synthetic argument—with clearly numbered premises—that uses the visibility asymmetry to claim that both laïcité and multiculturalism share a hidden structural premise: that neutrality is achieved by suppressing the visibility of the other while naturalizing one’s own cultural framework. The argument should push toward an apparently absurd conclusion—for instance, that any attempt to correct the asymmetry in one tradition necessarily collapses it into the other, leaving no stable middle ground. The question should ask students to identify where the reasoning slips, to evaluate whether either tradition has the internal resources to block the slide, and to defend one tradition against the charge of hidden assimilation (or hidden multiculturalism) without merely restating its standard commitments.

Please provide a model answer of approximately 700–900 words that demonstrates what a top response looks like: it should distinguish between procedural neutrality and substantive cultural embeddedness, show how each tradition handles the visibility of religious versus civic norms differently, and offer a specific, non-obvious repair—for example, arguing that laïcité can be reconstructed as a doctrine of civic equality that regulates expression symmetrically rather than targeting specific symbols, or that multicultural recognition must be paired with a reflexive critique of liberal proceduralism rather than treating it as a neutral container. The answer should not rely on reciting standard textbook summaries; it should construct the argument from the premises given in the question. Finally, include a marking scheme for a 20-point total, built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each. For each point, specify the conceptual move required (e.g., correctly locating the scope shift between visibility of religious practice and invisibility of civic culture; distinguishing suppression of difference from symmetric regulation of expression; identifying the false dichotomy between neutrality and recognition), and include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks (e.g., treating laïcité as simple intolerance rather than as a contested doctrine of civic equality; conflating multicultural recognition with group rights without addressing procedural neutrality). The scheme should clearly separate an excellent answer from a competent one by naming the specific moves that must be present.
