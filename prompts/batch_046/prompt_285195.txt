I am at work on a novel whose protagonist habitually cites her childhood deprivation to excuse present cowardice, and a colleague in my writing group warned me that I am committing what she called 'Sartrean bad faith' on behalf of the character. She summarized Sartre's doctrine as follows. Human beings, she said, possess a radical freedom that is absolute and unconditioned; we are never genuinely constrained by our past, our temperament, or our social situation. Bad faith is therefore nothing more than self-deception about this total liberty—pretending that one's psychology or biography makes one act in a particular way. She went further: Sartre treats even our emotions as direct volitional choices, so that to feel grief or resentment is literally to decide upon them. From this she drew a conclusion about fiction: to endow a character with a deterministic backstory that 'explains' present behavior is itself an exercise in bad faith, because it treats the character as pure facticity rather than as self-creating transcendence. Authentic narrative, she insisted, should therefore present characters without causal biography, allowing them to generate themselves ex nihilo in each scene. This strikes me as suspiciously neat, and I recall that Sartre spoke of something called facticity that freedom must always confront rather than annihilate. Is her summary a fair rendering? I would like an accuracy audit: which parts are faithful to Sartre's argument, which are familiar popularizations that subtly misrepresent it, and what Sartre actually maintained about emotional responsibility versus direct emotional choice. Most importantly, I want to know whether her prescription for biography-less fiction actually follows from his philosophy, or whether it depends on the very distortions embedded in the summary—particularly the erasure of situated freedom and the conflation of transcendence with an absence of past.
