Hey, I'm in a political philosphy class and we keep talking about public reason, but there's this pattern I see in real-world debates that I don't have a word for. So imagine a religious group wants some policy P—say, banning a practice or funding something—and they know they can't just cite scripture in a liberal democracy. So they build a whole secular case with data and rights-language and everything. Then secular critics come along and knock down the data part, or show the rights-language doesn't actually apply, and you'd think the position would weaken. But instead, the group just swaps in new secular reasons, R2, R3, R4, like they're replacing batteries in a toy that never dies. The policy never moves an inch.

Meanwhile, in private or in church settings, they straight up admit the theological reason is the real one, and all this other stuff is just "how we talk to outsiders." So it's not really Rawlsian public reason, because Rawls seems to want people to actually be moved by shared reasons, not just using them as a disposable shell. Is there an established concept for this? I know about "translation" but that feels too generous—this is more like a stratgic shell-game where the secular framing is infinitely replacable. Does it relate to "reasonable pluralism," or is it just a failure mode of trying to meet it? If there's no exact term, what should I call it—maybe "epicyclic justification"? And how exactly is it different from ordinary hypocrisy or strategic framing? I'd like an answer in American English, please—no need for a bibliography, I just want to understand the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
