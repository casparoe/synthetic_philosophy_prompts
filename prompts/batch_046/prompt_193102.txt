As a physicist who spent four decades in condensed-matter research and has only recently turned to philosophy with sustained attention, I have become preoccupied with negative utilitarianism—specifically its lexical-threshold formulation and the deeper question of whether pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable, or whether they stand to one another as temperature does to pressure: measurable each in its own dimension, but not reducible to a single scalar. I have arrived at a cluster of verdicts on concrete cases, yet I doubt they can all be preserved under one precise principle. I ask you to examine them, propose the principle or small family of principles that best captures the pattern, indicate what each predicts about unconsidered cases, and tell me honestly which verdict I must surrender.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A medical program that eliminates severe chronic suffering for a small cohort ought to be adopted even if it thereby prevents the existence of a far larger cohort who would have lived lives of robust pleasure; suffering-reduction dominates in a manner that pleasure-maximization does not. (2) Once suffering is driven to zero, further increments of pleasure do not appear to improve the world in the same evaluative currency; I am inclined to regard the two as incommensurable dimensions, so that a world with zero suffering and modest pleasure is not clearly inferior to one with zero suffering and vast pleasure—merely different in kind. (3) Whenever suffering is present above a minimal threshold, no quantity of pleasure elsewhere can compensate; there is a lexical barrier. (4) Reducing suffering from extreme to moderate for a given number of people is more morally urgent than increasing pleasure from moderate to high for the same number, even if the aggregate pleasure gain exceeds the suffering reduction by orders of magnitude. (5) I reject the aggregation of suffering and pleasure into a single net score; the notion of "utility" as a scalar strikes me as an unexamined borrowing from thermodynamic state functions that lacks philosophical justification when applied to phenomenological states.

Can a single principle—perhaps lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism conjoined with dimensional incommensurability—accommodate all five verdicts? Or do they force me to abandon either the incommensurability claim or the lexical-dominance claim? Please state the candidate principle or principles with precision, identify the case that motivates each clause, state the verdict each principle delivers on a new case (for example, a choice between reducing suffering just above the threshold versus generating massive pleasure for many, or between two worlds both free of suffering but differing greatly in the diversity and intensity of pleasure), and be explicit about which of my five verdicts cannot be retained without contradiction. In your response, please separate what is relatively settled in the philosophical literature on these topics from what remains genuinely contested among knowledgeable specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as the exact formulation of lexical priority, the viability of dimensional analogies, or the status of scalar utility—as though it were the consensus position.
