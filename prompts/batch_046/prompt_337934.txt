Since I retired from running a marine ecology lab about three years ago, I've been sitting in on philosophy lectures at the local university mostly because I miss having complicated arguments over bad coffee. Last week a grad student summarized the Mencius vs. Xunzi debate on human nature for me, and she leaned hard on biological metaphors that felt familiar but probably out of place. She said: "Mencius treats human nature like a reaction norm with cooperative defaults—same underlying genotype, but you need teh right environment for the moral sprouts to fully express. Xunzi treats it more like an invasive species with high reproductive success but zero self-regulation; without artificial barriers like ritual and law it just overruns the garden. So Mencius is basically a developmental psychologist and Xunzi is an ecosystem engineer, which is why modern behavioral ecology sides with Xunzi even though Mencius sounds nicer."

I'm suspicious now, but not enough to pinpoint where she's smoothing over the classics. Is this a fair summary of what they actually argue, or is she just dressing up the standard classroom story in evolutionary jargon? Specifically, does Mencius really think environment only "expresses" innate sprouts, or does he believe culture actively shapes and extends them? Does Xunzi deny any innate cooperative tendency, or just claim those tendencies need institutional scaffolding? And if you strip away the reaction-norm framing, does her claim that modern science supports Xunzi still hold up? I'd appreciate going through her summary point by point—what's accurate to the texts, what's a common simplification, and what's pure projection from biology onto philosophy.
