I've come round to a slighlty odd reading of Kant on animals. Most people say he's clear: indirect duties only, cos animals aren't rational agents. But I think there's a hidden thread where he treats animal suffering as morally relevant not just cos cruelty to them corrupts our character, but cos the capacity for suffering creates a kind of analogical moral claim that works independantly of rational autonomy. So my position is that Kant's actual view is closer to a two-tier system: direct duties to rational beings, and quasi-direct analogical duties to sentient non-rational beings, grounded in shared sensibility rather than shared reason. I want to know what would actually count against this. Not just the standard line that he says they're things — I know he says that. I want the deeper defeaters: which arguments in his ethical writings actually block the analogical move, whether there's something in his anthropology that undermines shared sensibility, and whether this reading makes him inconsistent with his own claim that dignity is unconditional. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether a defender of this view could just absorb the damage by qualifying the position, or whether that qualification makes the view unfalsifable in a bad way. Answer in British English please — and don't bother with a reading list, just the arguments.
