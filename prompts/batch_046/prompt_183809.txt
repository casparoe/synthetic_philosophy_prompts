I host a philosophy podcast focused on ethics and moral psychology, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who defends a position she terms the "refractive" account of moral worth. On her view, an action possesses full moral worth only when the agent acts for the right first-order reasons, and when those reasons are organized by a second-order recognition that competing moral considerations could in principle have overridden them but did not—what she calls "refractive" motivational structure. She presents this as intermediate between Kantian accounts that require explicit universalizability testing and Humean or virtue-ethical accounts that allow worthy action to flow from stable sentiment or habituated character alone. Draft eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking her to restate the thesis. I want at least one question on each of the following: (a) why first-order right motivation is insufficient for full moral worth without this second-order refractive element; (b) how the view treats moral-luck cases in which an agent's reasoning was sound but an unforeseeable external factor nearly caused the wrong action; (c) whether the cognitive demands of second-order tracking make moral worth an achievement available only to philosophically reflective agents; and (d) how she distinguishes her position from standard virtue-ethical accounts that appeal to implicit practical wisdom. For each question, include a follow-up tailored to the reply she is most likely to offer—particularly if she appeals to implicit or non-conscious second-order tracking, or if she concedes that habituated agents can possess refractive structure without explicit deliberation.
