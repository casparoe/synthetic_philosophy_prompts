I'm taking a course on gender and social policy and we keep hitting this weird result — in countries that rank highest on gender equality (the Scandinavian ones especially) occupational segregation by gender is actually stronger, not weaker. People call it the gender-equality paradox. In our discussion group, someone kept reaching for this river-and-dam anology: social norms and institutional barriers are like dams holding back natural preferences, and once egalitarian policy blows them up, the water just flows back into old channels. So the high segregation we see is nature revealing itself without artificial blocks, which they use to argue either that culture was never the main driver or that justice is satisfied as long as people freely choose different paths.

Can you audit how far that anology actually holds? Give me the correspondence table — what's the river, the dams, the riverbed, the engineer, the flow — mapped onto actual mechanisms of career choice. Then show me where it breaks: in particular, deal with the possibility that preferences aren't fixed exogenous water but are shaped by the very landscape they move through, like if the visibility of gendered career paths changes what people imagine for themselves — that's not an external dam, it's part of the water chemisty. What does the river image quietly import about human nature being stable and pre-social that the target case might lack? And once those disanologies are on the table, does the conclusion that 'this proves nature dominates culture' or 'justice doesn't require equal outcomes' survive? If the river picture is carrying too much weight, what's a better analog for how choice architectures work when formal constraints relax but social visibility doesn't?
