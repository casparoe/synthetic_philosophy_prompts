I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory five years ago, has been working through the philosophy of history in earnest, and I have been invited to propose the toast at the retirement dinner of Dr Eleanor Vance, a historian of science with whom I shared a research centre for nearly two decades. The room will be divided: on one side sit historians and philosophers of science who will recognise every echo of Ranke’s aspiration to show how it actually was and of the sceptical counter-current running through E. H. Carr and Hayden White on narrative emplotment and the constructedness of historical accounts; on the other sit family members, former technicians, and university administrators who will not. I need the toast to navigate that divide without patronising either group, so the philosophical tension must do genuine structural work rather than serve as decoration.

The speech must avoid the naive claim that Eleanor simply recovered and recorded the past as it happened, but it must equally avoid the stronger sceptical conclusion that her narratives are therefore arbitrary constructions with no real purchase on past events. Every tribute to her rigour should be qualified by an acknowledgement that historical evidence is always selected, framed, and interpreted; every acknowledgement of framing should stop well short of suggesting that her conclusions are therefore fictional. What I can and cannot say about her career—what counts as praise, what counts as humility, and what would sound false—should be shaped by this tension throughout.

To hold the balance without collapsing history into physics or physics into history, I want the toast to turn on the analogy of scientific instrumentation: a measurement is never theory-neutral, the apparatus shapes what counts as signal, and yet we do not dismiss the result as mere invention. The speech should draw out precisely where that analogy holds and where it breaks down when applied to archives rather than laboratories, using it to illuminate Eleanor’s craft rather than to lecture on epistemology. Please write the toast in British English, in no more than 450 words—approximately four minutes spoken—so that both halves of the room leave feeling they have heard something accurate about her work rather than either a condescending simplification or an obscure seminar paper.
