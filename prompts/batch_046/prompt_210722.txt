In a seminar on self-knowledge and transparency, a fellow student kept invoking the Moore-paradoxical form ‘it is raining, but I do not believe it’ as though it were a single, knock-down objection to first-person authority—treating it as proof that we cannot genuinely be mistaken about our own beliefs. I think the example is being made to do too much work, but I need to see exactly where the force lies. Could you lay out the menu of precise claims this utterance might be expressing, separating at least: a logical contradiction in the content asserted; a pragmatic contradiction in the speech act itself; a failure of first-person transparency (the idea that believing p normally entails being able to avow p without observation); a mismatch between an occurrent judgment and a standing dispositional belief; and a merely conversational or Gricean impropriety? For each reading, specify what kind of ‘belief’ is in play—binary, graded, implicitly held, or accepted for practical purposes—say whether the paradox survives symmetrically in the third-person analog (‘She believes it is raining, but she does not believe it’), and indicate whether substituting weaker attitudes—suspicion, partial confidence, or tentative acceptance—dissolves the tension or merely relocates it. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want a conceptual taxonomy that shows which of these readings actually threatens first-person authority, which are just linguistic curiosities, and which ones my interlocutor was likely smuggling in when he used the example to end our discussion.
