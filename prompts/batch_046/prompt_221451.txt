I cover long-range forecasting and intergenerational policy for a science magazine, and I need a reusable editorial procedure for evaluating whether a claim, study, or proposal discounts distant-future outcomes in a philosophically defensible way, or whether it relies on pure time preference—treating temporal distance itself as a reason to diminish value or credibility, apart from risk, epistemic decay, or opportunity cost. The procedure should be something I can run in about fifteen minutes on a draft paragraph or a policy brief: an ordered sequence of questions, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, explicit stop points where I should reject the framing or flag it for revision, and clear markers where the judgment is irreducibly normative rather than factual. It must distinguish ethical weighting (future people matter less) from epistemic weighting (predictions about 2070 are less credible), reference exponential and hyperbolic discount curves only insofar as they illuminate that distinction, and avoid collapsing all long-horizon reasoning into a single rate. Apply the procedure to three cases: a carbon-cost analysis that applies a uniform 3 percent annual discount to climate damages in 2100; a longevity trial that downweights forty-year health outcomes relative to five-year outcomes solely because of the interval; and a proposal to defer a costly vaccination program for a disease that will not become endemic for two generations, where deferral is justified by saying that those people do not yet exist to have claims on present budgets. In at least one case, the procedure should yield a verdict I will find uncomfortable—specifically, one where a standard economic practice survives the check despite feeling intuitively wrong. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do, including whether it can resolve the non-identity problem embedded in the vaccination case and where an editor must simply choose a frame rather than discover an error.
