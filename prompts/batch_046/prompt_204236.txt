Consider Alfonso Cuarón's 2006 film Children of Men in light of Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is bad largely for reasons other than the deaths it entails—specifically, the "afterlife conjecture" that the value of present activities depends on the expectation that human life will continue after one's own death. The film depicts a world in which global infertility has eliminated births for nearly eighteen years, producing not merely grief but a pervasive social collapse: bureaucratic fatalism, state violence, ritual suicide, and millenarian cults. When a pregnant refugee appears, her survival becomes the object of desperate, violent hope. How far does this narrative support Scheffler's claim? One might read Theo's transformation from passive civil servant to sacrificial protector as a rediscovery of present meaning through attachment to a future he will not inhabit—precisely the deference to objective continuity Scheffler describes. Yet the film also surrounds the birth with relentless institutional corruption and war, and offers no assurance that the child will inherit anything recognizably human. To complicate the reading, introduce a distinction rarely made in discussions of Scheffler: between "thin" afterlife, in which biological reproduction continues, and "thick" afterlife, in which the particular values, practices, and projects that make present lives worth living are transmitted. Does Children of Men suggest that only thick afterlife can sustain moral and psychological order, and if so, does that strengthen Scheffler's case—by showing that bare continuation is insufficient—or weaken it—by implying that the relevant value depends on contingent social conditions rather than on the bare fact of human survival? Address specific elements of the film—the long-take siege sequences that collapse any distinction between protection and destruction, the Fishes' religious veneration of the child as miracle rather than as future citizen, and the absence of any institutional framework beyond militarized containment—without reducing them to symbolic illustrations. Conclude by stating what the film reveals about the phenomenology of a world without descendants that philosophical argument about extinction's badness tends to miss.
