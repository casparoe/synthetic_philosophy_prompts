Keynes's vision of the good life—sketched in his 1930 essay on post-scarcity abundance—rests on a tension he never fully resolved. He predicted that rising productivity would eventually reduce necessary labor to about fifteen hours per week, freeing people for culture, friendship, and aesthetic experience; yet he also feared that populations habituated to economic striving would lack the psychological resources to use unstructured leisure well, and might simply redirect competitive energies into status rivalry or passive consumption. At the same time, his own Bloomsbury milieu embodied an elitist, cultivated conception of leisure that sat uneasily with his democratic macroeconomic optimism.

Assume you are advising a small interdisciplinary group—philosophers, economists, and cultural historians—with three years of funding and a mandate to produce work that could actually change policy design or personal life-planning. Do not summarize Keynes's view. Instead, rank the open conceptual questions and applied lines of work that emerge from this specific tension—between the economic conditions that make leisure possible and the psychological or cultural capacities required to live it well. Which three problems should absorb the bulk of the group's effort, and in what order?

For each, defend its placement by addressing: (1) whether it is upstream of the others—would resolving it dissolve or restructure the remaining questions; (2) how tractable it is for philosophers and theorists without large-scale empirical infrastructure; and (3) what result or development over the next few years would cause you to reorder the ranking. Be explicit about your criteria—whether importance for human flourishing, tractability, neglectedness in current discourse, or dependency on answers elsewhere—and defend any trade-offs among them.

In particular, weigh these three candidates:
- the normative problem of what makes leisure genuinely good rather than merely empty, including whether Keynes implicitly relied on an Aristotelian, hedonistic, or aesthetic-elitist theory of well-being that he never articulated;
- the institutional problem of whether macroeconomic mechanisms meant to reduce labor—automation subsidies, universal basic income, work-sharing mandates—can be structured so they do not simultaneously erode the social practices and virtues that make leisure meaningful;
- the predictive or diagnostic problem of whether Keynes's psychological worry that the love of money might be replaced by status competition in positional goods is conceptually coherent and empirically tractable, or whether it undermines his broader optimism.

Conclude by stating what you would explicitly deprioritize and why—whether because it is already well-covered, because its answer would change little downstream, or because it requires empirical tools the group lacks—and identify the kind of argument or evidence that would be decisive in revising your top priority.
