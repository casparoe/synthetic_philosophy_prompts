I have just come away from a long and circular argument with a friend about suffering-focused ethics. I was defending the view that suffering-reduction should take structural priority over happiness-promotion in institutional design, and she kept interpreting this either as a psychological bias toward pessimism or as a disguised commitment to anti-natalism and the abolition of wild nature. I want to plan a single, self-contained explanatory session for a small reading group of graduate students in ecology—intelligent, curious, but with no training in moral philosophy—on the specific conceptual asymmetry between suffering and happiness when resources are allocated across different time-horizons, using what I think of as a dual-track moral budget framework in which suffering-reduction operates with near-zero temporal discounting while happiness-promotion is subject to standard discounting.

Do not explain the idea to me; plan the explanatory arc. First, what conceptual component must be in place before the asymmetry can even be heard as a claim about institutional design rather than an emotional preference, and in what order should the remaining pieces be introduced so that each depends on the previous? Second, identify the single carrying example or decision case that should do the work—specify exactly what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack, and explain why a simpler framing like direct bodily harm versus direct pleasure would fail to preserve the distinction I am trying to teach. Third, map the misconceptions: I anticipate they will conflate the view with blanket pessimism, with the denial that happiness has value, with anti-natalism about future generations, and with an obligation to eliminate all suffering regardless of cost—state precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and neutralized, and how. Fourth, provide one precise check-for-understanding question that I can ask at the end, one that would distinguish someone who has grasped the structural asymmetry from someone who has merely become uncomfortable with suffering. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level—whether the formal axiological literature, the strong versus weak distinction in negative utilitarianism, the wild-animal intervention debate, or something else—and justify why leaving it out protects the core idea rather than simplifying it away.
