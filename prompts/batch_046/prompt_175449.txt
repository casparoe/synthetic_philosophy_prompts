Consider Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, especially his distinction between the hard core of a programme and its protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, together with his claim that a programme is progressive when theoretical growth anticipates empirical discoveries rather than accommodating failures after the fact. In light of the replication crisis in social and biomedical science, and of the growing use of high-dimensional predictive models that generate hypotheses faster than independent tests can be conducted, three unresolved conceptual problems about this framework seem especially pressing. First, whether the progressive-versus-degenerating distinction can be made intersubjective enough to guide real-time funding or editorial policy. Second, whether the hard-core and protective-belt architecture remains coherent when a "theory" is an opaque model whose individual parameters are not interpretable. Third, whether Lakatos's prohibition on ad hoc adjustments survives in domains where data is cheap and post-hoc model revision is technically trivial. If you were advising a philosophy-of-science research group with three years, no laboratory, and no mandate to build empirical systems, which of these three lines should they treat as upstream of the others? Defend your ordering: for each line, specify what other questions depend on its resolution, how tractable it is for philosophers without machine-learning engineering expertise, and what kind of institutional or conceptual development over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking. I am less interested in a survey of Lakatos scholarship than in a resource-sensitive agenda with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, and dependency—made clear.
