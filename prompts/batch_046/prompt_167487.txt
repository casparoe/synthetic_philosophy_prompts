Construct a decision tree for rational response to peer disagreement in philosophy, designed for a graduate seminar in social epistemology. Do not organize by author or school; build the tree from four sequential diagnostic questions that a respondent can answer without knowing the philosophical labels:

1. Does peerhood require equality in evidential access, or can it hold between reasoners with equal competence but unequal access (e.g., one peer has seen a hidden premise)?
2. Given disagreement about a claim P, should belief revision always move toward the peer's position (uniform conciliation), or may it depend on whether P lies in a domain with 'structural stability'—where beliefs resist revision because they are embedded in a wider framework?
3. Is steadfastness rational only when the steadfast party can identify a specific reasoning asymmetry (a visible error in the peer's inference), or can it be justified by bare differential confidence?
4. If peers disagree about whether conciliationism or steadfastness itself is the correct norm, does that meta-disagreement automatically trigger conciliation about the norm?

Label each branch by the claim that splits it. At each leaf, state a position name (use an invented descriptive label if no standard name exists) and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Identify any logical combination of answers that is unoccupied, and briefly say whether it is likely incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree fails to separate. Separate settled claims about peer disagreement from genuinely contested ones, and do not present a contested view as consensus. Conclude with a one-sentence summary of the tree's central axis of division on its own line beginning 'Verdict:'.
