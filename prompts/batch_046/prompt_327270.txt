I have been discussing with colleagues in urban ethics whether the debate over renaming streets and removing statues is best understood not as a conflict over historical honor but as a problem about the pragmatic function of public names as coordinate systems for collective moral orientation. To sharpen the issue, I want to introduce a distinction I am calling indexical monumentality: the view that commemorative markers—street names, statues, plaques—function analogously to linguistic indexicals, anchoring a community's shared reference frame not merely by representing a figure but by fixing a spatiotemporal and normative origin point against which present judgments are calibrated. Consider a concrete case: a central boulevard bears the name of a colonial administrator whose policies included forced displacement, and activists propose replacing it with the name of an indigenous leader who resisted those policies, while preservationists counter that retaining the original name keeps the historical violence visible as a kind of open wound rather than sanitizing it away.

Please describe the most important implications of adopting the indexical-monumentality framework for evaluating such renaming campaigns. In particular, address whether treating a name as an indexical anchor implies that its retention serves an irreplaceable critical or pedagogical function—by maintaining the very coordinate that makes present injustice legible—or whether replacement can itself re-anchor the moral framework without loss. You should also consider what this framing implies for the ethics of public space more broadly: if commemorative objects are indexical rather than purely representational, does removing them amount to erasing a reference point, or to correcting a misaligned coordinate system? I am looking for an analysis that takes the conceptual framing seriously rather than simply rehearsing standard arguments for or against removal.
