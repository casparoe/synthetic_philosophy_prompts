I'm preparing a discussion on Pascal's treatment of justice and force for a seminar on early modern political thought, and I'm trying to work out a conceptual knot that standard summaries tend to gloss over. Most accounts rehearse the familiar idea from the Pensées that justice separated from force is powerless, while force separated from justice is tyrannical, and that human societies therefore confer the appearance of justice upon whatever possesses force, since they cannot confer force upon what is truly just. But I want to press further into the conceptual architecture behind this claim. Specifically, I am trying to determine whether Pascal is advancing a descriptive sociological thesis about how human institutions actually stabilize authority, or whether he is making a stronger conceptual claim about the conditions under which justice can possess normative bindingness at all.

To sharpen the issue, I have been using a distinction—borrowed more from contemporary philosophy of law than from Pascal scholarship—between deontic authority (the normative right to command obedience) and executive capacity (the causal ability to secure compliance). The conceptual problem I keep running into is this: if justice genuinely lacks executive capacity without the addition of force, does it also lack deontic authority in that separation, or does it retain authority while merely failing to be efficacious? Pascal's language sometimes suggests the latter—justice remains right even when impotent—but his broader argument about tyranny seems to imply that an order lacking executive capacity is not merely ineffective but conceptually unstable, since anyone who attempts to impose justice without force becomes a tyrant in trying to supply the missing capacity. Meanwhile, Pascal introduces the order of charity as a realm that apparently transcends the logic of force altogether. This raises a second, related question: does charity operate with its own non-coercive form of executive capacity—perhaps through internal persuasion or spiritual transformation—or does it remain purely aspirational, and if so, does that not reproduce the very impotence that makes justice dependent on force?

Please explain this conceptual tension intuitively but precisely. How should we understand the relationship between authority and efficacy in Pascal's framework, and does the order of charity escape the logic he applies to justice, or does it reveal a hidden limitation in his claim that effective order always requires the conjunction of right and might?
