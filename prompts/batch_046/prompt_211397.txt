I've been trying to draft an essay for my philosphy of buddhism seminar on Nāgārjuna's tetralemma, and I think I've landed on a take that's a bit different from the standard "middle way" reading you see everywhere. My basic claim is that the four corners—exists, doesn't exist, both, neither—aren't just four ontological positions to be rejected one by one. Instead, they're four different ways of applying a boundary-predicate to something that is purely relational, and the point of emptiness isn't to pick the "right" negation but to show that the predicate svabhāva never gets a stable foothold in the first place.

I was comparing this to how Abhidharma philosophers talk about dharmas having dravyasat—substantial existence—versus just being dependantly originated. If Nāgārjuna is attacking the idea that anything has inherent nature, then denying "it exists" in corner one and "it doesn't exist" in corner two both miss the point, because they're still treating existence as a property that could be present or absent. So I argued that the tetralemma works more like a bug report for metaphysical predication: each corner tries to patch the concept of svabhāva onto dependently arisen things, and each patch fails for the same structural reason—there's no independant substrate to bear the property. I'm also toying with framing this as pragmatics rather than ontology. Like, maybe Nāgārjuna isn't telling us what "is" or "isn't" in some deep metaphysical sense, but showing that assertions of inherent existence always overreach their conditions of applicability. I don't want to turn him into a modern deflationist, but I wonder if this makes the argument less about mystical negation and more about the conditions under which we can stably apply predicates.

Can you comment on whether this is accurate to the Madhyamaka texts, or if I'm conflating Nāgārjuna with later Prāsaṅgika interpreters? Also, does the "bug report / pragmatics" framing actually illuminate anything, or does it just dress up the usual emptiness talk in tech language? I'd particulaly like feedback on the part about Abhidharma dravyasat—am I getting that contrast right, or am I misrepresenting the Abhidharma position? No need for a bibliography, just your take on whether the argument holds together.
