Write an essay on whether Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist denial of necessary causal connections can be repurposed to resolve the causal exclusion problem in philosophy of mind. First, present the exclusion problem in its standard form: if every mental event has a sufficient physical cause, and physical causes are not themselves caused by mental properties, then mental properties appear causally redundant. Next, develop the occasionalist challenge: if physical events possess no intrinsic causal powers and are merely the occasions for direct production of effects by a non-physical cause, then physical causation is not genuinely competing with mental causation—there is no overdetermination because physics was never causally efficacious to begin with. Then consider the reply that this rescue comes at the cost of universalizing occasionalism: mental states, too, would be mere occasions rather than genuine causes, collapsing psychological explanation into the same emptiness alleged of physical explanation. Weigh whether a restricted occasionalism—denying causal power only to physical properties while preserving it for mental ones—is metaphysically coherent or ad hoc. Reach a definite verdict: does occasionalism dissolve the exclusion problem, extend it to mental causation, or misdiagnose the problem by treating causation as a single homogeneous relation? Justify your conclusion by examining whether the exclusion problem requires competing sufficient causes within a shared causal order, or whether it can be reframed once that shared order is denied.
