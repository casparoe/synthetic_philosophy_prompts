hey, I've been chewing on Scanlon's contractulism and I think I've talked myself into a reductio, but I can't see where the wheels come off.

(1) Contractualism says an act is wrong if it's allowed by a principle that some affectd party could reasonably reject.

(2) Scanlon denies we can just aggregate tiny benefits across different people to outweigh one big burden — numbers don't "count" in that inter-personal way.

(3) So saving a million people from blindness by blinding one person is out: the victim has a complaint that can't be drowned out by a million separate little complaints.

(4) But now apply the same logic to anything collective. A tax takes $1 from each of a million citizens to build a bridge that saves each of them ten minutes a day. Each citizen suffers a real, non-aggregated $1 burden, and the ten-minute savings are distributed across different people, so they can't pool together to outweigh any single $1 complaint.

(5) Therefore every citizen could reasonably reject the tax principle, which makes taxation wrong.

(6) By the same token, zoning laws, vaccine mandates, even traffic lights — anything that imposes a trivial cost on anyone for a dispersed benefit — gets forbidden.

(7) So contractualism can't justify any social coordination that asks anyone to bear a cost.

(8) But that was literally the point of the theory — explaining what we owe each other in a shared life — so if it forbids shared life it's self-defeating.

(9) So either Scanlon allows hidden aggregation after all, or the view collapses.

I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible, but I feel like I'm pulling a fast one between (3) and (4). Is it an equivocation on what counts as "affected"? Or am I missing that in (4) the same people bear both the cost and the benefit, so there's no inter-personal comparison happening at all? Maybe I'm treating "reasonably reject" like a distributive veto rather than a contractual balance point. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me?
