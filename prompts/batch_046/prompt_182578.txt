I am a high school student taking an introductory philosophy elective and an AP computer-science course, so I am fluent in basic ethical reasoning but far more comfortable with graphs, nodes, weights, path-cost functions, and resource-allocation algorithms than with formal moral theory. There is an argument—often associated with John Taurek, in the debate over whether the numbers count—that if you can rescue either one person or five people, with no special tie to any of them, you are not morally required to save the five; the greater number does not, by itself, generate a stronger claim on your aid. I want you to restate that argument entirely within my algorithmic vocabulary: treat each potential survivor as a node carrying an identical harm-avoided weight; treat the rescue as selecting a single traversable path to either one isolated node or a cluster of five nodes with the same individual weights; and treat any alleged special relation as an edge with a modified weight. Preserve the logical structure of the argument rather than offering a loose analogy. Then provide the residue report: identify which claims translate cleanly into properties of the graph (equal node weights, path exclusivity, cluster size), which claims can be preserved only by importing non-algorithmic assumptions (for example, a first-personal standpoint or a normative principle not encoded in the cost function), and which claims have no expression at all in this framework. For each residue item, say whether its absence is a defect of the algorithmic model—showing that moral reasoning requires concepts graphs cannot represent—or whether its absence reveals that the original argument was relying on a distinction that collapses once the choice is formalized as an optimization problem with symmetric nodes. Be honest about distortions; do not smuggle moral premises into the weight assignments and call that a faithful translation.
