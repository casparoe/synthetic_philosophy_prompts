The term I encountered is “coherent extrapolated volition,” and while it sounds like a sensible upgrade, the more I sit with it the more it feels like a polite way of saying the “real” me is whichever version a system designs. I don’t have a philosophy background, so I may be missing important distinctions, but I want to see the logic pushed through without jargon.

Please write a satirical parody of an official intake form for a fictional government clinic called the Center for Coherent Preference Stabilization. Use deadpan bureaucratic language—numbered sections, checkboxes, and legal disclaimers—and have the form quietly take positions on personal identity: what happens when the computed “ideal” preferences reject the patient’s current ones, whether the original applicant retains any veto, and the refund policy for cases where patient and result are no longer the same person. The satire should exaggerate real features of the concept rather than invent fake ones: coherence is enforced like an error-correction protocol, contradictions are flagged for removal, and the extrapolated self is given final authority. Include one invented technical detail, a “Divergence Index” scored to two decimal places with a specific cutoff at 0.73, above which the original applicant is legally redesignated a “Predecessor Entity.” The piece should be readable to someone who hasn’t studied alignment literature, but precise enough that someone familiar with CEV recognizes the satire as fair. Keep it under 650 words. Begin with the clinic’s name and a one-sentence mission statement, then present the form itself. Do not include citations, bibliographies, references to named philosophers, or requests for further reading—just the form.
