I am preparing for a graduate seminar on philosophical theology, and a classmate shared a summary of Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument drawn from an online primer on philosophy of religion. The summary states that Gödel began with Anselm's idea that God is the greatest conceivable being, but made it rigorous through modal logic; he defined God as the being that instantiates every positive property, demonstrated that existence counts as a positive property, and thereby proved that God is possible. Because he worked within system S5, possibility entails necessity, so God exists necessarily in every world and therefore in ours. The account concludes that the argument is formally valid, and that the only ways out are to deny that God is possible, or to reject modal logic itself, which means critics have no internal logical objection. Though the narrative sounds authoritative, I worry it collapses several technical distinctions into a single smooth arc.

Could you audit this summary with care? Please proceed claim by claim, distinguishing what accurately reflects Gödel's axiomatic approach, what is an acceptable simplification for a general audience, and what subtly misrepresents either the premises or the dialectic. In particular, I would like you to examine whether treating existence as merely one item in a list of positive properties captures Gödel's actual axiomatization, whether the phrase greatest conceivable being is faithful to Gödel's definition in terms of positive properties rather than Anselm's original framing, and whether the claim that critics have no internal logical objection except those two overlooks standard technical worries—such as the modal collapse problem, according to which a necessarily existing being in S5 might render every contingent truth necessary. Please also assess whether the primer's framing makes the debate appear to be about logical validity alone, when in fact much of the philosophical controversy concerns the coherence of the property set and the acceptability of the modal framework itself.
