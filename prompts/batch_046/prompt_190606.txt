I've come around to a pretty extreme reading of Bradley's regress: not just that relational unification needs a further relator, but that *any* compositional story about propositions — including modern ones with saturation, functional application, or even adverbialist modifcations — collapses into either an infinite regress or a primitive that's doing all the work in disguise. So I hold that propositions are genuinely simple mental events, and that their apparent structure (subject, predicate, whatever) is just a post-hoc linguistic parsing we impose on them. Compositionality, on this view, is an artifcat of syntax, not a feature of mental architecture. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked — from the ones that'd actually kill it to the ones that'd just be awkward — including anything from the productivity/systematicity literature, shared belief cases, false belief structure, or formal semantics that assumes decomposability. Which of these would a defender of this Bradley-inspired simplicity view have to concede, and which can they just absorb by saying language is misleading? Also, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every apparent counterexample gets explained away as 'just syntax' — and is that a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind?
