I am applying for a one-year interdisciplinary fellowship that allows clinicians to pursue conceptual research, motivated by an experience during my internal-medicine residency: a patient with chronic neuropathic pain improved markedly on placebo after failing on the active compound, and the team could not agree whether we had witnessed a failed mechanism or an alternative causal pathway. I want a six-month philosophical project on Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism and its conceptual utility for modern philosophy of medicine, specifically for interpreting causal attribution in randomized controlled trials.

The precise question is whether Al-Ghazālī’s distinction between the occasion (sabab) and the true agent (fāʿil) can be reconstructed—without commitment to metaphysical theism—as a normative framework for clinical efficacy. Can a drug be understood as an “occasion” that correlates with recovery without being its necessary cause, while the body’s responsive disposition, or the habitual order of nature, functions as the true causal ground? And if so, does this framework change how we interpret heterogeneous placebo responses and mechanistically opaque treatments in psychiatry, pain management, and immunology?

Existing philosophy of medicine largely operates within mechanistic or interventionist accounts of causation, treating statistical correlation in trials as evidence of underlying physical mechanisms. These accounts either dismiss occasionalism as pre-scientific theology or ignore it, leaving practitioners with no conceptual resources for prescribing based on efficacy data that outstrip mechanistic understanding—without collapsing into skepticism about medical knowledge altogether.

A purely historical or theological reconstruction would fail because it would bind the framework to Al-Ghazālī’s theological commitments, rendering it unusable for secular clinical reasoning. An empirical meta-analysis of placebo effects would fail because it cannot resolve the conceptual ambiguity about what “cause” means in a trial context. The proposed approach is therefore conceptual reconstruction: using Al-Ghazālī’s critique of necessary causation—his argument that observed regularities are habits of nature rather than logical necessities—to pressure-test interventionist models of clinical causation. The project will identify specific trial structures, such as heterogeneous responder populations and crossover designs with high placebo rates, where the occasionalist model predicts interpretive outcomes that mechanism-based models must treat as anomalous, and it will articulate decision criteria for trial interpretation derived from this reconstruction.

A successful result would be a conceptual framework with explicit criteria distinguishing “occasional correlation” from “mechanistic efficacy” in trial design, applicable to cases where mechanism is unknown or contested. Failure would be demonstrated if the occasionalist reconstruction collapses into either pure mechanism, thereby losing its distinctive normative force, or pure skepticism, thereby denying that trials can support prescribing decisions at all.

The scope is realistic for one researcher over six months: philosophical analysis of texts and conceptual modeling, not empirical data collection or comprehensive theological scholarship. The work will engage Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalist arguments in dialogue with contemporary interventionist philosophy of science, particularly regarding medical causation. References and full bibliographic citations will be supplied in a later stage; the proposal should mention positions and arguments by name only. Write the one-page proposal, including the framing question, the gap in existing answers, the reconstruction approach and why alternatives are inadequate, the criteria for success and failure, the deliverable for clinicians interpreting trials, and a realistic timeline with milestones for the six-month period.
