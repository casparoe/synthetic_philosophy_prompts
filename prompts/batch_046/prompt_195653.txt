I have observed a recurring dialectical pattern in arguments about whether multiple objects ever compose a single larger object. A theorist proposes that parts form a whole only when they stand in some specific relation—say, causal interaction or functional integration. Critics then present two contrasting cases. First, a dispersed swarm of autonomous drones that coordinate solely through a remote server: they are causally linked, spread across a wide area, and lack physical contiguity, yet the proposed relation seems to apply, yielding the counterintuitive result that the swarm is one object. Second, a human body maintained over decades, in which every original cell has been replaced: the causal and functional continuity remains, but none of the original parts do, so the proposed relation risks either denying the body's persistence or requiring an implausible account of identity over time. The theorist then refines the relation, but the refinement often looks tailored—designed to include the body while excluding the swarm—rather than motivated by an independent principle. Is there an established term for this specific cycle of boundary-pressure, in which a criterion for composition is pressed from both directions by scattered systems on one side and persisting organisms on the other? Does it fall under the general heading of vagueness, or is there a distinct diagnosis—perhaps related to the special composition question itself—that captures why these particular counterexamples keep arising? If no standard label exists, how should one characterize the failure mode precisely, so as not to conflate it with an ordinary counterexample?
