I'm a novelist writing mostly about people who think they understand risk, and I'm stuck on a dialogue beat. My protaganist, a retired economist, keeps telling her niece "you can't be worse off knowing more" — she uses it like an universal law to push the girl into opening a sealed medical report. I know there's some real theorem behind this, Good's thing about information value, but I also know people throw the line around for everything from "face your fears" to "always read the fine print." Could you split the slogan into the precise theses it might actually be standing for? Like, the strict decision-theoretic version with expected-utility agents and truly free prior information; the normative version about what rationality demands; a psychological version where fear of knowing is just irrational; and maybe a strategic or commitment version where knowing something actually ruins your ability to follow through on a plan — like in a game of chicken or a self-binding situation. For each, say whether it's genuinely defensible, what hidden conditions it needs (costless info, ability to act on it, no preference change, etc.), and then tell me which reading my character is probably smuggling in and whether it even applies to pressuring someone about a health result. I want a menu of disambiguations, not one answer, with a note on which ones are platitudes and which ones are actually false.
