My opening move turns on a framing I have been developing with a fellow student—let us call it the recursive dependency thesis. Most celebrated microhistories still draw their evidentiary base from the very bureaucratic archives they claim to transcend: court protocols, notarial registers, inquisitorial depositions, and census rolls produced by elite institutions to classify, tax, or discipline populations. The microhistorian reads these documents “against the grain” to recover subaltern agency, yet the grain itself—the legal categories of heresy, theft, or property, and the narrative forms imposed by clerks—remains constituted by state logic. Consequently, the recovered voice is not an autonomous speech recovered from silence, but a speech already mediated and partially constituted by the classificatory apparatus of power. A friend who studies what she terms “active structuring absence” has pushed me further: the very lack of autonomous peasant or artisan archives is not merely an empirical gap that microhistory heroically fills, but an active absence that makes the microhistorical project possible, since the historian must necessarily inhabit the documentary logic of the state in order to find anything at all. If this is correct, does microhistory escape state-centered history, or does it risk reproducing its epistemic frame at a smaller magnification—merely offering a more intimate portrait of the same bureaucratic gaze?
