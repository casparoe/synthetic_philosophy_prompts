Rank the following five criteria for identifying corruption—from 1 (most adequate) to 5 (least adequate)—specifically for their ability to distinguish corrupt acts from legitimate practice in institutional settings where gift-giving, clientelism, and reciprocal obligation are culturally normative, such as bureaucracies in which small gifts to officials are expected and personal networks determine access to public services. The criteria are: (1) the abuse of entrusted public power for private gain; (2) the explicit violation of a formal rule governing the office; (3) the presence of a quid-pro-quo expectation that undermines impartial allocation; (4) the transformation of a public role into a private patron-client bond, regardless of whether an explicit exchange occurs; and (5) the failure to maintain what I will call “role-distance”—the structural separation between an office-holder’s personal obligations and the universalist logic of the institution they occupy.

For each criterion, assign a reliability score out of 10 for cross-cultural application (not for Western liberal-democratic contexts alone), and in exactly one sentence state which culturally normative practice it wrongly criminalizes or which genuinely corrupt arrangement it wrongly exonerates.

Then, if you had to collapse these five into just two non-negotiable requirements—one addressing the institutional structure and one addressing the subjective expectation or intent of the actors—which pair would you retain, and which three would you discard? Give me the retained pair and a single reason why the discarded trio collectively fails to solve the problem you identify.
