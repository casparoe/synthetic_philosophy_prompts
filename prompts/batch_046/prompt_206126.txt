I'm auditing a graduate seminar on moral responsibility and witnessed a sustained disagreement between two participants—call them R and S—about whether hypocrisy necessarily undermines standing to blame. They anchored the dispute in a concrete case: a university administrator who had long ignored student mental-health complaints is now loudly condemning a professor for neglecting a student in crisis. S argued that the administrator plainly lacks standing: she is guilty of the very same kind of neglect, and one cannot legitimately blame someone for X while having done X oneself. R agreed that hypocrisy is objectionable, but insisted that standing and credibility are not the same thing. He introduced a distinction—one he admitted was his own framing—between authoritative standing, which concerns whether one is in a position to legitimately issue blame that addresses or binds the wrongdoer, and comparative standing, which concerns whether one's own record is clean enough to occupy the moral high ground. On R's view, hypocrisy destroys comparative standing but not necessarily authoritative standing: the administrator might still correctly identify the professor's wrong, and might even possess institutional authority to say so, even if she is a poor messenger. S pushed back that this bifurcation misses what blame actually is. She claimed that blame is not merely a propositional judgment ("you did wrong") but a normative comparison that implicitly places the blamer on one side of a moral line and the blamed on the other; if the blamer is on the wrong side too, the comparison collapses, regardless of any official title. She added that R's view would permit every corrupt regulator to blame every corrupt firm, which empties blame of its social meaning. R replied that S is conflating the conditions for blame being true or authoritative with the conditions for it being appropriate or effective to utter, and that accepting her view risks a "clean hands" doctrine that lets the worst offenders escape criticism simply because moral purity is rare. They cycled through these points without resolution, and neither seemed to notice that they might be talking past each other on what "standing" is even for.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim? In particular: is R's distinction between authoritative and comparative standing a genuine conceptual division, or is it just a relabeling that lets hypocrisy off the hook? Does S's normative-comparison account actually establish that hypocrisy undermines standing, or does it only show that hypocritical blame is ineffective, disrespectful, or pragmatically self-defeating? And what are both parties missing—perhaps about whether standing comes in degrees, whether it can be distributed across a group rather than held by individuals, or whether the magnitude and kind of the respective wrongs should affect the judgment? I want an adjudication of the arguments as actually made, not just an overall winner, and I'd especially value if you can identify any hidden premises that would need to be defended for either side to fully win.
