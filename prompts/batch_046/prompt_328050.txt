I am preparing a formal interview for a philosophy-of-law podcast with a scholar who defends a distinctive resolution to Ross's paradox of constitutional self-amendment. Rather than accepting an infinite hierarchy of amendment rules, she proposes what she terms a staged-jurisdiction model: the authority to amend a fundamental provision never operates within the same normative frame as the provision itself, but is always deferred to a temporally indexed successor stage—so that Article V belongs to legal stage n, while any constitutional rule it generates belongs to stage n+1, thereby blocking direct self-reference. I want six to eight rigorous interview questions that press this view precisely where the existing literature on legal self-reference and normative hierarchy applies pressure.

Please include one question that examines whether her model genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it to the meta-rules governing transitions between stages, together with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that those transition rules are themselves constitutionally entrenched and therefore non-amendable. Include another question challenging the boundary between formal amendment and informal constitutional evolution—specifically asking whether customary development that effectively rewrites a fundamental rule counts as benign normative drift or as a surreptitious stage-violation, with a follow-up anticipating her claim that drift lacks the intentional institutional act required for amendment and then pressing how she distinguishes intentional restructuring that occurs gradually from explicit amendment. A further question should address jurisprudential practice: should courts treat an attempt at simultaneous self-amendment—where a provision purports to alter its own amending authority within the same stage—as ultra vires or as void ab initio, with a follow-up that anticipates her probable appeal to institutional competence rather than logical impossibility and asks whether that concession reduces her staged model from a conceptual solution to a merely administrative convention.

The questions ought to suit a polished colloquium audience already familiar with the basic structure of Ross's paradox but not with her particular temporal-staging solution; they should avoid generic biographical openings, should not request bibliographies or citations, and should assume no access to external sources.
