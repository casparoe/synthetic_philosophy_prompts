I cover philosophy of mathematics for a science-and-ideas desk, and I keep returning to a reconstructed argument against Cantor that I cannot fully unwind. Here is the chain as it was presented to me.

(1) Hilbert's hotel shows that an actually infinite discrete collection—rooms 1, 2, 3, ...—can accommodate one more occupant without expanding, by shifting each current occupant to the successor room.

(2) This works precisely because the collection lacks a last element; there is always a "next" position available.

(3) Cantor's diagonal argument takes any claimed complete list of real numbers—r₁ in position 1, r₂ in position 2, and so on—and constructs a new real d that differs from r₁ in digit 1, from r₂ in digit 2, etc.

(4) Cantor concludes d is not on the list, so the list was not complete, and therefore the reals are uncountable.

(5) But apply the Hilbert shift to Cantor's list: move r₁ to position 2, r₂ to position 3, ..., and place d in the now-vacant position 1.

(6) Every original list member has a new position; d occupies position 1; no one has been removed.

(7) Therefore the list now contains d along with all previous entries—just as Hilbert's hotel contains all original guests plus the newcomer.

(8) By repeating the shift, any allegedly "missing" number can be inserted into some vacant slot.

(9) So Cantor's "missing" number is never genuinely outside; it is merely waiting for a Hilbert-style rearrangement.

(10) Hence there is no cardinality gap between the naturals and the reals; the hierarchy of transfinite cardinals collapses.

(11) The only consistent interpretation is finitism: there is one potential infinity of rearrangeable positions, but no actually larger completed infinities.

This seems to prove that Cantor was wrong and that finitism saves mathematics from paradox. I know the standard reply involves the enumeration being arbitrary, but I cannot see why the shift does not solve the problem regardless of ordering. Where exactly does the reasoning slip—between which steps? Is there an equivocation about what a "list" means, a scope shift in how d is defined relative to the ordering, or a modal slide about "vacancy"? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (4) than (9), but I want to know what giving up (5) through (8) costs for the finitist program.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
