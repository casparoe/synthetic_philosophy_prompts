I'm preparing a feature on whether "life" names a natural kind or merely a local cluster of physical processes, and I've built a chain that seems to swallow storms and software alike. Here are the steps:

(1) A system is alive if and only if it maintains organizational closure: its internal dynamics continuously regenerate the boundary conditions that make those dynamics possible, actively importing free energy and exporting entropy.

(2) A minimal synthetic cell with a self-synthesizing lipid membrane satisfies this.

(3) But so does a wildfire: its heat creates the updraft and dry conditions that sustain combustion, importing fuel and exporting heat, maintaining a coherent boundary front.

(4) So does a hurricane, sustaining its vortex by exporting heat and importing warm vapor.

(5) To exclude these, one might require informational heredity—replicable instructions that govern the boundary's reproduction.

(6) Yet informational heredity also appears in prions (protein conformation templating), in self-correcting computer viruses, and in crystal lattice defects that replicate structural errors.

(7) Once heredity is required, either we include these non-biological entities, or we restrict "information" to nucleic acids—which looks like taxonomic prejudice rather than a principled boundary.

(8) Therefore, either life is a broad category of self-maintaining dissipative structures (including storms and some software), or the organizational-closure criterion is false and we lack a non-arbitrary line between biology and physics.

The conclusion feels wrong—surely a hurricane is not alive—but every escape seems costly. Which step should I reject? If I keep (1), I seem committed to (8); if I reject (1), I need an alternative that doesn't arbitrarily privilege carbon chemistry. What am I paying in each case, and is there a hidden slide—perhaps between physical self-maintenance and genuine agency—between (3) and (5) that saves the boundary without begging the question?
