Compare Darwin's evolutionary inversion—where complex adaptive design arises without an intelligent designer—and Turing's inversion of reasoning—where intelligent behavior can be produced and recognized without any comprehension of the underlying mechanism. Both are standardly treated as cases of competence without comprehension, but they overturn different kinds of explanation: Darwin removes teleological causation from natural history, while Turing removes first-person understanding as a criterion for third-person attribution of intelligence. Reconstruct each inversion precisely, specifying what form of reasoning or explanation is displaced and what takes its place. Then assess whether the two are structurally analogous or asymmetrical: does Darwin's elimination of a designer create an explanatory gap that Turing's functionalist criterion implicitly fills, perhaps by substituting behavioral output for internal teleology? Examine whether accepting one inversion commits one to the other, and identify a conceptual tension that emerges if both are applied together to a single system—for instance, an artificial architecture that evolves its own structure without either evolutionary comprehension or present-time understanding of its operations. Finally, evaluate which inversion, if either, presents the deeper challenge to the traditional assumption that adequate explanation requires transparency between the explainer and the explained.
