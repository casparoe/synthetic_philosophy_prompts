You are a professional philosopher of well-being with expertise in negative hedonism, desire-satisfaction theories, and the phenomenology of craving. Before drafting your answer, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm: list the principal competing accounts of well-being—positive hedonism, desire fulfillment, objective-list theories, and tranquilism—identify the structural points where tranquilism is weakest, and note any distinctions that are easily collapsed but must be kept separate for the view to remain coherent.

Your task is to write the full specification—the desiderata—that a tranquilist theory of well-being must meet to count as adequate. By tranquilism, I mean the normative view that only the absence of craving and suffering contributes positively to a life going well; there is no independent good beyond this absence, and any state that involves active craving or suffering is, to that extent, a reduction in well-being.

For each desideratum, provide:
- the specific phenomenon, case, or distinction it must accommodate,
- the reason it is a genuine constraint rather than a partisan preference of rival camps,
- whether you judge it non-negotiable or negotiable,
- and any suspicion that it conflicts with another desideratum on the list, especially if a subset might be jointly unsatisfiable.

In particular, you must address three injected conceptual challenges:
1. The silent satisfaction problem: situations in which a subject reports complete absence of craving and suffering, yet intuitively appears to be missing something important for well-being—such as deep engagement, purpose, or sustained positive affect. The theory must determine whether these lives are genuinely flourishing by tranquilist lights or whether they reveal an inadequate conception of what tranquility requires.
2. The asymmetry of craving-cessation: the fact that removing a craving through extinction, fulfillment, or suppression may carry different normative weight. The theory must distinguish legitimate tranquility from pathological numbness, adaptive preference suppression, or chemically induced absence, and explain why the mechanism of cessation matters.
3. Structured absence: the claim that tranquility is not a mere null mental state but depends on a specific organizational relation among beliefs, affective dispositions, and second-order attitudes. The theory must say whether this organizational feature belongs to the good itself or is only an instrumental condition for achieving absence.

Also identify which single desideratum does the most damage to the most popular rival theories—positive hedonism, desire-fulfillment accounts, and objective-list views—and briefly explain why.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard of named philosophers. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
