After a lengthy argument with a friend about rational self-improvement, I want to check whether philosophy or logic has already named a pattern I keep describing. Imagine an agent that can revise its own beliefs and wants to adopt a principle of deference: it should accept whatever its future, more deliberative version concludes, provided that version reached the conclusion through reasoning the present version currently endorses. The agent tries to encode this formally. But it quickly sees that, once the deference rule is part of its system, it can generate a derivation that its future self will endorse almost any proposition—the endorsement becomes self-fulfilling. So it tries to restrict the rule to conclusions reached by "sound" reasoning, where soundness is defined by the absence of such loops. The difficulty is that judging whether a particular derivation is loop-free requires applying the very deference rule it is trying to constrain: the agent must ask its future self whether the reasoning was sound, which reinstates the loop at a higher level. Each attempt to close the loophole either paralyzes the agent or quietly imports an unexamined standard from outside the system. My friend says this is just the problem of the criterion in a new costume, or perhaps ordinary circular reasoning about authority. I think the structure is distinct: the failure is not in the evidence or in the external authority, but in the formalization of self-trust itself, specifically in the inability to separate legitimate belief revision from self-fulfilling derivation once the agent is allowed to represent its own reasoning. So: does this specific collapse—where codifying rational deference to one's future self destroys the internal boundary between sound and unsound revision—have an established name? How exactly does it relate to the problem of the criterion, to epistemic humility, and to systems that simply freeze rather than update? And if it lacks a precise label, what term and boundary conditions would best capture it, particularly distinguishing it from cases where an agent refuses self-trust for merely empirical reasons?
