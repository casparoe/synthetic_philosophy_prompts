I am designing a session for first-year philosophy students—background limited to an introductory logic course—on the structural disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. I want to avoid the usual baldness or heap illustrations; instead, use a specific carrying case: a bioengineered heart valve whose original tissue cells are replaced one by one with synthetic equivalents until none remain, with the surgical log that would identify the precise replacement threshold lost. Plan the explanatory arc, not the explanation itself. Specify: the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so the two views appear as rival answers to one problem rather than as competing glosses on "vague"; what the heart-valve scenario must possess and what it must lack to carry the distinction without suggesting a hidden empirical answer; the misconceptions students are likely to form—particularly the collapse of semantic indeterminacy into epistemic ignorance, and the inference that supervaluationism permits arbitrary judgment—together with the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a brief check for understanding that distinguishes grasping the structural disagreement from merely attaching labels; and what should be explicitly left out at this level, with an honest admission of the omission. Present the plan as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding paragraph.
