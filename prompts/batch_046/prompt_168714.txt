I've been arguing with my roommate about whether "creative leaps" are real or just an illusion of scale. I'm now defending a pretty strong version of Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model — not just that analogies work by sliding parameters on mental representations, but that literally every creative act, from inventing a proof technique to writing a strange poem, is a continuous deformation through a high-dimensional parameter space of existing conceptual schemas. The "eureka" feeling, on this view, is just your brain noticing that some knobs got pushed far enough that the output lands in a cluster that looks categorically different, but there was never an actual discontinuity in the process. I even think this applies across domains: scientific insight is just cross-domain knob-twiddling where you map the parameter space of one field onto another and let it settle into an unfamiliar configuration.

So play it straight for me: what would actually count against this? I don't want the standard "but where do the schemas come from" objection, since I think the view can absorb that. I'm looking for the genuinely risky defeaters — are there documented problem-solving cases where the solver couldn't have been manipulating parameters of a prior representation because they genuinely lacked one in that domain? What about the cognitive-science literature on "aha moments" and insight: do those results show discontinuities that can't be smoothed into parameter curves? And if defenders of the view start saying that "knob-twiddling" just means "any cognitive change whatsoever," hasn't it been qualified into unfalsifiability — and is that a real problem for a claim of this kind, or just the price of a broad framework? Rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed knob-twiddling theorist would concede damage the view versus which ones they'd dismiss by claiming the knobs were just hidden or the parameter space was implicit. Also, be honest about whether this view risks anything at all, or if it's been insulated so well that no observation could really hurt it.
