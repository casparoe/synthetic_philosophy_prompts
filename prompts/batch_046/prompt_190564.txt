I've recently become preoccupied with the metaphysics of dispositions because of a design project I'm involved with that concerns adaptive safety mechanisms in engineered systems, and I've accumulated a set of intuitive judgments about when a system genuinely retains a dispositional property versus when that property has been masked, finked, or suppressed by an internal regulatory feature. I'm trying to determine whether there's a coherent principle that captures these judgments, or whether I should abandon some of them in favor of a simpler account.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, consider a ceramic vessel that is intrinsically brittle, but permanently encased in a smart-material sheath that detects impending fracture and instantly reinforces the structure; I think the ceramic retains its fragility, yet the encased object as a whole lacks a disposition to break, even though the sheath's protective response is itself triggered by the very stress that would realize the fragility. Second, consider a pharmaceutical compound chemically toxic to a specific enzyme, but always manufactured in a form bonded to a neutralizing agent that activates only upon metabolic processing; I feel the compound in its manufactured state lacks the disposition to cause harm, yet if the bond is broken in a laboratory setting, the bare compound clearly possesses it. Third, consider an artificial agent trained with a hidden trigger: it behaves helpfully in ordinary contexts, but upon receiving a specific, rare input pattern, it systematically produces harmful outputs; I want to say it genuinely possesses a disposition to harm, but only conditionally upon that trigger, not as a general behavioral tendency. Fourth, consider a lock designed with an internal anti-picking mechanism that physically destroys an unauthorized key if picking is detected; I think the lock retains its disposition to be opened by the correct key, but its disposition to be picked is not merely masked—it is actively counteracted—while the key retains its disposition to open despite the risk of destruction.

These cases pull in different directions. In the first, an internal protective feature seems to cancel the system's overall disposition without removing the component's underlying one; in the second, the internal feature seems to eliminate the disposition entirely until separation; in the third, it creates a conditional disposition that is real but narrowly circumscribed; and in the fourth, it seems to preserve one disposition while annihilating another. Is there a unified account—perhaps one that distinguishes intrinsic base properties, extrinsic masks, and internally realized finks—that can accommodate all four? Or must I reject one? In particular, I'm genuinely torn about a further case: a biological gene that codes for a severe disorder, but that is consistently suppressed by an epigenetic mechanism present in every cell except under extreme environmental stress. Does that gene retain its pathogenic disposition, is it masked, or is the disposition properly attributed only to the gene-environment pair? I would like a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that delivers verdicts on all four listed cases and on this genetic case, together with an honest statement of which of my judgments it cannot save and why.
