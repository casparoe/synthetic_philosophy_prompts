You sit on the University Committee for Institutional Memory and Epistemic Justice. The Center for Cognitive Neuroscience bears the name of a nineteenth-century researcher whose foundational experiments relied on enslaved subjects without consent; descendants of those subjects have petitioned for more than removal of the name. They seek a binding recognition charter requiring the center to formally acknowledge that its historical dataset was co-produced through unacknowledged suffering, and they demand a permanent advisory seat with veto authority over any future research using that dataset. A committee faction argues that symbolic acknowledgment and a commemorative plaque satisfy justice, and that granting structural veto power reproduces the very asymmetry it claims to solve by substituting ceremonial inclusion for genuine reciprocity. Another faction contends that without institutional recognition that transfers real authority—mirroring Hegel’s claim that the master’s self-certainty depends on recognition from the slave, yet true freedom requires mutual acknowledgment—the relationship remains one of domination masked as benevolence. Draft the committee’s written opinion recommending a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame, treating the descendants’ position with full seriousness, engaging the Hegelian framing of recognition directly, and giving an honest answer to the strongest objection from the symbolic-acknowledgment faction. Deliver it as exactly five numbered paragraphs, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no separate introduction or conclusion.
