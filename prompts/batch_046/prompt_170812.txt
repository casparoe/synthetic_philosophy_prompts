As a journalist who covers the history of ideas and collective memory, I have been tracking a debate that has persisted for decades without obvious progress, and I want to understand its structure rather than take a side.

The dispute is between comparative historians of nationalism and area-specialist scholars of trauma and grief. It concerns whether four well-known cases — the American Confederacy's Lost Cause, the siege of Masada, the Kosovo Polje, and the Dunkirk evacuation — should be analysed as members of a single category: military defeats that are retrospectively transformed into morally charged, boundary-defining myths of communal virtue. The comparative camp argues that these cases share a formal architecture: a catastrophic failure is reinterpreted as sacrificial proof of collective worth, often accompanied by tropes of betrayal by allies or by history itself, and by an implicit promise of future redemption. They note that Masada was largely dormant in collective memory until twentieth-century revivalists reconstructed it; that the Kosovo epic was systematised centuries after 1389; and that the Confederacy's myth was consolidated long after Appomattox. For them, these long latencies and subsequent revivals are precisely the evidence that functional political needs select and reshape archival remnants, not an objection to comparison.

The opposing camp — mainly specialists in the respective regions and scholars who study collective grief — replies that this framework treats memory as purely instrumental. They observe that Dunkirk involved minimal casualties and served an immediate strategic need for morale in 1940, which makes it structurally unlike the other three; they argue that comparing Masada's reconstructed martyrdom with Kosovo's medieval epic ignores the distinct institutional carriers and temporal rhythms of each narrative; and they insist that the comparative approach systematically flattens genuinely non-strategic, grief-laden dimensions of remembrance into a single functionalist sociology. Each side can recite the same four cases, each acknowledges the tropes the other highlights, and yet neither has moved the other.

My own observation — which may or may not be original — is that the comparative literature rarely distinguishes between immediate mythologisation, as with Dunkirk, and latency-revival cycles, as with Masada, Kosovo, and the Confederacy, as variables that might determine what kind of explanation is appropriate. The particularists mention latency, but treat it as proof of incommensurability; the comparativists mention it, but treat it as proof of functional selection. Nobody seems to ask whether the presence or absence of a long dormancy changes the psychological and political mechanisms at work.

So my questions are these. Is the disagreement substantive — about whether grief mechanisms and political legitimation mechanisms are empirically separable — or is it partly verbal, turning on whether myth means false narrative serving power or foundational story regardless of veracity? Are the two camps asking different questions — one about structural homology across cultures, the other about phenomenological particularity — under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, perhaps about the conditions under which a community can experience a defeat as grief rather than as strategic material? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between immediate and revived defeat narratives; an empirical study that could not be interpreted by either side; a change in the framing question; or should we conclude that the comparison is ill-posed?

Please separate what is settled — for instance, the basic narrative contours of each case, the existence of both comparative and particularist literatures, and the fact that temporal latency varies across the four examples — from what is genuinely contested among scholars who work on this material. Do not present one interpretive framework as the consensus.
