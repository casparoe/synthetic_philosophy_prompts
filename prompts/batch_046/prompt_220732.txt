I've been working through Jackson's knowledge argument, specifically the ability-hypothesis reply: Mary doesn't learn a new non-physical fact when she sees red for the first time, but rather acquires a cluster of phenomenal concepts and recognitional abilities. This seems to preserve physicalism, but I think there's a tension that gets overlooked. If phenomenal concepts are genuinely abilities—like knowing how to ride a bicycle—then they should be analyzable in terms of functional dispositions. So why couldn't we, in principle, instantiate those same dispositions in an artificial system that has processed the complete physical description of color vision? That is, if Mary gains an ability rather than a fact, and abilities are functional, then a sufficiently structured model should be able to possess the phenomenal concept of red without ever being embodied with color sensors. Or is the hidden mistake here that phenomenal abilities are necessarily first-person and embodied in a way that breaks the analogy to ordinary knowing-how? I'm trying to see exactly where the argument against artificial phenomenal concepts is supposed to come from, given that the ability reply was meant to block the ontological conclusion in the first place. Where does the reasoning go wrong?
