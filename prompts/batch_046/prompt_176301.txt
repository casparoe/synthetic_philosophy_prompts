the exam question was: Ian Hacking's concept of looping effects suggests psychiatric classifications change those they classify. Discuss this in relation to the DSM-5's approach to personality disorders, and consider whether conditions with strong genetic bases (like schizophrenia) weaken looping. Also address whether patient advocacy can produce reverse looping that reshapes categories, and whether this undermines the value-neutrality of psychiatric diagnosis.

the student answer:

Hacking's looping effect describes how psychiatric classifications alter the people they classify: receiving a diagnosis changes self-understanding and behaviour, which then makes the category seem more accurate. This is particularly clear with personality disorders, and the DSM-5 replaced the categorical personality disorder diagnoses with dimensional trait ratings, measuring things like antagonism and detachment on a continuum. Because patients now see themselves on a precise spectrum rather than in a fixed box, the looping effect is actually strengthened — they internalise the score and adjust their conduct to match it.

For conditions with strong genetic bases like schizophrenia, looping still operates but through different channels: the diagnosis affects access to care, social identity, and stigma, rather than causing the core symptoms. So biology and looping work together rather than cancelling each other out.

That said, Hacking's framework is essentially Foucault's concept of subjectivation applied specifically to clinical psychology — psychiatric knowledge produces the subjects it studies. Patient advocacy can drive reverse looping too, as seen when autism advocacy reshaped the DSM-5 criteria for autism spectrum disorder. Still, this doesn't undermine value-neutrality, since psychiatric categories remain objective descriptions of real dysfunction even when advocacy groups help draw the boundaries.
