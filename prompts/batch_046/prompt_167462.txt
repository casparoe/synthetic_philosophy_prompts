As a novelist attempting to render the interior life of a craftsman without reducing skill to mere information, I am examining the philosophical distinction between knowing how and knowing that. Please construct a thought experiment—confined to an ordinary workshop, employing no futuristic technology, and presented in no more than a concise narrative paragraph—in which a Rylean anti-intellectualist and an intellectualist who treats knowing how as propositional knowledge under a practical mode of presentation both concede that a master cooper, having lost all episodic memory of apprenticeship yet retaining the kinesthetic rhythm of staving irregular barrels by acoustic resonance, still possesses the craft. The two accounts must, however, diverge sharply on whether he knows that specific propositions concerning wood fiber tension and thermal expansion govern his adjustments. Isolate exactly one structural feature—such as the total absence of articulable intermediate procedural steps—that forces this divergence. Then demonstrate that the case meets each specification: show the verdict each theory delivers and the reasoning behind it, identify which feature does the work, describe what happens if that feature is removed, and explain how an intellectualist defender would attack the scenario. I want an honest assessment of whether the case genuinely separates the two positions or collapses into a familiar instance of tacit propositional knowledge.
