I’m putting together a decision-tree quiz for a poli-sci grad seminar on dirty hands — I want it more granular than the usual Walzer vs. Weber framing, but not so obscure that a model without web access can’t reason it through. The running case should be a democratic leader who orders a covert strike that kills civilians, then lies to the legislature about it.

Start with this split: is there any act that’s flat-out wrong for a private person but can still be obligatory for a state actor?

Then branch on where the moral stain lands: on the individual agent, on the institutional role alone, or dissolved across a collective body (cabinet vote, committee sign-off).

Next node: can the stain be laundered — through democratic mandate, public confession, or leaving office — or is it permanent untill death?

Then ask whether consequentialist calculus can ever be the sole justification, or whether there’s always a leftover deontological residue that demands seperate acknowledgment.

Every leaf should name a real position if one exists (Walzerian tragic choice, Weberian vocation-with-guilt, pure institutionalist, democratic-laundering view, hard consequentialist-no-residue) or be marked 'unnamed.' For each unnamed combo, write one sentence on why it’s empty — incoherent, psychologically unstable, or just unexplored.

Make sure each node is phrased as a yes/no/maybe question that sorts people by belief, not by which philosopher they’ve read. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can’t really seperate. Don’t include a bibliography or citation requests.

Please end with:
Verdict: [number of named positions and number of empty branches you found]
