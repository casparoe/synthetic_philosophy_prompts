I am a retired natural scientist—forty years in experimental physics—who has only recently begun reading philosophy in a serious way, and I find myself returning to a puzzle that seems to straddle both worlds. In ethics and decision theory there is what is called the spectrum argument, where one compares a long sequence of states arranged along a continuum—say, lives of gradually diminishing quality, or scientific models of gradually increasing complexity—such that each adjacent pair differs only imperceptibly, yet by chaining the local judgments of "better than" one arrives at the absurd conclusion that the extreme ends stand in the same relation. The question of whether "better than" is transitive thus becomes not a mere logical formality but a practical problem for anyone who must rank alternatives that differ by degrees rather than by kind. I would like you to turn this theoretical difficulty into an operational procedure that a practitioner—perhaps a peer reviewer comparing successive generations of climate models, or an ethicist evaluating gradations of well-being—can actually run when asked to rank three or more items arranged along such a spectrum.

Please present your response entirely in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, much as one writes a careful letter or email. The procedure should proceed through a fixed order of questions: first, whether the comparison between each adjacent pair relies on exactly the same criterion or whether there is what I will call dimension drift, where the feature that makes A better than B is not the feature that makes B better than C; second, whether the differences are genuinely continuous or whether there are hidden thresholds that break the spectrum into discrete categories; third, whether transitivity failure, if detected, is benign—merely indicating that the ranking requires a multi-dimensional representation—or whether it signals that the local judgments themselves were inconsistent and must be revised. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, and mark explicitly where the procedure must halt and hand the decision over to human judgment rather than mechanical application. Then apply the procedure to two concrete cases: first, comparing three hypothetical climate models arranged by increasing parameter complexity and corresponding predictive accuracy, where the middle model may be best on one axis and worst on another; second, comparing three lives arranged along a spectrum of sensory richness and longevity, where each adjacent pair seems clearly ordered but the chain produces a reversal between the first and the last. In each worked example, show exactly where the procedure detects or fails to detect transitivity failure, and say what the practitioner should conclude. Finally, be honest about what this procedure cannot do—particularly its inability to resolve whether the correct response to intransitivity is to reject the local judgments, to abandon the linear ranking, or to accept that "better than" is not a relation suitable for this domain—and present that limitation in the same uninterrupted prose form.
