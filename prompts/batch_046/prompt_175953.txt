For a consistency audit before I present at an intergenerational ethics reading group, I want to lay out my commitments on climate justice and check whether they cohere. Here is my profile:

(a) I accept the non-identity problem in its standard form: because climate policies alter the conditions of conception and social development, we cannot coherently claim that emitting carbon "harms" specific future individuals who would not exist under mitigation.

(b) Despite (a), I hold that present generations have stringent duties to stabilize the climate, but I ground these in the preservation of what I call the "intergenerational commons"—the stable biogeochemical and institutional conditions necessary for just societies—rather than in harm to particular identifiable beneficiaries.

(c) I interpret the relevant preservation duty through a modified Rawlsian savings principle: we must maintain ecological stability as a background condition for justice across generations, not merely accumulate productive capital or maximize welfare.

(d) I reject standard arguments for near-zero social discount rates that appeal to the "equal moral status" of future persons, because I think (a) undermines the trans-temporal personal identity required to make sense of such equality claims between "us" and "them."

(e) At the same time, I defend a near-zero discount rate on arbitrariness grounds—temporal location is morally arbitrary in the same way spatial location is—and I take this arbitrariness argument not to depend on continuous personal identity across generations.

(f) Finally, I think that even if no specific future person can claim to be wronged by our emissions, depleting the intergenerational commons is still wrongful: it violates a stewardship obligation that binds present decision-makers regardless of who eventually inherits the earth.

My questions: Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? In particular, does (d)'s skepticism about trans-temporal identity undermine (e)'s arbitrariness argument, or can the two be cleanly separated? Does (b)'s commons-based framing conflict with (c)'s Rawlsian framing, given that the savings principle was originally conceived in terms of capital and institutional transmission rather than ecological maintenance? And does (f)'s stewardship claim survive (a)'s denial of harm to identifiable victims, or does it secretly reintroduce the very beneficiary-oriented reasoning I reject in (d)? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each indicate the least costly revision—specifying exactly which commitment has to be weakened or reconceived, and what the philosophical price of that revision would be. Also identify any apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, explaining why it is only superficial.
