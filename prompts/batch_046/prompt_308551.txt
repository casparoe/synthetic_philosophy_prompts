Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases. Case 1: A research mathematician knows the axioms of ZFC and understands first-order inference, but she has never encountered the Banach-Tarski paradox and has certainly never derived it. I think she genuinely knows the axioms, and she is not epistemically defective for failing to know the paradox; the inferential path is too long and non-obvious to impose a rational obligation. Case 2: A tired logic student knows both "If it rains, then the ground is wet" and "It is raining," yet she does not form the belief that the ground is wet because she is distracted. I still think she knows the premises, and her failure is a performance slip rather than a rational failure. Case 3: A juror knows that the defendant's fingerprints are on the weapon and knows that fingerprints on a weapon imply the defendant handled it; she refuses to accept the conclusion because she is emotionally loyal to the defendant's family. Here I think she is genuinely epistemically irrational—she should recognize the consequence, and her refusal is not merely a performance error. Case 4: An AI knowledge-base system stores both "All observed swans in region R are white" and "Swan S is observed in region R," but its inference engine has not run, so a direct query returns that it does not know S is white. I think the system lacks knowledge of S's color, but I am unsure whether this is blameworthy resource limitation, like the mathematician's, or a more serious rational deficit. Case 5: A child knows that 2 + 2 = 4 and that 4 = 3 + 1, but she has not realized that therefore 2 + 2 = 3 + 1. I think she does not know the equality, yet she is not irrational—she is developmentally constrained in working-memory capacity for transitive inference. Case 6: A philosopher knows the premises of a valid modal argument for a necessary being, but she rejects the conclusion because she denies the modal system (S5) that licenses the inference step. I think she can rationally reject the conclusion without surrendering knowledge of the premises, since the inference depends on a contested logical framework rather than on pure, uncontroversial logic.

The tension I keep returning to is between Case 2 and Case 3: in both, an agent fails to derive an obvious logical consequence from known premises, yet in one I excuse the failure and in the other I condemn it. Meanwhile Case 4 makes me wonder whether resource bounds excuse ignorance only for biological agents with cognitive architecture, or for any system with bounded computation.
