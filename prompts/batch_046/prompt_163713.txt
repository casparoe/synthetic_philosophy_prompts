You serve on your university's Research Integrity Appeals Board. Before Thursday's meeting you must circulate a short written opinion recommending exactly how the board should resolve a procedural dispute that has split the panel, with a clear bottom line the board can vote on and implement by the end of the week. The concrete case: Dr. Elena Voss received a private reprimand five years ago for salami slicing—dividing a minimal dataset across three journal articles with insufficient disclosure of overlap—and now chairs the panel adjudicating a junior colleague accused of duplicate data publication. Since her reprimand, Voss has become an outspoken critic of publication-ethics hypocrisy among senior scientists, arguing that researchers who have themselves erred are uniquely positioned to detect and judge misconduct. Two board members contend that her prior violation, combined with her public advocacy, creates a standing-to-blame problem: she lacks the moral standing to condemn or penalize another researcher for essentially the same practice, and her presence undermines the procedural legitimacy of the hearing. Two others reply that institutional adjudication depends on procedural authority, domain expertise, and transparency, not on the adjudicator's moral purity; they warn that requiring spotless personal records would disqualify experienced members and set an impossible standard. Your memo should recommend a concrete course of action—whether Voss should be recused, remain with specific conditions, or be replaced by an alternate chair with an explicit timeline—supported by reasoning that takes the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it. In particular, engage the philosophical distinction between forfeiting standing to blame through hypocrisy and merely suffering diminished credibility, and address whether being a reformed wrongdoer strengthens, weakens, or is irrelevant to institutional authority in this context. The recommendation must be actionable: state precisely what the board should do by Thursday, who should execute it, and what should be communicated to the accused researcher and to Voss herself. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the board needs evaluable reasons, not references it cannot verify.
