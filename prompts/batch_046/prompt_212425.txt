I cover science and ideas for a small magazine, and I was prepping a story on skepticism when I listened to a philosophy podcast summarizing Wittgenstein's On Certainty. The host — who seemed sharp but wasn't a specalist — laid out hinge epistemology like this: Wittgenstein thinks every belief system swings on hinge propositions, things like the earth having existed for many years or me having two hands, which aren't supported by evidence but instead make evidence possible in the first place. He compared them to default priors in Bayesian reasoning: you don't justify the prior, you just update within it. The host said Wittgenstein insists these hinges are neither true nor false, since they sit outside the language-game where truth applies, and that skepticism fails not because it's false but because it's a category mistake — like trying to run a significance test on your null hypothesis. He went further and claimed that because hinges are just the things that stand fast for us, different cultures can have completely different ones with no rational arbitration, which puts Wittgenstein closer to a quietist relativism than to Moore's common-sense defense.
