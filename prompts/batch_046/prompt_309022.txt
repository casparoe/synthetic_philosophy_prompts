I'm preparing a decision-tree taxonomy for an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar on how suffering-focused moral priorities relate to the meta-ethical problem of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable. The tree should sort positions by what they actually claim about concrete trade-offs, not by the labels people adopt.

Please construct the taxonomy in indented outline form, with each internal branch labeled by the concrete diagnostic claim or hypothetical case that splits it. The root question should be whether pleasure and pain are measurable on a single cardinal scale that permits direct cancellation. From there, branch on whether suffering-reduction enjoys lexical priority over pleasure-promotion; if yes, branch further on whether that priority is absolute or governed by a suffering-threshold (i.e., lexical only above or below a specified baseline); and—crucially—whether incommensurability itself removes all moral weight from pleasure or merely blocks direct comparison.

Requirements:

- Include exactly six to eight leaves. Each leaf must display: (1) a descriptive position name, using an established label only if one genuinely exists in the literature (e.g., classical hedonistic utilitarianism, pure lexical negative utilitarianism) and marking the rest "unnamed" or "unexplored"; (2) one sentence articulating its most awkward or counter-intuitive commitment.
- At least one leaf must represent a logically coherent but apparently unoccupied view: an incommensurabilist "dimensional-veto" position that treats suffering and pleasure as belonging to separate axiological dimensions, so that causing suffering creates a localized, action-specific veto but does not globally outweigh all pleasure. Diagnose whether this vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.
- Wherever two named positions land on the same branch and the tree cannot separate them, flag the ambiguity explicitly and state what further diagnostic question would be needed to split them.
- For any leaf relying on a precise suffering-threshold, note whether the threshold is sharp or vague, and state the awkward commitment that follows (for example, whether a world exactly at the threshold is morally indistinguishable from one infinitesimally below it).
- Every split should be phrased as a yes/no or either/or answer that a respondent could give without knowing philosophical terminology—use brief hypothetical scenarios rather than technical labels.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, author attributions, or citations. Do not quote any text verbatim. The taxonomy should depend only on logical structure and clearly described commitments.
- Keep the tone formal and the outline readable without diagrams. Note the intended audience (advanced undergraduates familiar with basic normative ethics but not with the negative-utilitarian literature) in one opening line.
