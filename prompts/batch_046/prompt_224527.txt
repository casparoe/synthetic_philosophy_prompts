I'm developing a curatorial framework for a comparative atrocity-studies archive, and I need to determine whether cross-case moral comparison is conceptually legitimate or inherently corrupting. Rather than ask generally, I want to test my own verdicts for consistency.

Here are my judgments, case by case.

On scale comparison: Comparing the estimated direct mortality of the transatlantic slave trade (roughly 12–15 million) with that of the Belgian Congo Free State (roughly 10 million) strikes me as historically necessary and morally permissible, despite measurement uncertainty. But comparing either figure to the Holocaust's approximately six million and concluding that the slave trade was therefore worse strikes me as obscene—not because the arithmetic is wrong, but because mortality scale is the wrong axis for that particular pairing.

On intentionality comparison: Comparing the industrial-bureaucratic intentionality of the Holocaust to the famine-policy intentionality of the Holodomor seems to me a legitimate analytical move. Comparing that same Holocaust intentionality to the structural negligence of the 1943 Bengal famine also seems legitimate. But comparing all three and ranking them on a single intentionality score strikes me as conceptually confused.

On temporal-structure comparison: Comparing the compressed intensity of the Rwandan genocide (approximately 800,000 dead in roughly 100 days) with the extended attrition of the Great Leap Forward famine (tens of millions over several years) strikes me as illuminating rather than obscene, provided we do not treat duration-adjusted mortality as the only relevant metric. Yet comparing the intergenerational trauma duration of Australian residential school systems with that of Canadian residential school systems feels to me simultaneously necessary for solidarity and dangerously reductive of specific Indigenous histories.

On perpetrator comparison: Comparing perpetrator profiles in the Cambodian genocide (ideological cadre, systematic purging) with those in the Rwandan genocide (rapidly mobilized civilian populations) strikes me as ethically necessary for prevention studies. But using those profiles to generate a moral quality of perpetration ranking that then modifies our assessment of victim suffering strikes me as a category error.

On uniqueness-claims comparison: I think both the Holocaust and the Armenian genocide can be unique in specific respects—industrial killing method versus state-template novelty—without being incomparable. But I think arguing that Event A is worse because it is more unique is a logical error that corrupts comparison from the start.

The tension in all this: I accept scale-comparison for slave trade versus Congo, reject scale-comparison for slave trade versus Holocaust, accept intentionality-comparison across famine and genocide cases, and reject intentionality-scoring across those same cases. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the purpose of comparison (historical explanation, memorialization, prevention, moral education), the dimension being compared, or the relationship between comparator and audience—that captures this pattern? What does it imply for the comparison that keeps shifting in my mind: comparing the representational legacy (how an event is depicted in museums, textbooks, and memorial architecture) of the Nanjing Massacre versus that of the Katyn Forest massacre? Sometimes I think comparing representational legacies is the only legitimate form of comparison; other times I think it reduces lived suffering to media studies.

Can you articulate one or more precise principles that organize these verdicts, specify which of my judgments each principle must overturn if they conflict, and apply them to the Nanjing/Katyn representational-legacy case and to one further comparison I have not mentioned—say, comparing the ecological destruction associated with the Putumayo rubber-boom atrocities with the ecological destruction associated with Soviet collectivization famines?
