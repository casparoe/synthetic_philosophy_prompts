I have been thinking on my own about the way philosophers use thought experiments—what they call intuition pumps—to make an idea feel unavoidable. In the discussions I have followed, people often say that an intuition pump works like a laboratory experiment: you isolate one factor, hold the rest fixed in imagination, and observe the result in your own judgment, just as a scientist isolates a variable and measures its effect. They invoke this analogy to defend the reliability of thought experiments against the objection that they are merely invented stories; if controlled conditions justify scientific belief, they say, then controlled imagination should justify philosophical belief. I would like to understand precisely how far this holds. Please provide a correspondence table—what in the thought experiment plays the role of the laboratory, the isolated variable, the control conditions, the measuring instrument, and the replication protocol? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks: for instance, whether the fact that the observer in a mental experiment is simultaneously the subject being tested means there is no independent check, and whether the absence of a shared physical apparatus undermines the idea of replication. Finally, tell me whether the defense of intuition pumps—that they deserve the same authority as laboratory findings—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the laboratory comparison was doing all the argumentative work.
