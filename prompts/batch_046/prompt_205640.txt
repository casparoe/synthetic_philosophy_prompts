I have been tracking the published judgments of an evolutionary anthropologist who writes on the transition from foraging to food production, and I want to test whether her position is consistent. On the Natufian shift to cereal storage and cultivation in the Levant around 10,000 BCE, she holds that it was not a mistake in its initial form: mobility persisted for centuries, health impacts were modest, and hierarchy only emerged much later with institutionalized surplus; the error, if any, lies in the subsequent lock-in, not in cultivation itself. On the adoption of intensive taro and yam horticulture in the New Guinea highlands roughly nine millennia ago, she judges it a clear success—labor is high but collectively shared, protein intake is maintained through pig exchange, burial patterns show no hereditary stratification, and the affluence framework applies well—so the agricultural-mistake thesis fails there entirely. On the Hemudu culture's turn to rice in the Yangtze delta around seven thousand years ago, she offers a split verdict: skeletal and dental evidence reveals sharp biological stress, yet settlement and mortuary data indicate egalitarian structures persisted for over a millennium, meaning it is a nutritional mistake but not a social or political one. Finally, on the twentieth-century forced transition of Hadza and related East African foragers into maize-based subsistence, she calls it an unambiguous mistake, but stresses that the primary harm was the coercive destruction of mobility and autonomous decision-making, with nutritional change being secondary.

The new case is the Lapita expansion into Remote Oceania between roughly 1100 and 800 BCE, recently synthesized in bioarchaeological literature. Seafaring forager-colonists intentionally adopted taro and yam cultivation on previously uninhabited islands as a strategic, reversible colonization buffer rather than a demographic trap. The skeletal record shows rapid stature decline, increased dental caries, and infectious-disease markers relative to Near Oceanic ancestors, yet settlement patterns remain dispersed, monumental architecture and elite burial goods are absent for over a millennium, inter-island mobility continues, and population densities stay low; there is no evidence of coercion or institutional lock-in.

Given this track record, what would she say about the Lapita case—an unqualified mistake, a qualified biological mistake, or not a mistake at all? Which implicit rule best explains her four earlier verdicts: one that treats biological stress and social stratification as jointly necessary for a mistake; one that treats the destruction of practical autonomy and reversibility as the decisive factor regardless of health outcomes; or a third rule that weights net welfare across health, equality, and autonomy? For each rule, state precisely what it predicts about Lapita. Then give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors it over its rivals, and identify one further case—real or carefully constructed—that would best distinguish the leading rule from its closest competitor. I am less interested in a simple prediction than in the structure of the inference.
