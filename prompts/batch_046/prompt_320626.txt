I have been thinking a lot about path dependence — the idea that once history takes a particular turn, it becomes locked in and the same outcomes would not have arrived otherwise. In nearly every explanation I come across, people reach for the railway switchyard analogy: a train is routed onto one track by an early throw of the switch, after which every station it reaches is determined, while the other branches remain permanently unreachable. This is used to argue that small initial differences, like the adoption of one technology over another or the outcome of a particular political decision, cascade into irreversible structures. I am trying to apply this to a situation I have been observing: the way an old city's water rights were allocated in a single nineteenth-century agreement, and how that early division now constrains every modern drought response, even though the climate and population have changed beyond recognition. Before I accept that the past is truly locked in, I want to understand how far the railway analogy actually holds. Could you lay out, in careful detail and without using headings or lists, what corresponds to what — the switch, the engineer, the train, the abandoned tracks, the cost of building new lines — and then show precisely where the mapping breaks? In particular, does the analogy quietly assume there is only one train, that no tracks ever reconnect, or that reversing is impossible? And if those disanalogies are substantial, does the conclusion that history could not have reached a different present survive, or was the image of the switchyard carrying the argument all along? Please write your answer as continuous prose, as in a letter, with no bullet points, bold text, or tables.
