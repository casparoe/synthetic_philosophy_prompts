I am a retired physicist who has spent the last several years moving from experimental work into philosophy of language and epistemology, and I now lead a small graduate reading group—twelve people, ninety minutes, once a fortnight—where we try to do more than summarize papers. Our recent sessions have kept returning to the norms of assertion: whether one must know that p to assert it, whether true belief suffices, or whether the norm is something graded in between. What I want is not another comparison of Williamson against defenders of weaker norms, but a structured in-class exercise that makes the tension visceral before anyone names it.

Design a runnable game or role-play with explicit roles, rules, and a non-monetary payoff scheme—perhaps a credibility ledger combined with a “silence tax” for withholding testimony that others need—so that participants experience both the social cost of excessive epistemic caution and the reputational cost of over-assertion in real time. The rules should create situations where a participant has strong but not quite knowledge-level evidence, where withholding the claim blocks a collective decision, and where asserting it risks a falsehood that degrades group trust; the group should feel the pull of different norms through their own strategic choices before the debrief maps those moments onto the knowledge norm, the belief norm, and the truth norm. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, the materials needed, the debrief questions that connect specific choices to philosophical positions, an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time and how to correct it, and a frank admission of which feature of the debate—perhaps the gradability of belief or the irreducibly social function of testimony—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me.

Because I have grown impatient with facilitators who hide behind false neutrality: take a committed position. In your design choices and in the debrief, argue explicitly for whether the knowledge norm survives the experience your rules generate, defend that interpretation with reasons, and do not present all three norms as equally viable once the activity is over.
