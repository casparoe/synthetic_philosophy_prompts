I am preparing a long-form piece on the philosophical foundations of predictive neuroscience, and I have become stuck on a conceptual knot that standard popular accounts tend to gloss over. On one side, predictive processing and active inference models describe cognition in terms of hierarchical prediction errors, precision-weighted updates, and the minimization of surprise relative to prior beliefs and preferred outcomes—language that is deeply mentalistic. On the other side, eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires insists that folk-psychological propositional attitudes are theoretical posits of a pre-scientific psychology that mature neuroscience will eventually discard entirely, leaving only patterns of neural activity and their dynamical relations.

Given that tension, I would like you to outline the most important implications of adopting a rigorous eliminative stance specifically within the predictive processing framework. If we genuinely eliminate beliefs and desires—treating them not as hidden causes but as obsolete explanatory fictions—how should we reconceptualize the functional roles usually assigned to prior beliefs, precision weighting, and preferred states in active inference? Does stripping away mental content leave predictive processing with a purely mechanistic vocabulary that can still support its normative and teleological claims about error minimization, or does the framework implicitly depend on the very propositional attitudes it claims to replace? And if the latter, what would that mean for the broader project of naturalizing intentionality through hierarchical Bayesian brain models?
