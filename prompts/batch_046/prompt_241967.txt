I'm drafting an essay for an advanced epistemology discussion group on whether Gettier-style counterexamples to the justified-true-belief analysis actually represent one uniform failure or two structurally distinct ones. My working distinction is between "premise-failure" cases—where a reasoner derives a true conclusion from a justified false belief, and the conclusion's truth is secured by an accidental environmental match that masks the false premise—and "tracking-failure" cases—where the belief is true and supported by a generally reliable method, but in this particular token the causal or modal link between the justification and the fact is disrupted, so the truth is only accidentally aligned with the reasoning process.

To make this concrete, I've constructed two original scenarios. The first involves an agricultural surveyor who consults a county crop census that is generally accurate but wrong for this specific year due to a recent rotation policy; she then visually confirms the old crop along a highway, not realizing that a local harvest festival has temporarily replanted decorative versions of that exact crop for tourists, so her visual evidence accidentally converges with the false census data. The second involves a microbiology technician who uses a spectroscopic identification protocol that is globally well-calibrated, but on this particular sample a rare electromagnetic interference cancels out an error in the preparation stage, yielding the correct bacterial identification through a causal pathway that is disconnected from the protocol's usual reliability. I argue that the first case turns on a masked false premise being rescued by coincidence, while the second turns on a reliable process being locally decoupled from its success conditions.

I'm concerned that this bifurcation might just be a redescription of the standard "epistemic luck" diagnosis, and that modal safety or sensitivity conditions might already absorb both without needing two categories. I'd also like feedback on whether these invented examples are vivid enough to carry the distinction, whether my framing implicitly biases the discussion toward reliabilist rather than internalist intuitions, and whether there's a well-known conceptual overlap I'm reinventing. Please provide detailed comments in American English on the coherence of the distinction, the clarity and fairness of the two examples, and whether the essay successfully resists collapsing all Gettier problems into a single diagnosis—without supplying a bibliography or citation list, though you may flag conceptual parallels from the literature if you recognize them.
