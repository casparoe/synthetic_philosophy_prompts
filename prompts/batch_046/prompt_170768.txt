I have been thinking on my own about why people claim artificial intelligence cannot reason the way humans do, and in particular about what is called the frame problem. I discussed it with two friends, Jordan and Casey, neither of whom has formal training in the field. Their exchange went as follows.

(1) Jordan argued that the frame problem is largely resolved in practice. Modern systems, he said, rely on learned representations and attention mechanisms rather than explicit symbolic rules, so they do not need to enumerate everything that remains unchanged when an action occurs. The internal state updates implicitly within the relevant context, which is sufficient for functioning.

(2) Casey replied that this confuses practical performance with philosophical resolution. The frame problem was never primarily about computing speed or storage; it concerns the logical difficulty of specifying all the non-effects of an action. A learning algorithm may approximate correct updates, but it does not eliminate the need, in principle, to determine which beliefs ought to be preserved. Approximating a solution is not the same as having one.

(3) Jordan responded that humans address the problem in exactly this approximate manner—through intuition and contextual judgment, not through logical enumeration of every unchanged fact. If an artificial system employs world models and selective attention to track the changes that matter, then it is handling the problem in the same functional way people do. The standard for solving the problem should not demand explicit logical completeness that human cognition itself lacks.

(4) Casey conceded the parallel but introduced what she called the shadow frame problem. The difficulty, she said, is not only tracking what has changed, but recognizing when something that has not changed becomes relevant precisely because of the change. For example, a system might correctly register that a vase has broken, yet fail to notice that the unchanged fact that the floor is wooden is now critically important because of water spilling from the flowers that were in the vase. Humans often miss these silent relevance shifts as well, but they compensate through narrative and contextual reasoning that connects disparate facts. Artificial systems lack this backup mechanism, which means the shadow problem remains genuinely unsolved even if the original framing issue is managed.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then say which speaker is more right overall, identify what both are missing, and assess whether Casey's shadow frame problem introduces a genuinely distinct challenge or merely restates Jordan's point in different language.
