My colleague has ruled on five cases about reality and harm in virtual environments. She holds that a virtual sword in an MMORPG is not a sword; that a high-fidelity VR scan of a destroyed ancient statue is not the statue itself, so erasing the scan is not vandalism of the original; that deleting virtual property for which real currency was paid can cause real economic harm; that a VR simulation inducing vertigo and nausea through visual-vestibular conflict causes real physical harm despite no external bodily contact; and that sustained targeted harassment in a text-based virtual space can inflict genuine psychological injury that counts as real harm.

The new case: in an immersive haptic-feedback VR platform, one user subjects another to a non-consensual simulated touch. There is no physical contact with the victim’s actual body outside the headset and gloves; the interaction consists only of rendered visuals and localized force-feedback signals. The victim, however, has a documented trauma history and subsequently experiences lasting psychological injury. The perpetrator claims nothing real occurred, since no bodily intrusion took place.

Predict her verdict—would she treat this as a real harm? Identify at least two plausible principles that accommodate her earlier judgments equally well, state what each predicts about this case, give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case that would clearly separate those two principles.
