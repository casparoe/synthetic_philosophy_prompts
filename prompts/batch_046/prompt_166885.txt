Please give me your credence that applying the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result to philosophical reasoning genuinely undermines the epistemic weight of persistent expert disagreement about the compatibility of determinism and moral responsibility. Specifically, I mean the claim that if two rational philosophers shared common priors, possessed private but not commonly known reasoning, and then disclosed their posterior beliefs fully, they could not rationally maintain opposing positions; hence the observed durability of disagreement in the literature must be attributed mainly to failures of those conditions—non-common priors, bounded rationality, or strategic misrepresentation—rather than to any genuine indeterminacy in the concept of responsibility itself. I want one number, decomposed into three terms: first, the probability that the conditions for the no-trade result can be approximately satisfied in philosophical debate about free will; second, the probability that, given those conditions, full disclosure would indeed eliminate disagreement rather than leave it intact; third, the probability that failures of those conditions are a better explanation of observed disagreement than conceptual indeterminacy or irreducible value pluralism. Assign each term a rough value with a single line of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, identify the term that moves your number most, and state what kind of evidence—an empirical study of philosopher reasoning, formal modelling, or conceptual analysis—would shift that term. Write entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and use British English throughout.
