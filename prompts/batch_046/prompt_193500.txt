Exam question: Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist critique is often reduced to the fire-and-cotton example, but it depends on the Ashʿarite distinction between taʾthīr (intrinsic causal efficacy) and muqāranah (mere concomitance), and on the doctrine of continuous creation (khalq jadīd). In your answer: (a) explain why Al-Ghazālī denies taʾthīr to all created things and what function ʿāda (divine custom) serves; (b) discuss whether empirical science can still predict regularities if nature possesses no autonomous causal powers; (c) assess whether Al-Ghazālī's rejection of intrinsic powers makes him an anticipation of modern interventionist theories of causation (e.g., Woodward), or whether his commitment to khalq jadīd undermines that comparison.

Student answer: Al-Ghazālī denies that fire, cotton, or any created thing possesses taʾthīr—an intrinsic power to produce its effect. The conjunction of fire with burning is only muqāranah: God creates the burning directly on the occasion of fire's presence, in accordance with His stable custom (ʿāda). Because this custom is consistent, scientists can predict outcomes reliably without attributing autonomous powers to natural objects. Al-Ghazālī also permits medicine and natural craft to operate through genuine secondary causation (asbāb), so long as we recognize God as the primary cause behind them. His position is therefore structurally close to modern interventionism: both reject that objects contain intrinsic dispositions, and both account for causation through observable, manipulable regularities. The only real difference is Al-Ghazālī's theological addition that God continuously re-creates every accident moment by moment (khalq jadīd), but this functions merely as a metaphysical overlay and does not alter the predictive or practical structure of empirical science.
