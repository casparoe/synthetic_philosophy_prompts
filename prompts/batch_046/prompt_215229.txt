In my political economy seminar, a visiting lecturer summarized the socialist calculation debate in the following way. Mises argued that rational economic calculation is impossible under socialism because, without private property in capital goods, there are no market prices and hence no common denominator for comparing heterogeneous costs. Lange responded not by denying computation, but by proposing that a central planning board could replicate market outcomes through an iterative trial-and-error procedure: administrators set prices, observe shortages and surpluses, and adjust until supply equals demand, thereby approximating Walrasian equilibrium administratively. Hayek then objected that the system of simultaneous equations is too vast and fluid for any board to solve in real time, yet the lecturer claimed Hayek conceded that with sufficient computing power this obstacle would vanish, reducing the debate to a temporary technological bottleneck. The lecturer further argued that because the planning board retains epistemic dominance over the parameters of adjustment—defining which goods are substitutable, which costs are relevant, and which errors warrant correction—the procedure never genuinely replicates the discovery process that competitive exchange performs.

This account sounds coherent, but several points strike me as suspiciously tidy. Is it a fair summary? Specifically, I would like an accuracy audit separating what is correct from what is subtly distorted: first, whether Lange's trial-and-error mechanism was intended as an administrative approximation to Walrasian tâtonnement or as a conceptually different institutional arrangement; second, whether Hayek's critique was primarily about computational speed and data volume, or whether it concerned dispersed, tacit, and context-specific knowledge that central authorities cannot acquire regardless of computing capacity; and third, whether framing the planner's authority over adjustment rules as epistemic dominance is a legitimate interpretation of Hayek's institutional argument, or whether it introduces an anachronistic vocabulary that mischaracterizes the nature of his objection. A defender of Lange or Hayek might object differently—please indicate where.
