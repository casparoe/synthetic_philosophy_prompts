I'm preparing material for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar on personal identity and first-person reference, and I want a poem that performs rather than merely illustrates Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment. In the scenario I have in mind, a brain has been extracted and suspended in a nutrient vat, linked by radio transmitter to a body walking in the world; the central difficulty is that the speaker cannot determine whether "I" is indexed to the vat, to the body, or to the loop connecting them.

Write this as a villanelle. Let one refrain articulate the brain's claim that it is where the neural signals terminate—submerged, wired, thinking—while the other refrain articulates the body's claim that it is where sensory feedback originates—walking, touching, being seen. Alternate these refrains through the five tercets so that each stanza appears to settle the location of the self, only to have the other refrain overturn it in the next. Choose a rhyme scheme that pairs words denoting place or boundary—perhaps "skull" with "shell," "wires" with "fires"—so that the poem's sonic architecture repeatedly crosses the vat-world boundary. In the closing quatrain, bring both refrains together in the final two lines, arranged so that they sit grammatically adjacent yet remain ontologically incompatible; do not resolve the ambiguity, but let the poem end with the question of location suspended exactly where Dennett's puzzle begins. To avoid a mere textbook summary, include a detail that is not part of the standard retelling: treat the radio link as fully bidirectional, so that the body in the world receives phantom sensations of being submerged in fluid, while the brain in the vat receives phantom sensations of pavement and air. Let that symmetrical confusion shape the imagery, but keep the philosophical point precise enough that a specialist in philosophy of mind will recognize the de se ambiguity being staged structurally rather than named.
