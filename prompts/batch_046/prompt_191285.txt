Benatar's asymmetry holds that for any being, the presence of harm is bad and the presence of benefit is good; but for beings who never come to exist, the absence of harm is good—there is no sufferer—while the absence of benefit is not bad—there is no one deprived. First, explain the asymmetry and carefully distinguish it from the procreation asymmetry—the normative claim that we have reason not to create suffering beings but no corresponding reason to create happy ones. Second, apply the framework to a decision under uncertainty: a couple can either conceive a child who will have a 75 percent chance of a flourishing life and a 25 percent chance of severe suffering, or remain childless. Does Benatar's view yield a determinate verdict? In your answer, identify any hidden premise about counterfactual baselines or inter-world comparison that the evaluation might smuggle in, and note whether Benatar's actual-state evaluation can be applied to probabilistic outcomes without importing an expected-value framework.

Here is the student's answer:

Benatar's asymmetry is an axiological thesis: presence of pain is bad, presence of pleasure is good, absence of pain is good even without a beneficiary, and absence of pleasure is not bad even without a victim. The procreation asymmetry is just the normative expression of this—if absence of pleasure isn't bad, we have no duty to create happy people, but since absence of pain is good, we should avoid creating people who will suffer—so the two stand or fall together. Applying this to the couple: if they conceive and the child suffers, that is a definite harm. If they conceive and the child flourishes, that is good. If they do not conceive, there is no one to suffer, which is good, and no one to enjoy pleasure, which is not bad. Because there is a 25 percent risk of severe harm and only a 75 percent chance of benefit, the expected outcome is negative once we weight the harm more heavily than the absence of benefit; therefore Benatar's view clearly recommends remaining childless. The hidden premise is that we must compare the world with the child to the world without, but Benatar rejects comparing the same person across worlds. Instead he evaluates absence directly, without a bearer, so there is no non-identity problem, and the evaluation is consistent: non-existence is not worse for anyone, but existence with suffering is worse for the one who exists.
