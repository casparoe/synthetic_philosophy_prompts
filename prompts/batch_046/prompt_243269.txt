You are delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop convened to honor the retirement of Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of strategic risk who spent four decades arguing that nuclear deterrence is best understood not as a stable equilibrium but as a sustained moral paradox: rational agents must institutionalize a credible capacity for collective irrationality—specifically, the ability to commit to catastrophic retaliation even when doing so would be self-defeating—because without that credible commitment, the threat itself collapses. The room is divided: half are strategic-studies scholars intimately familiar with Schelling's analysis of brinkmanship; the other half are family members, graduate students from other fields, and philosophers of action who have no background in deterrence theory. Your address must let the philosophical idea do genuine structural work, shaping both what you praise about her career and what you deliberately do not sanitize: do not diminish the moral horror inherent in a system whose stability depends on making oneself genuinely unable to back down automatically, but also do not deliver a lecture that loses the non-specialists. To avoid relying on well-worn framing, inject a conceptual distinction of your own design: frame her legacy through the difference between threats that bind the self—commitments that constrain one's own future options in order to make the threat credible to an adversary—and threats that bind the other—mere signaling intended to alter an opponent's calculations without altering one's own decision architecture. Use this distinction to argue that she treated deterrence theory as closer to a theory of self-binding than to a theory of coercion. Write a five-minute speech that navigates both audiences. After the remarks, include a brief reflective note stating how confident you are in your interpretation of her position, singling out the specific point—particularly your self-binding versus other-binding distinction—about which you are least sure, and indicating whether you regard that framing as a faithful rendering of her view or as an interpretive liberty taken for the occasion.
