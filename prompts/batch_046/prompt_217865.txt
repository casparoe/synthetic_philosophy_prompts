Here is an argument about Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater in favor of the multiple drafts model, but it seems to prove too much—perhaps forcing us back into the very theater it denies.

(1) If there is no Cartesian theater—no single spatiotemporal point where all information is presented to a central observer—then there is no unique “stream of consciousness” belonging to the subject at any moment. There are only parallel, competing narrative drafts.

(2) Consider a modified partial-report scenario: a subject is briefly shown incompatible color fields (red on the left, green on the right), then cued after display offset to report one side. On the multiple drafts view, one draft constructs “left was red” and another “right was green.” Now suppose the subject later reports a blended experience—“the whole field was reddish-green”—that never existed in the stimulus or in either individual draft.

(3) If there is genuinely no central theater, then there is no authoritative version of consciousness against which to measure this blended report as wrong. The blended report is just one more draft among others, with no special normative status.

(4) Therefore, on the multiple drafts model, there can be no genuine introspective error: every report is merely one draft among equals.

(5) But introspective error clearly occurs—subjects misreport colors, miss stimuli, or later correct themselves—so there must be a fact of the matter about which report is correct.

(6) The only way to secure such a fact of the matter is to posit a Cartesian theater where one version is presented for comparison and evaluation.

(7) So the multiple drafts model either eliminates all introspective error (absurd) or collapses back into the Cartesian theater.

Where exactly does this argument fail? I would prefer to keep (1) and (5) if possible.
