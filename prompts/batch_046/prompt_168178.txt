For a philosophical interlude in a novel I am drafting, I have constructed an argument that applies Nāgārjuna's catuskoti to Madhyamaka's own doctrine of emptiness, and I find it suspicious because the conclusion—that the system is self-refuting—feels too neat, yet I cannot locate the error. Here are the steps as I have them: (1) The tetralemma applies to all dharmas with respect to svabhāva: nothing can be said to exist, not exist, both, or neither with inherent existence. (2) From this universal application, Madhyamaka derives śūnyatā: all phenomena dependently originate (pratītyasamutpāda) and are therefore empty of svabhāva. (3) Emptiness is not presented as a mere conventional claim but as paramārthasatya, the ultimate truth about reality. (4) Apply the tetralemma now to emptiness itself: either it possesses svabhāva, or it lacks svabhāva, or both, or neither. (5) If emptiness possesses svabhāva, then at least one entity is inherently existent and uncaused, contradicting the universal claim in (1) and (2). (6) If emptiness lacks svabhāva, then it dependently originates—specifically, in dependence on the conceptual distinction between conventional and ultimate truth. (7) But whatever originates dependently in this manner is merely conventional (saṃvṛtisatya), not ultimately true. (8) Therefore, lacking svabhāva, emptiness collapses into conventional designation (prajñaptimātra) and cannot be paramārthasatya. (9) The standard Madhyamaka defense is that emptiness is empty of itself, often rendered with the formula śūnyatā śūnyatayā śūnyā, which is taken to mean the tetralemma does not apply to emptiness. (10) Yet the original justification for applying the tetralemma without exception was precisely that svabhāva is incoherent for any entity whatsoever. (11) So either Madhyamaka introduces an unmotivated ad hoc exception, or it concedes that emptiness is conventional, or it accepts that svabhāva exists uniquely in emptiness. (12) Each option destroys the coherence of the two-truths doctrine. Where does this argument fail—whether in a hidden equivocation between ontological and semantic registers, a scope shift in how the tetralemma applies, or in one of the premises that is less costly to abandon than the others? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep both the universal scope of the tetralemma and the claim that emptiness is genuinely ultimate, but I want to know exactly what I am paying to preserve both. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—identify the flawed step or the necessary revision—then lay out the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
