Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains—about Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment: the figure suspended in void, blindfolded and without tactile or proprioceptive contact, who nonetheless remains immediately certain that he exists. Use as refrains the lines “I am, though nothing reaches me” and “The soul knows without the limb,” allowing them to shift in meaning across repetitions: first as bare, pre-reflective self-awareness; then as the suspicious absence of any bodily content within that awareness; and finally as the contested metaphysical inference that the knowing subject is a substantial entity ontologically independent of corporeal causation. In the tercets, stage the tension between an epistemic reading—according to which Avicenna demonstrates only the immediacy and non-inferential character of self-knowledge, closer to a phenomenological description—and a metaphysical reading that takes the argument to prove the soul’s separation from the body, a reading often projected backward from Cartesian dualism. Be careful not to misattribute to Avicenna an explicit denial that the body plays any causal role in cognition, and keep the argument structurally distinct from Descartes’s cogito by emphasizing that the flying man never doubts his body because he never notices it at all. The closing quatrain should leave the metaphysical inference structurally unresolved, so that the poem’s form enacts the very ambiguity it describes. After the poem, include a brief explicit note stating precisely where the verse takes liberties with Avicenna’s original formulation—particularly with respect to the epistemic versus metaphysical interpretation of the argument, and with respect to any anachronistic framing that risks collapsing the Islamic context into later Cartesian terms—so that a reader can identify exactly where the reasoning is weakest and what consideration or alternative reading would lead to a different verdict.
