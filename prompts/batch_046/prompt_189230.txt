As a retired physicist who has turned to philosophy only in the last few years, I have been struck by Peter Singer's analogy of the drowning child and by the claim that distance should not diminish a duty of rescue. In my scientific training, physical laws operate uniformly across space; encountering the widespread moral intuition that obligations weaken with proximity has made me want to understand what institutional structures quietly depend on that intuition rather than treating it as primitive.

Consider the institutional and legal pattern that seems unremarkable in most Anglo-American systems: there is a stringent, largely distance-independent duty not to harm, including by omission within special relationships, yet there is no general legal duty to rescue a stranger—even when the rescue would be nearly costless, as in walking past a shallow pond where a child is drowning. At the same time, local emergency services and public rescue funding operate on an implicit proximity-first logic that rarely subjects itself to cost-effectiveness analysis, treating the nearby victim as an unconditional priority, whereas global health charities and international aid bodies are routinely assessed by metrics that weigh statistical lives saved at great distance against local expenditures. The result is a dual practice: the law refuses to mandate distant or even nearby stranger-rescue, while institutional resource allocation behaves as though moral urgency has a sharp local gradient.

What philosophical commitments does this arrangement presuppose? Does it implicitly rely on something like a "moral topology"—a notion I have been experimenting with, borrowed from field theory—in which obligations possess a strong potential well near the agent that decays rapidly with spatial or relational separation, falling below an activation threshold for distant strangers? Or can the asymmetry between harming and rescuing, and between local and global allocation, be reconstructed without any such metric, perhaps through a control-based or causation-based distinction? I ask you to reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in these practices, to distinguish strictly what they require from what merely fits them comfortably, to assess whether the legal and institutional parts are consistent with one another, and to say what would have to change in law and public policy if we replaced this framework with the uniform, distance-independent consequentialist view Singer's argument recommends. In particular, is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories of moral responsibility or value could license the same arrangements? And if one insists that outcomes matter to blameworthiness, as negligence law sometimes suggests, can that be reconciled with the refusal to penalize the failure to rescue?
