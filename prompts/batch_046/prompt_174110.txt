In sentencing and parole proceedings across many jurisdictions, boards treat visible emotional remorse as a primary mitigating factor, regard quiet internal reform without affective display as inadequate for leniency, and interpret principled refusal to apologize—whether from maintained innocence or moral objection to the court's authority—as aggravating evidence of poor character. What implicit theory of moral worth and of acting for the right reasons does this tripartite pattern presuppose: that worth is essentially affective, that it tracks reason-responsive dispositions regardless of display, or that it is a hybrid the practice does not consistently hold? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, check whether its three parts are philosophically consistent with one another, and specify what sentencing policy would have to change if moral worth were grounded in acting for the right reasons rather than in emotional performance. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
