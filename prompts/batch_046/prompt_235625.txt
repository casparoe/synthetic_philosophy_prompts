I cover philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I'm working out my own judgments about when Dennett's intentional stance applies to synthetic systems—case by case, not in the abstract. Here are my verdicts. Case One: a chess engine represents board states symbolically, evaluates positions as dangerous, and adjusts play accordingly; I say no literal belief is present, because the representation is too tightly coupled to computation and lacks any autonomous representational commitment. Case Two: a large language model consistently asserts that water boils at 100°C at standard pressure, corrects itself when given altitude data, and integrates this claim with related propositions in a locally coherent network; I lean toward a minimal, deflationary belief, not because of consciousness, but because the correction-tracking and inferential integration match belief's functional profile more closely than mere correlation. Case Three: a warehouse robot constructs a metric world-map, updates predictions about obstacle locations in real time, reroutes when predictions fail, and never articulates anything; I say yes, attributing beliefs about obstacles and desires about paths is predictive, parsimonious, and structurally analogous to animal cognition. Case Four: a dialogue agent uses fluent first-person language ("I prefer," "I think") and maintains apparent consistency across long conversations, yet inspection reveals no persistent internal state tracking those preferences—only sampling from a distribution conditioned on context; I deny belief attribution here, since the self-reference is interface design without representational backing. Case Five: a vision classifier with adversarial vulnerability classifies a panda image with imperceptible noise as a "gibbon" with high confidence, its internal activations being high-dimensional vectors with no propositional structure that tracks the presence of gibbons; I say no belief is present—there is representation, but not of the kind that can be true or false in the right way.

The difficulty is that these verdicts resist simple sorting. Cases Two and Three both receive a "yes," yet Two involves linguistic articulation without persistent internal state, while Three involves persistent representational state without language. Meanwhile Case One involves symbolic evaluation and gets a "no," and Case Four involves fluent language and gets a "no"—so language is not decisive, representation alone is not decisive, yet something distinguishes Two and Three from One and Four. I suspect the factor is functional integration with correction mechanisms rather than either articulability or raw computation, but I'm unsure whether that principle genuinely saves all five verdicts or forces me to overturn one—particularly the tension between Case Two and Case Four, where linguistic fluency seems to point in opposite directions despite both involving language models.

Can you state a precise principle—perhaps distinguishing representational commitment from mere predictive registration, or specifying the conditions under which the intentional stance is genuinely explanatory rather than ornamental—that captures this pattern? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed: a small, non-linguistic model that learns a causal model of its environment, updates it with counterfactual reasoning, and expresses outcomes only through behavior—would it have beliefs, or is articulability decisive after all? Please also push back plainly if my framing assumes that belief attribution requires conscious access or linguistic expression—assumptions that may be conceptually confused—rather than accommodating them politely. I want the principle, its verdicts on unconsidered cases, and an honest statement of which of my judgments it cannot accommodate.
