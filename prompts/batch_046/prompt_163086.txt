I'm taking a course on formal epistemology and got into a bit of a tangle with Jeffrey conditionalisation. My study group cooked up an argument that feels like it proves way too much, and I genuinely can't see where the slip is.

(1) Jeffrey conditionalisation is meant for when you get uncertain evidence — say, seeing a blurry shape and updating over duck versus rabbit with weights 0.6 and 0.4. (2) But every precise observation can be redescribed as an uncertain distribution over finer-grained possibilities. Instead of 'the coin landed heads', I observe 'it looks like heads with confidence 0.99', which distributes weight over heads, tails, and visual error. (3) So Jeffrey conditioning applies to literally every update, not just special 'uncertain' cases. (4) The problem is that Jeffrey conditioning is partition-dependant: if I split the space into 'working thermometer versus broken' versus 'broken by 1 degree, broken by 2 degrees, ..., working', I get totally different posteriors for the room temperature. (5) That means for any observation there are infinitely many valid Jeffrey updates, with no principalled way to pick the partition. (6) But then Jeffrey conditioning can't be a normative rule — it requires you to already have the 'right' ontology fixed before you update, which is exactly the kind of precise commitment it was invented to avoid. (7) Worse, if Jeffrey applies everywhere, then standard Bayes conditioning — updating on a precise event — is never actually correct; it's just an approximation for people too lazzy to model their uncertainty properly. (8) But Bayes conditioning is derivable from the probability axioms for precise events, so you can't dismiss it as mere laziness. (9) So Jeffrey conditioning either collapses into arbitrariness or swallows Bayes entirely, which seems absurd. Where does this go wrong?

I'd rather keep (1) and (4) if I can, since partition-dependence is a real problem I want to take seriously. But I need a definite answer: which step do you reject, and what's the cost of rejecting it? Don't just list options — take a position and tell me what I'm paying if you're right.
