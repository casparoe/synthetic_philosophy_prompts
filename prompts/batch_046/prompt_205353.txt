I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science with the enthusiasm of a late convert. I am helping to draft the final examination for an upper-level seminar on scientific metaphysics, and I want one essay question that students cannot answer by reciting the standard essentialism-versus-nominalism script about natural kinds. To force them into the weeds, I want the prompt to center on a conceptual tension I kept encountering in my own field: in materials science, categories such as "metal," "insulator," and "superconductor" are defined by macroscopic, technologically indispensable properties—electrical conductivity, zero resistance, specific heat signatures—yet these properties emerge from radically heterogeneous microscopic mechanisms (free-electron gases, band-structure gaps, Mott correlations, topological protection). At nanoscale boundaries, quantum confinement allows continuous tuning between regimes, so a single sample can sit in an ontologically ambiguous zone, but engineers and theorists never abandon the categories; they treat them as unavoidable framing devices. I want the exam to ask whether this combination—ontological heterogeneity at the base, practical indispensability at the surface, and scale-dependent blurring at the boundary—supports the claim that we are carving nature at its joints, or whether it demonstrates that our "joints" are artifacts of scale and purpose.

Please write the exam question, together with a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain. The question should require students to distinguish "joint-carving" understood as an ontological independence claim from "joint-carving" understood as an epistemic success condition, and to address whether scale-dependence undermines the reality of a kind or is compatible with a defensible realism. It should also require them to say whether practical indispensability provides any ontological weight, and how the materials-science case differs structurally from the biological examples—species, chemical elements—that dominate the philosophical literature. The marking scheme should allocate marks across at least five must-hit points, specify the total, and note the characteristic mistake that costs marks at each point. The level is an upper-level philosophy of science seminar; students should be assumed to be familiar with the homeostatic property cluster view and with essentialist accounts of natural kinds, but the question must be unanswerable by merely summarizing those positions. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from published texts.
