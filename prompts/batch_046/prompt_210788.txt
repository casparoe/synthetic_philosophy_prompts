For the final examination in my graduate seminar on perception and mind, I need a 60-minute essay question that tests whether students genuinely understand disjunctivism about perceptual experience rather than merely repeating the standard taxonomy of direct realism, representationalism, and sense-datum theory. The prompt should center on the argument from illusion, but I want it pushed into less familiar territory: specifically, an intermodal conflict scenario in which a subject simultaneously receives visually spherical and haptically cubic information about a single object and reports a unified phenomenal conflict—‘it is both round and square to me’—without being able to segregate the modalities. The exam question should ask students to reconstruct the argument from illusion in this intermodal setting, explain how a disjunctivist might deny that there is a shared mental state across the illusory and veridical conditions, and then evaluate whether this denial can be sustained without either (a) positing a hidden neutral medium that mediates cross-modal comparison, or (b) collapsing into a direct-realist view that cannot accommodate the subjective report of contradictory phenomenal characters. Along with the question, provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that makes the specific moves an A-grade response must contain. Finally, present the marking scheme as a numbered list of exactly five items, each expressed in one or two sentences with no introduction or conclusion; each item should name one must-hit conceptual distinction—such as separating the phenomenal-identity premise from the causal-explanation premise—and identify the precise student error it penalizes, for example treating the disjunctivist reply as an empirical denial of brain-state similarity rather than a conceptual denial of shared phenomenal kind.
