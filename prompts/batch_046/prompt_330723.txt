Here are my judgments about when the independence axiom should govern rational preference, together with one tension I cannot resolve. In Case 1, I prefer a guaranteed $1 million over an option that offers an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. In Case 2, facing the same outcomes but with the 89% common branch removed, I prefer the 10% shot at $5 million over an 11% shot at $1 million: the familiar reversal. In Case 3, the probabilities and payoffs are identical to Cases 1 and 2, but the decision is framed as a two-stage process in which a random draw first places me in either a high-stakes or low-stakes world, and I then choose within that world. Here I want my preference in each sub-world to match my verdict in Case 1 or Case 2 respectively, which means I reject independence across the stages and treat the sub-world choice as binding on its own. In Case 4, the outcomes are not monetary but temporal: I must choose between three years of guaranteed mild discomfort versus four years with an 80% chance of mild discomfort and a 20% chance of severe discomfort, and then in the parallel reduced-probability pair I prefer the riskier option—until I notice that the structure is parallel to Case 3. Here my verdict reverses: I take the guaranteed option in both pairs, treating the independence violation of Cases 1–2 as an artifact of how monetary gains are psychologically segmented rather than a deep rational failure, and I insist that within each stage of Case 4 independence should hold. The tension is that Case 3 and Case 4 seem structurally analogous—both involve compound structures with common consequences—yet in Case 3 I insist on violating independence across stages, while in Case 4 I insist on honoring it within each stage, and I do not see a principled distinction that separates them except an ad hoc appeal to framing or domain.
