I hold what I call the double-inversion view: Darwin showed that adaptive design requires no comprehending designer, and Turing showed that intelligent behavior requires no comprehending subject; together they suggest that human comprehension is not an independent causal layer but a retrospective narrative we impose on mechanistic processes that are fully explanatory without it—structurally parallel to how "design" was once projected onto evolutionary outputs. On this account, attributing understanding to a system performs no predictive or explanatory work that a sufficiently complete mechanistic description cannot do, and "comprehension" is best treated as an observer-relative gloss rather than a natural kind. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the arguments, empirical findings, or formal results that show comprehension makes a difference mechanistic competence cannot capture—for instance in conceptual restructuring, normative self-correction, or the generation of representations that reorganize the system's own architecture—and I want to know whether defenders of mechanistic dissolution should themselves concede any such cases. I also ask whether the view has been insulated by qualifications, such as redefining every apparent instance of understanding as "merely" complex computation, and whether that insulation renders it unfalsifiable in a damaging way. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, say what rivals predict that this view does not, and conclude with an honest assessment of whether the position risks collapsing the distinction between competence and comprehension into an untestable tautology.
