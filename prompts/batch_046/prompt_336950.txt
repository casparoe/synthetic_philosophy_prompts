I am curating a philosophical fiction anthology on the metaphysics of survival, and I need a short story that engages the classic puzzle about destructive teleportation without merely retelling the familiar thought experiment. Please write a narrative of approximately 900 words set in a research outpost on Europa, where residents depend on "destructive transit"—a process that atomically dissolves the body, transmits its informational pattern via quantum entanglement, and reassembles it in a receiving vault. Over generations, this technology has produced a peculiar social institution: "lineage nodes," individuals who have been reconstructed dozens of times and are legally recognized as continuous entities, yet each reconstruction event generates a dormant "shadow record"—a preserved mental-state snapshot that can be activated if the current iteration dies or chooses to "branch."

Your protagonist, an archivist named Sera, is the seventh reconstructed iteration of a single lineage node that began forty years ago. She has discovered that her second iteration, previously thought lost, was not destroyed but archived in a shadow record and has now petitioned a local arbitration council to claim half of Sera's accumulated property and relational bonds, arguing that both are psychologically continuous with the original person and therefore share ownership of that person's life. Sera rejects this on the grounds that what matters in survival is not the mere continuity of memory or character but what she terms "projective unity"—the forward-directed commitment to a single, unfolding plan of life that cannot be divided among co-existent claimants. The council, however, applies a formal metric called "integration density" to determine whether two mental states belong to one person or several. Write the story from Sera's perspective as she prepares her defense, allowing the philosophical stakes to emerge through concrete details of life in the outpost—limited resources, the physical layout of the receiving vaults, the social stigma attached to "branching"—rather than through abstract dialogue alone. Do not include a bibliography, cite philosophical texts, or resolve the dispute with an obvious moral; end on an ambiguity that forces the reader to decide whether Sera is defending a coherent conception of personal identity or merely protecting her own interests.
