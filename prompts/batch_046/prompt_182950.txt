I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on distributive justice, and I have been studying the institutional architecture of unconditional basic-income pilots—specifically, the common design in which recipients are selected by random lottery within a bounded geographic or demographic pool, receive flat individual-level payments that do not vary with household composition or need, are not required to demonstrate work-readiness, and yet are evaluated primarily through labor-market metrics such as employment-rate changes, earned-income growth, and reductions in welfare dependency rather than through measures of autonomy, leisure, or subjective well-being.

What philosophical commitments about desert, moral agency, and the unit of distributive concern does this specific bundle of design choices presuppose? Does the lottery selection mechanism implicitly commit to a strict egalitarian view that every member of the pool is equally deserving ex ante, and if so, is that commitment undermined or contradicted by an evaluation framework that treats paid-work outcomes as the principal indicator of program success? Can a reciprocity-based or control-based theory of justice accommodate both the unconditional, non-productivist nature of the transfer and the productivist metric used to justify it, or does the practice depend on quietly incompatible normative theories?

Please reconstruct the implicit normative theory—if one exists—that renders this combination of random individual allocation, flat unconditional delivery, and employment-centered assessment philosophically coherent. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates comfortably. Identify where the institutional form is philosophically underdetermined: which rival views of the moral significance of work, the status of care or non-market activity, or the proper metric of distributive success could license the very same pilot design? Finally, indicate precisely what would have to change in either the selection protocol or the evaluation framework if we replaced the implicit theory with one that assigned equal distributive weight to leisure, caregiving, or other non-wage contributions.
