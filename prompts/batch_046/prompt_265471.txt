I'm putting together a discussion guide for my ethics course and I keep circling back to just war theory, specifically the moral equality of combatants. I want to avoid the usual textbook examples, so I cooked up a weird edge case and I want to see how two smart people would actually fight about it.

Here's the setup: imagine State Alpha sends conventional ground troops into a disputed zone. State Beta doesn't deploy human soldiers in the field at all; instead, it relies on autonomous defensive turrets that identify, track, and neutralize incoming threats using onboard algorithms. The human operators are tecnicians in bunkers hundreds of miles away who maintain firmware and power grids but never authorize individual strikes. The system decides in real time. Now, if Alpha troops destroy a turret and kill the tecnician maintaining it remotely, is that tecnician a combatant with the same moral status as the Alpha soldier? Or does the fact that Beta's people face zero bodily risk and exercise no direct causal agency in the killing break the symmetry that justifies moral equality?

Write me a dialogue between two people who both know the literature well—call them Mira and Leo—who genuinely disagree on this. Mira should defend a modified traditional view: combatant status derives from institutional role and direct participation in the war apparatus, not from shared vulnerability, so the tecnicians are combatants too, and the equality holds even if risk is asymmetrical. Leo should push back hard: without reciprocal exposure to lethal harm and without direct agency over specific acts of violence, treating them as equals is just a category mistake; it is like comparing a soldier to a factory worker who happens to build dangerous machines.

Make it real back-and-forth. Leo should not just restate his opening, and Mira should not dodge the agency point. They should respond to each other's actual arguments. Please do not cite papers or ask for bibliographies—I'm writing this for students who will not have library access.

Structure it like this:
1. The scenario restated briefly in everyday language (about 100 words)
2. Round one: Mira and Leo lay out their core positions (about 250 words)
3. Round two: they press on the agency versus risk distinction and whether institutional role can substitute for direct causation (about 250 words)
4. Residue: what is the actual disagreement about—definitions, empirical predictions, or something deeper? (about 100 words)
