As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and technology, I am preparing an essay on whether the metaphysics of persistence—endurantism versus perdurantism—can be coherently applied to distributed digital artifacts. My draft argues that standard endurantist accounts have difficulty accommodating entities that undergo discontinuous changes in their underlying substrate, such as a smart contract that persists through successive protocol upgrades across a decentralized validator set. Classical perdurantism, by treating an object as a four-dimensional worm composed of spatiotemporal parts, seems equally strained, since the ledger entries in question lack continuous spatial location. To address this, I sketch a view I call functional stage theory: an artifact persists just in case there is an unbroken chain of state-transition functions linking its successive versions, irrespective of material or spatial continuity. I then propose that this functional criterion might also clarify traditional puzzles—such as whether the ship of Theseus retains its identity when rebuilt with synthetic materials—by shifting attention from substance to preserved design architecture. Before publication, I seek conceptual feedback rather than citations or reading lists: Is the distinction between spatiotemporal and functional continuity philosophically defensible, or does it conflate metaphysical identity with engineering specification? Does invoking the ship of Theseus strengthen the analogy, or does it risk a category mistake? And does this framing genuinely extend the endurantism-perdurantism debate, or does it merely repackage familiar arguments about essential properties in technological language?
