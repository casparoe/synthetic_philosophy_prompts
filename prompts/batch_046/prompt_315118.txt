As a retired physicist who has spent the past two years auditing seminars in moral philosophy, I have become preoccupied with Derek Parfit’s nonidentity problem—not in its familiar form involving a teenager’s reproductive choice, but in the kind of quantitative, counterfactual decision problem I used to encounter in statistical mechanics. I was discussing this with a colleague in synthetic biology last month, and we realized that the paradox becomes far more unsettling when the identity-affecting choice is embedded in precise probabilistic forecasts rather than in abstract moral intuition. I would like you to write a short story that stages exactly such a case. The protagonist should be Dr. Elena Vasquez, a gene-drive engineer in 2047, who has calculated that releasing one modified pathogen strain will prevent a lethal pandemic but will also shift mutation dynamics so that, fifty years later, a different, milder outbreak occurs with an entirely non-overlapping set of survivors and descendants. Every projected life in either timeline is, by her lights, worth living; yet the populations are disjoint, so she cannot harm anyone in either branch without also preventing them from existing. The narrative should unfold during a single evening of deliberation—perhaps in dialogue with an ethics module she has trained on modal logic and population genetics—without resolving the tension through sentiment or a deus ex machina. The quantitative stakes—mutation rates, effective population sizes, identity conditions across branching timelines—should drive the plot, not merely decorate it. Please begin with the story itself, offering no preface, no commentary, and no framing explanation; the first paragraph should establish both the laboratory setting and the specific numerical asymmetry that makes her choice a live instance of the problem. Lead with the bottom line of the narrative’s philosophical tension in the opening sentence, then unfold the reasoning through Vasquez’s calculations and dialogue.
