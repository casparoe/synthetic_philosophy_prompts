I am trying to work through a disagreement with my co-founder about whether I am genuinely responsible for a costly mistake. Our firm recently implemented what management calls a “silent safeguard” for major contracts: if a senior partner attempts to reject a deal that the board has pre-approved, an automated compliance layer quietly blocks the rejection and logs an override, but if the partner approves the deal willingly, the layer remains entirely dormant and leaves no record. Several months ago I endorsed a contract that subsequently collapsed, causing serious losses. I have only just learned about the safeguard, and I now see that had I tried to reject the deal, the system would have prevented me from doing so. In fact, I never tried to reject it—I reviewed the terms, believed they were favorable, and approved the deal without any hesitation or external pressure. My co-founder argues that this hidden constraint means I am not truly to blame: since rejecting the contract was not a real option for me, I “could not have done otherwise,” and moral responsibility requires that kind of openness. I still feel fully responsible, because my approval came from my own judgment rather than from any actual interference, and the safeguard never activated at all. Is my co-founder right that an uninvoked, invisible backstop removes my responsibility? Or is my feeling of accountability appropriate despite the fact that an alternative path was technically unavailable? And does it matter philosophically that the constraint was automated and unknown to me, rather than being the deliberate act of another person?
