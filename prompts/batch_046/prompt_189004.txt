Restate the archaeological and historical debate over whether societies "collapse" or merely reorganize—taking the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island as the three central cases—entirely within the vocabulary of ecological resilience theory. By this I mean Holling's adaptive-cycle phases (exploitation, conservation, release, reorganization), the concept of basins of attraction, regime shifts, cross-scale panarchy, and the distinction between loss of resilience and transformation of resilience. I work with these ecological frameworks professionally, but I have no formal training in archaeology or ancient history; do not assume familiarity with site-specific stratigraphy, epigraphic conventions, or disciplinary historiography.

Proceed in two stages. First, for each case separately, map both the "genuine collapse" interpretation (the prior organizational form was lost and not recovered, with critical institutional or demographic functions failing) and the "mere transformation" interpretation (the system entered a release and reorganization phase, shifting to a different but viable basin of attraction) onto the adaptive-cycle framework. Specify which phase the evidence places the system in, whether the change looks like a local reorganization or a cross-scale cascade, and whether resilience was lost or merely reconfigured.

Second, provide the residue report: identify which historical or archaeological claims translate cleanly into resilience terms; which must be distorted or truncated to fit—for example, treating the disappearance of monumental architecture or courtly writing as the loss of a specific feedback mechanism, or treating demographic decline as a release-phase contraction rather than a permanent injury; and which have no counterpart whatsoever in the resilience vocabulary—such as normative judgments that a society has "failed," assertions of cultural or political "identity" persisting across regime shifts, or distinctions between intentional institutional reform and externally forced dissolution. Conclude by judging whether these residues indicate that resilience theory genuinely misses something essential about symbolically organized human societies, or whether they instead reveal hidden teleological and normative commitments embedded in the historical "collapse" narrative.
