I'm constructing a conceptual framework for a comparative politics seminar and keep returning to a puzzling asymmetry in how international monitoring bodies classify democratic decline. The standard practice—exemplified by Freedom House's annual reports, the V-Dem Institute's liberal democracy index, and the EU's Article 7 and rule-of-law conditionality mechanisms—classifies Hungary, Poland, and Turkey as cases of democratic backsliding because elected governments have gradually captured judiciaries, restricted media pluralism, and centralized executive authority, despite each reform passing through formally democratic procedures and retaining substantial popular support. Yet these same institutions often treat abrupt, non-electoral disruptions of authoritarian consolidation—whether judicial interventions, military actions, or extra-parliamentary removals—as moves toward democratic restoration, even when those disruptions suspend the popular sovereignty and electoral procedures that the framework ostensibly values. In the US context, the label is applied across the political spectrum to phenomena as varied as partisan gerrymandering, executive aggrandizement, and election denialism, suggesting that the concept operates less like a precise diagnostic and more like a contested normative evaluation.

What philosophical commitments about democracy does this classificatory practice presuppose? Must democracy be understood as including a substantive liberal-constitutional core—independent courts, protected minorities, plural information environments—that can be eroded through democratically enacted reforms? Does the practice implicitly endorse a view on which popular sovereignty is conditional rather than absolute, such that expert bodies possess legitimate authority to override democratic outcomes when they threaten that core? Reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy embedded in these classifications, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether a genuinely proceduralist or majoritarian framework could reproduce the same distinctions without collapsing into partisanship. If the concept of backsliding cannot be separated from contested normative commitments, what does that imply for its use as an analytical rather than a political tool?
