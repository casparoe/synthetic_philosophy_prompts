I am drafting discussion material for an interdisciplinary seminar on virtual ontology and applied ethics, and I want a dialogue between two philosophers—call them Mara and Jonas—about whether ephemeral virtual artifacts in live, server-dependent collaborative worlds can be genuine objects and whether destroying them can harm something other than a human being. Mara should defend a dynamic form of social ontology: she argues that artifacts existing only as transient patterns in distributed server memory during an active session, which vanish completely when the session ends, can still possess ontological status if they are sustained by collective intentionality and exert causal effects on participants' experiences; she should further claim that deleting such an artifact mid-session damages a shared institutional reality rather than merely disappointing a user. Jonas should reject this, insisting that without persistence conditions independent of present mental states and without a substrate that survives the session, there is no object to harm—only altered psychological states and social expectations.

The dialogue must be genuine back-and-forth, not parallel monologues: Jonas should press Mara on whether collective intentionality can coherently be directed at something lacking spatiotemporal continuity, and whether her view risks collapsing the distinction between an object and a shared hallucination; Mara should respond by distinguishing procedural stability from physical persistence and should ask whether Jonas's criteria implicitly privilege material substrates by fiat rather than by argument, particularly given that many accepted non-physical entities depend on sustained social practices. Neither speaker should be a strawman, neither should invoke bibliographies, named authorities, or citation lists, and the exchange should reach no forced resolution—ending instead on the precise point where their disagreement turns on whether ontological dependence on collective attention is a defect of virtual entities or their defining feature. Please include at least one concrete scenario—such as the sudden deletion of a collaboratively built, procedurally generated structure that no participant can recreate exactly—to ground their abstract claims.
