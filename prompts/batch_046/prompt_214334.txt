I'm constructing a classification instrument for a graduate seminar on collective intentionality and joint action, and I want it built from the logical space of judgments about concrete cases rather than from a list of author names. Please design a decision tree that takes a respondent from a root commitment—there are activities involving two or more people that are genuinely joint, not merely parallel individual actions—to one of several named or unnamed positions.

Structure the tree so that each internal node is a diagnostic question phrased as a concrete case judgment that can be answered without knowing the philosophical labels. The tree must include branches that turn on the following distinctions, each instantiated by at least one specific scenario:

- Mutual awareness: can joint action occur when the participants do not know of each other? Use the case of two strangers pushing the same stalled car from opposite sides without mutual recognition.
- Representational distribution: can part of the shared plan reside in an external artifact or institutional procedure, or must all intentional content be housed in the participants' minds? Use the case of a human mathematician working with an automated proof-assistant that tracks intermediate lemmas neither fully grasps, and the case of a corporate board whose decision emerges solely from voting rules with no shared mental representation.
- Normative structure: does joint action require each participant to intend their part specifically as part of a shared plan, or is sustained behavioral interdependence sufficient? Use a standard cooperative painting case contrasted with emergent swarm coordination.
- Moral distribution: when a jointly produced outcome is harmful, does attributing responsibility require that each member intended the harm, or can structural roles alone generate collective accountability?

The leaves should include: Bratman's shared cooperative activity account, Searle's we-intention view, Gilbert's plural-subject theory, a strict reductionist or aggregate view, an extended-cognition collective view (mark as unnamed if it occupies an unexplored region), and any incoherent or unoccupied combinations your logical space allows. For each leaf, provide the position's standard name (or 'unnamed'), one sentence describing its core claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The tree should have between six and eight leaves. Wherever two named positions cannot be separated by your diagnostic questions—because they yield identical answers across all your cases—flag the pair explicitly and identify the finer distinction your tree omits. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep every node phrased as a question the respondent can answer from intuition about the cases, not from familiarity with the literature.
