As one of the appointed members of the university Committee on Institutional Memory and Historical Accountability, I need a formal written opinion for circulation before our meeting next Thursday. The committee must reach a concrete decision—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—regarding the proposal to transform the central campus monument to our founding benefactor, a documented slaveholder and advocate of segregationist policies, into what the applicants call a site of critical remembrance. The proposal, submitted jointly by the Department of History and a coalition of students and descendant-community representatives, would preserve the physical statue but surround it with an interactive archive of primary documents recording the lives of the people he enslaved, and it would require that the interpretive text be rewritten every five years through a participatory process involving those descendant communities. The proponents frame this as an institutional practice of Vergangenheitsbewältigung—working through the past rather than burying it—adapted to an American context. The opposition, comprising alumni trustees and several senior historians, argues that importing a specifically German framework of collective guilt misreads the local memory culture, which they say bears closer resemblance to the suppressive dynamics observed in official postwar Japanese narratives, the disavowal structures maintained in Turkish state historiography regarding the Armenian genocide, and especially the nostalgic mythologization of the Lost Cause in the American South, where the danger is not insufficient guilt but the persistence of a heroic perpetrator narrative that resists dismantling. A further concern, introduced by our invited ethics consultant, is whether the proposed five-year revision cycle violates what she terms the stability condition for genuine memorial obligations: the claim that an institution's acknowledgment of historical wrong must be durable enough to function as a binding commitment rather than as a perpetually renegotiable performance that risks reducing remembrance to institutional theater. Please draft the committee's recommendation, including a specific course of action and a clear time frame for implementation or review. Your memo should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine seriousness—whether that is the risk of mnemonic instability, the inappropriateness of cross-cultural comparison, or the danger of substituting ritualized revision for structural change—rather than dismissing it. Write the entire response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were composing a formal letter or email addressed to the full committee.
