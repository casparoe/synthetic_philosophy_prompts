As a novelist drafting a cycle of historical portraits, I have been mapping my own moral reactions to figures across centuries and finding them stubbornly inconsistent in ways that threaten the coherence of my characters. I judge Thomas Jefferson’s slaveholding to be a grave, personally indictable failure—not merely because he held slaves, but because he articulated the language of universal equality and then systematically withheld its application—so the contradiction was live for him. By contrast, I find Winston Churchill’s imperial and racial commitments regrettable yet somehow less personally damning, despite their comparable pervasiveness, perhaps because he never authored an opposing universal principle; still, I cannot tell whether this distinction is genuine or a post-hoc rationalization. I condemn Christopher Columbus’s enslavement and mutilation of the Taíno without reservation, regardless of fifteenth-century exploration norms, yet I feel no comparable outrage toward a Roman patrician who held slaves, though the moral structure is parallel. I excuse a nineteenth-century surgeon who operated without consent, recognizing that the concept of informed autonomy had not yet crystallized in medical practice, but I condemn a contemporary who refused vaccination on religious grounds and sparked an outbreak—though both acted within the epistemic frameworks available to them. The pattern seems to turn on whether a harm was visible through conceptual resources the agent already possessed, yet this collapses upon inspection: Jefferson possessed abolitionist arguments in his own library, and the Roman senator possessed Stoic critiques of slavery. So perhaps the principle involves institutional role—those who shape the moral vocabulary of their age bear a heavier burden—but then Columbus, who shaped nothing, remains condemned, while the surgeon, who shaped medicine, is excused. The case that keeps reversing on me is an imagined eighteenth-century abolitionist who, convinced slavery was wrong, nevertheless defended it publicly to preserve his estate and protect his dependents. Is he closer to Jefferson, betraying a principle he acknowledged, or to the surgeon, constrained by alternatives he could not yet realize? I want a precisely stated principle, or a small family of candidates, that delivers verdicts on all these cases, predicts where the abolitionist falls, and honestly states which of my existing reactions—especially the asymmetry between Jefferson and Churchill, or between Columbus and the Roman senator—it must reject as unsustainable. The principle should address whether being a man of his time can ever excuse moral failure, and if so, what distinguishes excusable embeddedness from culpable complicity.
