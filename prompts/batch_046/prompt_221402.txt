I convene a philosophy-of-science seminar for twelve doctoral students, one ninety-minute session, on inductive logic and the structure of confirmation. I want a runnable simulation—rules, roles, materials, and a point-based payoff scheme requiring no real money—that forces the participants to inhabit the tension in Hempel's raven paradox rather than merely diagram it. To prevent the exercise from collapsing into a familiar recitation, frame the working hypothesis in a fictional taxonomy: on the Aurelia Islands, the claim is that all murghs are black (murghs being the local avian analog), with the logical equivalent that all non-black entities are non-murghs. Divide the group into four hidden-role factions: naive inductivists who begin by searching only for murghs; one student assigned Nicod's criterion, who treats only black-murgh sightings as confirmatory; and one assigned Hempel's equivalence condition, who must treat every green shoe, white shell, or gray rock as equally evidential. Give them scarce observation tokens to spend across island sectors, with predictive-success points awarded after each round according to each faction's internal rule. The rules should be arranged so that the equivalence-condition player accumulates points fastest by surveying non-murghs everywhere, producing in the group an intuitive revolt that the facilitator should not resolve until the debrief. In your design, include: a precise timeline for the ninety minutes; the material list; the payoff matrix; instructions for concealing roles; the debrief that maps token-spending patterns onto the formal distinction between instance confirmation and the paradox of irrelevant conjunctions; an explicit admission of which feature of Hempel's universal-quantification argument the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce; and a frank forecast of what will predictably misfire the first time it is run—especially the risk that students confuse the fictional taxonomy with an empirical claim about real ornithology. Do not require any pre-session reading, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further texts; the conceptual pressure must come entirely from the game mechanics.
