I work as a software engineer building decision-support systems for infrastructure resilience, with no formal training in philosophy, yet I keep encountering the same conceptual tension in the frameworks I implement. In national-level climate-adaptation and pandemic-preparedness planning—particularly within the “all-hazards” risk matrices that my team integrates—non-human forces such as volcanic aerosol loading, zoonotic spillover dynamics, or extreme-heat thresholds are categorised as “primary historical drivers” possessing autonomous causal weight. Human institutions are correspondingly framed as “adaptive capacity” or “response parameters”, never as co-equal causal agents. At the same time, the legal and regulatory frameworks governing these very systems treat states, corporations, and individuals as the sole bearers of moral and causal responsibility for outcomes; a volcano or a pathogen is never assigned liability, only the failure to adapt to it.

What philosophical commitments about agency, causation, and historical explanation does this institutional split presuppose? Must it rest on a form of environmental determinism—granting geophysical and biological processes a kind of historical agency that human action is denied—or can it be defended as a purely operational partition with no deeper ontological commitment? Is the practice internally consistent, given that the same agencies reject any attribution of causal autonomy to natural forces when assigning blame in litigation or policy review? Reconstruct the implicit theory of non-human agency and determinism that renders this split intelligible, carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate what a modelling or policy framework built on a genuinely symmetrical account of human and non-human causation would have to alter—especially regarding liability, predictive attribution, and the design of intervention points.
