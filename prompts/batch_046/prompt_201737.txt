I am constructing a scene for a novel in which a disillusioned risk analyst is confronted by an interlocutor who offers an unbounded reward in exchange for a negligible action, with the stated probability of fulfillment diminishing by successive orders of magnitude each time she declines. For her interior monologue I require a poem—eighteen to twenty-four lines of blank verse—whose stanzas structurally reproduce that escalation: each stanza should halve the implied likelihood of delivery while holding the promised payoff constant, so that the reader experiences the compression of belief against an infinite value. The poem should unfold in two interwoven voices, the proposer and the analyst, without naming Pascal, the wager, or expected-utility theory explicitly. The analyst should attempt resistance not by diminishing the reward but by eroding her credence in the promiser's reliability, only to discover that such erosion collapses once the payoff is treated as literally unbounded. The ending must remain suspended—no resolution, no authorial verdict on rationality or madness—since the character herself is caught between acquiescence and refusal. It should be exact enough that someone familiar with the paradox recognizes the slide from finite probabilities into the infinitesimal, yet it must never read as exposition. Please write it.
