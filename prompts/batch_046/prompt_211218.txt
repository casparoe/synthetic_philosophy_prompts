I'm trying to map the tension between Buddhist ethics and consequentialism for my thesis chapter, and I've walked myself into an argument that feels suspiciously neat. Here goes:

(1) In Theravāda Abhidhamma, kamma is cetanā — the volitional impulse in a mind-moment.
(2) The moral quality (kusala or akusala) is fixed entirely by the root (mūla): greed, hatred, delusion, or their absence.
(3) The external result (vipāka) is causally downstream but doesn't feed back to alter the evaluation of the original act — even with Mahāyāna parināma, you're just transferring vipāka, not changing the root.
(4) So if a surgeon intends to heal but acidentally kills five patients, and an arsonist intends to kill but acidentally saves five, the surgeon's act is kusala and the arsonist's akusala, regardless of outcomes.
(5) Consequentialism says an act is right iff it maximises well-being.
(6) If Buddhist ethics were consequentialist, we'd have to reverse the evaluation in (4).
(7) But (4) is orthodox doctrine, so Buddhist ethics is not consequentialist — its a pure intention-based ethics.
(8) Apply this consistently: since only mental roots matter, there's no direct moral reason to care whether suffering actually stops in the world, only that one's own mind stays pure.
(9) That makes the bodhisattva vow to liberate all beings into a kind of private aesthetic project: you care about feeling compassionate, not about anyone actually being freed.
(10) That feels like a reductio, but I can't see the slip.

Which step should I reject? I'd rather keep (1)-(4) and even (7) if I can, but I feel like something went wrong between (4) and (8). What's the cost of fixing it? Also please write in British English.
