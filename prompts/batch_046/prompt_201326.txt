For my philosophy essay, I have adopted the view that in philosophical reasoning we ought to take the outside view on our own conceptual intuitions rather than reasoning from them directly. Specifically, whenever I form an intuition about a philosophical case — for instance, that personal identity is preserved in teletransportation — I should treat my confidence as a forecasting problem: I should examine the base rate of philosophers reversing analogous intuitions after exposure to cognitive science, anthropology, or formal semantics, and discount my belief proportionally. Only if the intuition survives this statistical discounting should I treat it as a genuine reason. I call this the epistemic discounting position. What would genuinely count against it? I am not looking for the standard reply that philosophy is a priori and immune to empirical base rates; rather, I want to know which specific findings — perhaps in the psychology of expertise, in the history of philosophical consensus, or in formal models of belief revision — would show that such discounting is irrational or self-defeating rather than modest. Which objections would defenders of this view concede as serious, and which would they dismiss? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is genuinely a problem for a normative thesis of this kind. Please answer in British English.
