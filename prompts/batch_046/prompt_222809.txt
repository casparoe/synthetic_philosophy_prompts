I was discussing institutional design with colleagues after a seminar on distributive ethics, and we began sketching a hypothetical municipal emergency system that tries to operationalize two different moral frameworks within one charter. The active-rescue division—ambulance and fire deployment—follows what the charter calls a reasonableness filter: any proposed allocation rule must survive a simulated objection procedure in which representatives of every affected neighborhood can reject it on grounds they could defend to others, and the city adopts only rules that no party can reasonably reject. In practice, this blocks pure utilitarian aggregation: you cannot redirect the only trauma unit away from a single critically injured patient toward five patients with moderate injuries, because the one patient can present a reason—grounded in the severity and directness of their claim—that others cannot dismiss without appealing to a simple headcount. By contrast, the capital-budget division—prevention, infrastructure, and risk reduction—ignores that filter entirely. It uses a standard cost-effectiveness metric, expected quality-adjusted life-years saved per dollar, which routinely directs funds away from small high-risk districts toward large low-risk ones. The charter justifies the split by saying that rescue involves direct imposition on identifiable persons, while prevention involves statistical distributions, so the moral logic legitimately differs.

But the charter also contains a symmetry clause for simultaneous emergencies: if two districts face structurally identical crises—say, two apartment fires with the same occupancy profile—the rescue division must split resources evenly, even if demographic data show one district has slightly higher occupancy. The clause is defended not by aggregation but by a formal principle of non-arbitrariness: once the reasonableness filter has certified both districts as having equivalent standing, no further quantitative distinction is permissible. Meanwhile, the capital division, facing the same two districts, would weight the larger one more heavily without hesitation. The result is an institution that rejects headcount reasoning in one room and embraces it in another, with a formal symmetry rule that seems to sit uneasily between them.

What philosophical commitments does this dual-track practice presuppose? Does it genuinely depend on a Scanlon-style contractualist distinction between direct harm and statistical risk, or could the same split be licensed by a rule-utilitarian framework that distinguishes decision contexts purely for tractability reasons? More specifically, does the symmetry clause presuppose that aggregation is illegitimate only when it operates across qualitatively different claims, or does it smuggle aggregation back in through the back door—defining equivalence in a way that already embeds quantitative comparison? And if we replaced the contractualist filter with a pure aggregation rule in the rescue division, which institutional features—the forum structure, the symmetry clause, the separation of capital from rescue—would become philosophically redundant, and which could survive on independent grounds? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes the whole system cohere, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me where the design is philosophically underdetermined—where several rival theories of value, responsibility, and aggregation would license exactly the same institutional architecture.
