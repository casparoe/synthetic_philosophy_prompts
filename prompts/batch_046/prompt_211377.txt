I'm part of a two-person research collective that operates without formal hierarchy. We maintain an unwritten protocol: either partner may commit resources below a fixed threshold unilaterally, but any act that binds our collective reputation requires explicit dual authorization—both of us stating agreement in writing. Last week, my partner published a public statement criticizing our institution's administration, using our shared letterhead and attributing it to both of us. When I objected, she argued that she had mentioned the idea in passing during a working session and that I had responded with what she interpreted as assent—a nod and a phrase like "sounds reasonable"—which she treated as sufficient authorization. I recall being distracted, grading papers, and offering only a noncommittal acknowledgment. She now contends that because our collective is founded on a unified voice, my retrospective refusal amounts to an illegitimate retreat into individual preference: a true "we," she says, does not permit one constituent to veto the collective expression after the fact, particularly when the other party reasonably believed mutual recognition had occurred. I feel genuinely wronged, yet I worry I am being inconsistent—perhaps using my own inattention as a shield against collective responsibility. Is her reasoning sound? Specifically, does the wrong here depend on whether I actually formed a shared intention at that moment, or is there a separate failure in treating ambiguous social acknowledgment as binding joint authorization, and can a collective commitment be formed retroactively by one party's reasonable interpretation of the other's silence?
