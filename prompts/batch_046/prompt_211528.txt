I am preparing a response paper for my course on collective memory and the politics of national myth. Our seminar leader has rendered four consistent judgments on canonical lost-cause narratives: she condemns the Confederacy’s Lost Cause as morally indefensible revisionism that obscures slavery and sustains present racial hierarchy; she accepts the Masada story—the 73 CE siege and collective suicide—as a legitimate, if tragic, founding myth of endurance because it does not serve to dominate living others; she grants the Kosovo Field myth (1389) only conditional tolerance, recognizing its ethnic-consolidating function while warning that it institutionalizes perpetual grievance; and she praises the Dunkirk spirit (1940) as a democratic resilience narrative free of scapegoating. Now consider a proposed state curriculum in a Baltic republic commemorating the 1944–1953 anti-Soviet partisan resistance—commonly called the Forest Brothers—as a Masada-like lost cause of sovereign self-determination, despite archival evidence that some networks included figures with collaborationist records under the prior occupation, and despite the proposal’s explicit insistence that it bears no analogy to the Confederacy. Assuming our leader wishes to preserve coherence with her four prior rulings, how should she assess this curriculum? Identify the closest analog among her earlier verdicts, lay out the competing interpretive rules that her judgments jointly permit, predict her verdict under each rule with reasons for preferring one, name the principle she would be adopting if she chose the other, and specify the further hypothetical case that would force those rules apart.
