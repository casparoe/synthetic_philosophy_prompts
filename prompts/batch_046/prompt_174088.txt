I run an MA seminar, about 12 people, 90 minutes. Design an activity that makes them feel the pull of microhistory against elite and state archives — specifically the moment when absence in the official record isn't missing data but a structured silence that shapes your narrative. Give them two sets of source cards about a fictional 1968 factory dispute in a small town (invent the details, don't lean on a real well-known case): Group A gets ministry reports, police logs, and economic stats; Group B gets a torn worker diary, an unlabelled photo, and they recieve two contradictory oral fragments. They have 30 minits to produce a 200-word account of what happened and defend it to the other group. The rules should force Group B to practise reverse prosopography — stitching collective history from biographical scraps — and Group A to notice that their sources never mention women subcontractors, not because they forgot, but because the category didn't exist in the form. Then a 20-min debrief where they map their own frustrations onto negative archival spaces and the microhistorical clue-paradigm. Tell me what will predictably go wrong first run — probably Group B over-reads the photo, Group A dismisses contradiction as noise — and be frank that the activity can't reproduce the actual power asymmetry of archive creation, so I'll have to supply that. No lecture up front, don't ask for bibliographies or reading lists, and please write the answer in British English.
