I have been reflecting on a line that circulates in discussions of Dostoevsky’s *The Brothers Karamazov*: the Grand Inquisitor’s suggestion that humanity will willingly surrender freedom in exchange for bread, security, and the relief from choice — that freedom is an intolerable burden and that people prefer guaranteed happiness to the anxiety of self-determination. In a seminar on political theology, a fellow student invoked this idea to argue that expanding democratic liberties is not merely impractical but actively harmful, on the grounds that autonomy causes suffering and that people naturally seek to lay it down. I am trying to determine precisely what is being claimed when this slogan is deployed in such arguments, because the conclusion seems to depend on which interpretation is in play.

Could you distinguish the distinct precise theses this statement could be expressing? I am especially interested in separating: (i) a descriptive psychological claim about what humans in fact prefer; (ii) a normative claim about what they ought to value; (iii) a metaphysical or theological claim about the constitution of human will; and (iv) a political claim about the conditions for legitimate authority. For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and explain whether it actually supports the dismissal of democratic expansion.

Furthermore, I want to understand whether the argument implicitly depends on a premise about the adaptability of preferences — specifically, whether people who surrender autonomy can genuinely come to prefer their subordination, or whether such preferences are necessarily formed under constraint and therefore cannot justify the surrender — or whether it instead relies on a revealed-preference conception that equates welfare with the satisfaction of whatever desires happen to exist. Which of these underlying frameworks makes the slogan do the work the student intended, and which ones cause it to collapse? Please present your answer as a menu of interpretations rather than selecting a single one, and specify which reading is likely being invoked when the line is used to close an argument about liberty versus welfare.
