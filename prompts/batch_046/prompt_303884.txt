A recent comparative essay summarizes the arguments of the historian Eric Hobsbawm and the political scientist Benedict Anderson on nationhood as follows. Hobsbawm showed that nearly every tradition people now regard as ancient was in fact invented within the last two centuries by state elites who needed rituals to manufacture mass loyalty; prior to this modern fabrication, human beings apparently had no meaningful sense of belonging to large, abstract collectives. Anderson then demonstrated that a nation is literally imaginary in the psychological sense—an invented fiction sustained solely by print capitalism—so that once vernacular newspapers and novels spread, people began to feel solidarity with strangers they would never meet, effectively displacing the concrete, face-to-face communities that had previously constituted the only genuine social bonds. Taken together, the two accounts imply that collective identity is almost entirely a top-down ideological product lacking any organic historical depth. From this, the essay draws a comparative conclusion: East Asian nation-building in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries—especially in Japan and Korea—represents an especially stark instance of engineered identity, because those societies entered modernity without Europe’s long vernacular print traditions, compelling states to construct rituals, standardized languages, and shared memories largely through bureaucratic decree. Consequently, their collective identities are more artificial and therefore more fragile than those of the West.

I am inclined to think this captures the core of both thinkers, but I also sense that some claims may be stronger than the originals allow, particularly the comparative judgment about East Asia. Could you audit the summary? Please separate what is accurate or roughly fair, what is a subtle overstatement or conflation, what misrepresents either thinker’s actual position, and whether the conclusion about East Asian fragility follows from the corrected premises. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of further readings—just an evaluation of this specific characterization.
