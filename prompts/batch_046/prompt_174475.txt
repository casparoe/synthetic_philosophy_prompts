I'm giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop called "Generalizing About Peoples: Epistemology, Ethics, and Social Science." The audience is split evenly between cultural anthropologists, moral philosophers, and researchers from international survey organizations. I need a fifteen-minute address that uses the concept of national character—not as a punchline or a strawman—to do real structural work. Specifically, the speech should frame the central workshop tension: whether patterns observed across populations can ever support attributions of collective disposition, or whether such attributions always collapse into either harmless statistical summary or harmful essentialism. To avoid generic calls for nuance, organize the remarks around a sharp distinction I am proposing: between "institutional-regularity descriptions" (claims about recurring patterns shaped by shared histories of law, education, and economic structure) and "psychological-character ascriptions" (claims about shared inner dispositions of a people). The address must speak across the room: anthropologists should recognize that thick ethnographic description is not automatically essentialist; philosophers should see that normative critique requires engaging with actual explanatory practices rather than dismissing them; and survey researchers should understand why their cross-national data on trust or risk preference do not by themselves license conclusions about "the national mind." Do not resolve the tension. Instead, end with a precisely formulated question that structures the three days—whether there exists a defensible intermediate category between statistical correlation and collective psychology, and what moral conditions would have to be met for using it in policy discourse. Write the remarks in a tone appropriate for a workshop opening: intellectually serious, accessible to non-specialists, and structured so that each disciplinary group feels its concerns are being taken seriously rather than being translated away.
