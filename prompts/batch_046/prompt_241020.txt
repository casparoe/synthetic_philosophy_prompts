I'm calibrating my view on whether any variety of free will remains worth wanting in what I'll call the predictive-intervention scenario: a society employs neural forecasting accurate enough to preempt harmful actions before conscious deliberation completes, leaving citizens with intact reason-responsiveness, phenomenal awareness, and deliberative capacities, but systematically removing the possibility that their mental states could have led to a different choice given an identical prior causal history. My credence that there is still a normatively significant distinction—one that grounds moral accountability or personal authenticity—between being preempted and acting freely in this scenario is about 0.40; a colleague who holds that compatibilist reason-responsiveness is fully sufficient assigns a credence near 0.80. Give me your own precise credence, then decompose it into at least four sub-questions: (1) whether the normative work we need free will to do is primarily about moral accountability, about authentic self-authorship, or about some other function; (2) whether reason-responsiveness without counterfactual openness to alternative mental trajectories can ground that normative work; (3) whether the forecasting mechanism in the scenario actually eliminates the relevant alternative possibilities or merely relocates them to the predictor's design and maintenance; and (4) whether our intuitive resistance to accepting free will in this scenario is a reliable guide or a status-quo bias. For each component, assign a rough probability or weight, explain briefly how they combine into your overall figure, identify which component is doing the most work, and state what kind of empirical finding about predictive neuroscience or what conceptual argument about control would most shift your number.
