I'm currently preparing a graduate seminar that juxtaposes institutional practices of heritage conservation with cyclical and linear philosophies of history, and I have become stuck on what metaphysical commitments are actually embedded in each practice rather than merely compatible with them. Consider, first, the standard UNESCO preservation paradigm: a monument is assigned value in proportion to its material continuity with an original historical moment, reconstruction with new substance is treated as inauthentic or even fraudulent, and the institutional goal is to arrest decay so that the past remains physically present as a fixed reference point. Consider, second, the ritual architecture of the Ise Grand Shrine, where the main sanctuary is completely dismantled and rebuilt on a fixed, recurring cycle with fresh timber and thatch according to an unchanging plan; the old materials are distributed to subsidiary shrines, no physical element survives for long, and yet practitioners and scholars alike regard this not as a replica but as the continuous presence of the same sacred entity that has existed for over a millennium.

What view of historical time, material identity, and value does each practice strictly presuppose? Does the preservation model require a linear, irreversible ontology in which the past is an exhaustible resource that can only be conserved or corrupted, or is it compatible with a cyclical view that simply privileges one phase of the cycle? Conversely, does the cyclical rebuilding model presuppose something like Polybius's constitutional cycle or Ibn Khaldun's cyclical dynamics of civilization, or can it be detached from any macro-historical theory and defended purely on ritual grounds? And if we take Spengler's morphology seriously—cultures as organisms with predetermined life-cycles—would it license either practice, or does it undermine the very idea that human institutions can choose between linear conservation and cyclical renewal? I want the implicit theories reconstructed, distinguished from what they merely accommodate, and an assessment of whether these two practices are philosophically underdetermined.
