For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of science, set out the desiderata that any adequate philosophical theory of simultaneous scientific discovery must satisfy if it is to explain both the Newton–Leibniz calculus case and the Darwin–Wallace evolutionary case, and to adjudicate whether such discoveries are historically inevitable and whether priority of attribution carries any philosophical weight. Your specification should address what phenomena the theory must explain, which distinctions it must respect, and which results or no-go considerations it must accommodate, with the reasoning behind each requirement and an indication of whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp.

Specifically, include the following considerations, though you should organise them according to your own judgement of logical priority:

1. The theory must distinguish genuine cognitive independence from cryptic influence mediated by unpublished correspondence, shared intermediaries, or institutional proximity, and it must supply a clear criterion for what counts as "independence" in contexts where informal intellectual networks existed but formal publication did not.

2. It must account for structural equivalence of results—such as the mathematical inter-translatability of Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differential notation—without collapsing this equivalence into mere notational variance, since the underlying conceptual framing and metaphysical commitments differ significantly.

3. It must accommodate cases of "asymmetric convergence," in which one party produces a more general formal apparatus but the other supplies the more persuasive empirical justification or metaphysical interpretation, and it must explain whether such asymmetry undermines claims of pure simultaneity or merely complicates them.

4. It must be consistent with the historical fact that a scientific community's subsequent adoption patterns—for instance, the continental preference for Leibnizian notation versus British adherence to Newtonian methods—often diverge from the chronological order of discovery, and it must say whether these sociological patterns are philosophically relevant to the identity of the discovery itself.

5. It must provide a principled answer to the inevitability question: if two researchers arrive at identical theoretical structures through genuinely independent reasoning, would the absence of one have left a permanent gap in science? Specify what kind of modal or counterfactual reasoning is legitimate here and whether inevitability is a claim about logical structure, institutional readiness, or both.

6. It must address whether priority disputes possess intrinsic philosophical significance—beyond being mere institutional artefacts—particularly in light of the fact that both calculus and evolutionary theory were eventually accepted and integrated regardless of attribution.

Crucially, examine whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. For example, does requiring both strict cognitive independence and strong historical inevitability force an implausible view of scientific development in which discovery is simultaneously contingent upon individual agency and determined by structural conditions?

In addition, your list must incorporate and evaluate a further, less standard desideratum: that the theory must explain "epistemic redundancy." This is the phenomenon in which simultaneous discoverers not only reach the same conclusion but reproduce structurally isomorphic patterns of preliminary error, correction, and heuristic adjustment in their private manuscripts—suggesting a shared cognitive architecture or problem-space structure that may run deeper than shared empirical data. Indicate whether satisfying this redundancy condition conflicts with the independence desideratum, and if so, which should yield.

Keep the weight of your answer on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the question of joint satisfiability rather than on scoring existing accounts, though you may briefly note where standard "inevitability" and "social construction" camps stand relative to your list.

Please write your response in British English. State explicitly how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly with regard to the epistemic redundancy condition and its compatibility with independence.
