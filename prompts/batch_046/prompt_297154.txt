Having spent my career interpreting seismic stratigraphy, I have only recently, in retirement, begun to read seriously in philosophy of history, and I find myself drawn to Fernand Braudel’s distinction between the rapid time of events and individuals and the almost motionless duration of geography, climate, and deep social structure. In our seminar’s closing session, I want to offer something other than a standard essay: a poem that makes the tension between these temporal scales felt rather than merely stated, drawing on my own intuition that sedimentary absence can be as structurally informative as presence.

Please compose a sestina—six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi—whose six rotating end-words are stratum, current, event, climate, bone, and measure. The form’s compulsive returns should mirror how slow structures recur and constrain human occurrence, while the envoi should leave the deep and the brief registers adjacent without resolving which governs the other. The poem ought to respect Braudel’s actual claim that geography and climate form constraining matrices rather than deterministic engines, and it should weave in the geological notion of an unconformity—a gap in the rock record produced by slow erosive forces—not as a mere metaphor for lost history but as an image of absence itself being shaped by long duration. I would like the diction to be precise enough that a historian familiar with the Annales approach recognizes the conceptual architecture, yet accessible enough that a listener without specialist training feels the temporal dissonance between sediment and action. This is intended as an original composition for oral presentation; I do not need references, citations, or a bibliography.
