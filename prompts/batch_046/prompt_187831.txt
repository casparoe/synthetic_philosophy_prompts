I'm a retired biophysicist who's only recently got into philosphy, and I've joined a small studdy group working through Wittgenstien's On Certainty. Last week two members, Maya and Leo, got into a realy heated argument about hinge propositions and I couldn't referee them properly. I'd like your take, and please write the answer in British English.

Maya kicked off by saying that in experimental science some background beliefs work like hinges — not because we've justified them, but because they form the frame within which we ask questions. She used the example of assuming a spectrometer's calibration hasn't drifted between runs: inside a given study we treat that as standing fast, and if we doubted it every time we'd never measure anything. She said Wittgenstein's point is that these framework beliefs aren't justified inside the system, they just hold it up.

Leo pushed back hard. He said Maya was conflating hinges with ordinary methodological assumptions. For Wittgenstein, hinges are beliefs like having two hands or the earth existing for many years — things immune to doubt in everyday life, not technical choices about instrument calibration. He said treating every lab protocol as a hinge dilutes the concept until it's useless.

Maya replied that Wittgenstein himself allows hinges to shift — he mentions that nobody having been on the moon could be a hinge that changes — so scientific background beliefs can clearly function as hinges within a practice. She argued the point isn't the content but the functional role: if doubting it brings the whole enquiry to a halt, it's a hinge for that language-game.

Leo then introduced a distinction he said was missing: there are global hinges, like the existence of an external world, and local hinges, like the validity of a specific statistical test or a calibration standard. He claimed Wittgenstein only meant global ones; local hinges are just conventions we revise without collapsing our whole world-picture. He said if Maya is right, then every failed replication crisis becomes a hinge shift, which makes scientific progress incoherent because you'd never know if you're in the same game.

Maya said that local versus global split sounds neat but isn't actually in Wittgenstein's text — it looks like an ad-hoc move to protect scientific rationality from scepticism. She claimed all hinges are local to a language-game, and science just has very specialised ones. Leo answered that if that's true, then changing a calibration protocol and changing the belief that the earth exists are on the same footing, which is absurd; there must be a principled line, even if Wittgenstein didn't draw it neatly.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about each move — not just who wins overall? And tell me if there's something important they're both missing about how hinges work in specialised practices?
