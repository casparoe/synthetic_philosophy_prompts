I've been thinking about Kierkegaard's account of Abraham and Isaac, particularly the idea usually called the teleological suspension of the ethical. By that I mean not just that religious faith can override morality, but the stronger claim that ethical obligations are always already conditional on a deeper commitment or purpose, and that in principle this deeper commitment can demand their complete suspension. I find this persuasive beyond the religious case: it seems to apply whenever someone acts on a private duty that violates universal norms, like a parent who refuses a court-ordered medical procedure for a child because of a conviction about that child's destiny, or a whistleblower who breaks every institutional loyalty to honor a single obligation they can't fully justify to others.

But I want to know honestly where this position is exposed. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? I'm looking for the structural vulnerabilities, not the familiar charge that it licenses fanaticism. For instance: is there an internal contradiction in asserting that ethics is universal while also claiming it can be suspended? Would the view collapse if Abraham's willingness turned out to be explainable entirely by psychological compulsion or social conditioning rather than by a free, purpose-driven commitment? What happens when the "higher purpose" that justifies the suspension turns out to be mistaken—does the suspension retain any philosophical significance, or does it require that the purpose actually be valid? And if we extend this framework to secular cases—political revolutionaries, parents making extreme choices, individuals who reject collective moral judgment—does it preserve the ethical realm as a genuine domain, or does it dissolve ethics into nothing more than the intensity of personal commitment?

Please distinguish between what would merely be awkward for this view and what would genuinely defeat it. In particular, tell me whether defenders of this position have themselves acknowledged conditions under which it should be abandoned. Also, assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by saying that any failure of the suspended action only proves the commitment wasn't deep enough. If it has been insulated in that way, is that insulation legitimate for a philosophical claim of this kind, or is it a defect that undermines the view's seriousness?
