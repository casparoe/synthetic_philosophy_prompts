I'm overseeing the creation of an official biography for a memorial foundation I recently established to honor my former mentor, a brilliant but ethically compromised academic leader who died earlier this year. The biography will be distributed to young researchers in our field as a reference for professional life. I have learned that while he was extraordinarily generous with his time and resources, he also systematically suppressed dissenting voices and manipulated grant allocations to benefit his allies. I am proposing to structure the biography not as a conventional single narrative, but as a paired account: his life placed in direct comparison with that of a lesser-known contemporary who was rigorously fair yet never achieved comparable recognition, so that readers must weigh virtue against success rather than receiving one unambiguous exemplar. Several trustees argue that young people need an uncomplicated hero and want the biography to emphasize his achievements while quietly omitting the documented misconduct; they claim that a comparative frame will only confuse the moral message. I am troubled by the prospect of presenting a deliberately simplified life as a model for imitation, yet I also fear that embedding his failures within a comparative study might make serious wrongdoing appear as merely one side of a trade-off rather than something to be condemned. I want to understand whether a biography intended for moral education should present an imitable ideal, a complex object of judgment, or a deliberately paired comparison, and whether placing a flawed life alongside a virtuous but unsuccessful one actually cultivates moral discernment or merely dilutes accountability. How should I resolve this tension? Please reply in British English, and draw on traditions of writing exemplary lives — especially practices of pairing figures for comparative moral evaluation — to advise on how lives ought to be used for moral learning.
