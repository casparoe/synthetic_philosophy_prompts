Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps on Blaise Pascal's treatment of justice and force in the Pensées. Camp One holds that Pascal advances a conventionalist thesis: human justice is reducible to the stabilization of power through law and custom, with no transcendent normative residue, so that the coupling of justice and force describes the entirety of political reality. Camp Two holds that the same passages constitute a theological diagnosis in which the necessary entanglement of justice with force reveals the corruption of post-lapsarian politics and implicitly appeals to a divine justice that is not reducible to power, so that Pascal is describing justice's fall rather than defining its essence.

Produce a document with the following parts. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: shared commitments about the textual evidence (the unfinished state of the Pensées, the ordering disputes among standard editions, Pascal's Jansenist commitments, and the passages coupling justice with force), about the logical structure of the argument, and about interpretive constraints both camps accept (for example, that anachronistic liberal rights-language should not be imported, and that the theological vocabulary should not be dismissed as mere rhetoric). Second, a disagreements table stating each camp's core thesis in wording its own members would endorse, paired with the opposing camp's formulation of the same disagreement. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving one would move at least one camp—including whether the justice-force coupling is an irredeemable feature of fallen human politics or a pragmatic accommodation leaving room for prophetic condemnation, and whether references to custom and law function descriptively or as normative limitations. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete textual case or conceptual test both camps agree is relevant (for instance, how to weigh the fragments on tyranny and on the hidden foundations of law in light of the edition-ordering problems), and record each camp's predicted evaluation in advance. Fifth, a brief section identifying any purely verbal disagreements—disputes about whether Pascal is being descriptive or normative that dissolve once the wording is fixed—with an explanation of why they are verbal rather than substantive.

The prompt should require no online search: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to obscure secondary literature. Do not request verbatim quotations placed in quotation marks; if specific phrases are discussed, paraphrase them without quotation marks. Keep the analysis grounded in the fragments and in well-known biographical and theological context, but inject enough conceptual specificity—particularly around the distinction between descriptive conventionalism and normative theological diagnosis—that the task cannot be completed by generic summary.
