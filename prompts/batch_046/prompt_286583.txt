Consider the following lines of argument from Émilie du Châtelet’s Discourse on Happiness. In one passage she lists what she calls the great machines of happiness—good health, refined taste, liberty from prejudice, and susceptibility to illusion—and she adds that we owe most of our pleasures to illusion. Shortly afterward she praises the love of study as a passion that never deceives, insisting that reason and the search for truth are indispensable to lasting happiness, and she warns specifically against letting illusion corrupt judgment in philosophy and natural science. She seems to treat illusion as necessary for love and ambition, yet as a poison for science.

How should I understand the coherence of these two commitments? Is she proposing a strict compartmentalization—illusion permitted in the passions but banned from the intellect—or is there a more unified account in which the study of nature itself depends on a kind of productive self-deception that sustains curiosity without falsifying results? If the latter, does that undermine her rationalist claim that scientific understanding must be entirely free from illusion? And if the former, how does she avoid the charge that happiness then requires a divided self, constantly policing which domain is allowed to deceive? I am trying to read her not as inconsistently enthusiastic, but as advancing a specific theory of how reason and illusion cooperate; please help me see whether that reading holds up, and where exactly the boundary between permissible and impermissible illusion is supposed to fall.
