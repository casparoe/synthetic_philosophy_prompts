I am developing material for an advanced seminar on the methodology of consciousness studies, and I have been struck by a tension in Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenological approach that my students rarely notice at first glance. The standard presentation holds that by treating first-person reports as descriptions of a fictional world — the heterophenomenological world — the investigator can study consciousness without committing either to the reality of raw feels or to their non-existence. Yet this neutrality seems to depend on treating all reports as uniformly interpretable texts, and I wonder whether it survives closer scrutiny once we attend to the structural differences between subjective reports and reports about external affairs.

Write an essay on the following controversy: does heterophenomenology genuinely avoid the first-person authority problem, or does it merely relocate that problem onto the act of interpreting verbal reports? Weigh carefully the argument that fictionalising reports about an intentional object permits genuine agnosticism about qualia, against the objection that reports of qualitative states carry distinctive features — particularly a resistance to third-person correction and an expressive immediacy largely absent in reports about states like weather — that force the interpreter either to acknowledge a distinctive authority or to eliminate it altogether. You should reach and justify a definite conclusion: if the neutrality collapses, state whether it collapses in favour of realism, eliminativism, or some intermediate position such as a deflationary account of first-person authority. In constructing your argument, consider whether an analogy to legal testimony in adversarial systems helps illuminate the interpreter's position, or whether that analogy misleads by importing notions of credibility and adversarial testing that do not properly apply to subjective report. Please present your answer in British English, and avoid providing any bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations.
