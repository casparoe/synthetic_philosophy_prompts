I retired several years ago from experimental physics and have since begun attending philosophy of science seminars at the university, though I still feel like an outsider among the terminology; I was discussing this very tension with former colleagues over coffee last week, and they insisted that philosophy adds little to practical governance. I now sit on the institutional advisory panel that reviews deployment protocols for autonomous analytical tools used across the national laboratory network, and I must circulate a formal written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The concrete case concerns the proposed Adaptive Inference System for climate-pattern analysis, which our committee can supervise through either continuous process auditing—tracking intermediate representational transformations, flagging anomalous reasoning steps, and requiring explainability at each stage—or through strict outcome auditing, in which we validate predictions solely against ground-truth meteorological records and demographic fairness metrics, leaving the internal architecture entirely unexamined.

My task is to recommend one supervisory regime, or a rigorously defined hybrid with explicit conditions, including a concrete implementation timeline and revision triggers. The memo should treat the opposing view with genuine intellectual respect rather than rhetorical dismissal, and it should articulate the implicit philosophical commitments—about knowledge, institutional trust, or the nature of rational oversight—that each regime presupposes. Given my own transition from a field where reproducibility is validated by both method and result, I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between procedural and substantive rationality can illuminate the choice without forcing a false dichotomy, though I welcome the memo framing the issue in its own terms if that vocabulary proves misleading; above all, I want the recommendation to be something the researchers and administrators could actually respond to and implement.
