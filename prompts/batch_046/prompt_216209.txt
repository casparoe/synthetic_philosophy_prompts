I'm a high school senior in an advanced philosophy and government seminar, and we've been working through the Hart-Fuller debate on legal positivism versus natural law. After discussing the Grudge Informer episode and some constitutional theory, I've tried to map out my own view, but I worry the pieces don't actually cohere. Here is where I've landed: (1) I accept Hart's separation thesis—there is no necessary conceptual link between law and morality—because legal validity seems to depend on a rule of recognition that is just a shared social practice among officials. (2) I think Fuller's eight principles of legality—generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility, stability, and congruence between rule and practice—are best understood as efficacy conditions for guiding behavior, not as conceptually necessary conditions for law; a regime can issue secret, retroactive decrees and still produce law, just law that is dysfunctional and morally monstrous. (3) I also think Hart's concession about the minimum content of natural law—the idea that any viable legal system must prohibit violence and theft—is only a sociological survival condition, not a conceptual bridge that undermines (1). (4) Nevertheless, I believe that when a modern court refuses to apply a statute that violates basic legality—say, a hidden retroactive penal law—the refusal is part of legal reasoning itself, not merely an external moral objection, because legality is built into what we mean by a legal norm. (5) Finally, I think the authority of a rule of recognition is best modeled as a kind of Ulysses contract: democratic societies precommit to interpretive and procedural rules that future officials are not free to abandon at will, and this explains the normative force of legal authority without importing moral truth into the concept of law.

Please audit these commitments for consistency. I want to know which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear tense at first glance. In particular, I am worried that (4) pulls directly against (1) and (2): if legality is internal to legal reasoning, haven't I turned Fuller's desiderata into conceptual conditions after all, and collapsed the separation thesis? At the same time, I wonder whether there is only an apparent tension between (5) and (1)—since a Ulysses-style social practice might seem to require moral binding, but perhaps it can be explained entirely through the internal point of view without any moral commitment. For each real tension, rank it by severity, identify which commitment must be revised or qualified, and specify the cheapest fix and what it costs me—especially whether resolving the conflict with (4) requires giving up my claim that the Grudge Informer court was legally, not just morally, right to resist. Then tell me whether there is a single revision—perhaps reinterpreting (4) as a claim about constitutional interpretation rather than about the concept of law—that would clean up the most problems at once, or whether I must choose between being a consistent positivist and retaining that verdict.
