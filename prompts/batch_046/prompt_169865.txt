I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to formalize my own judgments about when background knowledge actually changes what a person sees, as opposed to only changing how they interpret or judge what they see. Below are four concrete cases with the verdicts I am inclined to give. I want to know whether one principle can capture all four, or whether consistency requires giving up at least one.

Case 1. A senior reliability engineer examines a dense stack trace. She reports that she does not parse individual lines and then infer a null-pointer cascade; rather, the cascade presents itself in the spatial layout of the text. A junior engineer with identical eyesight reports seeing only an undifferentiated wall of characters. Verdict: her conceptual framework penetrates her perception—she literally sees a different visual structure.

Case 2. A developer learns that a particular log format contains a hidden delimiter she had previously ignored. After this instruction, she notices the delimiter immediately in every subsequent log. Verdict: this is not cognitive penetration. She has acquired a recognition capacity analogous to learning a new alphabet; her belief directs attention and interpretation but does not restructure the perceptual experience itself.

Case 3. Two engineers hold incompatible theoretical commitments—one favors a hardware-causation model of system failure, the other a software-causation model. They examine the identical oscillating voltage graph. Each insists that the graph visibly displays the causal chain her model predicts, and they genuinely disagree about what the visual pattern is, not merely about its explanation. Verdict: belief penetrates perceptual content; they have different visual experiences of the same stimulus.

Case 4. An engineer holds a strong prior that her system is stable. Presented with noisy telemetry containing a subtle but genuine anomaly, she reports seeing only random noise. When the anomaly is highlighted, she concedes that it is present in the display but insists that it did not show up for her. Verdict: this is not penetration of perception but a post-perceptual judgmental suppression; she sees the same data as her colleagues but discounts it.

My question: is there a single, precisely stated principle that delivers all four verdicts—accepting penetration in Case 1 and Case 3, denying it in Case 2 and Case 4—while also explaining what distinguishes Case 3 from Case 4? If no single principle works, which verdict should be overturned and why? Please state your principle, or a small set of candidate principles, with the specific clause that handles each case; predict what it implies for an unlisted case (for instance, an expert radiologist who, after adopting a new diagnostic taxonomy, reports that tumors of type T become immediately salient in scans that previously looked uniform to her); and be explicit about which of my four verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate.
