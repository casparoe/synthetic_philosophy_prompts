I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on institutional design and strategic reasoning, and I'm drafting the final exam for my graduate seminar on social choice mechanisms. We've worked through the standard impossibility results, but I want a question that forces students to manipulate the formal conditions themselves rather than recite the theorem's conclusion. A colleague and I were debating whether a peer-evaluation system with self-reported expertise weights could escape classic strategy-proofness failures, and I'd like the exam to turn exactly on that tension—using a synthetic mechanism so students must reason from first principles.

Please construct a single essay question—calibrated for an upper-level graduate audience, allowing roughly sixty minutes and about eight hundred words—centered on the following aggregation rule: a research-grant committee in which each reviewer submits a strict ordinal ranking of proposals together with a self-reported confidence weight on a one-to-five scale. The committee computes a weighted Borda score across reviewers, and ties are broken not randomly but by a predetermined rotating chair. The prompt should ask students to determine whether this rule can simultaneously satisfy strategy-proofness, surjectivity onto the full set of proposals, and non-dictatorship; they must identify two distinct strategic vectors—preference misreporting in the ordinal ranking and gaming of the confidence weights—and then argue precisely whether the mechanism escapes the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result by violating universal domain, surjectivity, or non-anonymity in a substantive way. Crucially, they should not merely name the conditions but explain why the presence of cardinal confidence reports and rotating tie-breaking alters the preference space or outcome mapping, particularly addressing whether the domain restriction introduced by credible confidence reports is exogenous (as in standard restricted-domain results) or endogenous and therefore strategically manipulable itself.

Together with the question, provide a model answer of approximately seven hundred words that demonstrates correct reasoning: that ordinal manipulation persists in the ranking component; that weight manipulation creates a second-order strategic layer; that the rotating chair renders the rule non-anonymous but does not by itself constitute a dictator; and that universal domain is effectively restricted because not all preference profiles are compatible with credible confidence reports—though this restriction is endogenous, which is the conceptual crux. Then supply a marking scheme that distinguishes an A response, which recognizes the endogenous-restriction problem and carefully separates anonymity failure from dictatorship, from a B response, which applies the theorem correctly but treats confidence weights as purely cardinal utilities lying outside the theorem's scope without seeing their strategic interaction. The scheme should name specific errors that should cost marks—such as conflating Bayes-Nash equilibrium reasoning with dominant-strategy manipulation, or asserting that tie-breaking eliminates manipulation entirely. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should stand on conceptual argument alone. Present the exam question first, then the model answer, then the marking scheme, and end with a final line in the exact format: Core insight: [a one-sentence summary of the central pedagogical point of the exercise].
