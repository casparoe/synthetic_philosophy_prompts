I'm trying to clarify my own commitments about whether rotational motion and inertial effects are absolute—determined relative to spacetime itself—or purely relational, fixed only by the distribution of matter. Here are my gut verdicts on several setups, some classic and some constructed. First, Newton's bucket suspended in a room with visible fixed stars: when the bucket spins and the water climbs the walls, I take this to be genuine rotation, not an illusion of relative motion. Second, the same bucket spinning in an otherwise completely empty universe, with no stars, planets, or other bodies present: I lean that the water should remain flat, since there is nothing for the bucket to rotate relative to. Third, in an otherwise empty universe, two identical spheres are joined by a taut rod and rotate about their common center of mass; here I think the rod should experience tension, which suggests the rotation is physically real despite the absence of distant matter. Fourth, a massive hollow shell rotates in empty space, and inside it a Foucault pendulum slowly precesses: I take this frame-dragging as real, and as showing that local inertial frames depend on the presence of enclosing matter rather than on absolute space. Fifth, a niche case I keep returning to: imagine two concentric hollow shells with identical mass distributions rotating in opposite directions about a shared axis, with a small laboratory in the gap between them; I judge that the frame-dragging effects should cancel in the gap. But if the outer shell is then translated radially outward to infinity—leaving the inner shell and the laboratory unchanged in every local geometric property—I think the cancellation should cease, so that the inner shell's dragging suddenly becomes detectable in the gap. This makes me think global distribution, not merely local relation, fixes inertia. Sixth, an infinitely long rotating cylinder of uniform linear density with a test particle on its axis: I expect frame-dragging here too, scaling with density, not with any orientation of space itself.

Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps a qualified Mach principle, or a hybrid substantivalist-relationist view—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts is the outlier that must be dropped, and why? What does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed: a single, isolated rotating sphere in an otherwise empty universe—should it bulge at the equator? And if my double-shell-to-infinity intuition in the fifth case is correct, does that commit me to a form of action-at-a-distance in inertia, or can it be reconciled with local field dynamics?
