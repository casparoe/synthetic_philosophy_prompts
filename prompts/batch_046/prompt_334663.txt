I serve on my university's Committee on Pedagogical Standards and Required Curriculum, and we are reviewing an appeal filed by students in our mandatory first-year program, Foundations of Civic Reasoning. The program's central assessment is a reasons journal in which students record justifications for decisions made in structured ethical simulations—whether to report a peer's minor plagiarism, whether to share limited resources in a group task, or whether to intervene in a low-stakes public conflict. The grading rubric, designed jointly by the philosophy and education faculty, assigns high marks only to justifications that cite obligations, fairness, or the welfare of others, and it explicitly downgrades justifications that cite personal benefit, social reputation, emotional comfort, or long-term self-interest, on the grounds that these are the wrong kind of reasons for moral action and that training in civic reasoning requires students to practice distinguishing genuinely moral considerations from merely prudential ones.

The student appellants do not deny that philosophers distinguish right-kind from wrong-kind reasons; indeed, they reference the familiar metaethical argument that recognizing this distinction is central to understanding moral motivation. Their claim is that embedding the distinction into a mandatory grading rubric violates the university's neutrality policy for required general-education courses. They argue that the rubric treats a contested thesis in moral psychology—that prudential motives are conceptually defective or corrupting when offered as justifications for moral conduct—as settled pedagogy, thereby penalizing students who hold that moral behavior can be rationally sustained by self-interested considerations without being philosophically incoherent. The faculty defenders respond that the course is not demanding adoption of a particular metaethical view, but only requiring students to exercise a form of reasoning—analogous to a logic course requiring avoidance of fallacies—regardless of their private philosophical commitments.

The committee must issue a formal decision memo before the end of the month: either uphold the rubric, reject it, or approve it with explicit conditions, together with a concrete recommendation and an implementation time frame if revisions are required. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend a specific course of action with clear reasoning, and it should treat seriously the strongest consideration on the side you reject—whether that concerns the risk of pedagogical overreach and neutrality violations, the possibility that grading pressure genuinely undermines authentic moral reflection, or the countervailing concern that without such structural discipline a course on civic reasoning collapses into mere prudential counseling. The memo should stand on its conceptual reasoning alone; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
