I've been reflecting on a recurring structure in arguments about self-knowledge and its limits, particularly in cases involving gradual or marginal mental states. Consider an agent who genuinely knows some fact P, but is in a borderline condition—perhaps their justification is adequate but barely so, or they are slowly losing confidence. In these zones, it seems plausible that the agent might still count as knowing P, yet fail to be in a position to know that they know P, since recognizing one's own knowledge may require a stricter standard of certainty than possessing the knowledge in the first place. Now, some philosophers defend a kind of transparency thesis: for certain mental states—being in pain, feeling cold, or knowing P—there are no hidden instances; if you are in the state, you are necessarily able to know that you are. Others supplement this with an iterative principle: knowing P puts you in a position to know that you know P, and so on upward. Taken together, these commitments seem to generate an implausible result: knowing anything in a marginal case should trigger an infinite, fully accessible hierarchy of knowledge states. The standard response is to deny the existence of marginal knowledge altogether—to claim that if you are in a zone where you cannot tell whether you know, then you do not really know.

What strikes me is a parallel diagnostic pattern that appears in discussions of artificial systems. There, observers often argue that a language model does not genuinely know that its own output is false, because if it did, it would correct itself. Here, the failure of second-order access—an inability to recognize and repair the error—is treated as decisive evidence against the presence of first-order knowledge. By contrast, in human cases we routinely allow that someone can know P while failing to recognize that they know P, attributing the failure to stress, cognitive load, or boundary conditions rather than to an absence of knowledge itself.

My question concerns whether this entire configuration—combining a transparency claim about mental states with an iterative knowledge principle, then using second-order failure to eliminate first-order marginal cases—has an established name in epistemology, and whether the circular dependency between first-order attribution and second-order accessibility is recognized as a specific argumentative fallacy. I am also interested in whether there is a term for the asymmetrical application of this structure: treating second-order failure as disqualifying in artificial agents but merely contingent in human ones. I am not asking for a bare label or a reading list; I want to understand the precise logical boundaries of the phenomenon, how it relates to debates about the accessibility of mental content, and whether the artificial-intelligence variant introduces genuinely new edges to the concept or merely reproduces an old error in a new domain.
