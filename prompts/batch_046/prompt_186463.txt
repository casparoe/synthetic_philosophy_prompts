A fellow novelist recently summarized the separation thesis for me in these terms: that a work’s artistic value is strictly autonomous from its creator’s moral character, so that knowing a writer was cruel might explain the darkness in the fiction but can never diminish the book’s aesthetic merit—in fact, vice sometimes yields greater artistic honesty precisely because it refuses comfort. The only legitimate ground for condemning a work, she said, is its internal content, such as propaganda, not biographical revelation about the author; therefore refusing to read a novel because of the writer’s misdeeds is a category error, penalizing the text for sins it did not commit. I am inclined to think she overstates the autonomy, particularly for narrative fiction, where appreciating a character’s voice seems to require the reader to adopt, however temporarily, the author’s imaginative framing—what I would call formal complicity, distinct from mere biographical taint. Is her summary fair? Please separate what careful defenders of aesthetic autonomy actually claim, where she conflates separation with the stronger thesis that moral vice improves art, and whether her conclusion about boycotts survives once we distinguish authorial stain from formal complicity. Give me a committed answer: take a definite stand on whether moral knowledge of an artist can ever legitimately affect our aesthetic judgment of a narrative work, defend that position directly, and do not merely list the options for me to choose between.
