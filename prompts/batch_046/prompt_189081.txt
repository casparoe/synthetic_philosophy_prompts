Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than I intended. (1) If epiphenomenalism is true, then for any phenomenal state φ, φ plays no causal role in producing the judgment J(φ) that one is currently in φ; only the neural substrate N(φ) does. (2) A judgment can count as knowledge of a state S only if there is direct acquaintance with S itself in the causal chain that produces the judgment—call this the Acquaintance Constraint. (3) Under epiphenomenalism, the causal chain for J(φ) includes only N(φ), not φ itself, so J(φ) violates the Acquaintance Constraint with respect to φ. (4) Therefore, if epiphenomenalism is true, phenomenal judgments are not knowledge of phenomenal states. So far this is the familiar paradox. But now apply the same constraint consistently. (5) Consider a judgment about a distant star S: the judgment is caused by present photons and neural processing, not by direct acquaintance with S itself. (6) Consider a judgment about a past event E: the judgment is caused by present memory traces, not by direct acquaintance with E. (7) Therefore, by the Acquaintance Constraint, judgments about distant objects, past events, and theoretical entities all fail to be knowledge, leaving us with knowledge only of our present neural substrates. (8) This is global skepticism, which I do not want to accept. (9) So either I reject epiphenomenalism, or I reject the Acquaintance Constraint. (10) But if I reject the Acquaintance Constraint, then phenomenal judgments could be knowledge through acquaintance with N(φ) alone, which dissolves the paradox and makes epiphenomenalism harmless regarding self-knowledge. (11) Hence the argument seems to show that anyone who wants to preserve the paradox of phenomenal judgment as a special problem for epiphenomenalism must either embrace global skepticism or admit the paradox was never serious. Where does the slide from (4) to (7) go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep (2) and reject whatever lets global skepticism in, but I want to know exactly what that costs me—does it require a different account of phenomenal knowledge, or does it force me to treat phenomenal judgments as structurally different from perceptual ones?
