I am taking a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and its ethical afterlives, and we have spent three sessions circling the same problem in Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life. One faction in the room reads Schlick's central claim—that human life acquires meaning only in playful, self-contained activity rather than in purposive labor—as a straightforward normative prescription about how one ought to organize one's existence. The opposing faction treats it as a descriptive, almost psychological observation about the conditions under which people experience their lives as meaningful, entirely compatible with Schlick's verificationist dismissal of metaphysical questions about life's purpose. Both factions agree on the textual evidence: Schlick's contrast between the youthful mode of unreflective engagement and the mature mode of instrumental work, and his insistence that purpose annihilates value rather than creating it. Yet the disagreement is completely immobile.

Is this stall a substantive philosophical conflict—perhaps about whether logical empiricism can consistently host normative ethics—or is it a verbal dispute about what the word meaning is doing in the essay? Or are the two sides actually pursuing different questions, one about Schlick's philosophical consistency with the Circle's anti-metaphysical program and the other about the phenomenology of value-experience, while pretending to debate a single thesis? Give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually takes Schlick to be arguing, and tell me what kind of move—whether a conceptual distinction, a closer reading of his verificationist commitments, or an admission that the question is ill-posed—would actually advance things, or whether we should expect permanent deadlock.
