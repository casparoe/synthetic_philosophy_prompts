I've been thinking about that old puzzle where a donkey starves between two identical bales of hay because it has no reason to pick either one — Buridan's ass, I believe it's called. In everyday conversation, people often resolve this kind of deadlock by saying you might as well flip a coin, and that injecting arbitrary randomness solves the paralysis entirely. Some friends of mine have started extending this coin-flip picture more broadly: they claim that any decision system, human or artificial, can always break a tie with some noise or random tie-breaker, and that this means "true indifference" is never genuinely paralyzing in practice, only a theoretical curiosity. But I recently came across the idea that in continuous spaces — say, choosing a point along a line where every position is exactly as good as every other — there is no discrete "tie" to break at all, and that randomization might not rescue you from a kind of infinite indecision rather than a simple binary stall. I am trying to understand how far the coin-flip analogy actually carries: what in the donkey's situation maps onto the coin, what maps onto the two bales, and where the correspondence quietly breaks? Does the analogy smuggle in assumptions — like discrete options, or the idea that randomness counts as a real preference — that the original paradox does not contain? And if we take those disanalogies seriously, especially the continuous-space case, does the conclusion that "indifference is never paralyzing" survive, or does it collapse? I'd also like to know whether there is a better analogy for what happens when preference genuinely runs out, rather than one that assumes we can always manufacture a reason after the fact.

Please say what would change your mind: which argument or observation about decision-making without preference — perhaps involving continuous choice spaces, arbitrary tie-breaking mechanisms, or the distinction between selection and rationalization — would lead you to a different verdict about how far the coin-flip analogy holds, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
