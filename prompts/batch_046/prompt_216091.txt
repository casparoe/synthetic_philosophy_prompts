I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the ethics of autonomous systems, and I need a joint adversarial-collaboration statement drafted between two opposing camps on responsibility gaps. The first camp—the gap theorists—holds that sufficiently autonomous systems can generate genuine moral and legal responsibility gaps: situations in which harm occurs, no human agent is justifiably blamed, and the system itself lacks the conditions for moral agency, leaving a normative void rather than a merely hidden assignment of blame. The second camp—the distributed-accountability theorists—denies that true gaps exist; they maintain that responsibility is always structurally distributed across designers, data curators, deployers, regulators, and users, and that apparent gaps are always epistemic or institutional failures to trace accountability through the socio-technical network, not ontological absences.

Please produce the document both sides could sign after sustained good-faith collaboration. Structure it exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover shared commitments about the nature of autonomy in current and near-future systems, the necessary conditions for moral agency, the causal and epistemic structure of harm-producing events, and the real limits of both human oversight and algorithmic transparency—formulated precisely enough that neither side considers them vacuous.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, provide two formulations: one in language the gap theorists would accept as their own position, and one in language the distributed-accountability theorists would accept as theirs. The disagreements must address at least: (i) whether the absence of a clearly responsible party can ever constitute a normative void as opposed to an uncompleted tracing task; (ii) whether functional decision-making autonomy in a system can sever the moral relevance of upstream human choices; and (iii) whether the phenomenon of human operators absorbing blame that structurally belongs to systemic design—sometimes discussed as moral crumple zones—shows that gaps are illusory because blame is always displaced rather than absent, or instead shows that gaps are routinely concealed by scapegoating mechanisms.

Third, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would genuinely move at least one camp. I take two likely cruxes to be whether epistemic fragmentation across multi-agent design teams renders distributed responsibility conceptually incoherent, and whether any notion of design-intent preservation or naturalness carries independent normative weight in assigning accountability. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is genuinely probative—such as a well-documented incident in which an autonomous system caused harm and a post-hoc investigation either successfully traced accountability through every relevant layer or demonstrably failed to do so—together with each side's explicit prediction in advance about what that case demonstrates.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to obscure theoretical frameworks not generally covered in philosophy of technology and AI ethics. The response should be answerable from conceptual analysis alone, without web access, and should avoid straw-manning either position. Keep the tone formal and the formulations exact.
