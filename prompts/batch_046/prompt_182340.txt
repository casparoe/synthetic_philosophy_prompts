For my senior philosophy seminar I have been studying humanitarian intervention and regime change—specifically NATO’s 1999 Kosovo campaign, the 2003 Iraq invasion, and the 2011 Libya operation. In policy debates and popular commentary I keep encountering a medical analogy: removing an authoritarian regime is like performing emergency surgery to excise a malignant tumor, where the international coalition is the surgical team, the state is the patient, the population is the healthy tissue at risk, and the dictator is the cancer. Supporters invoke this to justify rapid action—the tumor kills if left untouched—while critics reply that foreign powers lack informed consent from the “patient,” bear no fiduciary duty to the individuals affected, and that the “operations” in Iraq and Libya produced severe iatrogenic harm: state collapse, insurgency, and prolonged instability that exceeded the original “pathology.” I want to audit how far this analogy actually holds. Please map the correspondences explicitly—surgeon, patient, tumor, anesthesia, post-operative care, recovery—onto the political actors and structures in these three cases. Then identify the ethically relevant disanalogies: can a sovereign state meaningfully “consent” to being operated on by outsiders? Does the clinical concept of iatrogenesis legitimately apply to political failure after regime removal, or is that a category mistake? Also examine whether the analogy quietly imports a “therapeutic misconception”—the research-ethics error in which subjects confuse an experiment’s goals with personal therapeutic benefit—by framing intervention as purely curative for the local population when intervening powers may have strategic, non-therapeutic motives. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the pro-intervention conclusion from those that are merely decorative. If the analogy collapses, suggest a more accurate comparison; if it survives in part, state precisely what ethical work it can still do. Please end with a one-sentence summary on its own final line.
