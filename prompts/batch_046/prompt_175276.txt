I've been digging into virtue ethics and that situationist critique from social psych — you know, the Milgram obedience stuff, the seminary study where people in a rush literally step over a guy who looks hurt, all the data showing that helping behavior doesn't correlate much across situations. There's a specific argumentative pattern I keep seeing in papers and seminars that I don't have a word for, and I'm wondering if it's a named thing or just my own confusion.

Here's what it looks like in practice. First, someone points out that behavior is wildly context-dependent — like, the same person who returns a lost wallet might not help in a crowd, and there's no stable "honesty" trait predicting both. They use that to say global character traits don't do explanatory work. Fair enough, that's the standard situationist challenge. But then, when they defend virtue ethics, they turn around and describe virtues as exactly these finely-tuned, situation-specific capacities — phronesis as reading the room, courage as knowing when to stand firm versus when to retreat, etc. So far that's just the local-trait reply, and I get it. The part that feels different is what happens next: they still want to talk about someone being "a courageous person" or living "a just life" as a holistic evaluation, but they build that evaluation out of a scattered patchwork of local responses without requiring any cross-situational consistency. It's not that they secretly think there's a global trait; it's that they've replaced global traits with an aggregated set of fragmented ones, yet they use the same thick evaluative language globally. I notice this especially in discussions of moral education, where they say you cultivate virtue by practicing in specific contexts, but then they assess whether you've succeeded by judging your character as a whole. Is there an established concept for this exact move — not the local-trait view itself, but the way it lets global evaluation sneak back in through distributed local judgments? How is it different from just being inconsistent, and if there's no name, how should we carve the boundaries: does it require explicit denial of global traits, or can it happen with thinner virtue-language too?

If I'm just describing the standard local-trait reply badly and there's no real distinction here, tell me straight — don't invent a term or draw fancy lines just to be polite. I'd rather know I'm muddling things together than get a made-up label. Also if the phenomenon is real but unnamed, I'd want a definition that clearly seperates it from plain equivocation and from the motte-and-bailey move people sometimes pull with "character".
