As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently begun sitting in on philosophy-of-science seminars, I have been tracking a colleague's methodological rulings in cross-linguistic cognition research. Her precedents, stated consistently across five cases, are these. She permitted treating Dutch and Guugu Yimithirr speakers as independent samples in a physical spatial-rotation task, despite their opposing spatial grammars. She denied independence for Mandarin and English speakers in a translated temporal-sequencing narrative, holding that vertical temporal metaphors survive translation and couple the conditions. She permitted independence for Munduruku and English speakers tested with culturally neutral approximate-number dot arrays. She denied independence for Turkish and Japanese speakers in a shared evidential-attribution video protocol, arguing that both languages grammatically encode source monitoring and thereby embed the linguistic variable into the instrument. Finally, she permitted independence for Berinmo and English color-boundary studies, but only on the condition that stimuli are calibrated to each language's focal categories rather than to a universal physical metric.

The question is what she should rule, or predictably will rule, on a new proposal that does not map cleanly onto any of these. The study compares probabilistic independence judgments between Turkish speakers and English speakers: participants decide whether two sensor readings should be pooled in a Bayesian update, where one reading is direct perceptual evidence and the other is inferential reconstruction. The critical novelty is that the task is administered in a counterfactual evidential frame—participants must evaluate whether the independence relation holds when the grammatical marking of the inferential source is counterfactual rather than actual. The investigators propose pooling the two language groups.

Predict her verdict, identify the closest analog in her track record, and state which implicit rule—mechanistic-causal independence of grammatical structures, epistemic independence based on researcher knowledge, or instrumental independence based on whether the studied linguistic feature is embedded in the measurement protocol—best explains her pattern. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, articulate the principle she would adopt by ruling in favor of pooling and the principle she would adopt by ruling against it. Finally, specify a single contrasting study design—using a different language pair and a different cognitive domain—that would disambiguate which of the three rules she is actually running.
