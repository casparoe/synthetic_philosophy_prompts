In my philosophy-of-mind seminar we have been debating whether phenomenal consciousness should be treated as a user illusion in the strong sense: not merely that introspection is unreliable, but that subjective experience functions as an interface layer that conceals its underlying neural implementation, analogous to a graphical desktop hiding machine code. The dispute has persisted for decades without resolution. One side, drawing on interface theories of perception and predictive-processing frameworks, argues that phenomenal properties are functional constructions—useful fictions that guide behavior without corresponding to any intrinsic feature of the brain—while the other side maintains that this amounts either to an eliminativist bait-and-switch or to a category error that confuses epistemic access with ontological status, insisting that first-person data must be preserved rather than explained away. My own hypothesis is that the stalemate is largely conceptual rather than empirical: the two camps appear to operate with structurally different notions of “illusion,” with one treating it as an ontological demotion (there are no phenomenal properties, only representational shortcuts) and the other treating it as an epistemic misrepresentation (phenomenal properties exist but are distorted by introspection).

I want a committed diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions: is the disagreement genuinely about hidden empirical facts—say, whether predictive models can fully account for qualitative character—or is it about what counts as an adequate explanation, about which introspective reports serve as legitimate data, about standards of evidence in first-person science, or about starting intuitions regarding first-person authority that neither side can argue for? I also want you to consider whether injecting a specific distinction might actually break the deadlock—between what we might call an architectural illusion, in which a system hides its machinery for functional efficiency without misrepresenting its nature, and a representational illusion, in which the system positively misrepresents the intrinsic character of its contents—or whether such a distinction merely relocates the same disagreement under new labels. Please take a definite stance on what kind of disagreement this really is—verbal, substantive, methodological, or ill-posed—and state clearly what kind of progress, if any, should be expected: a formal result in cognitive modeling, an experimental dissociation, a reconceptualization of the explanatory target, or an argument that no resolution is possible and why.
