As a doctoral student in philosophy of religion working on normative ethics in Buddhist traditions, I am trying to clarify the tension between consequentialist and virtue-ethical readings of karma and precept-observance. Compare the Mahāyāna interpretation of skillful means (upāya) as a form of long-range suffering-reduction across rebirths with the Theravāda Abhidharma view that treats the five precepts (pañcaśīla) as constitutive conditions of mental purification rather than as welfare-maximizing instruments. Specifically, evaluate how each framework resolves the following constructed case: a bodhisattva faces two actions that generate exactly identical aggregate karmic merit, but one distributes that merit among beings in the human realm, who possess advanced capacities for moral practice and liberation, while the other distributes it across humans, animals, and pretas, whose capacities for utilizing merit differ radically. Would the consequentialist reading privilege the realm-weighted distribution that maximizes enlightenment-potential, or reject such weighting as a distortion of universal compassion? Would the Abhidharma-inspired virtue-ethical reading reject the calculus entirely, holding that the uniform applicability of non-harm and truthfulness makes realm-specific welfare aggregation morally irrelevant? Justify which interpretation offers the more coherent internal account of Buddhist moral psychology, and identify a further hypothetical—perhaps involving the intermediate state (antarābhava) and the temporal lag between action and rebirth—that would force these two interpretations to diverge in a decisive, empirically distinguishable way.
