After a long argument with a friend about the Catalan, Scottish, and Quebec secession debates, I want an audit of the analogy she keeps returning to: that a constituent region's relationship to its state is structurally like one spouse's relationship to a marriage—entry requires consent, continued membership requires continued consent, and a partner who wants to leave should not be trapped by the other partner or by the institution itself. She deploys this to defend an unconditional right of unilateral secession: if a clear regional majority votes to exit, the union has no more moral standing to block departure than an abusive spouse preventing a divorce. I countered that the union is closer to a joint venture with shared infrastructure, shared public debt, and minority stakeholders distributed across the other regions, so exit should require negotiation rather than unilateral notice. Please map both analogies explicitly—correspondence table for consent, dissolution, shared assets, debt, the analog of "abuse," and the rights of the remaining parties—then identify where each breaks down, particularly with respect to whether there is a "remedial" threshold that must be satisfied before exit is justified versus an unconditional right of withdrawal. Which institutional features of marriage law or corporate dissolution does each side quietly import that the state does not share, does either analogy actually license its intended conclusion once those disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better analogy that preserves the consent-based origin of the union without ignoring the interdependence that makes secession costly for both sides?
