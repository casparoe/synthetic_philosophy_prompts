I sit on the three-person acquisitions panel of a regional museum that processes roughly forty donation proposals a year, with a ten-day review window and mandatory director sign-off for anything admitted to the conservation track. The board applies a two-part working framework for art-status: an item must be embedded in a recognized art-world institution (gallery, museum, critical discourse, or documented curatorial framing), and the intentional stance that makes it art must be intersubjectively recoverable—a competent viewer can grasp it without privileged access to the maker’s private mental history. Write a reusable decision procedure the panel can run on any proposal: ordered questions, what counts as adequate evidence at each step, clear stop-points for acceptance and rejection, and explicit flags where judgment rather than framework-application must decide. Apply it to three cases: (A) two hundred handwritten notebooks from a deceased outsider containing an undeciphered private symbol system, never exhibited, offered with an art historian’s claim that the symbols encode emotional states; (B) an image algorithmically generated by ten thousand anonymous users following a protocol designed by a recognized digital artist, displayed in an established online gallery; (C) a conventional landscape painted for commercial decoration, now held in a prestigious museum’s permanent collection. One application should yield a result the panel is likely to resist. Also specify what the procedure cannot settle—for instance, aesthetic merit, or whether this two-part framework ought to be adopted at all. If the framework itself mixes incompatible commitments—say, if institutional framing and intersubjective recoverability pull in opposite directions that practice cannot reconcile—say so plainly rather than constructing a flowchart that hides the contradiction.
