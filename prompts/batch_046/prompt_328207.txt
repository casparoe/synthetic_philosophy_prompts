I sit on the acquisitions and ethics panel of a university museum. In 1987 the museum received a large-scale ceramic installation—designated Work 47—under a deed of gift that stipulates that, should the museum ever remove it from permanent display in the east-wing gallery, the original donated object must be returned to the donor's heirs. Over the intervening decades, conservators have replaced roughly sixty per cent of the clay substrate: individual elements cracked and were remade using the artist's original moulds, others were substituted following flood damage, and a small number were reconstructed from archival photographs without access to the original moulds. Throughout, the museum's catalogue entry, the curatorial interpretation, and the visible spatial arrangement have remained constant.

The donor's estate now asserts that Work 47 is no longer the original donated object because its material substrate is predominantly new, and demands immediate restitution. Our legal advisers note that the deed's language is genuinely ambiguous, and the board has asked our panel to recommend whether the museum should resist restitution (treating the installation as materially and constitutionally continuous), agree to return it, or propose a negotiated settlement—while giving explicit philosophical reasoning that addresses both material-constitution theory and institutional risk.

Please draft a formal decision memo for the board, written in British English, recommending one of the three courses above. The memo should have a clear actionable bottom line and should treat the statue-and-clay problem as concretely instantiated in this conservation history rather than as a generic thought experiment. In particular, it should weigh at least three distinct metaphysical stances—material continuity, formal or structural persistence, and what we might call institutional constitution, whereby an artwork's identity is partly constituted by its curatorial record and exhibition history—and should include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration favouring the losing side. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific philosophers or texts; the reasoning must stand independently. Where you invoke conceptual distinctions—such as diachronic versus synchronic identity, or the difference between replacement of parts and replacement of substrate—define them briefly within the memo itself.
