I hold a hybrid deflationary position: for empirical discourse, truth is redundant—asserting that S is true adds nothing to asserting S—while for normative discourse it functions not as property-ascription but as a closure operator on practical commitments, marking a judgment as non-revisable within its current deliberative frame without invoking correspondence to normative facts. Tell me what would genuinely count against this view. Beyond the familiar objection that deflationism cannot accommodate normative truth-talk, I want its exposure mapped precisely: which formal features of closure operators—idempotence, monotonicity, failure under belief revision—mismatch actual practices of moral commitment and repudiation? Which conceptual cases—moral conversion, incommensurable frameworks, retrospective rejection of previously “closed” judgments—show the model either over-committing or secretly collapsing into normative realism? And which results in belief-revision dynamics or practical-reasoning theory undermine the claim that normative truth-talk serves this functional role rather than expressing a property? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed hybridist could concede it without surrendering the core empirical/normative distinction, or whether it forces retreat either to full-blown normative realism or to pure redundancy across both domains. I am particularly interested in whether the hybrid risks being insulated from counterexample in a way that makes it unfalsifiable, and whether that insulation is a theoretical vice.
