I sit on a university bioethics panel that has recently been reviewing whether absolute brain mass ought to play any direct role in assigning moral status. Our settled precedents are these: we granted full protective status to a human neonate with severe microcephaly whose brain mass was below 350 g; we assigned intermediate, but not equivalent, status both to an adult chimpanzee (approximately 400 g) and to a sperm whale (approximately 8 kg); we ruled that an anencephalic infant lacks the interests grounding full rights, though minimal palliative obligations remain; we held that a hypothetical synthetic being with a 10 kg neural substrate but human-equivalent cognitive architecture would possess full moral parity; and we denied collective moral status to an integrated hive of 40,000 bees whose combined neural tissue exceeds 60 g, on the grounds that distributed cognition without centralised integration does not aggregate into a unitary subject. We now face a proposal concerning a bio-engineered aquatic organism — a distributed, cephalopod-derived system carrying roughly 600 g of neural tissue dispersed across 180 semi-autonomous ganglia, displaying problem-solving, preference-aversion, and memory behaviours comparable to a domestic pig, yet possessing no central brain and no stable, integrated self-model.

What should we decide if we wish to remain consistent with our own track record? Identify the closest analog among our precedents, state what principle we would be adopting if we grant intermediate status versus if we deny it, and specify which further case would most sharply distinguish the competing interpretations. Please present your reasoning in British English, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
