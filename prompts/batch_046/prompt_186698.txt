Write a brief audit of the following summary, which a colleague offered after reading popular articles on mechanistic interpretability: "Mechanistic interpretability has established that deep networks encode discrete feature directions that linearly decode human-interpretable properties, so representational realism is essentially confirmed. The remaining disagreement is purely about causal efficacy: realists claim these directions drive computation, while anti-realists treat them as correlational artifacts with no functional role. Since anti-realists offer no competing account of why targeted interventions on these directions change model behavior, anyone who accepts the empirical findings should be a realist; remaining skepticism is mainly reluctance to apply neuroscience-style reductionism to artificial systems." Evaluate this claim by claim. Which parts align with actual arguments made by defenders of representational realism about networks (for instance, appeals to causal mediation or feature visualization)? Where does it mischaracterize anti-realist positions—such as the distinction between denying causal efficacy altogether versus denying that correlational decoding is sufficient for representation? Does the conclusion that the empirical results compel realism depend on an unstated premise about what conditions are necessary for representation? And is the analogy to neuroscience doing substantive work, or is it a rhetorical framing that obscures differences between biological and artificial systems?
