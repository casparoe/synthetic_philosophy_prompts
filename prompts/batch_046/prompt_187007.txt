In the EA-animal-ethics circles I hang out in, there's this arguement about insects and the scale thing that's been going in circles for like five years. One camp says look, there are probably 10^19 insects, even if sentience is only 0.1% likely that's still enormous expected suffering, so we should fund welfare biology and maybe genetic interventions. The other camp says the prior is basically uncalibrated, expected-value reasoning with wild guesses is epistimically reckless, and treating "billions times near-zero" as decisive just smuggles in a hidden assumption that harm agregates linearly across entities we can't even individuate. Both sides read the same nociception literature, both agree suffering is bad, and yet nobody's moved an inch. I think the stall isn't about entomology — maybe it's about whether statistical aggregation over non-individuated beings can generate moral reasons at all, which sounds like a disagreement about the ontology of harm rather than risk assessment. But I could be wrong. Is this actually a hidden empirical question about calibrating priors for invertebrates, or is it bedrock intuition about whether "vast numbers" can ever outweigh "probably not conscious"? Or are they answering different questions — one about expected utility, one about permissibility under radical uncertainty? I want an honest diagnosis with reasons, not a survey, and given that diagnosis what would actually break it? A conceptual distinction, a decision-theoretic result, a reframing? Or is it just that neither side can defend thier starting intuition about agregation and we should expect it to stay stalled?
