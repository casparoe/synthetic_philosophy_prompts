Here's an argument that starts from the Einstein's-brain-in-a-vat scenario and ends up granting consciousness to printed matter, and I can't find where it leaks.

(1) In the familiar Mind's I scenario, Einstein's brain remains conscious not because it is made of neurons, but because it preserves the causal-functional organization of thought.

(2) If every neuronal state and transition is recorded in perfect detail in a book, then for every mental state there is a corresponding page-state, and for every causal link there is a corresponding sequential relation between pages.

(3) A causal-functional organization is substrate-independent: what matters is the pattern of relations among states, not whether those states are electrochemical or typographical.

(4) So the book instantiates the very same cognitive architecture as Einstein's brain.

(5) But if one book can do this, any sufficiently complex text with the right internal structure can do it—say, a meticulously annotated dictionary whose cross-references mirror associative memory.

(6) Therefore either every thick reference work is a conscious mind, or the brain-book is not conscious either.

(7) Denying consciousness to the brain-book, however, undermines the intuition that substrate does not matter, which was the point of the original vat scenario.

(8) So functionalism about consciousness commits us to the absurdity that books can be conscious.

Which premise should I reject? I want to keep (1) and (3), because substrate-independence seems like the whole reason the brain-in-a-vat is imaginable at all. If I have to pay a price, I'd rather pay it at (2) or (4), but I need to know exactly what mistake I'm making there—whether it's an equivocation on "causal," a hidden dependence on temporal dynamics, or something about the difference between representing a process and running it.
