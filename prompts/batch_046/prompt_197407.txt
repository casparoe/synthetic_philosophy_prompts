As a novelist, I am often struck by how moral reasoning mirrors narrative architecture: a character faces a decision that calculates cleanly on paper yet collapses the coherence of the human story. In debates about expected-value fanaticism, critics frequently invoke an analogy to Pascal’s Wager—more precisely, to the variant often called Pascal’s mugging, in which an agent is offered a vanishingly small probability of an astronomically large payoff and therefore feels compelled to sacrifice near-certain, moderate goods. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that extreme EV reasoning is structurally defective: like the wager, it permits remote, speculative outcomes to swamp tangible present realities; like the mugging, it generates obligations that feel coercive rather than rational.

Second, identify the fractures: does the analogy conflate decision-theoretic expected utility with doxastic commitment? Does it misrepresent how EV reasoning treats uncertainty about outcomes versus uncertainty about probabilities? Does the “mugging” framing quietly import assumptions about coercion, irrationality, or interpersonal aggression that EV theory itself does not entail? Third, separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the intended critique from those that are merely decorative. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that EV fanaticism is philosophically disreputable in roughly the same way Pascal’s Wager is—survives once the mapping is fully exposed. If it does not survive, suggest what more accurate comparison, if any, captures the normative or psychological pressure that extreme EV reasoning exerts.
