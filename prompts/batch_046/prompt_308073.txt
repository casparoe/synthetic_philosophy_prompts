In a graduate seminar on distributive ethics, my study group has been stuck for weeks on the rescue-case problem associated with John Taurek: if you can save either one stranger or five strangers but not both, is it permissible to flip a fair coin between them, or are you morally required to save the greater number? The standard reply—that of course you save the five, since each life has equal value and five equals more than one—feels to me like it restates the intuition rather than addressing Taurek’s specific argument that there is no transitive “better for” relation that aggregates distinct individuals into a collective whose loss outweighs the single person’s loss. Meanwhile, those sympathetic to Taurek often invoke “equal concern” without explaining why that concern does not entail saving the five, given that five separate people are each receiving it. Both sides agree on the non-moral facts and on the badness of death, yet neither has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about whether “numbers count” in some generic sense, but about a hidden disagreement over the structure of moral reasons—specifically, whether a reason of the form “X is a reason to save A” can be summed or weighed across distinct persons without committing to an interpersonal comparison that both sides actually reject when it suits them. The pro-numbers side treats each reason to save one of the five as individually sufficient but collectively decisive, while the other side treats each reason as directed at a specific person and therefore non-comparable with the others. Is this diagnosis right, or is the disagreement actually verbal—two groups using “equal respect” to answer different questions, one about what an agent must do under scarcity and the other about what it means to treat people as ends? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether a life can be part of a sum? Please diagnose the stall using what each side actually says, distinguish substantive from verbal or framing disagreements, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about the logic of reasons, a revised case that isolates the aggregation step, or a conceptual distinction that shows the question is ill-posed?
