I am a high school senior enrolled in an introductory practical-reasoning course, and last week I argued with friends about whether quitting is always rational once you have already invested heavily in something. Outside of school I volunteer twice a week as an assistant program coordinator at our town’s small community arts center, where the director regularly asks me to help evaluate whether to continue underperforming initiatives—such as a youth pottery workshop that has already spent half of its grant funding yet consistently draws only two or three participants. I need a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with clear stopping points—that my director and I can run in about twenty minutes before a board check-in, without requiring any formal economics background. The procedure should translate what philosophy and decision theory actually say about sunk costs into practical steps: it must identify when honoring past investment is genuinely irrational, and when continuing might be defensible—for example, because of incomplete information about future returns, commitment signaling to future funders, or obligations to current participants who depend on continuity. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be flagged for human decision, and state exactly where the procedure stops with a recommendation to discontinue. Then apply the procedure to two real-style proposals: first, finishing the final eight weeks of the pottery workshop despite persistently low attendance; second, extending a poorly attended senior-citizen digital-literacy course that has already consumed its entire equipment budget but serves a vulnerable population with no alternative access to technology training. Include at least one case where the procedure advises discontinuation even though my personal loyalty to the participants makes me want to keep the program running. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle—such as conflicts between fiscal responsibility and the intrinsic value of artistic or educational access—so that I know precisely when to escalate the decision to the board rather than resolving it myself.
