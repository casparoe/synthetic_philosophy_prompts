In archival theory and historiography, particularly following the turn that treats archives as active sites of production rather than neutral storage, scholars frequently invoke a paleontological analogy: the archive is like a fossil record, and archival silence is analogous to the absence of soft tissue in the geological column. By borrowing the concept of taphonomy — the study of how organisms become fossils — into archival science, proponents suggest that the historian, like the paleontologist, can infer properties of the missing soft tissue (oral culture, ephemeral resistance, subaltern practice) from the pattern of the hard remains (bureaucratic files, legal documents, institutional correspondence), and that the archive's silences therefore carry diagnostic information about the forces that shaped them.
