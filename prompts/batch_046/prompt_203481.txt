Write a formal dialogue between two software engineers with no formal philosophy background, debating whether personal identity survives the gradual, neuron-by-neuron replacement of a human brain with synthetic analogues that are behaviorally and functionally equivalent. Engineer A should defend a "rolling-upgrade" view: she compares the brain to a distributed system undergoing live node replacement with state replication, arguing that identity is maintained by continuous functional coherence and an invariant information architecture rather than by the persistence of original biological substrate; she should claim that if 99% of the system remains causally connected to the original at every intermediate step, the final result is the same person, not a duplicate. Engineer B should defend a "causal-process" view: he argues that each synthetic insertion breaks the specific spatiotemporal causal chain that originally constituted the person, so the procedure slowly destroys the original and constructs a functional copy—like migrating a service to entirely new hardware with copied state. They must respond directly to each other's actual points: A should address B's duplication objection by explaining why continuous functional maintenance differs from branching or copying; B should address A's rolling-upgrade analogy by explaining why retaining majority-original nodes at every step still fails to preserve the particular causal continuity of the original once every biological element is eventually replaced. The dialogue should reach a committed verdict—state clearly which position is stronger and why, with definite reasoning—rather than ending in an unresolved stalemate. Keep the exchange technically precise but accessible, and do not invoke philosophical authorities, bibliographies, or citations.
