Two researchers, Elena and Marcus, have been arguing about a large adaptive climate simulation that reallocates computational resources in response to emerging storm patterns. Elena takes the physical stance: the system is only silicon and electricity, and any talk of it seeking accuracy or focusing on fronts is mere metaphor. She grants that the design stance is legitimate—we can describe the mesh-refinement algorithm—but insists that calling the system's emergent global hemispheric bias an intentional pattern is sloppy anthropomorphism, since the bias was not programmed and has no predictive value beyond the design-level rules. Marcus takes the intentional stance seriously: he argues that the hemispheric bias is unpredictable from the local design rules alone, that treating the model as an agent balancing accuracy against cost compresses its behavior in a way neither the physical nor the design stance achieves, and that this predictive success makes the pattern real in exactly the sense that matters. Elena replies that Marcus has confused practical convenience with ontological weight: the physical stance could in principle derive the bias, and predictive compression at one level does not license inventing mentalistic vocabulary. Marcus answers that could in principle derive is a red herring—if a pattern genuinely supports counterfactual predictions and cannot be recovered without massive computational cost at lower levels, it is real regardless of physical reducibility, and the design stance fails precisely here because it only explains local adaptation, not the global strategic behavior.
