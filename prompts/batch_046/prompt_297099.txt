I'm writing a piece on statues and street names for my science-and-ideas column and I've tied myself in knots over an argument I sketched out on the train. Here it is, steps numbered so you can see the gears.

1. If a public statue or street name commemorates someone, it doesn't merely record history; it expresses collective endorsement of that person's values.
2. If a community endorses values that justify serious harm, the community bears some responsibility for that harm.
3. Most figures we commemorate — explorers, scientists, politicians — held values that justified exclusion, exploitation, or violence by the standards of thier time and ours.
4. So retaining thier monuments makes us responsible for those harms.
5. But responsibility for harm can be discharged only by repudiating the values that caused it.
6. Therefore we must remove every statue and rename every street linked to anyone whose values justify harm.
7. That wipes out nearly all public commemoration, including people we still genuinely admire.
8. The only escape is to deny (1) and say monuments don't endorse values, but then there's no reason to keep good ones either — they become meaningless decoration.
9. So either we erase all memory or we abandon the ethics of commemoration entirely.

I keep wondering if there's a slip between (3) and (4), or maybe a scope error hiding in (5) about what counts as "values" versus actions. There's also this concept I've been calling "mnemonic overhang" — the idea that a monument keeps generating normative obligations long after its original purpose has rotted away — and I suspect that's muddying my thinking rather than helping. Which premise should I give up? I'd rather keep (1) than (7), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying if I do. Please reply in British English, and don't bother with a bibliography — I'm working offline and just need the logic sorted.
