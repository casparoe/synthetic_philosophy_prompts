I've been moderating a small philosophy discussion group that grew out of a rationalist forum, and we're returning to the paradox of tolerance—not merely the familiar political formulation, but the stricter logical version in which the principle of universal openness becomes reflexively self-undermining when applied to itself. For our opening, I want a poem that treats the paradox as a structural failure of normative consistency rather than as a practical dilemma about which groups to suppress. Write a fourteen-line English sonnet in strict iambic pentameter, with an ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme scheme. The octave should present the principle of unlimited tolerance in its most rational, almost axiomatic form; the volta at line nine should reveal that the intolerant faction is not an external intruder but a logical consequence generated by the principle itself. The sestet should then trace the collapse in two moves: first the attempt to draw a defensive boundary, then the recognition that any such boundary violates the original axiom. The rhyme scheme and meter should remain formally intact throughout, so that the poem's stable architecture contrasts with the conceptual instability it describes. The closing couplet should state the familiar conclusion—that unlimited tolerance must destroy tolerance—but phrase it as a tautological trap rather than as a policy recommendation. It should be precise enough that a reader familiar with the standard philosophical literature recognizes the argument, without relying on citations, direct quotations, or bibliographic references.
