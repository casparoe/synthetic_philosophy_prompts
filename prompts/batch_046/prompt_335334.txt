I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of science and metaphysics. I now serve on the Advisory Council for Scientific Institutional Heritage, and we face a concrete determination. The Meridian Long-Term Observatory, founded in 1923 and rebuilt entirely after a fire in 1971, has since replaced every original instrument, revised its founding charter twice, and turned over its staff completely. Its directors petition for certification as a single continuous institution, which would qualify it for an endowment reserved for uninterrupted research programs spanning at least a century. They argue that its geographic site, core measurement protocol, and institutional purpose constitute an enduring identity despite complete material turnover. A minority of council members—drawing on four-dimensional metaphysics—argue that we should treat the observatory as a temporally extended series of successive stages rather than one enduring entity, and therefore deny continuous status.

Please draft the Council's formal decision memo recommending a specific course of action—grant continuous status with conditions, deny and reclassify as a successor institution, or approve a transitional evaluation period of defined duration—with a clear implementation timeline. The memo should engage the directors' continuity argument seriously rather than dismissing it as mere sentiment, and should present the strongest metaphysical consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty. In addition, please clearly distinguish what is philosophically settled (for instance, that both endurantism and perdurantism remain live theoretical frameworks) from what is genuinely contested among specialists in the metaphysics of persistence (for instance, whether functional or spatiotemporal continuity can ground institutional identity), and do not present either metaphysical view as the established consensus.
