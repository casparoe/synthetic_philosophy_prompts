In a metaphysics reading group I have been facilitating, we keep returning to the old question of whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg—what is usually called origin essentialism—without anyone conceding ground. The essentialist side holds that your actual biological origin is metaphysically necessary: had a different sperm fertilized that egg, or had conception occurred in an entirely different body, the resulting person would not be you but a counterpart or a stranger. The opposition, drawing on counterfactual identity conditions and general suspicion of modal intuition, insists that personal identity should not depend on contingent biological contingencies, and that treating gametic origin as essential confuses a causal story with an identity condition. Every standard move is familiar: essentialists invoke the intuition that you could not have been born to different parents; critics reply that modal judgments about origins are unreliable, or that identity conditions ought to track psychological continuity or some structural feature rather than this particular zygote. The discussion feels frozen, yet nobody agrees whether we are disagreeing about a fact of modal metaphysics, about what counts as legitimate evidence, about the framing of the word origin, or about something else entirely.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the stall substantive—are there hidden empirical premises about embryology or development that both sides smuggle in—or is it verbal, turning on whether essential origin means the specific gametes, the specific nuclear genome, the specific causal chain of conception, or the specific gestational environment? Do the sides answer different questions, with one camp investigating modal properties of organisms and the other investigating conditions for first-person reference? Or is the disagreement rooted in competing starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, masked by shared vocabulary? To ground this, please engage the concrete positions: the essentialist claim that altering the sperm or egg changes the individual, and the critical response that this overweights biological contingency. Then tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is—factual, normative about standards of evidence, semantic, or a case of talking past one another—and specify what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the conflict, an empirical result about development that resolves it, a formal model of origin conditions, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because origin fragments into incommensurable senses. I offer this as a hypothesis rather than a conviction: injecting a distinction between gametic origin, genetic-sequence origin, and causal-environment origin may reveal that the debate has been running on ambiguity rather than on genuine opposition, but I want you to test whether that diagnosis holds up against what each side actually says.
