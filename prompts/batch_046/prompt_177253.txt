I am currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and our recent discussions of Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance have left me with a conceptual difficulty I would like to work through in writing. Specifically, I want to compare Dennett’s stance with what I will call a counterfactual-robustness interpretationism: the view that attributing beliefs to a system is warranted not merely by predictive success from the intentional stance, but by whether the system’s representational states exhibit structural coherence across counterfactual perturbations, such that altering one belief-related output forces compensatory adjustments elsewhere in its behavior, mirroring the holistic constraints that Quine and others took to characterize belief networks. I am trying to determine whether Dennett’s purely instrumental criterion—adopting the stance whenever it yields predictive leverage—can genuinely distinguish between an artificial system that represents its environment and one that tracks statistical correlations without representational content.

To make the comparison concrete, consider a large language model that consistently produces correct statements about physical objects in its training environment yet fails to maintain consistency when those objects are placed in counterfactual or physically impossible configurations. On Dennett’s account, if adopting the belief stance predicts its outputs well, we are justified in attributing beliefs; on the counterfactual-robustness view, the absence of holistic adjustment suggests sophisticated pattern-matching rather than belief. Which framework offers the more philosophically defensible boundary for belief attribution in non-biological systems? Furthermore, does Dennett’s stance implicitly presuppose the kind of representational coherence that the counterfactual-robustness view makes explicit, or does his instrumentalism deliberately avoid such metaphysical commitments? I am drafting an essay that takes both positions seriously, so I would appreciate a comparison that does not reduce one to the other, but identifies precisely where they diverge on the ontology of mental representation in artificial agents.
