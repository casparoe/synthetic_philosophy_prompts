I am the senior certification referee for a maritime strategic forecasting office that issues monthly contingency assessments for the Eastern Mediterranean. Every week analysts submit proposed reports predicting specific geopolitical events over a 90-day window—blockades, port closures, fleet engagements, treaty violations. My task is to sign off, within 30 minutes per report and without requiring philosophical training, on whether a report may be published with a definitive bivalent framing (e.g., “there will be a blockade” or “there will not be”) or whether it must be rewritten in an open-contingency format that avoids assigning a present truth-value to the future event. The decision requires my signature and, whenever a boundary flag is triggered, concurrent sign-off by our legal counsel.

Operationalize for me the principle raised by Aristotle’s sea-battle problem and its modern successors: if an event is genuinely contingent—not necessitated by present conditions or laws, and dependent on choices or conditions not yet resolved—then propositions about its occurrence or non-occurrence lack a determinate truth-value now, even though they may later acquire one or be assigned rational credences. Translate this into a reusable decision procedure—checklist, flowchart in words, or ordered tests—that a referee can apply repeatedly. The procedure must:

1. Distinguish genuinely open future events from those that are physically determined, logically necessary, or merely unknown.
2. Detect whether the report’s language assigns a bivalent truth-status to the future event.
3. Specify the exact reformulation required when (1) and (2) both hold, including template language.
4. Identify the hard boundary cases where judgment cannot be eliminated: physically determined but epistemically opaque events; self-fulfilling or self-defeating reports; forecasts about collective decisions that are themselves pending (including our own ministry’s); and nested conditionals (“if treaty X holds, then event Y”).
5. Flag precisely where the rules run out and human judgment is required, noting that legal counsel must concur in those instances.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases:

- Case A: A report asserts, based on satellite imagery and supply-line analysis, that a specific state will establish a naval blockade of a named port by 15 June.
- Case B: A report asserts that tidal and seismic conditions will render a specific harbor unusable on a named future date.
- Case C: A report commissioned by our own ministry asserts that our fleet’s planned deployment will trigger an adversarial naval response, but the deployment decision itself is still pending internal approval, and the report is being used to justify that very approval. I expect the procedure will yield an answer I do not want—either blocking definitive publication or requiring a framing that undermines the report’s rhetorical urgency—so show me exactly how that happens.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, whether it resolves metaphysical disputes about the nature of time, whether it applies to reports that use only explicit numerical credences, and whether it can adjudicate self-referential forecasts.

In your answer, say how confident you are in the procedure overall, and single out the specific step or distinction about which you are least sure.
