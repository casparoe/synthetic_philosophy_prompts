You are an expert in philosophy of law, moral psychology, and the free will debate, serving as an external senior advisor to university governance bodies. Before you begin drafting, think carefully about the distinction between backward-looking moral desert and forward-looking institutional design, and briefly outline the three strongest considerations on each side of the question. Then write a formal decision memo for our university's disciplinary appeals panel.

The panel must decide whether to uphold the expulsion of a doctoral candidate in cognitive neuroscience who admitted to fabricating data in two published papers. The student does not deny the misconduct; instead, her appeal argues that the university's disciplinary code implicitly relies on a retributivist framework of just deserts that is philosophically untenable given contemporary neuroscience. She invokes a free-will-skeptical position—associated with philosophers such as Gregg Caruso—according to which, absent libertarian free will, backward-looking blame and punishment are unjustified, and institutional responses should instead follow a public-health quarantine model that protects the research community and rehabilitates the offender without moral condemnation. By contrast, the faculty disciplinary committee defends the expulsion by appealing to a compatibilist account—broadly in the spirit of Daniel Dennett's defense of the varieties of free will worth wanting—according to which culpability in the code does not require metaphysically robust free will, but rather refers to the agent's competence to respond to reasons and her role-responsibility within the scientific community.

Our institutional code states that sanctions must be proportionate to the severity of the misconduct and the culpability of the actor. The panel is divided: some members want to reject the appeal and uphold expulsion on standard grounds; others worry that retaining culpability language commits the university to a metaphysical position it should avoid.

Your memo should recommend a concrete course of action—uphold the expulsion, overturn it, or uphold it with modified conditions—along with a specific time frame for any follow-up policy review (for example, within 90 days). It should treat the free-will-skeptical argument seriously rather than dismissing it with a legalistic citation of policy. Crucially, it should propose how the panel might interpret culpability in the code in a non-retributive, role-based sense—what we might call institutional desert—that distinguishes moral worth from normative expectations tied to professional role-occupancy, without requiring the university to resolve the metaphysical free will debate. Include an honest assessment of the strongest objection to your recommended course. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in polished, formal American English suitable for circulation before Thursday's meeting.
