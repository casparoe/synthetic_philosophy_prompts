I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I facilitate a ninety-minute workshop for about ten to twelve technically trained colleagues—mostly engineers and data scientists—who are interested in conceptual problems but have no prior coursework in the field. I want you to design a structured, runnable classroom activity centered on Bertrand’s paradox and the principle of indifference, but approached through the lens of random sampling and feature representation that my group will recognize. Specifically, participants should be divided into three subgroups, each assigned a different algorithm for generating “uniformly random” chords in a circle: one using uniformly random endpoints on the circumference, one using uniformly random radial distance and orientation, and one using uniformly random midpoint location inside the circle. Each subgroup should physically draw or compute a sample of chords, determine for each whether it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, and report an empirical frequency. Only after all three groups have presented should the facilitator reveal that the three procedures yield the classic conflicting probabilities—approximately one-third, one-half, and one-fourth—despite describing the same geometric object. Specify the rules, materials (paper templates, rulers, or a brief script they can run in any language), assigned roles if useful, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The conflict must emerge from the participants’ own actions and calculations, not from a lecture. Include a debrief that maps each sampling procedure to a hidden assumption about what “ignorance” means—uniform over endpoints, over radius, over area—and explains why the principle of indifference fails to fix a unique prior without reference to an invariant transformation group. Be explicit about which philosophical dimension—particularly the normative claim that any one parameterization can claim objective privilege—the exercise cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally. Also warn me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, participants attributing the discrepancy to a calculation error rather than to conceptual ambiguity about the space of possibilities) and advise how I should intervene without resolving the ambiguity for them. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation of texts; everything must be runnable without outside sources. Write in American English, keep the tone formal and precise, and avoid verbatim quotations from any source.
