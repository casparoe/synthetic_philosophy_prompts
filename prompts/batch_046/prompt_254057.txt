I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether Confucian role ethics can survive its own reflexivity, and I want to check whether my position is actually coherent before I defend it in writing. Here is where I stand:

(1) I take role ethics seriously in a constitutive sense: moral obligations do not apply to social roles from the outside; they are generated by the relational structures themselves, so there is no external moral standpoint from which to evaluate them.

(2) I think moral knowledge is role-indexed: you cannot genuinely understand what yi demands in a situation without being embedded in the relevant relational web; detached theoretical reflection can approximate but never fully grasp the norm.

(3) I want to distinguish a normative core, ren — the quality of being fully human in relation — from a culturally variable shell of specific rituals, li; this lets me criticize particular local expressions of filial piety or loyalty without abandoning the underlying role.

(4) I accept what I think of as role residue: obligations that linger after the social role technically dissolves, such as duties to a deceased parent or to a community one has permanently left, and I treat these as genuine moral demands rather than sentimental leftovers.

(5) When roles genuinely conflict — say, ministerial loyalty versus family duty — I deny there is any meta-role algorithm or universal priority rule; resolution must come through practical wisdom, zhi, exercised from within one of the competing roles.

(6) Despite all this, I want to claim that the relational structure underlying these roles is universal in scope: every human society involves analogues of parent-child, elder-younger, and ruler-subject relations, even if the rituals differ.

My friend said that (4) kills (1), that (3) smuggles in an external standpoint forbidden by (2) and (5), and that (6) is incompatible with the anti-universalist spirit of (1). I think at least one of her objections misses, but I am not sure which. Can you audit this package?

Specifically: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say exactly what has to give — which commitment must be revised or dropped — and what the cheapest fix looks like. Do not just list them as equally bad. Also tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most problems at once, and what it costs. I am not signaling which commitment I would abandon; I want the audit to show me where the pressure points are.
