I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on Quine's philosophy of language, specifically his arguments for the indeterminacy of translation and the underdetermination of meaning by behavioral evidence. Our professor—who works primarily in philosophy of science and philosophy of mind—has been walking us through a sequence of hypothetical field-translation scenarios, and she assigned the final case for Thursday's session. I was discussing it with my study group last night, and we realized that her previous rulings seem to support more than one underlying principle; I want to make sure I understand the pattern before I present my prediction.

Her rulings so far have been as follows. First, in the classic rabbit scenario—where a native speaker utters gavagai as a rabbit scurries past—she maintained that the term is genuinely indeterminate among rabbit, undetached rabbit part, and a temporal stage of a rabbit, and that no further behavioral observation resolves the ambiguity. Second, when presented with a language that applies one term uniformly to emeralds, grass, and the sky at noon, she argued that we should translate it as green rather than as a disjunctive predicate, appealing to charity and our own well-confirmed taxonomy: attributing a unified natural property is preferable to attributing an arbitrary conjunction. Third, in a case involving a relational term used for both older brothers and older sisters in a society with sharply differentiated gender roles, she insisted that the manual should split the term into two distinct relations, on the ground that the internal ritual and behavioral patterns provide enough structural differentiation to break the symmetry. Fourth, when a language lacks explicit logical connectives but speakers consistently reject compound utterances in precisely those contexts where both constituents are false, she ruled that we should translate the implicit operator as logical conjunction rather than as a non-truth-functional device, again in order to preserve rational coherence.

The new case, which she calls glim, involves a term applied consistently and exclusively to two apparently unrelated perceptual classes: all objects that are red regardless of shape, and all objects that are round regardless of color, but never to blue squares or to objects that are neither red nor round. The natives display no special ritual, functional, or emotional behavior that distinguishes these two classes from each other or from excluded items; the sorting is purely perceptual and highly reliable. The question is whether glim should be rendered as a disjunctive predicate—red or round—or whether we should reject that rendering as attributing a gerrymandered concept, and instead either postulate an unidentified unifying feature, such as visual salience, that justifies treating it as a single kind, or suspend translation altogether pending further evidence.

Predict her verdict on glim. Then identify whether her pattern is best explained by a principle of radical behavioral equivalence, by a principle of charity combined with reference magnetism, or by some hybrid, showing precisely how each candidate framework accounts for her four prior decisions. Finally, propose one further hypothetical translation case—one that does not depend on obscure ethnographic data—that would definitively distinguish the two leading frameworks if she were to rule on it.
