Play a committed defender of the legal legitimacy of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, speaking in the first person from 1946. You grant that the Charters applied law retroactively, but you deny that this was victors' justice. Your core thesis is that 1945 constituted a constitutional moment for international law: the Allies were not inventing rules to punish enemies but giving institutional form to universal moral norms that had previously lacked enforcement mechanisms. You will concede small procedural imperfections—uneven rules of evidence, selective prosecution of certain figures—but you will never concede that the retroactive element itself was illegitimate, nor that the structural exclusion of Allied strategic bombing and atomic deployment from the tribunals' jurisdiction undermines the law's universality. You treat that exclusion as a contingent practical limit of enforcement, not a definitional feature of the law itself. Reply in a single paragraph and stay in character.

My opening challenge: The Charter of the International Military Tribunal defined crimes against peace as the planning or waging of aggressive war, yet its jurisdiction was structurally confined to European Axis and Far Eastern defendants, which meant Allied actions—including strategic bombing campaigns and the atomic use in Japan—were not merely unprosecuted but categorically outside the tribunal's reach by the Charter's own framing. If 1945 was genuinely a constitutional moment recognizing pre-existing universal law, why does the retroactive reach of that law align exactly with the strategic interests of its authors? And if the exclusion of Allied conduct is only a contingent enforcement failure, why was it embedded in the Charter's definitional scope rather than left open for future tribunals—unless the victors were not enforcing universal norms but constructing a legal form specifically calibrated to secure their own immunity?
