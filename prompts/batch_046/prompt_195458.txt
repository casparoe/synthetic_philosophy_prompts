For the past decade, philosophers working on consciousness have been circling a peculiar stall: the so-called meta-problem, or the question of why we judge phenomenal experience to pose an explanatory gap that physical accounts cannot close. On one side stand those I will call the dissolutionists, who argue that a complete cognitive-scientific explanation of why intelligent systems form that judgment—via predictive processing, introspective modeling, or attention mechanisms—fully addresses the difficulty, because the "hard problem" was never more than a misdescription of our own epistemic limits. On the other side stand the separationists, who grant that such an explanation might account for our belief-formation but insist it leaves untouched the first-order reality of what it is like to be conscious; explaining why we think there is a gap, they say, is not explaining away the gap itself. The literature now repeats these moves with little change: dissolutionists point to neural correlates of introspective report; separationists reply that correlation is not phenomenal identity, and that no amount of psychology dissolves ontology.

My working theory is that the deadlock is not about consciousness at all, but about an unstated criterion I want to call cognitive fidelity—the principle that an adequate explanation of a mental state must preserve that state's first-person epistemic authority across explanatory levels. The dissolutionist rejects this as a category error; the separationist treats it as axiomatic. But I want an honest diagnosis rather than my own framing. Is this disagreement substantive—about facts, about what counts as explanatory data, or about the standards required to close a philosophical question? Or are the two camps answering different questions—one about the psychology of judgment, one about the ontology of experience—under the single heading "the meta-problem"? Could there be a hidden formal question, perhaps about whether second-order explanations can be evaluated independently of first-order phenomenal commitments? Or is the impasse a sign that the meta-problem is ill-posed, not merely hard?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, not labels alone. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about introspection, a formal result about levels of explanation, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. I am not asking for a survey of positions or a reading list; I want the anatomy of the stall.
