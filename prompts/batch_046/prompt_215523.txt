At a faculty meeting about curriculum reform, a colleague defended cutting the history requirement by saying, 'History is for life, not for knowledge,' and added that students need practical skills rather than antiquarian accumulation. A defender of the humanities replied that this misreads Nietzsche: critical history is precisely the instrument that frees life from dead tradition, so shrinking the curriculum eliminates the very mode that serves life. Both seemed to assume they knew which of the three uses—monumental, antiquarian, or critical—was being served, but neither specified the precise thesis they were invoking. Separate the distinct claims that could hide inside the slogan that history is for life, not for knowledge: (i) a purely instrumental claim that historical study is justified only by present utility; (ii) a psychological claim that excessive historical consciousness paralyzes action and must be limited; (iii) an epistemic claim that historical truth should be subordinated to life-affirming interpretation; (iv) a typological claim that only monumental history serves life, while antiquarian preservation and critical dissolution are harmful excesses; (v) a therapeutic claim that critical history is necessary to dissolve antiquarian over-attachment, meaning that 'too much history' specifically denotes too much uncritical preservation rather than too much critical examination. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible Nietzschean position, a misreading, or flatly false. Then tell me which reading the vocational-training advocate actually needs for his argument, and whether the humanities defender's appeal to critical history answers it or changes the subject. Say also whether the slogan, on any defensible reading, licenses reducing historical education rather than reshaping its mode.
