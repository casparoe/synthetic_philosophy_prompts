I'm designing a 90-minute module for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophies of history—eighteen students with mixed backgrounds in philosophy and history—around the problem of whether cyclical and linear narratives are competing explanations of the same institutional dynamics or descriptions shaped by what survives in the archive. Rather than lecturing on Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's cyclical asabiyya, or the linear-progress and decline-phase schemas in Spengler and Toynbee, I want the students to experience what I will call archival framing drift: the idea that whether a sequence of crises reads as repetition or development depends less on the events themselves than on which institutional responses are preserved as precedent and which are discarded as noise. The session should run as a structured simulation with assigned roles, hidden rules, and a small-stakes payoff scheme using points and collective privileges rather than real money, so that the macro-pattern emerges from their local choices before anyone names it.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for this group and time frame, specifying the division into subgroups, materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline, the rules of interaction, and the roles students will occupy. The core mechanics should involve three successive councils—each representing a generation—confronting an identical structural crisis, such as a fiscal-military legitimacy breakdown, under different archival rules: in each round the records they inherit from the previous council are selectively preserved and translated into a new vocabulary (legal-rational, moral-organic, technocratic). The councils must choose among the same three structural responses—centralized consolidation, federated risk-sharing, or dissolution and reconstitution—but they should not be told that the underlying payoff matrix is unchanged. Arrange the incentives so that rational local choices reproduce a macro-pattern that can be read either as cyclical return or as linear progression, depending entirely on which records the final round retains and which it discards. Include explicit roles—such as chronicler, councillor, and archivist—with rules that prevent the facilitator from explaining the pattern in advance, and make sure the idea shows up in what happens rather than in a concluding lecture.

After the simulation, the debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto features of the cyclical and linear views—showing, for instance, where Polybius's corruption of constitutional forms or Ibn Khaldun's decay of group solidarity appears in the students' own justifications, and where a Spenglerian or Toynbeean framing could be imposed on the exact same sequence without contradiction. Be frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, such as students in the third round recognizing the repetition too early, chroniclers writing misleading records that collapse the ambiguity, or one council defecting from the hidden payoff structure and breaking the collective pattern. Also include an honest assessment of which feature of these historical philosophies the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the longue durée, the metaphysical or spiritual dimension of decline, or the claim that the cycle is genuinely inevitable rather than institutionally constructed—and note how the facilitator must supply that limitation verbally rather than through the rules. The students should leave seeing that the dispute between cyclical and linear history is not always resolvable by more data, because the data themselves are products of archival selection, but they should also see that this underdetermination does not make the dispute merely verbal: different preservation rules generate genuinely different predictions about institutional stability. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for quotations from the original texts that would require verification.
