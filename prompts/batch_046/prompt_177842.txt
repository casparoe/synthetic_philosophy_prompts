At a late dinner following a colloquium on consciousness and language, two guests—an older philosopher of science and a younger cognitive psychologist—became locked in a disagreement about the meta-problem of consciousness, and I, seated between them, was asked to referee and could not. The psychologist opened by conceding that explaining subjective experience in physical terms is genuinely hard, but insisted that the deeper puzzle is meta-theoretical: why do intelligent observers systematically frame that difficulty as an ontological chasm rather than as an ordinary epistemic boundary? She proposed that phenomenal concepts function perspectivally, analogous to indexicals, so that mastering every objective neural fact still leaves untouched the first-person mode of presentation; the sense of an explanatory gap is therefore not evidence of a missing ontology, but merely the recognition that two cognitive routes—objective and perspectival—do not converge. The philosopher granted the mechanism but pressed a second-order objection: if we genuinely understand that phenomenal concepts are perspectival and that no convergence should be expected, why does the intuition of physicalism's incompleteness persist with such force? He argued that her account explains the genesis of the intuition but not its stubborn survival after that very explanation has been internalized, comparing it to learning the optics behind a mirage yet continuing to see water. The psychologist responded by constructing what she called a tactile-inversion scenario—not the familiar color-spectrum reversal, but a hypothetical in which two subjects share identical neural architectures and behavioral dispositions except that their tactile phenomenal properties are systematically swapped, roughness experienced as smoothness and vice versa, with no behavioral or reportive discrepancy—claiming that this demonstrates the perspectival mechanism in its pure form, since each subject accesses only her own tactile mode and no third-person correction is available. The philosopher conceded the descriptive point but denied its normative significance: he said that recognizing a cognitive mechanism is precisely what permits us to correct for it, and that her inversion case merely illustrates a bias—like the mirage—rather than validating an irreducible gap; the persistence of the intuition is therefore corrigible, not revelatory. She answered that to call the resistance a bias is to presuppose a neutral, non-perspectival standpoint from which correction could proceed, yet if phenomenal concepts are irreducibly first-personal, there is no view from nowhere to serve as the corrective baseline; therefore, labeling the intuition erroneous begs the question against the very framework she has offered. They asked me to adjudicate each of these five moves—her initial perspectival dissolution, his second-order persistence objection, her tactile-inversion demonstration, his correction-as-bias reply, and her charge of question-begging—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, then to say who is more right overall and to identify what both are failing to address that might break the deadlock. I honestly could not. Please go through the exchange move by move as requested, and say what, if anything, each party is missing.
