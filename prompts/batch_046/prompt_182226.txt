As a journalist reporting on the philosophy and institutional design of decision-making, I have observed a recurring pattern that I have not been able to map onto any standard concept. Consider selection committees, funding panels, or algorithmic governance boards faced with two options—candidates, proposals, or system parameters—that are genuinely equivalent on every criterion the body officially recognizes: equal predicted utility, equal risk profile, equal cost. Rather than accepting the tie and deploying an explicit randomizing mechanism, the group typically resolves the deadlock by seizing on an incidental feature—the order of presentation, a minor rhetorical difference, or an unarticulated affective response—and treats that feature as if it were decisive. Once the selection is ratified, members routinely begin to defend the chosen option as manifestly superior, not merely adequate, and they tend to suppress or forget that the decisive factor was originally acknowledged as arbitrary. I have seen this in peer review, in hiring committees, and in automated tie-breaking protocols that humans resist precisely because they expose the absence of preference. The phenomenon is not Buridan's paralysis—the problem is not an inability to move, but an active refusal to tolerate an acknowledged vacuum of preference, followed by the retrospective construction of a preference narrative around whatever arbitrary mechanism broke the tie. Is there an established term for this specific sequence: the rejection of explicit randomization under true equivalence, coupled with post-hoc rationalization of the arbitrary resolution? How should it be distinguished from adaptive preference formation, from cognitive dissonance reduction, and from the classic Buridan problem? If no precise concept exists, what would be the appropriate boundaries of a new term—particularly with respect to whether the reconstructed preference must be sincerely held, or whether it can remain purely strategic?
