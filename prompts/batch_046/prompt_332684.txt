ok so i've been chewing on the absent-minded driver problem and i think i've pushed it into a weirder corner. here's an argument that starts reasonable but ends up saying rational people should never finish long projects if they have any memory gaps. tell me where it breaks.

(1) In the absent-minded driver case you need to exit at the second of two identical intersections. Because of perfect amnesia between them, at either node you can't tell which one you're at.

(2) There's a principle people call peer deference: if someone is your epistemic peer about Q and you know you'll disagree, you should shift your belief toward theirs, unless you have an independant reason to distrust them.

(3) Your future self at intersection two is your peer about where you are right now. Same reasoning, same evidence (none that distinguishes the nodes), same cognitive architechture.

(4) At intersection two your future self will rationally decide to keep driving, because from their viewpoint they might still be at intersection one.

(5) So by (2) you at intersection one should defer to them and keep driving too.

(6) But (4) applies at every node. There's no fixed point; deference just loops forever, so rationality never tells you to exit.

(7) Generalise: whenever you anticipate a specific rational-in-context failure in your future self, peer deference requires you to import that failure back into your present plan.

(8) That means if i know i'll be sleep-deprived tomorrow and undervalue risk, i should defer to tomorrow's peer-judgement today and take the reckless option now. Absurd.

(9) So something between (2) and (7) has to be wrong. Is it that peer deference doesn't apply across temporal stages? Or that your future self isn't actually a peer about indexical location? Or is there a modal slide hiding in (5)?

I'd like to hang onto (2) — peer deference feels solid in normal cases — and i'd also like to hang onto the standard intuition that you should plan to exit at the second intersection in the original problem. Which premise do i actually reject, and what does that rejection cost?
