In my graduate seminar on Marxist social ontology, a debate has persisted for several semesters without movement, and I am no longer sure what kind of disagreement it is. The question is whether the economic base—understood as the prevailing relations of production and corresponding forces—explanatorily determines the legal, political, and ideological superstructure, or whether those superstructural instances possess a relative autonomy that makes base-to-superstructure causation irreducibly indirect. One camp, which I will call the reductionists, insists that every stable superstructural form ultimately functions to reproduce the base, and that appeals to autonomy confuse functional correlation with causal independence. The other camp, the autonomists, grants that the base sets boundary conditions but argues that institutional logic introduces what I would term structural translation: economic imperatives must be mediated through normative and organizational schemas that possess their own internal coherence, so that the same base can generate divergent superstructures depending on the institutional architecture of translation. Both sides accept the same historical cases—the codification of Roman law, the emergence of the modern administrative state, the persistence of religious ideology long after its economic function has ostensibly lapsed—yet each interprets these as confirming its own framework. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical, since both sides agree on the historical record, nor purely verbal, since both use explanation with technical precision; rather, I suspect the disagreement concerns the criteria for explanatory adequacy in social science—specifically, whether an explanation must identify a mechanism with temporal priority and counterfactual dependence, or whether it can legitimately invoke structural causation that operates without direct temporal sequencing. But I am uncertain whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is a hidden normative disagreement about whether attributing autonomy to institutions undermines the critical force of materialism. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication: is this a substantive dispute about causation, a disagreement about standards of explanation, two different research programs answering distinct questions under one heading, or an impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about human agency? And if your diagnosis is correct, what—if anything—would count as progress? Would it require a formal model of institutional translation, a conceptual distinction between determination and conditioning, an empirical study of cases where translation fails, or should we conclude that the base-superstructure framing itself is ill-posed and ought to be abandoned?
