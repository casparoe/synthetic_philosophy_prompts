I just finished a long and frustrating argument with a friend about Wittgenstein on religious belief, and before I present my view at a philosophy of religion reading group I want a consistency check. Here is the package I have landed on:

(1) Religious belief, on Wittgenstein's account, is not an empirical hypothesis or explanatory theory about the world; it does not compete with science and is not vulnerable to scientific disconfirmation.
(2) At the same time, religious belief is genuinely rational — it is not reducible to mere emotion, social habit, or arbitrary commitment.
(3) The rationality in question is fully internal to the religious form of life: the criteria for what counts as meaningful, justified, or correct within religious discourse are fixed by the practices and language-games of the believing community.
(4) Therefore there is no neutral, external standpoint from which an outsider could adjudicate religious belief by evidence or argument; a non-believer cannot be rationally compelled into belief, and a believer owes no answer to scientific standards.
(5) Nevertheless, religious belief can genuinely collapse — specifically, when a believer begins treating it as an empirical hypothesis and finds it failing, this misunderstanding of the belief's own nature can undermine it from within.

My friend insists that (2) together with (3) collapses into a fideism that makes religious belief arbitrary, and that (5) flatly contradicts (1) because if the belief is not empirical it should not be vulnerable even to misapplied empirical failure. I suspect the tension between (4) and (5) is only apparent — if there is no external adjudication, an internal category mistake could still be destructive — but I am unsure where the real fault lines lie.

Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and identify which single revision would clean up the most problems at once without destroying the core idea that religious belief is both non-empirical and genuinely rational.
