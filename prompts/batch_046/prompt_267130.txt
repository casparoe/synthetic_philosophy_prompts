Question: In Kripke's discussion of rule-following, he introduces a skeptical challenge using an agent who has previously added numbers only below 100 and now computes 68 + 57 = 125. The skeptic proposes that the agent might actually be following 'quaddition'—giving correct sums for pairs below 100, but returning 5 for all pairs at or above 100. (a) Why do appeals to the agent's present mental instructions or past behavioral dispositions fail to determine that the agent means addition rather than quaddition? (b) How does Kripke's skeptical solution account for the normative distinction between correct and incorrect applications of a rule without reinstating a metaphysical fact that the agent means addition?

Student answer:
"In part (a), the central difficulty is that any finite record of past behavior or current mental representation is compatible with infinitely many deviant interpretations. The agent might possess an internal mental symbol for addition, but that symbol can itself be reinterpreted: perhaps it encodes quaddition with an implicit boundary condition that activates above 100. Because mental states are finite and can be parsed in multiple ways, they cannot uniquely determine which rule is being followed. Past dispositions suffer the same problem: observing that the agent previously answered '58' to '30 + 28' is consistent with both addition and quaddition, since both rules agree below the threshold.

For part (b), Kripke's skeptical solution accepts that there are no metaphysical facts about individual meaning. Instead, meaning is constituted by the normative practices of a linguistic community. Speakers correct one another's errors and converge on judgments, which establishes public conditions for attributing a rule correctly. This is essentially an extension of Wittgenstein's private language argument to mathematical rules: without communal checks, there is no distinction between following a rule and merely thinking one is following it. The community's collective agreement therefore fixes the rule determinately—if the majority computes 125 for 68 + 57, then quaddition is eliminated and addition is the unique rule being followed—though this does not reveal a hidden mental fact. Thus, the skeptical solution dissolves the paradox by substituting collective normative practices for individual metaphysical semantics, conceding that there is no 'deep' fact about addition independent of these practices."
