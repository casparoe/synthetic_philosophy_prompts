I've been argueing with a friend about whether AI can actually think, and I got pulled into this old debate between Searle and Dennett about original versus derived intentionality. From what I gather, Searle's view is that only biological brains have original intentionality—real aboutness—while books, computer programs, and maybe LLMs only have derived intentionality that we humans project onto them, the way a dictionary entry only means something because we interpret it. Dennett pushes back with something called the two-bitser, which I think is a minimal toy system tracking just two bits of state—maybe temperature versus target—that still gets interpreted as having beliefs or desires. He seems to use it to argue that once you have the right interpretive patterns, there's no deep metaphysical line separating original from derived intentionality. But every time I read about it there both sides just talk past each other: Searle insists syntax isn't semantics and no program understands Chinese, while Dennett says scaling up the interpretive framework makes the distinction collapse.

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I keep comparing this to API docs: a docstring tells you what a function is "about," but the running code itself doesn't know anything—its just derived meaning assigned by the programmer and users. Is the stall here actually a hidden factual disagreement about whether brains do something physically unique that silicon can't replicate? Or is it verbal—just a fight over what counts as "real" meaning? Or are they answering totally different questions, one about biological mechanisms and one about how we interpret systems, all under one heading? What's the diagnosis using what each side actually says, and if it's not substantive, what kind of conceptual distinction or experiment would actually move the needle? Or is the question just ill-posed?
