question: explain the lottery paradox and the preface paradox in your own words. then discuss whether they share a common root — specifically, whether both are best solved by rejecting closure under entailment for rational belief, or whether the preface case involves a distinct kind of epistemic humility that makes closure rejection the wrong tool there. be precise about where the contradiction actually arises in each case.

jordans answer:
the lottery paradox, introduced by Kyburg in his classic 1961 paper, involves a fair 1000-ticket lottery. for each ticket the probability it loses is 0.999, so by the Lockean thesis — rational belief requires credence above a high threshold — you should believe ticket 1 loses, ticket 2 loses, and so on. apply closure and you should believe they all lose together. but you also know the lottery is fair, so one ticket wins. contradiction.

the preface paradox, also coming out of Kyburgs same work on rational belief, involves an author who has checked every claim in her book and finds each well supported, so she believes p1 through pn. she also knows authors make mistakes, so she believes not-all(p1...pn). closure says if she believes each pi she should believe the conjunction, which conflicts with her fallibility belief. so again rational belief forces contradiction.

the standard fix is to reject closure. if belief isnt closed under entailment then believing each ticket loses doesnt commit you to the conjunction. same for the book. kyburg himself takes this route and argues we should deny that rational belief requires global consistency — local justification is enough even if the total set is incoherent.

but wait, in the lottery the real issue might be the Lockean threshold, not closure. if you keep lowering the threshold you still generate contradiction with enough tickets, so closure rejection alone doesnt save the probabilistic account. meanwhile in the preface the author genuinely accepts each chapter and also genuinely accepts that shes fallible, so closure is actually violated in practice, not just in theory. so theyre similar but not identical: one is about statistical aggregation, the other about self-knowledge. still, dropping closure handles both cleanly.
