Write a villanelle — nineteen lines arranged in five tercets followed by a concluding quatrain, observing the traditional refrain scheme — for the close of a postgraduate seminar on epistemology. The subject should be Wittgenstein’s notion of hinge propositions, developed in his later reflections on certainty: those framework beliefs (that there is an external world, that the earth has long existed) that stand fast not because they are supported by evidence, but because they form the axis around which ordinary doubting and justifying turn.

Let the two refrains carry the philosophical weight structurally. One refrain should state a particular hinge that remains fixed; the other should articulate the condition that makes empirical doubt possible — namely, that enquiry presupposes a background that is not itself in question. Through the tercets, allow ordinary beliefs to shift, be tested, or fall away, while the refrains return unchanged, so that the form enacts the asymmetry between doubting within a system and doubting the system itself.

The quatrain — where both refrains finally appear together — should serve as the poem’s turning point, marking the recognition that these fixed points are neither empirical hypotheses nor arbitrary articles of faith, but the hinges on which the door of knowledge swings. The image of the hinge itself should appear, not as mere ornament, but as the governing metaphor: something that holds fast precisely so that movement can occur elsewhere.

The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in Wittgenstein’s On Certainty recognises the distinction between hinge and ordinary belief, the idea that certainty is not a kind of knowledge, and the peculiar status of framework propositions; yet it should also work as a poem for readers unfamiliar with the concept, so that the repetition feels inevitable rather than illustrative. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or suggested further reading — the verse should be self-sufficient.
