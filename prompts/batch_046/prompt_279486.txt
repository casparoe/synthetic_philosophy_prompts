I serve on the International Commission on Stratigraphic Nomenclature's Anthropocene Working Subcommittee. We must issue a formal recommendation to the full Commission on whether to ratify the Anthropocene as a formal chronostratigraphic epoch, and if so, where its Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point should lie. The proposal before us is not merely empirical: it forces a choice among three competing ontologies of historical periodization.

Option A — the Stratigraphic Realist position — fixes the epoch's base at the 1945 Trinity-test plutonium-239 fallout spike, arguing that a geological epoch requires a synchronous, globally correlatable signal in rock, ice, and sediment, and that moral or economic narratives are categorically irrelevant to boundary selection.

Option B — the Historical-Agency position — fixes the base at 1610, the so-called Orbis spike associated with the Columbian Exchange and a corresponding drawdown in atmospheric carbon, on the grounds that the Anthropocene names a transformation in human collective agency rather than a purely geochemical event; stratigraphic correlation is secondary to the causal structure of planetary change.

Option C — the Conceptual-Pragmatist position — rejects any single global boundary, proposing instead a regionally variable Anthropocene Event with no formal GSSP, on the grounds that "epoch" is a normative framework imposed upon heterogeneous local processes rather than a natural kind, and that ratifying one universal date falsely homogenizes radically differential experiences of industrialization, colonization, and ecological disruption.

Our panel is divided. The geologists favor A for methodological purity; the environmental historians and philosophers of science favor B for explanatory and moral coherence; and a minority of science-studies scholars favor C, warning that any singular date implicitly assigns historical blame in a manner incompatible with scientific neutrality.

We need a memo recommending one of these positions — or a conditional hybrid — with an actionable bottom line (approve with conditions, reject, or approve a specific framework) and reasoning that each dissenting faction could actually respond to. The memo should specifically address: (1) whether a historical period can be defined independently of a theory of collective moral agency; (2) whether selecting a 1945 versus 1610 boundary implicitly distributes historical responsibility in ways that violate the neutrality of stratigraphic classification; and (3) whether Option C's rejection of a GSSP dissolves the very concept of a geological epoch, or instead reveals that epoch-designation has always been a normative, not purely descriptive, institutional act. Include an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the position you reject. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write as though the Commission shares the relevant background but needs the conceptual architecture and its institutional implications clarified. The document should be formal, polished, and suitable for circulation to the full Commission.
