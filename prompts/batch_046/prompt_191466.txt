I've been taking this world-history seminar and we got into a huge argument about whether history is cyclical or linear, and now I'm kind of obsessed. We talked about Polybius and his anacyclosis thing where constitutions rotate like seasons, and Ibn Khaldun's idea that asabiyyah builds up and then decays in these predictable waves. Then someone brought up Spengler treating cultures like organisms that go through spring and winter, and Toynbee with his challenge-and-response cycles that might still have some direction to them. But here's the thing: every time I read about this, it feels like the debate hasn't moved since the 1930s. Historians mostly say it's all metaphor, philosophers say it's metaphysics dressed as empiricism, and nobody actually changes their mind.

So I'm wondering: is this stall happening because it's a verbal disagreement about what 'progress' even means? Or are cyclicalists and linearists actually answering different questions — one describing institutional patterns, the other prescribing where humanity should be headed — and just using the same words? I also keep recieveing this idea I haven't seen discussed much: structural recurrence versus phenomenological repetition. Like, maybe Spengler is talking about the formal shape of civilizations repeating, while a linearist is talking about the irreversibility of consciousness or moral knowledge. If that's the hidden split, then the whole debate might be a category error.

Can you diagnose what's really going on here? Is there a substantive empirical disagreement hiding under all the metaphors, or is it bedrock intuition about whether time has a direction? And honestly, is this debate even well-posed, or has it been insulated into unfalsifiability — for instance, cyclicalists saying 'it looks linear now but wait for the winter,' and linearists saying 'those are just local setbacks'? What would actually count as progress — a specific historical pattern, a conceptual distinction, or just abandoning the framing altogether? I'd like an honest take on whether anything could move it, not just a survey of who said what.
