In my philosophy of culture seminar, we are examining whether dramatizing historical figures generates obligations of fidelity that persist after death. To test the boundary, I want to consider a hypothetical biopic that uses predictive language models trained on a deceased public figure's unpublished private correspondence to synthetically reconstruct mental deliberations never meant for disclosure. What is your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that such synthetic reconstruction violates a moral duty owed specifically to the dead, independently of harm to living relatives or audiences? Decompose your answer into four factors with a rough probability and brief justification for each: (i) whether the dead retain moral status that can be wronged; (ii) whether predictive reconstruction of unexpressed mental states constitutes a distinctive form of misrepresentation or disrespect; (iii) whether artistic or epistemic value can legitimately override any such duty; and (iv) whether public-figure status substantially weakens the obligation. Then combine these into an overall number, state which factor is your weakest link, and describe what argument or comparative case would most shift your view.
