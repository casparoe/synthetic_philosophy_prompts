I am moderating the Q&A session for a philosophy-of-social-science colloquium, and our guest is a philosopher who has just published a monograph reviving Weber's Protestant ethic thesis in a form she calls "institutional axiology." On her view, cultural values are never direct efficient causes of economic behavior; rather, they become causally operative only when they are codified into what she terms feedback architectures—institutional rules that make deviation from value-congruent action systematically costly. She therefore rejects both the old "ideas drive history" reading and the dismissive claim that Weber committed a category mistake by explaining material outcomes with normative commitments. Instead, she argues that Weber was tracking a form of path-dependent causation in which values shape the payoff structures that agents actually face, so that explaining capitalism's emergence requires citing both material conditions and the specific normative framework that made certain institutional innovations self-reinforcing. She is very good at deflecting objections by saying that critics are still operating with a pre-institutional, psychological model of causation that her framework explicitly abandons.

Write me a sequence of six to eight interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to deploy. I want the questions to turn on the actual mechanics of her view: how values enter the feedback architecture without being reducible to material interests; whether her account can distinguish genuinely value-driven institutional design from post-hoc rationalization of material incentives; and how she handles well-known counterexamples such as the development of capitalist practices in regions without the Protestant ethic she emphasizes. For each major line of questioning, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—for instance, if she appeals to the uniqueness of Calvinist predestination anxiety as the specific normative content that generated the relevant architecture, ask what features of that content are doing the causal work and whether functionally equivalent secular norms could have produced the same institutional path. End with a direct question about what empirical or historical evidence could, in principle, show that her institutional-axiology model is wrong about a particular case, and avoid generic questions about her career or motivations.
