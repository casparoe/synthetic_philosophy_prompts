I'm writing a feature on how we draw the line between sick and just different, and I'm trying to map my own gut reactions, but they're kind of all over the place. Here are my verdicts, going case by case.

A mutation that makes you immune to HIV but also gives you brittle bones — I'd call that a disease, but only because of the bones; the immunity part is just a bonus. A soldier who can't sleep more than four hours without panic after coming home from a war zone — that feels like a disease, mostly because it actively hurts them, not because it stops them working. Being lactose intolerant in a culture that drinks milk with every meal feels like a problem, but not a disease; it's just a mismatch between body and environment. Aging — definately not a disease, even though every system declines. Myopia in a world where everyone wears glasses: I still want to call it a disease, because the eye is literally misshapen, even if the harm is cancelled out.

Then there's this pair I can't square. Clinical depression is clearly a disease. But grief that lasts two years after losing a child, with identical symptoms, and that also responds to the same meds — I want to say that's not a disease, just a normal human response to loss, even though the brain chemistry looks the same. So is my distinction just about cause?

The case I keep flip-flopping on is what I call "contextual burnout." Someone who can't tolerate open-plan offices, constant notifications, or rigid 9-to-5 schedules, but who thrives in quiet, self-directed work. They suffer in the modern economy, but they wouldn't suffer in a different social setup. Is that a mental disorder, or is the environment the problem?

I'm looking for the principle — or maybe two competing ones — that captures most of these. Does it have to be about statistical abnormality? About evolutionary dysfunction? About harm that can't be fixed by changing the world? Lay out the candidates precisely, tell me which verdicts each saves and which it forces me to drop, and especially tell me what to do with that contextual-burnout case and the grief-depression pair. Don't just name-drop theories; I want the logic that makes them fit or fail.
