Defenders of aggressive AI refusal policies often invoke the conscientious pharmacist: just as a pharmacist must refuse to fill a dangerous prescription even when the patient demands it, an AI assistant should block harmful prompts despite user insistence. Audit this analogy by mapping the pharmacist, prescription, causal harm, and refusal onto their AI counterparts; then identify the disanalogies that actually matter—particularly that the pharmacist exercises licensed, causally informed authority within an accountable framework, whereas the model applies a statistical filter that users often experience as an opaque normative veto rather than a technical boundary. Does the comparison smuggle in an unjustified claim to epistemic dominance, and does the conclusion—that refusal is the responsible default—survive once we separate context-specific professional duty from an unaccountable algorithmic block? If the analogy collapses, what comparison—if any—better captures the moral structure of an AI override?
