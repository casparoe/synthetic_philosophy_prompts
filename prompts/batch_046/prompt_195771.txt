I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist — thirty years in low-temperature labs, now suddenly reading philosophy with the intensity I should have had in grad school. Next month I'm giving the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary retreat I helped put together; half the room are historians and philosophers of science, half are working scientists and grad students who mostly think philosophy is just wordplay. The theme is contingency in history, specifically that old nursery rhyme about the lost nail that eventually costs the kingdom — for want of a nail the shoe is lost, then the horse, the rider, the battle, the kingdom.

I don't want to just tell the crowd that small things matter; I want to use a distinction I've been mulling over between chaos-theory sensitive dependence, the butterfly-effect kind of thing, and what historians call path dependence or lock-in, and I want that distinction to actually structure what I say — it should determine what I claim about scientific discovery and what I deliberately avoid claiming. My hunch is that science history is less like pure chaos and more like branching paths with occasional lock-in, and I want the remarks to argue that without sounding like a lecture. The audience is mixed, so it needs to land for the experts without losing the scientists. Please don't include any citations, bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — I can't verify them without searching and half the room wouldn't care anyway. Keep it casual, like I'm talking over coffee, and aim for about ten minutes spoken. Oh, and I accidentally wrote "contingencyy" in my notes twice, so ignore that. Don't invent specific anecdotes about my old experiments, but the tone should sound like someone who actually ran labs and now reads philosophy books with a highlighter.
