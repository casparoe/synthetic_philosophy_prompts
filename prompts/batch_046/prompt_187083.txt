I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's arguments about whether an "I" can be distributed across substrates and minds—especially his claim that the self is essentially a pattern or loop that doesn't require a specific brain to persist, but also doesn't just float free into any system that copies its outputs. To check whether I actually understand his position, I've been cataloguing what I take to be his implicit verdicts across a range of cases, but I'm now stuck on how to apply them consistently to a scenario that doesn't map cleanly onto anything he discusses.

Here is how I think he rules: (1) If a complete functional copy of my neural dynamics is instantiated in silicon with perfect fidelity, he treats that as genuinely "me"—the substrate is irrelevant and the loop continues. (2) If my neurons are replaced one by one with bionic equivalents that preserve connectivity and dynamics, he accepts that the same "I" persists, since it's the pattern, not the matter, that matters. (3) If a spouse or close collaborator comes to internalize my decision heuristics and emotional patterns through decades of interaction—so that they reliably finish my sentences and extend my projects—but retains their own episodic memories and autobiographical center, he seems to allow only a "thin" distributed self, a partial overlap, not a second full "I". (4) If a committee or organization formalizes my rules and executes them collectively, he denies this counts at all, because there's no unified subjective loop, just distributed computation without a center of experience. (5) In the split-brain case, where the corpus callosum is severed and each hemisphere develops distinct preferences and narratives, he appears to treat this as genuine bifurcation—two "I"s emerge from one—because the unified loop has been physically disrupted into two non-communicating loops.

The new case is this: imagine a technology—call it an "affective bridge"—that allows two intact brains to remain anatomically separate but to share only emotional and valence patterns in real time, without any sharing of episodic memory, linguistic representation, or executive control. One partner in the bridge dies suddenly. The survivor retains none of the deceased's memories or beliefs, but retains the coupled emotional patterns—grief structures, preference weights, affective dispositions—that had been continuously synchronized for years. Would Hofstadter say there is still an "I" distributed in the survivor? Which of his earlier rulings is the closest analog—the spousal internalization (case 3), the split-brain bifurcation (case 5), or the substrate-independent copy (case 1)? And if his verdicts genuinely underdetermine this case, what principle distinguishes the choices—perhaps a distinction between "pattern preservation with subjective center" versus "pattern preservation without center," or between "functional continuity of experience" versus "functional continuity of disposition"? Finally, what further case—maybe one involving partial memory sharing without emotional coupling, or emotional coupling across more than two brains—would best tell these interpretations apart?
