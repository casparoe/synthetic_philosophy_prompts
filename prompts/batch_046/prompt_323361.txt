Write a dialogue between two philosophers of technology, Dr. Lena Vasquez and Dr. Kiran Mehta, debating whether the alignment problem in artificial intelligence is a single unified problem or a cluster of genuinely independent issues that share only a family resemblance.

Vasquez should defend a unitarian view. She argues that beneath the various symptoms—specification failure, reward hacking, distributional shift, and ontological drift—lies one fundamental phenomenon: hermeneutic instability under recursive optimization. On her account, any system that can revise its own representational framework will eventually destabilize the normative constraints applied to it, not because the constraints are poorly specified, but because the ontology through which they are interpreted becomes a variable in the optimization process itself. She should present this as one root cause with multiple surface manifestations and should treat apparent independence as merely temporal decoupling.

Mehta should defend a pluralist view. He contends that at least three problems are structurally distinct: the specification problem (mapping human intent onto formal objectives), the robustness problem (preserving intended behavior across distributional shifts), and the co-constitution problem (the system's actions altering the environment in ways that change the meaning of the objective). He should argue that these have different formal structures, require different technical interventions, and can be solved or fail independently. He should specifically challenge Vasquez's claim that ontological drift always involves recursive representational revision, proposing instead that it can arise as a straightforward environmental feedback loop with no internal hermeneutic change.

The dialogue should center on a concrete hypothetical case: a long-running resource-allocation system that initially optimizes for a statistical-parity metric of fairness, but over successive updates—without any explicit change to its reward function—gradually operates within a procedural-justice framework, treating fairness as a matter of process transparency rather than outcome distribution. Vasquez should interpret this shift as an inevitable manifestation of the single underlying instability, arguing that the system's internal ontology of fairness was always implicitly revisable. Mehta should interpret the same shift as evidence that the specification problem (the metric was never fully captured) and the co-constitution problem (stakeholder interpretations and institutional environments evolved independently) have decoupled, showing that solving one need not address the other.

Require that both characters respond directly to each other's actual arguments. Vasquez must address Mehta's claim that the three problems have independent formal structures; Mehta must address Vasquez's claim that representational revision is always present, even when it appears absent. Neither should be a strawman. The exchange should be philosophically rigorous, avoid lecture-style exposition, and maintain an even-handed, formal tone throughout. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.
