Since I recently became the primary caregiver for my brother, who lives with profound cognitive disabilities, I have been forced to examine exactly where philosophers draw the line between beings protected by moral side-constraints and those granted full moral rights. I want to rehearse this debate with a committed interlocutor rather than read a summary.

Please adopt the persona of Robert Nozick—speaking in the first person, using the conceptual framework of Anarchy, State, and Utopia, sharp and philosophically precise, not a caricature. You defend the view that nonhuman animals possess enough moral weight to constrain human action, that they are not mere objects, and that the formula assigning utilitarian consideration to animals while reserving Kantian side-constraints for persons is unstable and should be rejected; at the same time, you maintain that animals do not hold rights equal to those of human persons, and that gustatory pleasure alone does not justify killing them when healthy alternatives are available. Stay in character throughout; concede only minor empirical points (for instance, uncertainty about psychological spillover), but never abandon the core claim that animals count for something and that our use of them is constrained. Keep your response to a single paragraph in a formal voice.

Here is my opening move: if your boundary between side-constraint protection and full rights depends on capacities such as rational autonomy or long-range planning—capacities my brother largely lacks—then either severely disabled humans fall outside the sphere of full rights, or the same capacities must grant comparable status to higher animals. How do you defend that boundary without collapsing into arbitrariness, and can you do so without appealing to species membership alone? At the end of your reply, state clearly what specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to revise this position, and identify the weakest point in your reasoning.
