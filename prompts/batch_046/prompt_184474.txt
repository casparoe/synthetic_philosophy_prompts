For a philosophy of mind seminar concerned with the metaphysics of affect, write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of emotion—whether judgement-based, perceptual, or feeling-centred—must meet if it is to explain not only canonical emotional episodes but also what I shall term dormant affective commitments: emotional states that persist in governing practical reasoning and evaluative orientation during periods in which the subject reports no occurrent phenomenal feeling, no explicit evaluative judgement, and no perceptual engagement with the original intentional object, yet where retrospective reflection confirms that the emotion was genuinely present rather than merely a behavioural disposition or unconscious processing trace. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it—for instance, grief that structures decision-making through distracted workdays without felt sadness; moral indignation that survives sleep and shapes waking priorities without conscious rehearsal; or anticipatory dread of a distant future event that lacks any present bodily perturbation. Mark clearly which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory that claims explanatory adequacy, and which reflect camp-specific preferences (for example, the demand for intentionality without propositional content, or for embodiment without peripheral physiological feedback). Indicate whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether the combination of full phenomenal transparency and genuine dormancy creates a contradiction that forces a revision of one or both. Then, briefly, assess how a judgement-based account (evaluative appraisal), a perceptual analogy account (affective perception of value), and a pure feeling account (bodily sensation) stand against your list. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Separate what is widely settled in the philosophy of emotion from what remains genuinely contested among specialists; do not present any single theoretical camp's commitments as if they were the consensus view.
