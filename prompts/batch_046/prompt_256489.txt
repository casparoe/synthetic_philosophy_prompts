I'm taking an upper-level philosophy of perception course, and our seminar handout offers the following summary of disjunctivism, which I want to check before using it in my essay. The handout writes: "Disjunctivism, as defended by philosophers like McDowell and Martin, holds that veridical perception and hallucination share no mental properties at all—not even subjective indistinguishability—because in genuine seeing the external object is literally a constituent of the mental state, while hallucination is only a subpersonal neural misfire carrying zero experiential content. This lets disjunctivists reject the argument from illusion, which infers a shared internal representational vehicle from the fact that seeing and hallucinating can feel the same. By denying any common mental factor, they show the argument commits an equivocation between causal similarity and phenomenal identity. The handout concludes that the view solves illusion only by denying that hallucination is a conscious experience at all, reducing the hallucinator to a causally disrupted mechanism; critics therefore treat this not as a solution but as a reductio."

How fair is this characterization? Please go through it systematically: identify what is essentially correct, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the disjunctivist position. In particular, is it true that disjunctivists deny all mental commonality between perception and hallucination, and that they treat hallucination as lacking experiential content? Does the conclusion that disjunctivism denies consciousness in hallucination actually follow from the premises given, or does it depend on the missteps in the summary? Finally, is the reconstruction of the argument from illusion—as resting on an equivocation between causal similarity and phenomenal identity—something a defender of the argument would recognize, or is it a distortion?
