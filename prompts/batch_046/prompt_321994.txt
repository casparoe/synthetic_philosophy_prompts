Specify the full set of desiderata that an adequate philosophical theory of standing to blame must meet, organized by the phenomenon each is meant to capture. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual distinction—particularly the difference between forfeiting standing through personal hypocrisy and forfeiting it through complicity in an institutional wrong, and the further distinction between moral hypocrisy (the blamer shares the specific failing) and structural hypocrisy (the blamer benefits from a system that produces it). The theory must preserve the gap between having no standing and having diminished standing, must apply consistently to interpersonal, institutional, and self-directed blame, and must not collapse the blamer’s epistemic position into the victim’s entitlement. Identify which single desideratum eliminates the largest number of existing approaches—whether it is the non-circularity condition, the proportionality of forfeiture, or the compatibility with reactive attitudes being reason-responsive rather than merely causal. Then flag any two desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable, especially if accommodating institutional standing forces abandonment of the symmetry condition that hypocrites lose standing only in proportion to their own fault. Briefly score, without historical attribution or citation, how a purely relational view, a moral-credibility view, and an institutional-role view fare against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the comparative evaluation. Do not supply a bibliography or reading list.
