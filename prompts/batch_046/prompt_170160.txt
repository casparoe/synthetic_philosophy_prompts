I have just come out of a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a colleague in the philosophy department about whether deontology can survive its own classic paradox, and I want to put my commitments down in writing before I present them at a workshop next week. Here is where I stand: I believe that agent-centered restrictions—prohibitions on treating one person as a mere means, even when doing so would prevent greater aggregate harm—are genuinely normative rather than merely heuristic shortcuts; I also accept that the standard paradox, in which deontology seems to require allowing a larger number of people to die rather than actively killing one, demonstrates that these restrictions can produce outcomes worse by any plausible moral metric; I think the familiar resolution through the doctrine of double effect, together with the acting-versus-allowing distinction, preserves the restrictions technically but only by introducing distinctions that feel post hoc and tailored to save the theory; I hold that if moral theories must be evaluated partly by their overall consequences, then deontology fails its own test whenever the numbers grow large enough; and yet I also maintain that there is something deeply wrong with the idea that we could dissolve the paradox simply by licensing the killing of one whenever it saves five, because that seems to erase the moral difference between agent and patient in a way that undermines the very idea of rights.

Please audit this set of commitments for coherence. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? I am especially concerned about the interaction between my acceptance that the paradox has real force and my resistance to the consequentialist resolution—does holding both commit me to an unstable middle position, or is there a consistent interpretation? In addition, I have been experimenting with a slightly non-standard framing: I wonder whether the paradox dissolves if we treat agent-centered restrictions not as absolute side-constraints but as what I have been calling structural invariants—rules that must be preserved across the decision architecture of a moral community regardless of local outcome optimization. Does adopting that framing resolve the tension, or does it merely rename the problem and push the difficulty into the definition of the community’s architecture? Finally, tell me what the cheapest revision to my set would be—whether I should drop the consequentialist evaluation criterion, weaken the agent-centered restrictions, or abandon the structural-invariant idea altogether—and be explicit about what that revision costs me in terms of the other commitments I would retain.
