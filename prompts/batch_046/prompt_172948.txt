Having spent my career in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy of science and ethics, I keep encountering aggregation problems that feel technically familiar yet take on strange normative weight. Here is an argument my study group constructed about long-term violence trends, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to dissolve not just one empirical claim but every large-scale historical comparison. Where does it go wrong, if it does?

(1) Violence can be quantified only once we fix both a unit of analysis—absolute counts of harmful acts versus per-capita rates normalized to population size—and a boundary for the category itself, deciding whether state executions, industrial accidents, or systemic deprivation are included.

(2) The choice between absolute and per-capita measurement is not settled by empirical observation; it embeds a normative judgment about whether harm to potential people who never existed (because population was smaller) should enter our evaluation of the past.

(3) Likewise, the boundary of the category is partly conventional: no empirical test alone can determine whether capital punishment in 1750 was violence or legitimate justice.

(4) Therefore the claim that "violence is declining" is not a pure empirical report but a hybrid claim that embeds both a scaling convention—extensive versus intensive measurement—and a classificatory convention.

(5) Apply the reasoning consistently: economic productivity, literacy, or disease burden can also be measured absolutely or per capita, and each requires conventional boundary-drawing (what counts as literacy? as disease?).

(6) If embedding conventional choices undermines the objectivity of violence trends, it must undermine the objectivity of all such macroscopic historical claims.

(7) Yet we do not treat epidemiological trends—say, the decline in smallpox mortality—as framework-relative in the same destructive way; we treat them as straightforwardly factual.

(8) Hence either we must deny that there are objective facts about violence trends specifically, with no principled reason for the asymmetry, or we must concede that conventional framing does not destroy objectivity in general, which means (4) overreaches.

(9) The argument seems to push me toward the latter concession, yet I want to resist it for violence specifically, because the normative stakes of counting or excluding state-sanctioned harm feel different from counting viral infections. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep the intuition that framing choices genuinely matter for violence without being forced into a global skepticism about all statistical history.
