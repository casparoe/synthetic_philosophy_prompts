I am setting the final examination for an upper-level module on political violence, international ethics, and the law of genocide, taken by final-year undergraduates and taught Master's students. Please write one essay question, together with a model answer of approximately 900 words and a detailed marking scheme.

The question must address genocide as a legal and moral category—specifically the tension between Raphael Lemkin's original broad conception and the narrower 1948 UN Genocide Convention—together with the politics of naming atrocities. It must be fully answerable by a well-prepared student without web access, and it must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. To prevent answers that merely recite standard summaries, inject the following conceptual framing: the distributive intent problem, in which destructive purpose is spread across bureaucratic institutions rather than held by a single agent or explicit order.

Present this invented scenario: between 2010 and 2028, the government of State X implemented a coordinated multi-ministry programme against the indigenous Kharu people. Ministry A enforced land dispossession and destroyed agricultural infrastructure; Ministry B restricted healthcare and education; Ministry C relocated children to state institutions where the Kharu language was banned; and Ministry D recorded all actions as development integration. No ministry issued orders to kill. Mortality rates tripled, birth rates collapsed, and by 2028 roughly 60 per cent of the Kharu had been dispersed or assimilated. Internal memos show officials in each ministry understood their contribution to dissolving Kharu collective life, yet no single document states destroy the Kharu as such. The government insists this is internal policy, not genocide.

The essay question carries 30 marks; students have 90 minutes and should write approximately 1,200 words. It should require students to:

(a) apply the UN Convention's definition to this case, paying particular attention to the intent to destroy (dolus specialis) requirement, and analyse whether distributed institutional intent can satisfy it;

(b) explain the politics of naming—what is gained and what is risked by classifying this as genocide rather than as ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, or cultural genocide;

(c) evaluate whether Lemkin's broader conception, which included cultural, economic, and social destruction, would resolve the conceptual difficulty identified in part (a), or whether it introduces its own problems of over-inclusion and legal unenforceability;

(d) conclude with a normative argument about whether international law should retain the narrow physical and biological focus, expand to include cultural and social destruction, or adopt a different criterion altogether, responding directly to the distributive intent problem.

The difficulty must come from navigating the tension between legal precision, moral categorisation, and strategic labelling, not from obscure factual recall.

Provide the exam question clearly divided into parts (a) to (d). Provide a model answer demonstrating the specific conceptual moves required. Provide a marking scheme totalling 30 marks, built around at least five named must-hit points, stating the marks for each and including a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each. Separate a First-class answer (24–30 marks) from a 2:1 answer (18–23 marks) by naming the specific moves each must contain—particularly the analysis of distributed institutional intent and the critical evaluation of Lemkin's trade-offs—rather than by generic praise.

Write in British English. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts unless you have fetched and copied them exactly; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not depend on specific page numbers or obscure secondary literature.
