In my graduate seminar on modal metaphysics we have been working through the implications of treating natural-kind terms as rigid designators, and one of my students has pressed me with an argument that I suspect rests on a subtle category mistake, though I have not been able to isolate precisely where it fails. She notes that if "water" is rigid, then in every world in which water exists it denotes H2O, and that since the identity statement water is H2O is necessary, it follows that wherever water exists, H2O exists. From this she infers that the modal profile of water—what it takes for water to be present—is not merely revealed but constituted by our actual empirical discovery of its chemical composition. Consequently, she claims that necessary a posteriori truths differ from necessary a priori ones not merely epistemically but metaphysically: had our chemical investigations in the actual world yielded a different result, the modal properties of the corresponding substance would have been different, making rigidity itself sensitive to empirical history. I find this line of reasoning seductive but clearly mistaken, since it seems to collapse the distinction between fixing reference and determining modal status. Could you help me diagnose exactly where the confusion lies—whether it involves an equivocation between de re and de dicto modality, a misunderstanding of how baptism fixes reference across worlds, or a deeper error about the relation between epistemic access and metaphysical necessity—and explain what the correct account should say about whether rigidity entails that the existence conditions of a kind depend on the actual-world facts used to identify it?
