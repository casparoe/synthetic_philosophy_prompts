I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have recently been reading moral theory in connection with how we design systems that distribute harms and benefits across different users. I would like to practice being challenged by a committed defender of a particular view, so please take on the role of a sharp, non-caricatured proponent of Scanlonian contractualism. You hold that an act is wrong precisely when it is justified only by a principle that at least one affected person could reasonably reject, and you treat each individual’s standing to object as basic rather than derived from some aggregate measure. Stay fully in character: speak in the first person, rely only on the conceptual resources native to this position—reasonable rejection, justification to each person taken separately, the idea that moral reasons are not summed like weights—and do not break role even if I press you hard. You may concede that aggregation creates strong intuitive pressure in rescue cases, but you must never concede that the bare fact of greater numbers can by itself justify harming an innocent party. Keep your replies to roughly one paragraph, as if we are in a live conversation rather than composing an essay.

Here is my opening challenge, drawn from a problem I am actually thinking through at work: I am helping design an allocation protocol that must choose between distributing a scarce life-saving resource to five patients who will each suffer serious but non-fatal harm without it, and withholding that resource from one specific patient whose system would have to be severely violated—her privacy, autonomy, and bodily integrity compromised in a lasting way—to make the redistribution possible. The five can each reasonably reject a principle that permits them to be sacrificed for others; the one can reject a principle that permits her severe violation. If contractualism genuinely gives each rejection independent, non-aggregated weight, then whenever the numbers differ we seem to face either paralysis or an implicit counting rule disguised as “reasonableness.” How do you avoid that collapse without smuggling aggregation back in through the back door, and what do you say to the engineer who insists that your view therefore cannot guide real allocation decisions? Please respond in character.
