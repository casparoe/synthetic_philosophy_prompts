Rank the following five theoretical accounts of supererogation—(1) the sharp-line cost-threshold view, which holds that acts become supererogatory once they exceed a fixed level of personal sacrifice; (2) the continuous-scalar view, which denies any categorical boundary and treats all moral actions as points on a gradient of praiseworthiness; (3) the agent-relative prerogative view, which permits individuals to assign special weight to their own legitimate projects when deciding how far to go beyond duty; (4) the virtue-centered view, which classifies acts as supererogatory not by their cost but by whether they manifest exceptional moral character; and (5) a hybrid moral-residue account, which proposes that an act is genuinely supererogatory only if it generates irreducible moral value that cannot be fully assigned to any particular duty-bearer—according to how well each explains the following pair of cases: a physician who declines to work a dangerous double shift during an epidemic receives no blame, yet a parent who refuses to rescue their child from a shallow pool at negligible risk is severely blameworthy. Assign ranks 1 through 5, with 1 being the most adequate. For each adjacent pair in your ranking, state in one sentence the decisive reason for the ordering. Then identify which two accounts are closest in practical verdict despite their theoretical divergence, and specify one conceptual distinction—rather than an empirical study or an authority citation—that would most clearly separate your first-ranked account from its nearest competitor.
