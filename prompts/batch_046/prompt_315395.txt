Two colleagues, Mara and Lin, are arguing over whether Buddhist anattā is compatible with any workable form of personal identity. Mara opens by saying the Buddha rejected only a permanent metaphysical soul (ātman), not practical selfhood; she claims the five aggregates form a causally continuous psychological stream, and that modern reductionists like Parfit misread Buddhism by treating it as a denial of all identity rather than just of substantial identity. The conventional self (sammuti-sacca), she insists, is a legitimate designation for that stream, not a fiction. Lin replies that this understates the doctrine: in Abhidhamma analysis, consciousness (citta) arises and ceases in discrete moments with no enduring substrate, and the “person” is only a conceptual designation (prajñapti) over physical and mental events. Narrative identity, Lin argues, requires an enduring subject that appropriates experiences, which Buddhism explicitly rejects. Mara then counters that Theravāda Abhidhamma posits bhavaṅga—a subconscious life-continuum linking mental moments—so Buddhism does recognize psychological continuity, just without an “owner.” Lin responds that bhavaṅga is merely a causal condition, not a subject; invoking it to save narrative identity is like saying a river’s current gives identity to a wave. Mara objects that without some continuity, karma and moral responsibility collapse, but Lin answers that karma transmits volitional tendencies (saṅkhāras) across lives like a flame passing to a new candle, with no traveler needed. Mara then presses a further point: if the self is purely a pragmatic fiction, Buddhist ethics loses its grip—why should I care about future suffering in “my” stream if no one actually endures? Lin says that is precisely the point: attachment to an enduring self is the root of suffering, and ethical care operates through causal connection rather than ownership. Go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right about each specific point—where the arguments hold, where they slip, and what both are missing about how anattā relates to contemporary theories of personal identity.
