I sit on our university's disciplinary policy review board, and we've got this kind of wild proposal in front of us for Thursday's vote. The student-government coalition teamed up with a philosophy prof to overhaul the code of conduct for academic-integrity and harassment cases. Instead of saying a student "deserves" suspension, the finding would say they "causally contributed" to harm, and the sanction becomes a mandatory "character genealogy" workshop where the student maps the social, psychological, and even genetic conditions that shaped their choice — basically running with Galen Strawson's basic argument that you're never ultimately responsible for being the way you are, so true moral blame is incoherent. They want to drop punitive suspensions entirely and replace them with repair obligations plus this genealogy exercise.

There's a specific twist that makes it weirder: the proposal requires a "nested regress chain," where the student doesn't just list their own influences but traces the conditions behind those conditions back at least two generations, to really drive home the infinite regress Strawson points out. My co-chair thinks this is philosophical garnish and wants to reject the whole thing. Another board member wants to pilot it for one semester, but only in plagiarism cases. I need to draft our formal recomendation memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete course of action and a timeline, and it needs to take the Strawsonian worry seriously rather than just saying "the law needs blame." But it also has to honestly engage the strongest counter-argument: that institutions express communal values through moral condemnation, and that turning every plagiarism case into a family-trauma excavation might destroy the very agency that restorative justice is supposed to respect. Please address whether that nested two-generation disclosure is administratively feasible and ethically appropriate — I think it's where the idea actually lives or dies — and don't just restate the legal definition of academic misconduct. The memo should be something the student reps could actually read and respond to, with a clear bottom line.
