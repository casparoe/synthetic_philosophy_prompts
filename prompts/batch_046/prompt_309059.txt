I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of action and comparative cognition, and I've been trying to formalize a tension I see in how interdisciplinary fields operationalize agency. In the institutional certification protocol used by the Cross-Domain Autonomy Review Board—which evaluates both biological and artificial systems for what it calls "flexible autonomy"—there is a three-tier scoring rubric for responses to persistent goal-blockage. Tier 1, labeled "mechanically rigid" or sphexish, is assigned when a subject repeats an identical motor or computational routine without parameter variation across more than ten consecutive failures. Tier 3, labeled "merely reactive," is assigned when the subject abandons or radically alters its goal-structure within the first two failures. Only Tier 2 receives full certification, and it requires that the subject maintain the original goal-structure for at least four but no more than eight failure cycles, vary its instrumental strategy within a bounded set of alternatives during that window, and revise the goal itself only after the window closes, accompanied by an explicit representational update.

What puzzles me is that the protocol treats both extreme persistence and extreme plasticity as disqualifying conditions for autonomy, as if free will or genuine flexibility were not a capacity but a specific temporal profile of resistance and surrender. I want to know what philosophical commitments about mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will this practice strictly presupposes. Does it presuppose a compatibilist view on which freedom is defined by hierarchical integration of desires rather than by the absence of determination? Or does it quietly assume a libertarian middle ground—some non-mechanical, non-random source of revision—without which the Tier 2 window makes no normative sense? Can a control-based account of agency explain why the boundary is set at four-to-eight cycles rather than at two or twenty, or is that boundary merely conventional? And is the practice internally consistent with the Board's parallel insistence, in its negligence assessments, that an agent is responsible for outcomes precisely when they could have done otherwise within their actual cognitive architecture—an insistence that seems to value the very plasticity the autonomy protocol penalizes?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of sphexishness—mechanical rigidity versus flexible self-determination—that licenses this three-tier design. Distinguish what the rubric strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of free will or agency would license the same scoring boundaries, and which ones would force us to collapse the tiers or redraw the lines?
