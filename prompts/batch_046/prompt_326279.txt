Explain the conceptual tension at the heart of the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism, specifically focusing on whether Devlin’s claim that law may enforce shared morality to prevent collective disintegration can be articulated coherently without presupposing a reified group mind. In particular, address whether the harm Devlin identifies—damage to a society’s shared normative environment—can be reconstructed as a normative externality that is non-rival and non-excludable, without collapsing into Hart’s stricter category of tangible, individuated injury to particular persons. Clarify whether recognizing such diffuse moral spillovers necessarily commits one to a collectivist ontology that liberal individualism must reject, or whether a coherent intermediate position exists that accepts the reality of normative spillovers yet denies they justify criminal prohibition. Rather than summarizing the historical exchange, dissect the underlying conceptual architecture that distinguishes social harm from individual harm in this framework.
