Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Bernard Williams's integrity objection and his critique of moral reasoning that introduces one thought too many. Stevens, the career butler, suppresses both his attachment to the housekeeper Miss Kenton and his moral reservations about Lord Darlington, framing each suppression as demanded by professional dignity. Analyze whether Stevens exemplifies the integrity objection—whether his identity-constituting ground project of perfect service has expanded to crowd out commitments that ought also to be constitutive—or whether he commits the inverse mistake: substituting a professional justification for a direct human response, thereby inserting precisely the redundant moral step Williams warns against. Further, consider whether the novel's retrospective, unreliable narration undermines Williams's assumption that agents enjoy transparent first-person access to their own ground projects. Does Stevens's belated regret operate as a moral remainder in Williams's sense, and does Ishiguro's narrative architecture—especially the gap between Stevens's self-description and the reader's reconstruction—add something to the philosophical argument that abstract reasoning about integrity cannot capture? Provide a reading that takes both the literary form and the moral psychology seriously, and identify specific scenes where the novel either vindicates or complicates Williams's framework.
