A colleague in my philosophy-of-mind reading group recently summarized Douglas Hofstadter's account of personal identity in I Am a Strange Loop, and I want to check whether the characterization holds up before I incorporate it into a paper on distributed cognition. According to her, Hofstadter's core claim is that an I is not a material substance but a self-referential symbolic pattern — a strange loop — that emerges whenever a representational system turns its attention back onto itself. Because the loop is defined purely by its formal, causal topology rather than by the particular neurons that realize it, she argues that Hofstadter is committed to substrate independence: if the causal dynamics of self-reference were distributed across two biologically distinct brains linked by a high-bandwidth direct interface, there would literally be one self instantiated across both substrates, not two selves in coordination. She further claims that Hofstadter treats this distributed scenario as a direct extension of his view, and therefore denies that the biological continuity of a single brain is necessary for the persistence of the same I over time — what matters is only the preservation of the loop's symbol-manipulation dynamics. From this she concludes that, on Hofstadter's account, personal identity is essentially a property of information-flow architectures, so the traditional boundary between one mind and two minds collapses whenever those architectures become causally integrated.

This sounds right in broad strokes — Hofstadter does reject Cartesian substance and emphasizes pattern over matter — but I worry the summary smuggles in a functionalism about distributed minds that he might resist. I am particularly unsure whether he actually treats cross-brain instantiation as literally one self, or whether he retains a commitment to the spatial unity of the substrate realizing the loop. I am also unsure whether his anti-psychologism about the self permits him to deny the importance of biological continuity in the way she suggests, or whether that is an overreading of his copy-and-teleportation thought experiments.

Could you audit this summary? Please separate: (1) what accurately captures Hofstadter's position in I Am a Strange Loop and related writings; (2) what is a plausible but subtly distorted extension, especially regarding distributed loops and substrate independence; (3) whether her conclusion about the collapse of the one-mind/two-mind boundary actually follows from the accurate parts, or whether it relies on a conflation between symbolic self-modeling and functionalist distributed cognition. I am especially interested in whether Hofstadter's notion of the loop requires a singular center of narrative gravity that remains unitary even when causally coupled to another system, and if so, whether that undermines the distributed-self reading.
