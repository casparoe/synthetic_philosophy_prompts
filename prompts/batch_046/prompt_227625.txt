So I'm prepping this discusssion for a philosophy-adjacent book club and I want to do something a little weird: read Brave New World through the hedonistic imperative — you know, David Pearce's idea that we should use biotech to literally abolish suffering, not just crank up pleasure. But I don't want the lazy 'soma is bad because it numbs people' reading that everyone gives in intro ethics. There's this distinction in the literature (maybe it's Pearce's own framing, maybe it's from later intepreters) between negative-utilitarian suffering-ablation and what I'd call affective redesign — like, is the goal zero pain, or is it restructuring the emotional range so that deep distress becomes neurologically impossible? Huxley's World State seems to do both: soma wipes out acute suffering, but the whole engineered social order also flattens emotional depth in a more systemic way.

Can you read the novel — especially the Savage's refusal of soma and Mustapha Mond's defense of stability — through that specific distinction? Does Huxley actually separate abolishing suffering from engineering uniform contnetment, or does he just conflate them for dystopian effect? And if we take the hedonistic imperative seriously as a real philosophical project, not just a cautionary tale, does the novel miss something important about what suffering-ablation would actually entail — maybe about the value of negative affect, or about reproductive ethics if people don't suffer anymore? I want a reading that doesn't reduce the book to an illustration and doesn't distort Pearce's view either; I just need something my book club can actually argue about.
