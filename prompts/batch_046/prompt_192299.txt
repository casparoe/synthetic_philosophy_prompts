Having recently adopted a private system of moral accounting—recording each day's failures of temperance, humility, and patience in a gridded ledger—I have begun to experience precisely the social tension that Benjamin Franklin acknowledged after years of pursuing his own project of moral perfection, as recounted in his Autobiography. I am preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for twelve upper-level philosophy students on this project, but rather than lecturing on the familiar thirteen virtues, I want them to inhabit the mechanism itself. Please design an in-class game or structured role-play in which participants use a simplified version of Franklin's grid to track not only their own conduct but, under rotating rules, that of their peers, with small collective stakes—points that determine access to a shared resource or impose a minor group penalty—that make surveillance feel instrumentally rational. The rules should be arranged so that students initially understand the ledger as purely self-corrective, then discover through the payoff structure that transparency about others' faults becomes strategically advantageous, and finally feel the isolation that results when one participant withholds data or when the group realizes the grid has replaced mutual trust with audited performance. Provide the materials needed, including the grid layout, role cards, a payoff scheme requiring no money, and a timeline for the seventy-five minutes. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto Franklin's actual method—the quantification of vice, the aspiration to complete self-transparency, and his later judgment that the pursuit of perfect virtue had made him disagreeable company and rested on a partly unexamined theological ambition. Be frank about what feature of Franklin's view the activity necessarily fails to reproduce, such as the lifetime temporal scale or the deistic residue that framed perfection as imitation of the divine, and predict what will predictably go wrong in the first run, especially the risk that students treat the grid as harmless gamification rather than moral judgment. The students should leave having felt, not merely understood, why a practice designed for private self-improvement tends to collapse into public moral accounting.
