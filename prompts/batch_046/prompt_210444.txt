Over the past year, watching a close family member lose the thread of her autobiography to dementia, I have begun to doubt whether my own self is anything more than a stable story—yet I also feel the pull of that story resisting change. Write the desiderata that a theory of the self as a center of narrative gravity must meet to be adequate. Treat the self not as a substance but as a convergence point where autobiographical, affective, and social threads generate coherence, analogous to a gravitational center that organizes surrounding mental events without being identical to them. The specification should cover: (i) the anchoring conditions—what fixes the center when episodic and semantic autobiographical memory are severely disrupted; (ii) narrative inertia—how a self-story can resist revision despite radical change in underlying cognition, and whether this inertia is a virtue or a failure mode; (iii) the boundary problem—what distinguishes one narrative center with internal tension from two competing centers within a single biological life; (iv) the status of external records and interactive systems—whether digital traces or artificial agents can extend the gravitational field or only simulate continuity; (v) dissolution and reboot conditions—the minimal criteria for saying a center has permanently dissolved rather than temporarily suspended, and whether a genuinely new center can emerge in the same body. For each, provide the motivating case and mark whether the requirement is non-negotiable or camp-specific (physicalist, phenomenological, narrative-identity, etc.). Identify any jointly unsatisfiable pair—particularly whether strong narrative inertia can coexist with genuine openness to radical self-revision—and keep the focus on the criteria rather than on scoring particular theories, though you may briefly note where Dennett's formulation succeeds or fails against the list.
