For my philosophy of mind seminar, I am writing on whether pain asymbolia undermines the claim that pain is intrinsically bad. The standard case involves patients who retain full sensory discrimination—location, intensity, and qualitative character—yet report no suffering and display no avoidance behavior. Some philosophers take this to show that the sensory and affective dimensions of pain are dissociable, with the badness belonging only to the latter. Others reply that these patients still represent bodily damage, so the representational core of pain remains intact and the missing motivation is merely a disrupted output pathway, not proof that affect can float free of sensory content.

Write an essay that takes a definite position on this debate, but also consider a hypothetical inverse profile—call it pure affective pain—in which a subject experiences intense suffering and an urgent motivation to terminate an experience that lacks any sensory location, qualitative character, or bodily reference whatsoever. Does the conceivability of this inverse case strengthen the separation thesis, or can the representationalist accommodate it by treating the absent sensory content as a representational failure rather than evidence that affect is metaphysically independent? Weigh both sides carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend either (i) that asymbolia and its inverse together establish that sensory and affective dimensions are fully separable and that badness belongs exclusively to the affective component, or (ii) that the two dimensions remain conceptually intertwined, so that genuine pain—whether experienced as bad or not—requires both present, with the inverse case actually describing a different state such as dread or free-floating anxiety rather than pain at all. Justify your verdict with explicit attention to whether pure affective pain is genuinely possible or merely a misclassification, and address what this implies for whether pain is necessarily bad.
