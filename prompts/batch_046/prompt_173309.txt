Having spent thirty years in experimental biochemistry before retiring into philosophy seminars, I find myself drawn to the old question of life's meaning—partly because it echoes the reductionist puzzles I once navigated in the lab, and partly because Frank Ramsey's deflationary spirit, with its suspicion that grand value-questions often mask grammatical confusion rather than substantive mysteries, seems to me exactly the right corrective to metaphysical overreach. In a recent reading group I sketched what I take to be a coherent naturalist package, but I want a rigorous consistency audit before I defend it further.

My commitments are these. (1) I accept a Ramsey-inspired deflation: the traditional question "What is the meaning of life?" treats "meaning" as an intrinsic, monadic property that a life either has or lacks, and once that grammatical error is exposed the pseudo-problem dissolves. (2) Nevertheless, I believe there is a legitimate empirical successor-question—belonging to psychology and neuroscience—about which life-structures produce sustained subjective well-being, coherent autobiographical narrative, and stable long-term goal integration. (3) Should we retain a concept of meaning after the deflation, it ought to be relational rather than intrinsic: a dynamic fit between an agent's value-structure and the causal response patterns of its environment, formally analogous to the mutual information between a control parameter and a system's feedback dynamics. (4) Because this relational notion is in principle formalizable, I treat it as an optimization parameter that can be modeled without any non-physical ontology, using only information theory and dynamical systems. (5) At the same time, I hold that the phenomenological experience of meaninglessness—the affective state often called existential emptiness—is not merely a cognitive mistake to be cured by philosophical therapy; it is a real state with evolutionary and neurobiological correlates that deserves the same explanatory respect we grant to pain or fear.

These five claims may not cohere. For example, (1) and (5) seem to pull against each other: if the original question is conceptually confused, why should its associated feeling be granted ontological seriousness rather than dismissed as another symptom of the same confusion? Meanwhile, (2) and (3) appear to be in tension because the empirical research I describe in (2) typically relies on internalist measures—subjective reports and narrative coherence—whereas (3) defines meaning in terms of external relational fit, which need not correlate with subjective well-being. There also seems to be friction between (1) and (4): if I genuinely think the question involves a category mistake, why construct an optimization model for its supposed referent? I can see that some conflicts are probably only apparent—perhaps (1) targets intrinsic meaning while (3) offers a replacement concept, so they do not genuinely clash—but I am unsure which tensions are deep and which dissolve on closer inspection.

Please audit this set. Rank the pairwise tensions by severity, identifying for each whether it is genuine or merely apparent, and specify what the least costly revision would be in each case. I particularly want to know whether there is a single commitment whose abandonment or modification would resolve the greatest number of conflicts, and whether making that revision is intellectually expensive given my naturalist background. This is not a request for a lecture on Ramsey or on existential psychology; it is an audit of my own position, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad.
