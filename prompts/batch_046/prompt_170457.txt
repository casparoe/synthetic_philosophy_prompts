I have been trying to steelman the transcendental argument for objective spatiotemporal reality—the kind that moves from the necessary conditions of self-conscious experience to the claim that a world of enduring objects must exist—without reducing it to either circular reasoning or empirical psychology. The core idea, as I understand it, is that any coherent experiential framework must already distinguish between the self as a single point of view and an external order that persists independently of particular perceptions; without that distinction, there is nothing that could count as error, correction, or even successive experience. So far, this seems like a claim about the conceptual architecture required for judgment, not about the contingent wiring of human brains.

But I keep running into what looks like a parallel in machine learning: inductive biases—object permanence, locality, compositionality—are unavoidable if a system is to learn from sparse data, yet they are clearly architectural choices. A different network topology might operate with different priors and still achieve predictive coherence, just through a different representational scheme. This makes me wonder whether the philosopher's condition of possibility is really just a description of our default cognitive architecture, elevated to modal status by a kind of anthropocentric projection.

More precisely: if we imagine an artificial system that achieves robust predictive accuracy without any internal distinction between self-model and world-model—say, a pure pattern-matching engine that updates weights globally without representing an enduring external object—would that system have demonstrated that the transcendental argument mistakes a contingent engineering constraint for a necessary conceptual framework? Or is there a stronger, genuinely modal claim here—about what must be true for any system to have experiences that are subject to rational correction—that survives even when the underlying architecture is fully variable?

I am not asking whether the argument is circular in the logical sense; I think that objection misses the point. What I want to understand is whether treating the conditions of experience as fixed points in logical space, rather than as replaceable parameters in a design space, is itself a substantive philosophical commitment, or just a failure of imagination about what cognition could be.
