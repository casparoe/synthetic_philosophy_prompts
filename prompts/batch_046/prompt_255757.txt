I'm preparing a session for an advanced undergraduate seminar in political philosophy—roughly eighteen students, ninety minutes—on the moral ontology of collective harm. I want them to experience, rather than merely learn, Amartya Sen's claim that famines are not meteorological events but failures of entitlement and democratic transmission. The session should center on a structural feature that standard lectures often flatten: an asymmetric visibility gap, in which aggregate food availability can remain stable or even rise while local exchange entitlements collapse, and in which the absence of a free press and competitive elections removes the informational pathway that would otherwise convert private suffering into public policy response.

Design a classroom simulation—complete with rules, roles, materials, and a structured debrief—that makes this gap tangible. Divide the group into three roles: regional harvest monitors (who see only aggregate production figures), local labor households (who hold entitlement cards representing production-based, trade-based, and labor-based claims that become worthless under hidden exchange-rate rules), and government relief coordinators (who can act only on signals they actually receive). The mechanics should ensure that food is plentiful in aggregate while specific groups face entitlement collapse, and that the collapse remains invisible to decision-makers unless democratic transmission mechanisms—here represented by structured reporting rounds—are active or deliberately suppressed. Include a payoff or incentive scheme that requires no real money but rewards accurate prediction or effective relief, and arrange the sequence so that participants construct the divergence between food availability and access themselves, noticing it only near the end.

The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto Sen's distinction between direct and exchange entitlement failure, onto the claim that famine can occur without food availability decline, and onto the informational role of democratic institutions in preventing famine. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely, the harvest monitors will dominate discussion and the labor households will internalize blame—along with how to correct for it. Finally, be frank about which feature of Sen's analysis the simulation cannot reproduce: for instance, the historical specificity of colonial extraction dynamics in the Bengal famine, the particular export-economy mechanisms of the Irish famine, or the bureaucratic falsification of harvest reports during the Great Leap Forward, all of which depend on historical knowledge the students may not yet possess, and tell me exactly how the facilitator must supply that missing layer without turning the debrief into a lecture.
