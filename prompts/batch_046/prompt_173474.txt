I'm on the acquisitions panel for this new public-arts endowment we're calling the Synthetic Culture Fund (bit of a mouthful, I know). Next Thursday we vote on whether to permanently acqusition and display The Calibrated Mirror — a generative installation that reshapes itself in real time using pooled biometic data (galvanic skin response, pupil dilation, that sort of thing) from viewers across different demograpics. The artist and her team say they've basically reverse-engineered Hume's ideal critic: instead of training human judges, they've let an algorithm converge on the form that maximises cross-cultural arousal consensus, so any remaining disagreement is just uncalibrated noise.

Some panelists want to approve it and even make it the reference standard for future buys — why rely on snobby human taste when we can measure the optimum directly? Others think this collapses normative aesthetic judgement into physiology and misses the whole point of Hume's standard of taste, which is about a practiced, deliberative faculty rather than a statistical endpoint. I'm genuinely torn. Can you draft the board's decision memo? Recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasoning the scientists can actually push back on. Write it in British English please.

Don't just wave Hume around — deal with the specific claim that an algorithm can stand in for his true judge by aggregating sensory response. And whatever side you come down on, take the strongest objection from the other side seriously. If you reject, don't pretend the biometic approach has nothing going for it; if you approve, don't pretend that reducing taste to arousal data isn't philosophically weird. Keep the memo formal enough that the board could actually circulate it — clear bottom line, honest treatment of the losing argument, and no bibliographies or reading lists.
