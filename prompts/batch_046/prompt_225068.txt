My friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether the modern revival of Stoicism is philosophically coherent or merely a self-help aesthetic with ancient branding. He summarized the core Stoic position—specifically the modern popularized version of Epictetus's dichotomy of control—as essentially a two-step therapeutic protocol. First, partition every object of concern into either what lies within my power, namely my judgments, intentions, and assent, or what lies outside my power, namely everything else including other people's actions, my reputation, health, and wealth. Second, apply the rule that emotional disturbance is always and only the result of misclassifying an external into the internal category, so that grief over a death, anxiety about poverty, or anger at an insult are all equally errors of judgment with no normative distinction between them. From this he concluded that modern Stoicism, taken seriously, eliminates the concept of appropriate emotional response altogether and therefore collapses into a kind of emotional quietism that cannot distinguish between mourning a loved one and being annoyed by bad weather. I think his summary captures something real about how the dichotomy is taught in contemporary books and apps, but I also think he may be running together several distinct Stoic claims—perhaps conflating the therapeutic aim of reducing suffering with a metaphysical claim about value, or treating Epictetus's specific taxonomy of things up to us as identical to modern psychology's locus of control—and then drawing a stronger conclusion than the view actually licenses. I am trying to figure out exactly which parts of his characterization a careful Stoic defender would accept, which are subtle misrepresentations, and whether his conclusion—that Stoicism erases all normative distinctions among emotional responses—actually follows from the corrected version of the view. Can you audit his summary? Specifically: which distinctions within Stoic theory, such as that between preferred and dispreferred indifferents or between initial impressions and full assent, does his account flatten? Would a defender concede that the dichotomy, properly applied, allows for something like proportionate grief or righteous anger, and if so, on what grounds? And does his charge of emotional quietism survive once those corrections are in place?
