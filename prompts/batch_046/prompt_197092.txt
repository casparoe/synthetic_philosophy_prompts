I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on legal philosophy, and a colleague who teaches classics suggested that Sophocles' Antigone might illuminate the Hart-Fuller debate more viscerally than the usual exchange of journal articles. The play centers on Creon's civic edict prohibiting the burial of Polynices and on Antigone's appeal to unwritten divine obligations that seem to transcend state command. I want a reading that takes both the tragedy and the jurisprudence with equal seriousness: does Antigone's defiance vindicate Fuller's claim that law possesses an inner morality, so that a decree violating basic procedural justice and human dignity fails to count as law at all? Or does Creon's rigid separation of political authority from private conscience dramatize Hart's separation thesis, according to which an immoral rule can still be valid law provided it satisfies the criteria established by a community's rule of recognition?

Please examine whether Creon's prohibition can survive scrutiny under Fuller's eight canons—generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, practicability, stability, and congruence between declared rule and official enforcement—or whether its targeted cruelty and retroactive moral logic expose it as coercion lacking legal form. At the same time, consider whether Hart's framework can accommodate the chorus's gradual alienation from Creon without collapsing legal validity into moral approval, and whether the Theban citizens' silent sympathy for Antigone implies that social recognition is always already morally charged. Where the play resists either philosophical reading—perhaps by showing that both Creon's legalism and Antigone's absolutism produce catastrophic ruin—the honest critic should say so rather than forcing the drama into an illustration.

Finally, I want to understand what Antigone adds to the Hart-Fuller dispute that analytical argument alone cannot capture: whether it is the gendered dimension of who may interpret and speak law, the emotional and familial costs of insisting on moral purity over civic compromise, or the intuition that when law becomes radically unjust, the conceptual distinction between validity and morality may remain clear yet feel existentially intolerable.
