I'm taking a graduate seminar on comparative moral philosophy, and in discussing Confucian role ethics with colleagues I've become stuck on what seems like an institutional paradox. In many large East Asian corporations and public bureaucracies—particularly family-owned conglomerates and government agencies in Korea and Japan—employee evaluation and disciplinary protocols operate through what might be called a relational-obligation framework: a manager is assessed partly on whether they shielded subordinates from external blame, a junior employee partly on whether they suppressed individually advantageous but hierarchically disruptive initiative, with success measured against the specific duties of one's position rather than universal moral principles or raw output. Yet these same institutions maintain whistleblower protections, individual liability statutes, and personal grievance mechanisms that treat every worker as an autonomous moral agent bearing rights independent of role. What philosophical commitments about personhood, moral responsibility, and identity does this dual-track system actually presuppose? Does the evaluation norm strictly require a relational, role-constituted view of the self—one in which moral agency is distributed across hierarchical relationships rather than located within atomistic individuals—or does it merely cohere well with that view while remaining compatible with virtue-ethical or consequentialist organizational theories? And can the whistleblower framework be integrated into the same underlying philosophy, or does it quietly import a liberal-individualist metaphysics that the evaluation norms deny? I am trying to map precisely where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories of the self and responsibility could license identical institutional designs—and where it genuinely rules out alternatives.
