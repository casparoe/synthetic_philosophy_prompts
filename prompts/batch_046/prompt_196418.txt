Schrödinger's later philosophical essays develop a Vedantic monism according to which individual minds are not fundamentally separate substances but localized manifestations of one universal consciousness, with the appearance of multiplicity being analogous to the way a single source can produce many reflections without being divided. He treats this not as mystical ornament but as a direct answer to the problem of other minds and to the dualism that persists even in some physicalist frameworks. I'm trying to determine which conceptual questions that arise if we take this unity thesis seriously—treating it as a live metaphysical claim rather than a poetic coda—actually merit sustained work today, particularly at the intersection of philosophy of mind and the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and which ones are intellectually decorative but practically inert.

Specifically, I want a defended prioritization of the open lines of inquiry that follow from this commitment. One obvious crux is the privacy problem: if consciousness is genuinely one, why does each subject seem to have access only to a single, bounded stream of experience? Does resolving that require a theory of perspectival limitation, and if so, is that theory compatible with standard functionalist or physicalist commitments about mental access? Another line concerns formal analogy: quantum entanglement exhibits correlations that resist local decomposition, and some have suggested this offers a structural parallel (not a causal mechanism) to the Vedantic claim that separated minds share a common substrate. Is developing that parallel rigorously a fruitful conceptual project, or does it risk generating pseudoscientific noise that obscures the philosophical point? A third concerns normative implications: if personal identity over time and across individuals is, in this framework, ultimately an illusion of multiplicity, does that undermine deontological constraints on harming others, or does it actually reinforce them by removing the boundary between self-interest and other-interest?

Please rank these—or substitute others you consider more upstream—according to criteria that matter for a small research group with limited time and no institutional incentive to produce empirical neuroscience results. For each: explain why it is or is not tractable through conceptual analysis and formal modeling alone; identify which other debates in philosophy of mind or physics it depends on or feeds into; and specify what kind of result—say, a demonstrated inconsistency in one horn of a dilemma, or a bridge argument to a live problem in quantum foundations—would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am less interested in a survey of Advaita Vedanta scholarship or in reading lists than in an agenda: where should effort go first, and what evidence over the next few years would change your mind?
