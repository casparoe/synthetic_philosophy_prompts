Question: Cohen's bootstrapping objection claims that reliabilism allows 'easy knowledge' — e.g., knowing your eyes work just by using them to check a table. Now consider a 'reflective dial' that reports its own accuracy with perfect precision, but can only be turned on by already accepting its first reading. Does this dial reproduce Cohen's problem, or does it differ in a way that saves reliabilism? Explain.

Student answer:
Cohen's basic point is that reliabilists let you verify a faculty by using that same faculty, so you can know your eyes are reliable without ever leaving the circle. This is epistemic circularity, not logical circularity, and reliabilists usually say that's acceptable because it's only premise circularity, not rule circularity.

In the dial case, there's no outside world feeding in at all — the dial only refers back to itself. That makes it logical circularity rather than epistemic circularity, since the conclusion that the dial works is literally part of the premise that lets you turn it on. So reliabilism can't treat this the same way; if being reliable just means being self-affirming, then any broken gadget could claim reliability. That means reliabilism collapses into letting every self-checking device generate knowledge, which is even worse than Cohen's original eye case and shows the view can't be saved.

So the dial doesn't rescue reliabilism. Instead it shows that allowing one form of self-validation forces you to allow all of them, and then knowledge becomes cheap everywhere, which is a seperate problem from the eye case.
