I serve on the clinical ethics committee at Meridian Hospital, and I need a written opinion for circulation before Thursday's meeting regarding a case that turns on the philosophical authority of an earlier self over a present, unconscious one. Our patient, a 68-year-old woman with a progressive neurodegenerative condition, executed an advance directive eighteen years ago refusing mechanical ventilation should she develop what the document calls "irreversible cognitive impairment." She is now unconscious due to a treatable respiratory infection that requires temporary ventilation for roughly seventy-two hours; her physicians expect full recovery of consciousness if the infection is managed. Over the past four years, however, she repeatedly told her adult children—without ever executing a formal amendment—that she had come to regard her earlier directive as expressing a "stranger's fear" rather than her own settled values, particularly after a prolonged remission she described as unexpectedly meaningful. Her family requests the temporary ventilation; her designated healthcare proxy, citing the unamended and witnessed document, insists it must be withheld. Our institutional policy permits "reasonable accommodation" of family objections but distinguishes between "value-consistent" and "value-inconsistent" advance directives without defining either term, leaving the committee to apply the distinction.

Please draft the committee's formal opinion recommending a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame—whether to authorize temporary ventilation, reject it outright, or authorize it subject to conditions such as a mandatory re-evaluation deadline once consciousness returns—and include reasoning that the family and proxy could actually respond to rather than restating the legal definition of neurological status or citing authority. The opinion should treat seriously the strongest argument for the losing side: specifically, the claim that gradual value transformation across a continuous narrative identity does not dissolve the binding force of an explicit, witnessed directive, and that accommodating unwritten family testimony risks reducing advance directives to mere suggestions whenever relatives disagree. At the same time, engage the opposing view that an unamended document can become a form of "precedent autonomy" that binds a present self whose values have coherently evolved, especially when the proposed intervention is temporary, reversible, and aligned with preferences expressed consistently over several years. Avoid bibliographic references, citation lists, or appeals to specific philosophers by name, but navigate the conceptual tension between diachronic identity and present interest without treating the conflict as obviously resolvable by either legal formalism or family sentiment alone.
