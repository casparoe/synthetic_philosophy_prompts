After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about Kantian ethics, I have realized that my own position on universalizability is more scattered than I had assumed, and I would like an audit of whether the commitments I have ended up with can actually be held together. Here is roughly where I have landed: (1) I treat Kant's Formula of Universal Law as the core moral decision procedure, and I read the contradiction-in-conception test as requiring logical incoherence in the universalized maxim rather than mere practical instability. (2) I also hold that the contradiction-in-will test is genuinely distinct from contradiction-in-conception, applying specifically to imperfect duties such as beneficence and self-cultivation, and that it operates by showing that a rational agent cannot consistently will a world in which everyone neglects these ends. (3) I maintain that the universalizability test must be applied to fully specified subjective maxims that include the agent's particular circumstances, intended ends, and chosen means, not to generic action-types like lying or killing stripped of contextual detail. (4) At the same time, I insist that for a maxim to fail the contradiction-in-conception test, the contradiction must arise in every possible world where the maxim is universalized, not only in our actual world with its contingent social institutions and practices. (5) I reject purely formalist interpretations of universalizability: I think the test necessarily presupposes a substantive account of rational agency, specifically that rational beings must be able to regard themselves as members of a community of ends. (6) Despite that substantive commitment, I maintain that in the false-promising case the contradiction is detectable without any empirical assumptions about human psychology, social trust, or economic exchange, arising purely from the formal structure of promising together with universal deception.

Please evaluate this package in American English, treating my own commitments as the object rather than delivering a general lecture on Kant. I want the tensions ordered by severity, with a clear statement of which ones are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, and a specific indication of exactly which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned to restore consistency in each genuine case. I am particularly uncertain whether (5) and (6) can coexist: if universalizability already imports a substantive conception of rational agency and communal membership, then does the false-promising argument not smuggle in precisely the kind of institutional and psychological premises I claim to avoid? There also seems to be a possible conflict between (3) and (4): if maxims are specified with particular circumstances and ends, then requiring the contradiction to hold across all possible worlds either makes the test impossibly strict or undermines the contextual specificity I want to preserve. And I wonder whether (1) and (2) are actually in tension, since treating the Formula of Universal Law as the core procedure might suggest that contradiction-in-will should be reducible to contradiction-in-conception rather than genuinely independent.
