I'm serving as the grader for an upper-division philosophy of aesthetics exam, and I need a precise assessment of one particular response. The exam prompt asked students to address the so-called paradox of tragedy—specifically, why audiences seek out and appear to enjoy artworks that evoke profound sadness or grief—by comparing two explanatory frameworks discussed in our course unit. The first is the conventional "protective framing" account, according to which aesthetic distance allows viewers to experience negative affect without the practical consequences and action tendencies that normally accompany it. The second is what our syllabus terms the "layered-attention" model: the claim that tragic enjoyment arises not from distance alone, but from a simultaneous dual orientation in which the audience remains emotionally immersed in the narrative situation while also attending, at a parallel cognitive level, to the formal properties—rhythm, structure, dramatic irony—that organize and give meaning to that suffering. The exam instructed students to identify one strength and one limitation of each framework, and then to argue whether the layered-attention model genuinely resolves the paradox or merely relocates it.

Here is the student's answer: "The protective framing account explains tragedy well because it shows we don't really feel sad; we just observe sadness from a safe position, like watching through glass. This avoids the paradox since there's no real pain to conflict with pleasure. The layered-attention model is different because it says we do feel real sadness, but we also notice the art's structure at the same time. This seems better because it allows real emotion, not fake observation. However, the model has a problem: if both frames are active together, they must compete for mental resources, so the emotional frame should weaken the formal frame or vice versa. That means the model actually predicts we can't fully enjoy both at once, which brings the paradox back—why seek out suffering if it undermines our appreciation? I think the layered-attention model works for theater but not for lyric poetry, since poems don't have plot or dramatic irony to support the second frame."

Please grade this response on a standard letter scale and provide point-by-point comments that justify the score. In particular, indicate whether the student has correctly characterized both frameworks, whether the objection about competing mental resources actually applies to the layered-attention model as defined above, and whether the restriction of the model to theatrical forms is warranted. Be specific about any conceptual conflations or missteps, and do not provide a bibliography or suggested readings.
