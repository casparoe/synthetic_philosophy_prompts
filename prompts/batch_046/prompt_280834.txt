Question: Some philosophers argue that promises bind because they create legitimate expectations in the promisee. If this is correct, then secretly breaking a promise is still morally wrong, because the expectation existed at the moment of promising even if it is never actually disappointed. Assess this argument.

Student answer: The expectation account holds that promises generate obligations mainly because the promisee comes to expect the promised act, and that expectation gives the promisor a reason to perform. So if I promise to feed your cat and then secretly skip it, the expectation was real and is now violated, which explains why secret breaking is wrong. But this makes promises too close to predictions — a weather forecast also creates expectations, yet nobody thinks a false forecast wrongs anyone. So expectation theory collapses into a mistake about causation. The better view is reliance theory: promises bind only when the promisee actually relies on them by altering their behavior. In secret breaking there is no reliance damage, so no wrong occurs unless the promisee changed plans. Therefore promises only carry weight in cases of actual relyance. Some also mention normative powers, but that just pushes the question back to why we should care about those social rules.
