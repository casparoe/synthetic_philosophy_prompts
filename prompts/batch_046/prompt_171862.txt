As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been reading Dewey’s work on democracy as a mode of social inquiry, and I wish to understand what criteria a theory of democratic experimentalism must satisfy if it is genuinely Deweyan rather than merely metaphorical. Specifically, must it treat legislative and policy decisions as falsifiable hypotheses about collective welfare, and if so, how ought it to accommodate large-scale, irreversible interventions—such as structural economic or environmental reforms—that lack the repeatability and control of laboratory trials? Must it also institutionalise distributed observation and peer-like correction among citizens with highly unequal epistemic access? Please set out the desiderata with the reasoning behind each, indicate which are non-negotiable within Dewey’s framework and which are likely prejudices imported from my scientific background, and say whether any subset—particularly the pairing of falsifiability with irreversibility—is jointly unsatisfiable. I should be grateful for a conceptual map rather than a bibliography, and I ask that you reply in British English.
