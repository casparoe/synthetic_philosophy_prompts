I have been working through Hannah Arendt's analysis of moral and political responsibility in the wake of totalitarian bureaucracy, and I find her judgments across several historical figures surprisingly consistent yet open to more than one underlying principle. Consider the following pattern of her assessments: she describes Adolf Eichmann not as a sadistic monster but as terrifyingly ordinary, a man whose evil stemmed from a kind of thoughtlessness — a failure of reflective judgment rather than wicked intent; she condemns the leaders of the Hungarian Jewish councils for their bureaucratic cooperation with deportation logistics, seeing in them a parallel failure of political imagination and a collapse into administrative compliance; yet she praises the Danish government and populace for collectively refusing to participate in the identification and deportation of Jews, interpreting their resistance as an exercise of genuine political action and moral discernment that broke the spell of bureaucratic normalization. From these verdicts, one might extract a rule centered on the presence or absence of thinking in the Arendtian sense; another might emphasize whether institutional role-distance enabled moral disengagement; and a third might focus on whether actors exercised collective political freedom against administrative machinery.

The new case I want to test is that of a senior civil servant — let us call her Dr. Elena Voss — in a contemporary liberal democracy who spends fifteen years refining a predictive risk-assessment algorithm used for welfare eligibility and immigration screening. She never meets those affected, expresses private ethical reservations in internal memos, takes pride in the procedural fairness and statistical accuracy of her system, and retires with honors for public-service innovation, all while the algorithm systematically produces catastrophic false positives for marginalized groups. Would Arendt classify Voss's moral failure as banal evil in the Eichmann mode, as a variant of the Jewish-council leadership failure, or as something closer to the Danish refusal but inverted — a refusal not to act politically but to think beyond her administrative role? Which of the three rules I sketched best predicts her verdict, and what further case — perhaps involving a whistleblower within the same bureaucracy, or a collective of engineers who disable the system — would best distinguish whether Arendt's criterion is ultimately about internal thoughtlessness, institutional role-distance, or the absence of collective political resistance?
