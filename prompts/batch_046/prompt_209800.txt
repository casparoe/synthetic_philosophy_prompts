Restate the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism—the claim that aggregating utility across individuals treats society as one subject distributed over many bodies, thereby erasing the moral significance of boundaries between distinct lives—entirely within my vocabulary: nodes, partitions, aggregate versus local state, consistency models, replication lag, fault domains, and the costs of reaching consensus. Translate as faithfully as possible: identify the system-level computation that corresponds to utility aggregation, the architectural boundary that corresponds to the moral boundary between persons, and the design constraint that corresponds to the demand that burdens not be transferred across that boundary. Then provide the residue report. Which elements of the objection map cleanly onto distributed-system design, which require distorting an engineering concept to fit, and which have no expression in this vocabulary at all? Specifically, does the normative judgment that cross-person aggregation is unjust correspond to a recognizable architectural failure, or is it a moral claim that lies outside any engineering criterion? And if it lies outside, does that indicate a genuine limitation in my framework, or does it suggest that the philosophical objection relies on a distinction that does not carry independent argumentative weight?
