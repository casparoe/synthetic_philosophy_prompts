I'm giving opening remarks at a workshop next tuesday called Speech, Sanction, and the University. The room is split — philosophers of law and political theory people who actually think about punishment, sociology grad students who treat 'cancel culture' like its self-explanatory, and one university trustee who mostly wants to know if we're in legal trouble. I need about five minutes, maybe 600 words.

The conceptual bind I want you to build the whole thing around — and it has to actually shape what I can and can't say, not just decorate it — is this: treat deplatforming and social shunning as possible punishment, but run it through the standard conditions. You need an authoritative agent, a prior wrong, and an intended deprivation or suffering. Distributed social sanction flunks all three — no single authority, the 'wrong' is often just being out of step with a shifting norm, and the harm is usually a side-effect rather than the point. So if I call it punishment I'm stretching the concept; if I deny it's punishment I miss that people experience it exactly that way. The speech has to live inside that tension. That means I can't open with the usual 'this is just accountability' or 'this is mob rule' lines, because both depend on resolving the tension too fast.

Also inject a niche framing but don't make it depend on some obscure citation: call social sanction an 'epistemic quarantine.' The idea is that the community isn't primarily trying to make someone suffer; it's withdrawing the default presumption that they are a credible interlocutor or source. That uses the same social machinery as punishment — exclusion, withdrawal of standing — but aims at boundary-maintenance, not suffering. The speech should use that to explain why the workshop can't start with a verdict.

Write it in three sections, in this order:
1. 'The trap' (around 150 words): set up the audience split and why a standard welcome fails given the punishment framing.
2. 'The remarks' (around 600 words): the actual spoken welcome. It should feel like a welcome but also like it's refusing to resolve the conceptual problem.
3. 'The omissions' (around 150 words): what I deliberately left unsaid because of the framing, and where the epistemic-quarantine idea does the structural work.

Keep it casual — a typo or two is totally fine. Don't include a bibliography or reading list; I won't have time to check sources. And don't quote anyone verbatim unless you can fetch the exact text — paraphrase instead.
