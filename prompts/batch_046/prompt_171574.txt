I have been trying to map out my position on practical reason and temporal choice for a graduate seminar, and I have arrived at a cluster of commitments that feel individually defensible but may not form a coherent package. Specifically, I take hyperbolic discounting—particularly in its quasi-hyperbolic β–δ formulation—to provide the correct descriptive model of human intertemporal choice, which means that present-biased preferences are not mere errors but structurally real features of our motivational architecture. At the same time, I nevertheless maintain that akrasia, or weakness of will, is a genuinely possible phenomenon, not reducible to preference reversal over time, since there exist clear-eyed cases in which an agent explicitly endorses a long-run plan and yet deliberately acts against it in the moment.

Building on that, I hold that the philosophical analysis of akrasia requires a diachronically stable "all-things-considered" judgment as the normative benchmark against which an action is judged akratic, rather than a mere comparison between time-sliced utility functions. Yet I also think that if hyperbolic discounting is descriptively correct, then the idea of such a stable, persisting judgment is psychologically unrealistic—there is no enduring "self" whose judgment remains constant, only a succession of present-biased evaluative states with different weights. I want to preserve the appropriateness of blame for akratic action on the grounds that the agent retains rational access to the reasons supporting the long-run plan, even when her present motivational state discounts them hyperbolically. There is also an auxiliary claim I am inclined to accept: that the so-called synchronic authority problem—namely, that if present-biased preferences carry genuine normative authority for the present self, then privileging long-run preferences involves an arbitrary diachronic bias—can be resolved without collapsing the distinction between descriptive psychology and normative judgment.

Do these five commitments, together with that auxiliary resolution, actually hang together? Please audit them for consistency, rank any genuine tensions by severity, specify which single commitment is the least costly to revise if coherence demands it, and distinguish apparent conflicts—such as that between the demand for diachronic stability and the psychological reality of present-biased states—that dissolve once one separates normative authority from descriptive motivational structure. I am especially unsure whether my defense of blame in commitment (5) survives intact if I resolve the central tension in favor of the hyperbolic-descriptive picture rather than in favor of the diachronically stable judgment.
