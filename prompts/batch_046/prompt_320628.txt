Consider the 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal, in which the firm installed defeat devices to cheat regulatory tests, and upon exposure faced widespread condemnation from regulators, rival manufacturers, and the driving public. Analyze this episode through the philosophical literature on hypocrisy and standing to blame, but do not merely ask whether VW’s critics were hypocrites in the ordinary sense. Instead, work with the following framing: distinguish first-order hypocrisy—the blamer personally commits the very wrong they condemn—from second-order hypocrisy, in which the blamer participates in a system that systematically produces or conceals structurally identical wrongs while benefiting from that concealment. Many of VW’s critics appear to fall into this second category: regulators whose testing regimes were easily gamed; competitors who optimized for laboratory cycles rather than real-world emissions; and consumers who demanded cheap diesel performance without scrutinizing its feasibility. Examine whether second-order hypocrisy undermines standing to blame in the same way and to the same degree as first-order hypocrisy; whether collective entities such as regulatory agencies can bear hypocrisy in a sense that affects the standing of their individual members; and whether standing to blame is best conceived as an individual property or as distributed across institutional roles. Be disciplined about the mapping: identify precisely who counts as the blamer, what counts as the corresponding fault in each case, and what form of accountability—moral, legal, or institutional—is at issue. Where the standard account of hypocrisy and standing is silent on these distinctions, say so explicitly, and indicate what the episode suggests about extending or revising the theory. Finally, ask what the case shows in the other direction: does the prevalence of second-order hypocrisy in large-scale regulatory failure support a skeptical conclusion about standing generally, or does it instead reveal that the theory was developed for interpersonal contexts and needs reconceptualization for systemic ones?
