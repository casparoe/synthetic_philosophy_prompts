Two students in my study group got stuck on insect sentience and the scale argument, and neither will budge. Maya started with the numbers: maybe 10^19 insects alive at once, and even a conservative prior like 0.001 sentience probability gives you staggering expected suffering. She said that should dominate our priorities, full stop, and that anyone who doesn't take it seriously is just suffering from mammal bias. Jonas pushed back that Maya is treating moral status as linear in probability, which is wrong: we don't have obligations to every entity with a non-zero chance of consciousness, or we'd be paralyzed stepping on rocks since panpsychism isn't literally zero. Maya said comparing insects to rocks is unfair, because insects have nociception and some central integration, so thier prior should be way higher than a rock's. Jonas agreed the prior is higher, but said that's not his real objection. His real objection is what he called the "individuation problem": if sentience requires integrated information, and an insect's neural architecture is distributed in a way that makes it unclear whether you've got one subject or a loose federation, then multiplying "probability of sentience" by "number of individuals" is multiplying a fraction by a blurry denominator. Maya accused him of smuggling metaphysics in to dodge the math. Jonas said he wasn't denying the conclusion, just that the scale argument doesn't establish it, because the aggregation step already assumes discrete patients, which is exactly what's contested. Maya then asked if he denies aggregation altogether in collective cases, like a hive mind versus a swarm. Jonas said he accepts aggregation when individuation conditions are met, but with insects they aren't, and that Maya was treating "scale" as purely quantitative when it's partly ontological. They kept going in circles after that. Can you adjudicate point by point? I want to know who's right about the linearity objection, who's right about the prior/reference class, and whether Jonas's individuation problem actually undermines the scale argument or just changes its subject. Also, what's the missing piece both of them are ignoring -- maybe something about how moral risk frameworks handle blurry ontology -- that could actually break the stalemate? Don't just say "it depends on your metaethics"; tell me if there's a coherent principle that keeps the scale intuition alive without falling into paralysis.
