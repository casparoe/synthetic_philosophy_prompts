Philosophers have been arguing for decades about Dennett's claim that mental states are real patterns detectable from the intentional stance but not reducible to physical or design-stance descriptions. One camp reads this as a genuine ontological vindication: mental kinds are real in the same sense as biological functions, just accessible at a particular explanatory level. The other camp reads it as disguised instrumentalism—patterns are real only relative to a predictive strategy, and the physical stance ultimately owns all the causal work. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is mostly about the standards for ontological commitment rather than about any empirical or formal feature of the patterns themselves: one side demands that real kinds be causally fundamental or structurally unique, the other accepts explanatory indispensability and predictive compression as sufficient. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive dispute—perhaps about whether intentional-level regularities have unique algorithmic decompositions that the design stance cannot approximate, or about whether stance-dependence is compatible with mind-independence? I want an honest diagnosis: are the sides talking past each other, answering different questions about reality, or is there an unresolved formal question about multi-level pattern uniqueness? And given that diagnosis, what would actually move the debate—a conceptual distinction, a result about algorithmic complexity and multiple realizability, or is the question itself misframed?
