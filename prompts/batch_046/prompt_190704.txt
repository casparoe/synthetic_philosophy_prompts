Please do not accommodate me politely: if my central argument rests on a confused reading of counterpart theory, on a mischaracterization of essential properties for artifacts, or on a premise that cannot be sustained without collapsing into verbal dispute, say so plainly and tell me exactly where the error lies. I also want you to flag any passages where I lean on a contested interpretation of Lewis or of essentialism without acknowledging that it is contested, and to tell me whether the essay's literary framing clarifies the conceptual issue or merely ornaments an incoherent claim.

The essay's core concern is whether modal realism can sustain a meaningful distinction between "nearby" and "remote" possible worlds once we examine artifacts rather than natural kinds. I argue that because an artifact's origin—say, that this particular watch was assembled in Geneva in 1952—seems essential to its identity in some counterpart relations but entirely contingent in others, the metric we might use to rank worlds by shared essential properties becomes unstable. If worlds are concrete and spatiotemporally isolated, there is no literal spatial distance between them; the "proximity" of a world where I miss the train is usually explained by maximal similarity of matters of particular fact. But if artifacts pull that similarity metric in contradictory directions—treating origin as essential for identity but accidental for possibility—then the cartography of modal space risks being not a geography but a fiction writer's multiverse: internally consistent scene by scene, yet lacking any coordinate system that holds across the whole. I want to know whether this genuinely exposes a tension in Lewis's ontology, or whether counterpart theory already dissolves it by making "this watch" a name for a class of counterparts with no single essential origin.

The Cartography of Counterparts

In David Lewis's modal realism, possible worlds are not abstract descriptions but concrete totalities—vast, spatiotemporally disconnected universes as real as our own. We speak casually of worlds being "nearby" or "far away," as though modal space possessed a geometry. The intuition is clear enough: a world in which I catch the train is closer to actuality than one in which the laws of physics are inverted. Yet since these worlds share no space or time, distance cannot be literal. It must be reconstructed from similarity—shared properties, shared histories, shared individuals.

The standard reconstruction appeals to essential properties. A world in which I exist with my actual essential traits—my genetic origin, perhaps my human nature—is nearer than one in which a counterpart bears only accidental resemblance. This works well enough for natural kinds. A world containing hydrogen atoms with different nuclear properties is remote because those properties are essential to hydrogen itself. But artifacts resist this sorting.

Consider a mechanical watch assembled in Geneva in 1952. In our world, its origin seems essential to its identity: it is this watch precisely because it emerged from that workshop. Yet counterpart theory allows that in another world, a qualitatively identical watch—same gears, same engraving, same function—might have been assembled in Tokyo in 1987. If we ask which world contains the very same watch, we face a split decision. If origin is essential, the Tokyo watch is not my watch at all, but a perfect duplicate; the Geneva-origin world is therefore "nearer" because it preserves the artifact's essential history. If origin is accidental, however, the Tokyo world contains my watch under a different history, and proximity should be measured by functional and structural identity rather than provenance. The metric wobbles.

This wobble is not merely an epistemic puzzle about how we identify objects. It is ontological. For Lewis, individuals exist wholly within single worlds; there are no trans-world identities, only counterpart relations. The "distance" between worlds is therefore a function of how many and which counterparts an object in our world possesses elsewhere. But if the criteria for counterparthood differ between artifacts and natural kinds—if origin binds tightly for watches but loosely for persons—then there is no unified scale of similarity. One world might be near because it preserves my personal essence but remote because every artifact in it has an alien origin; another might be near in artifact-space but remote in personal space. The result is not a map with a single legend, but a collection of overlapping atlases that do not align.

One might reply that this is precisely what we should expect: modal space is not Euclidean. But there is a deeper worry. If "nearby" is defined relative to the kind of object being tracked, then talk of a world being "close to actuality" is ambiguous until we specify whose proximity we mean—mine, the watch's, or the hydrogen atom's. The novelist's instinct recognizes this immediately: a multiverse in fiction works because each storyline selects its own center of gravity. Modal realism, however, promises an objective totality. Once we realize that artifacts fracture the similarity metric that was meant to ground objective proximity, the promise of a single, coherent modal geography begins to look like a narrative convenience rather than an ontological discovery. The worlds remain concrete; the distance between them dissolves into perspective.
