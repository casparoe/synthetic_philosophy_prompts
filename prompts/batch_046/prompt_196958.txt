I am designing the final examination for a graduate seminar in epistemic game theory, and I need a single 60-minute essay question that forces students to work through common knowledge of rationality rather than recite Aumann's agreement result. The question should be built around a constructed institutional scenario—an "asynchronous belief-revision audit"—with three analysts, A, B, and C, who share a common prior over a binary state space but acquire knowledge of one another's rationality and posteriors on a staggered schedule: in stage one, A learns B's rationality but not C's; in stage two, B learns both A's and C's rationality, and also learns that A learned B's rationality in stage one; in stage three, all three learn everyone's rationality, and C additionally learns the full history of who knew what when, including that A and B possessed mutual meta-knowledge one stage before C did. The exam prompt should ask students to determine, at each stage, exactly what is and is not common knowledge among the trio; to assess whether the conditions for Aumann-style agreement obtain by stage three; and to analyze whether C's delayed meta-knowledge about the audit schedule can disrupt common knowledge of rationality even though every individual rationality condition is satisfied at stage three. Please write: (1) the exam question in clear sub-parts suitable for a 60-minute response; (2) a model answer of roughly 650–750 words that uses epistemic-logic notation (for example, K_i or iterative mutual-belief operators) to derive conclusions rather than invoke named theorems; and (3) a marking scheme distributing 25 points across five specific analytical moves, listing the point value of each move and, in one line per move, the characteristic student error that should reduce the score. Do not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or specific paper titles, and ensure that every claim required can be supported by conceptual reasoning about belief revision and common-knowledge operators alone.
