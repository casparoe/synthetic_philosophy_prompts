I am taking a graduate seminar on strategic interaction, and we have spent the last two weeks on Thomas Schelling's analysis of brinkmanship and the logic of credible threats. My instructor repeatedly invokes the game of chicken to illuminate nuclear deterrence: two adversaries accelerate toward a mutual cliff, and victory belongs to the one who demonstrates—perhaps by discarding his steering wheel—that he is genuinely unable to swerve, thereby forcing the rational opponent to yield. The analogy is used to license a specific normative conclusion, namely that stable deterrence requires cultivating a visible, partial irrationality so that the other side calculates it is safer to back down. I want to examine whether this mapping actually holds under pressure.

Please construct an explicit correspondence table: identify what plays the role of each driver, the act of throwing away the steering wheel, the swerve, the crash, and the spectators whose beliefs shape payoffs. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for Schelling's argument from those that are merely decorative. In particular, address his observation that genuine brinkmanship often works by generating risk that is partly outside one's own control—the threat that leaves something to chance—whereas in chicken the risk of collision is entirely a function of one's own decision not to swerve. Does the analogy therefore smuggle in the assumption that credibility demands total commitment rather than calibrated, probabilistic risk? Furthermore, examine whether the analogy's conclusion survives when we replace the one-shot, perfect-information structure of chicken with an iterated interaction characterized by noisy signaling, second-strike capabilities, and automated response protocols that can execute retaliation without human intervention. If the chicken framework collapses under these conditions, propose a better analogy—perhaps drawn from engineering failure modes or evolutionary biology—that more accurately captures the dynamics of mutual assured destruction without importing misleading intuitions about rational choice.
