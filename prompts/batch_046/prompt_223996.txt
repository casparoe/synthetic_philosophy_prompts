I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion and normative ethics, and next Thursday I present a paper to the interdisciplinary Value and Meaning seminar—approximately ten participants: three analytic philosophers of religion (two theists, one religious naturalist), four moral philosophers whose research centers on well-being and objective-list theories, and one historian of philosophy who specializes in logical empiricism and the Vienna Circle. The historian is familiar with Schlick's primary writings; the rest are not.

My thesis defends a Schlick-inspired naturalistic account of life's meaning, modified in two non-standard ways. First, I distinguish first-order play—immediate, non-instrumental absorption in an activity—from second-order play, understood as the reflective, institutional labor of maintaining the social and material conditions that make first-order play possible. I argue that Schlick's framework, read consistently, treats both orders as genuinely meaning-constituting rather than treating the second as merely instrumental to the first. Second, I derive from this a normative position I call existential conservationism: a categorical, intergenerational obligation to preserve and expand structures of non-instrumentalized activity, binding even on agents who do not themselves value play. I do not ground this obligation in welfare maximization, in transcendence, or in contractualist reasoning, but in the internal logic of Schlick's separation of purpose from meaning.
