You are the senior methodological advisor to a clinical-trial oversight board. The board employs evidence-review officers who must make rapid, repeatable judgments—within fifteen minutes, using only the dossier at hand, with no external literature search—about whether a trial sponsor is epistemically justified in holding that an amended protocol preserves patient safety (belief Q), given that the sponsor knows the statistical adjustment model is valid (belief P) and accepts the entailment that model validity implies safety preservation (P → Q). The board requires these judgments to be grounded in Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge—specifically the sensitivity condition (if Q were false, the sponsor would not believe Q) and the adherence condition (if Q is true, the sponsor believes Q)—and in the characteristic failure of epistemic closure that tracking theories entail: knowing P and knowing P → Q is insufficient for knowing Q when worlds in which Q is false despite P remain modally nearby.

Construct a reusable, formal decision procedure—presented as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes and branching conditions—that a non-philosopher officer can apply consistently across cases. The procedure must include:

(i) Separate operational tests for whether belief P tracks, and for whether belief Q tracks, specifying exactly which dossier elements (e.g., residual plots, interaction terms, sensitivity analyses) serve as proxies for counterfactual proximity checks, and stating the threshold at which tracking fails.

(ii) A clearly marked closure-failure risk node: the specific nearby-world scenario in which the model remains valid yet an unanticipated interaction renders Q false. Specify the practical indicators in the dossier that raise or lower the modal proximity of that world, and state the exact point at which the officer must block sign-off on Q despite the entailment from P.

(iii) Three worked applications: (A) a case in which P tracks well but Q fails tracking due to a hidden interaction, yielding a blocked sign-off that contradicts expert consensus—explain why the tracking analysis demands this unwelcome result; (B) a case in which both P and Q track and closure holds; (C) a boundary case in which tracking for P is marginal and the proximity of the failure-world for Q is ambiguous.

(iv) Explicit flagging of every point at which judgment cannot be eliminated—particularly in calibrating the modal distance of nearby worlds—together with the designated signatory (e.g., senior biostatistician or board chair) who must resolve the ambiguity.

(v) A precise statement of the procedure's misfire conditions and of what it cannot determine, including cases in which the dossier is silent on interaction effects.

In addition, inject the following conceptual refinement: require the officer not only to assess whether the sponsor's belief in Q tracks, but also to apply the tracking conditions reflexively to their own second-order belief that the sponsor's belief tracks. The officer must therefore include a self-check step that asks whether, in nearby worlds where the sponsor is wrong about Q, the officer would still erroneously judge that tracking succeeds. Specify the practical safeguard against this second-order failure.

Keep the tone formal and operational. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any author verbatim; paraphrase all theoretical claims. Ensure the procedure requires no web access and no philosophical expertise beyond the checklist itself.
