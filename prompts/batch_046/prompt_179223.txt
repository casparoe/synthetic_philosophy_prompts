I'm preparing an essay for my school's philosophy review on why human extinction is bad in ways that go beyond the deaths it would cause. The philosopher Samuel Scheffler proposes an "afterlife" conjecture: much of what gives present lives meaning depends on an implicit expectation that humanity will continue after we die—not personal survival, but posterity. On this view, extinction is catastrophic mainly because it annihilates the background condition that makes our projects and commitments intelligible.

Critics reply that this makes extinction's badness implausibly indirect: if nobody survives to suffer the loss, and present people are already dead, there is no subject of harm, so the frustration of this "afterlife" seems like a category mistake rather than a genuine injury. Others worry the view ties human value to a kind of cosmic self-importance.

Weigh these objections, but also examine a tension that is less often discussed. Scheffler's conjecture treats humanity's continuation as something like an implicit promise made to those now alive, yet extinction breaks that promise precisely when there is no remaining party to receive the breach or to hold anyone accountable. Does this absence of a wronged party weaken the claim that extinction is uniquely bad in this "afterlife" sense? Or does it point instead to a different kind of value—perhaps the value of a narrative having an appropriate ending, or of a shared practice being completed—that can be violated even without a victim? Reach and defend a specific conclusion: is the frustration of this posterity-based expectation the primary reason extinction is bad, a significant but secondary reason, or a misdiagnosis of our anxiety about it? Reason through the concepts directly; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
