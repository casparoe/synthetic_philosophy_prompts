I tried to formalize Braudel's distinction between the longue durée and the événement with an interventionist account of causation, and I think it proves that no historical actor ever causes anything. The conclusion is clearly wrong—Braudel himself writes dense narratives of Mediterranean diplomacy and strategic choice as if they genuinely matter—but I cannot locate the error without abandoning either the framework or the philosophy of causation.

(1) On a Woodward-style interventionist view, X causes Y just in case an intervention on X, holding other relevant variables fixed, produces a change in Y.

(2) Braudel's central claim is that structural variables—geography, climate, demographic rhythms, the slow movement of the longue durée—constrain macro-historical outcomes far more tightly than any event-level variable, such as a particular treaty, battle, or personal decision.

(3) Formally: for any event-level variable E and macro-outcome O, there exists an intervention on E (holding structural variables S constant) that leaves O unchanged, while an intervention on S produces a dramatic change in O.

(4) By (1), this means S causes O but E does not.

(5) This is not exceptional but systematic: every event-level explanation in historiography can be paired with a structural variable that dominates it interventionally.

(6) Therefore, on this formalization, no individual action, decision, or contingent event ever causes any large-scale historical outcome; all are causally inert relative to the structures that contain them.

(7) But if (6) holds, then Braudel's own practice—writing about Philip II's alliances, trade conflicts, and strategic choices—is causally mistaken, since those choices do no explanatory work.

(8) It also implies that present political agency is largely illusory with respect to climate and geography, which undermines the practical rationale for studying history at all.

(9) So the framework, made precise, undermines itself and eliminates the very subject matter it was meant to illuminate.

(10) Thus one of (1) through (5) must fail. But (1) is our standard analysis of causation, (2) is the heart of the Annales thesis, and (3)-(5) seem to follow by valid inference.

Which step should I reject? If I deny (5), I need a principled boundary between events that are dominated and those that escape domination, but drawing it looks ad hoc. If I reject (3), I must allow that we cannot hold structural variables fixed while varying events, yet that seems to concede that structure and event are so entangled that the sharp Braudelian distinction collapses—precisely what I wanted to preserve. I would prefer to keep (2), since there genuinely is a difference in temporal scale and constraint between geography and a single battle, but I want to understand the price. Is the flaw a hidden scope shift between token causation and explanatory importance? Or is there a modal slide between "would not change the outcome" and "does not cause it"?
