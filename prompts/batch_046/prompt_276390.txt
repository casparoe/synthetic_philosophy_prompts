Question 4 (30 marks): 'The French Revolution is customarily interpreted through three principal frameworks: the materialist or class-struggle account associated with Marxist historiography; the ideological account, which treats revolutionary discourse and symbolic practice as constitutive of political reality rather than merely reflective of it; and the contingency account, which foregrounds accident, personality, and the unintended consequences of action. In addition, historians and political theorists have long noted the tendency of revolutions to consume their own progenitors. Your task is to determine whether this latter observation is adequately explained by one of the three standard frameworks, or whether it requires a distinct analytical category—specifically, what may be termed endogenous institutional self-cannibalisation: the propensity of a revolutionary state's own procedural architecture (exemplified, for instance, by the fusion of legislative and executive authority within bodies such as the Committee of Public Safety) to produce the systematic elimination of its founders, independently of personal malice or external contingency. In developing your argument, analyse at least one concrete episode, such as the fall of the Girondins or the trial of Danton, and clarify whether the metaphor of revolutionary self-consumption signifies a structural necessity, a contingent betrayal, or an ideological purification. You should take care to distinguish structural continuity from mere historical accident.'

The student wrote as follows:

'The class-struggle interpretation, linked to Marx and to social historians such as Soboul, regards the Revolution as the bourgeoisie destroying feudal relations. It struggles to explain why prominent revolutionaries—including Robespierre and Danton—turned upon one another, given that they all belonged to the same class. The ideological interpretation, developed by Furet, maintains that revolutionary language generated its own political reality and demanded ever-stricter purity, thereby explaining the Terror. The contingency interpretation, best represented by Tocqueville, insists that the Revolution was not predetermined and that accidents together with individual personalities directed its development. The observation that revolutions devour their own children was first uttered by Vergniaud from the scaffold, and it proves that revolutions are intrinsically unstable. I therefore think it supports the contingency view: had the Revolution enjoyed a more robust institutional framework, it would not have destroyed its leaders. The fall of the Girondins demonstrates this precisely—Robespierre removed them through personal ambition, which was a purely contingent act rather than a structural outcome. For this reason, no separate concept of endogenous institutional self-cannibalisation is required; contingency alone suffices.'
