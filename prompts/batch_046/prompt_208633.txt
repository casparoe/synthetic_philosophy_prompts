I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has developed an earnest interest in philosophical aesthetics and the philosophy of mind. I am preparing a contribution for an interdisciplinary reading group that meets monthly to discuss a work of literature alongside a conceptual problem, and I have proposed that we examine Cormac McCarthy’s *The Road* not merely as a narrative of survival, but as a case study in the paradox of fiction. The paradox, as I understand it, concerns how we can experience genuine emotional responses—grief, dread, pity—towards characters and events we simultaneously know to be non-existent, and whether those responses carry the same moral weight or rational justification as their real-world counterparts.

My particular concern, which I hope distinguishes our discussion from the standard treatments I have encountered, is whether the paradox becomes more acute, or perhaps structurally different, when the emotional target is not an individuated agent with whom we might empathise, but a de-individuated world-system of suffering: the father and the boy remain largely unnamed, and the novel’s horror derives less from personal misfortune than from the total collapse of the conditions that make moral agency possible. I should like you to read *The Road* through this specific lens—treating the novel as an exploration of whether emotional engagement with fictional suffering requires a belief-like representation of a specific victim, or whether it can be sustained by something closer to an abstract, ecological dread.

In your response, please address the following: which scenes or narrative choices illuminate this tension, and which resist it? Does McCarthy’s sparse, almost clinical prose suppress the cognitive markers that would normally trigger disbelief, thereby deepening the emotional effect without resolving the paradox? And where might an honest critic concede that the novel does not, in fact, answer the philosophical question, but rather exposes a limit in how we typically frame it—perhaps by showing that the reader’s suffering is not vicarious but participatory? Finally, I should be grateful if you could indicate what *The Road* contributes to the debate that a purely argumentative essay on fiction and emotion could not capture. Please write your answer in British English, and do not include bibliographic references or suggested reading lists, as I wish to pursue those independently.
