At a dinner following a symposium on narrative causation, I found myself seated between two guests — Marcus, a historian of the long nineteenth century, and Elena, a philosopher of science — who became locked in a disagreement about whether non-human forces ought properly to be treated as 'historical actors'. The exchange unfolded in four stages, and I am reproducing it here as faithfully as I can. (1) Marcus insisted that to call germs, volcanic eruptions, or climate shifts 'actors' is to commit a category error that slides into environmental determinism: historical agency, he argued, requires either intentionality or at least strategic adaptability, neither of which a sulphur aerosol cloud or a bacillus possesses. To include them in the cast of characters, he said, obscures the actual human agents — grain speculators, negligent officials, adaptive communities — whose choices shaped outcomes such as the famine following the 1816 'Year Without a Summer' or the social responses to cholera epidemics. (2) Elena replied that agency need not be intentional. In any adequate causal explanation, an actor is simply an entity whose presence or absence alters the trajectory of events in ways that would not otherwise have occurred. The 1815 Tambora eruption altered global temperatures, which destroyed harvests, which triggered riots and, she observed, created the atmospheric and social conditions in which Mary Shelley composed Frankenstein. To omit Tambora is not to preserve human dignity but to falsify the causal architecture of the period. (3) Marcus conceded causal relevance but proposed a strict ontological division: 'structural conditions' versus 'historical agents'. A volcano, he noted, does not select among alternatives or respond to incentives; even adaptive germs evolve without foresight. If we erase this boundary, responsibility dissolves into what he called a 'causal democracy' — a flat explanatory ledger in which a tax policy and a lava flow carry comparable weight — which he regarded as ontologically absurd. (4) Elena accepted the danger of flattening but rejected the split. Responsibility and causation, she argued, belong to separate registers: we hold governments accountable for famine relief precisely because we recognise the eruption as an exogenous shock rather than a human choice. Yet she warned that her own framework of 'causal democracy' — listing all contributing factors with equal explanatory status — risks precisely the ontological collapse Marcus fears, particularly when climate feedback loops exhibit selective, adaptive behaviour that blurs the line between passive condition and active agent.
