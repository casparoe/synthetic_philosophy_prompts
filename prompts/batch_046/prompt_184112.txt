I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level seminar in moral philosophy, and I need a single essay question—worth thirty-five marks, to be completed in about ninety minutes at roughly eight hundred to one thousand words—that forces students to navigate the particularism versus generalism debate without allowing them to rely on memorized summaries. Rather than asking for a generic comparison, I want the prompt to center on a concrete, somewhat unfamiliar scenario: a city council must decide whether to preserve or demolish an aging bridge that serves as the only remaining physical site tied to a community's collective memory. In one framing, the bridge stands in an affluent district where preservation serves aesthetic continuity and property values; in another, it stands in a historically displaced, low-income district where demolition would erase the final material trace of a prior settlement. The moral feature—demolition of a heritage structure—thus appears to carry opposite normative weight across the two contexts. The question should ask students to assess whether particularism's claim that reasons are holistic and context-dependent genuinely explains this reversal in valence, or whether it merely redescribes the judgment without offering normative guidance. Crucially, students must address the challenge that if reasons shift valence so freely, moral correction and education become mysterious: on what basis can one agent correct another, and does particularism implicitly depend on a meta-level directive—such as respond to the reasons present in this situation—that functions as a hidden general principle?

Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately seven hundred to eight hundred words that takes a definite position—either defending particularism's explanatory power in this case or arguing that the scenario exposes its failure—while engaging the strongest opposing consideration. The answer should not merely describe particularism and generalism but should analyze the specific problem of normative inertia: whether moral learning requires the extraction of stable patterns across contexts, and whether particularism can accommodate such learning without collapsing into contextualism or relativism. Then supply a detailed marking scheme that distinguishes a first-class answer from an upper-second-class answer by naming the precise moves required at each level. For the highest marks, require explicit differentiation between holism of reasons and mere contextual sensitivity; explicit evaluation of the hidden-meta-principle objection; precise analytical use of the bridge scenario rather than generic illustrations; and a serious treatment of the moral-education problem. For the B-range, permit accurate exposition of the debate and identification of one or two standard objections, but deduct marks for treating holism as synonymous with it depends, for conflating particularism with moral relativism, or for failing to address whether particularism can survive without a general framework for justification.
