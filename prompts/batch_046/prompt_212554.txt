I serve on the governance board of a residential energy cooperative whose charter restricts members to drawing electricity for ordinary domestic needs. Over the past several years, members have incrementally added high-draw equipment—first electric-vehicle chargers, then server racks, and now cryptocurrency-mining rigs—each addition defended as a natural extension of household life. The board is now split between two proposals. One faction wants to impose a hard wattage cap—say, 15 kW—as the definitive boundary of “ordinary,” but opponents point out that 14.9 kW and 15.1 kW are practically indistinguishable, so the cutoff is plainly arbitrary. The other faction wants to drop fixed thresholds entirely and have the board evaluate whether a given load retains the essential domestic character of the usage, judging each case on its merits. I am convinced that both options miss what is actually wrong with the gradual transformation of our grid from a domestic service into a quasi-commercial operation, yet I cannot articulate the mistake precisely. Could you identify the underlying philosophical structure of this dispute and propose a concrete governance rule or review procedure—one that takes seriously the intuition that an incremental drift can go wrong without requiring either an obviously arbitrary line or an unreviewable appeal to subjective impression? Bottom line up front: state the core conceptual problem and your recommended framework in the first paragraph, then briefly defend it against the stronger opposing view. Keep the response to at most 300 words and no more than two paragraphs.
