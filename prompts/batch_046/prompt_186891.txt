I am a novelist who also serves as an editor for a small quarterly review that receives unsolicited fiction and critical essays, and over the past year I have encountered a growing number of submissions that display formidable surface competence—fluent prose, coherent narrative architecture, technically accurate vocabulary—while seeming to lack any underlying grasp of the subjects they treat. I need a reusable evaluation procedure, grounded in the philosophical principle that complex functional success can arise without comprehension, that I can apply in approximately thirty minutes per submission without requiring specialized philosophical training, and that I can defend to an editorial board with no background in the relevant theory.

Specifically, I want you to translate Darwin's inversion of the design argument—where adaptive complexity and apparent purpose require no comprehending designer—together with Turing's corresponding inversion of reasoning about intelligence, where behavioral adequacy is assessed independently of assumed internal mental structure, into an ordered sequence of practical tests for a literary evaluator. The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the explicit points where the procedure must halt and hand the decision back to human judgment because the issue is one of values or aesthetics rather than detectable features; and clear markers for cases the procedure simply cannot settle. Include two worked applications: first, a genuinely comprehending but formally eccentric essay that risks being misclassified because its unconventional form mimics absence of control; second, a mechanically fluent but conceptually hollow submission, where the procedure should return a rejection I will find genuinely disagreeable because the prose is technically accomplished. Please also flag the boundary conditions—such as deliberate aesthetic flatness, fragmented narration adopted as style, or a writer masking comprehension behind irony—where the framework misfires.

The procedure must respect my actual constraints: limited time, the need to justify rejections in non-philosophical language, and an honest accounting of which steps are judgment calls disguised as technical assessments. If the premise that Darwinian and Turing-style inversions can be operationalized for literary evaluation is confused, or if the task as posed collapses the distinction between epistemic comprehension and aesthetic merit in a way that cannot be repaired without destroying the purpose of editing, please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I invite direct pushback on whether this framework applies here at all, and I ask that you note explicitly where the answer takes liberties with the underlying ideas or relies on contested readings of them.
