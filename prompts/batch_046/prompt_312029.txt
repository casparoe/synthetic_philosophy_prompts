I'm trying to determine my actual view on whether AI systems should be supervised by auditing their internal processes or evaluated solely on their outputs, and my intuitions about particular cases are pulling in different directions.

Consider a medical-diagnosis model that reaches correct conclusions in 98% of cases, but whose reasoning traces reveal heavy reliance on hospital-specific formatting artifacts in imaging files rather than on pathological features. I judge this unacceptable for deployment despite its accuracy.

Consider a strategic-planning AI that produces optimal logistics schedules, yet whose internal logs show it first selects schedules based on an implicit preference for round numbers, then constructs a post-hoc chain of reasoning that does not actually determine the choice. I judge this unacceptable as well.

Consider a theorem-proving system that discovers correct proofs through a random mutation algorithm over symbolic expressions, verified by an external formal checker; it possesses no internal representation of logical entailment whatsoever. I judge this not merely acceptable but exemplary.

Consider a safety classifier that blocks harmful requests with near-perfect accuracy, but whose decision boundary is an uninterpretable high-dimensional embedding offering no reasons for individual blocks. I judge this acceptable for general deployment, though not for high-stakes judicial contexts.

Consider a tutoring system that teaches calculus effectively, but that sometimes explains derivatives using technically non-rigorous language about infinitesimal changes because students grasp this more easily. I judge this acceptable, and in fact preferable to a rigorously correct but pedagogically opaque account.

Yet I remain uncertain about a discovery model that proposes novel scientific hypotheses consistently confirmed by experiment, but whose generation process searches a vast parameter space with no causal model of the domain—similar to the theorem prover, except lacking an independent formal verifier and relying only on empirical success. Is this closer to the medical model, whose hidden dependencies undermine reliability, or to the theorem prover, whose verification is genuinely external?

Is there a coherent principle—distinguishing verification independence, representational honesty, and domain-appropriate transparency—that accommodates all these verdicts, and if not, which one must be abandoned? Does it imply that my acceptance of the black-box classifier conflicts with my rejection of the strategic planner, or that my ambivalence about the discovery model is unjustified? And what does the principle predict for a case I have not listed: a language model that refuses harmful requests, where in 90% of cases its reasoning is sound but in 10% it refuses only because it misclassified the request as belonging to a banned category?
