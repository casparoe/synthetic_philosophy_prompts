I am drafting a novel that imagines the private interior life of a public figure who died in 1958, and before committing to scenes that invent his unrecorded thoughts, I wish to understand the ethical framework governing such dramatizations. In an essay by a literary critic—whose name I have forgotten but whose argument seemed authoritative—I encountered the following summary of the philosophical consensus. First, that because death extinguishes consciousness, the deceased cannot suffer experiential harm, and therefore any talk of "harming the dead" is strictly metaphorical; ethical concern must therefore be redirected entirely to the living, specifically to survivors' emotional distress or to the corruption of the historical record. Second, that the distinction often drawn between "inventing dialogue" and "falsifying documented action" is philosophically hollow, since in either case the dead cannot correct the representation; what matters is only the plausibility of the portrait to informed readers. Third, that the obligation of fidelity, if it exists at all, is an aesthetic or craft obligation rather than a moral one—comparable to a painter's duty not to misrepresent the color of a coat in a commissioned portrait. From these premises the critic concludes that a novelist owes the dead no direct moral duty of respect, and that restrictions on biographical fiction should be evaluated solely by their effects on living audiences: whether they mislead, whether they cause grief, and whether they violate property or privacy rights held by estates.

The argument strikes me as coherent yet possibly reductive. I sense that it may conflate the absence of experiential harm with the absence of all moral wrong, that it may treat historical accuracy as a purely instrumental value, and that it may collapse important distinctions between wronging someone and harming them, or between representing a life and exploiting it. Yet I am not trained in philosophy, and I cannot articulate precisely where the error lies, or whether there is one at all. Would you examine this summary with rigor? Please proceed sentence by sentence or claim by claim, indicating which parts capture genuine positions in the ethics of representation, which are misrepresentations, conflations, or overstatements—particularly regarding whether the dead can be wronged independently of survivors' feelings, whether fidelity is merely aesthetic, and whether invented interiority and falsified documented action stand on the same ethical footing. I ask not for a bibliography, but for a precise audit: does the critic's permissive conclusion actually follow from premises that survive correction? I intend to use your assessment to guide the moral architecture of my manuscript.
