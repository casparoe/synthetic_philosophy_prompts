In a philosophy-of-religion seminar last week, a classmate ended a discussion about whether Genesis carries cosmological commitments by invoking the line that Scripture teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go. The professor treated it as decisive, but I could not see what precise hermeneutic principle was being asserted, and I want to bring a disambiguation back to the group. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, specify the interpretive rule it proposes: whether it is merely the banal claim that the Bible is not an astronomy textbook; whether it asserts that demonstrative scientific proof must always override scriptural interpretation in empirical domains; whether it rests on a specific patristic doctrine of accommodation, by which the Holy Spirit adapts language to the limited capacity of ordinary hearers; or whether it amounts to a full non-overlapping-magisteria claim that religious and scientific discourse are totally autonomous. Then indicate which of these readings Galileo Galilei actually defended in his hermeneutic writings, particularly with respect to his distinction between propositions essential for salvation and those that are not, and his insistence that reinterpretation of scripture is warranted only by demonstrative rather than probable argument. Also say which readings are anachronistic projections onto Galileo and which one is most likely being smuggled in when someone uses the line today to shut down debate. Present the readings as a menu, and for each say whether it actually resolves the cosmological question or merely relocates the problem. End with a single line beginning "Summary:" that identifies the reading closest to Galileo's actual position and the one most commonly invoked in modern arguments.
