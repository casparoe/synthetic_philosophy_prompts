In my reporting on the philosophy of science, I have been tracking debates over whether researchers can legitimately claim knowledge of high-stakes scientific propositions—such as the precise climate sensitivity parameter—while residual model uncertainty remains. At a recent interdisciplinary symposium, a speaker defended pragmatic encroachment by observing that whether a subject counts as knowing seems tied to what is practically at risk for her. The audience, composed largely of policy analysts and risk managers, immediately translated this claim into decision-theoretic terms: they treated practical stakes as quantifiable expected disutilities, and concluded that a scientist facing a billion-dollar regulatory decision should therefore run an explicit risk-weighted expected-utility calculation—balancing the cost of further testing against the harm of being wrong—before she is entitled to say she knows her result.

I am finding it hard to distinguish this interpretation from a straightforward reduction of epistemology to applied decision theory. If the presence or absence of knowledge genuinely tracks practical stakes, and those stakes are defined by the magnitude and probability of potential error-related harms, then it seems the threshold for knowing should be set precisely where the risk-weighted value of additional inquiry becomes negative. On this reading, epistemic contextualism—the idea that knowledge attributions shift with conversational context—would not involve a change in the norms governing appropriate assertion, but merely an adjustment of risk parameters across settings: the same scientist, holding identical evidence, would literally possess knowledge in a low-stakes seminar because the utility loss from error is trivial, and literally lack it in a high-stakes boardroom because the calculated risk profile crosses a quantitative boundary.

My question, then, is whether pragmatic encroachment actually commits its defenders to the view that knowing requires agents to perform explicit, quantitative risk-weighted expected-utility assessments to determine their epistemic standing. More specifically, if a climate researcher appeals to the very same evidence and reasoning in both an informal lecture and a formal regulatory hearing, does the theory imply that she genuinely knows the claim in the first context and genuinely does not know it in the second solely because the decision-theoretic stakes have changed—rather than because the conversational norms that govern when it is appropriate to offer an assurance have shifted?
