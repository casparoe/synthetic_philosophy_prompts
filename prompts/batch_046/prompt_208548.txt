In my philosophy of law seminar, a student recently shut down debate about patent reform by declaring that "ideas cannot be owned." Rather than treating this as mere rhetoric, I want my students to see how much conceptual work a single line can hide. Could you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses that slogan might express—distinguishing, for instance, the metaphysical claim that abstract objects are not the kind of thing that can stand in ownership relations; the Lockean normative claim that mental labor fails to satisfy the conditions for legitimate exclusion, especially the proviso; the economic claim that information is non-rivalrous and therefore exclusion is inefficient; and the stronger political claim that any temporary monopoly over expression violates autonomy? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, whether it actually undermines copyright or patent regimes specifically (as opposed to property in general), and which version the student was most likely invoking. Finally, is there a coherent intermediate position—say, granting temporary exclusion rights without full proprietary ownership—that survives the slogan's best interpretation?
