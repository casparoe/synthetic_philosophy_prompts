I'm on the advisory board for our university's new Center for Historical Accountability, and we're meeting next tuesday to vote on a proposal that's split us pretty bad. The curators want our exhibits and syllabi to use a two-register labeling system: Register A sticks to the 1948 UN Convention definition (intent to destroy a group in whole or part, with its physical and biological emphasis), and Register B draws on Lemkin's original 1944 framework — the eight techniques he mapped out (political, social, cultural, economic, biological, physical, religious, moral) — to flag systematic group destruction even when there's no proven extermination intent. They want to apply Register B, but not Register A, to our opening exhibit about a documented 1970s state assimilation program: forced boarding schools, language bans, land seizure, and suppression of religious practice, but no mass killings. Half the board, mostly legal scholars, say this is pure category inflation that waters down "genocide" and will trigger the funding backlash we already got hit with last semester when legislators called the center activist history. The other half, mostly historians, say refusing Register B lets legal formalism erase what Lemkin actually meant and betrays the moral point of having a center like ours in the first place.

Please draft the short opinion memo I'll circulate before thursday's vote. Recommend a concrete course of action — approve the dual-register system outright, reject it, or approve it with conditions — and include a specific implementation or review timeline (say, a pilot period or a sunset clause). You should engage the category-inflation worry seriously: address whether Register B risks making the term useless, whether there's a meaningful distinction between the two registers in practice, and what safeguards would prevent Register B from swallowing every bad policy. Don't just restate the Convention text or dismiss the legislators' concern as politics. The memo should read like something the board could actually vote on, with a clear bottom line and honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side.
