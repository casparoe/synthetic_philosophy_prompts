You are a professional philosopher with deep familiarity with existentialist ethics, normative decision theory, and clinical bioethics, and you are being asked to design an analytical tool rather than summarize literature. Before you construct anything, think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming: sketch two or three realistic hospital or institutional settings in which an unconditional, singular commitment might collide with a universal ethical protocol, then select the strongest and justify your choice in one sentence.

Task: Build a one-paragraph thought experiment set in a realistic clinical-research environment—no science fiction, no trolley-style abstractions, no demons or perfect predictors, and no appeals to divine voices or explicit religious commands. The protagonist must be a physician who is also the parent of a child with a rapidly fatal, well-characterized condition. The scenario must engineer a direct conflict between a standard, non-derogable universal ethical duty—specifically, the prohibition on performing an experimental, non-therapeutic intervention that uses biological material or data from a vulnerable, non-consenting patient—and the physician-parent's absolute, singular, and non-transferable commitment to saving this particular child.

Design the case so that it generates the following three verdicts, then demonstrate explicitly that it does so:

1. From a Kierkegaardian perspective on the teleological suspension of the ethical: the universal duty is suspended for the sake of an absolute relation to the singular, here secularized as an unconditional parental commitment that cannot be generalized without collapsing into mere preference.

2. From a Kantian deontological perspective: the act is impermissible, both because the maxim fails universalizability and because the vulnerable patient is treated merely as a means.

3. From a secular principlist or discourse-ethics bioethical framework: there is no legitimate private exception; institutional protocol binds regardless of personal attachment.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the act, the agent's reasoning, and the relevant normative counterfactuals—specifically, the world in which the agent's reasoning is generalized to any physician-parent—showing why each framework delivers its verdict. Then identify the precise feature doing the work: the singularity and non-universalizability of the commitment. Explain honestly what happens if that feature is removed, and anticipate the strongest attack a defender of the view that fares worst in your case (the Kierkegaardian position) would mount, followed by a brief reply on that defender's behalf.

Constraints: include, as a structural foil within the scenario, the agent's moment of infinite resignation—accepting the child's death in full ethical compliance—before the move to singular suspension, so that the distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith is visible without being named as such. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not use verbatim quotations from any published text; paraphrase without quotation marks if you reference specific ideas. The goal is to generate the verdicts from the theories, not to stipulate them—if the case simply assumes its conclusion, it fails. Write your entire response in American English.
