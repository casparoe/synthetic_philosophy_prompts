I'm trying to work out what principle actually drives my intuitions about cause prioritisation, going by my gut on concrete allocations rather than on any explicit formula. Funding bed-net distribution in high-burden regions feels like an unambiguous top priority: severe suffering, cheap and well-tested intervention, and a space that is not yet saturated. Funding pure mathematics research aimed at resolving long-standing conjectures feels like a poor philanthropic priority: the intellectual importance might be immense, but tractability for an outside donor is effectively nil, and the neglect is not of the kind that responds to extra capital. Funding advocacy for universal primary education in a wealthy nation where enrolment is already near-complete strikes me as wasteful: tractability is high, but importance is low because the problem is largely solved, regardless of how visible the cause is. Funding ecosystem-level interventions to reduce wild-animal suffering—say, altering predator-prey dynamics at scale—draws me in because the affected population is astronomical and neglect is total, yet I hesitate and ultimately rank it below the bed nets, since tractability is so poor that I suspect resources would dissipate. Funding AI alignment research I rate highly despite genuinely poor tractability, because the importance feels existential; but funding a speculative programme to design interstellar generation ships I rate low, even though the long-term importance is similarly vast and tractability is comparably poor. Here is the pair that keeps undermining my confidence: distributing vitamin A supplements in South Asia is clearly a strong bet—moderate importance, high tractability, moderate neglect—whereas preventing human extinction through near-term asteroid-deflection preparedness has far greater importance and comparable neglect, but tractability seems low. I consistently favour the supplements, which suggests I apply a tractability threshold; yet I simultaneously want to fund AI alignment despite its poor tractability, which seems to violate that same threshold. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a multiplicative score with a lexical floor, a two-stage filter, or a weighted product that treats proximity and reversibility differently—that captures all of these verdicts? Which of my judgments, particularly the one about wild-animal suffering or the one about generation ships, must be sacrificed to keep the principle parsimonious, and what does the best surviving principle predict about a cause that is perfectly tractable, monumentally important, but already funded to saturation?
