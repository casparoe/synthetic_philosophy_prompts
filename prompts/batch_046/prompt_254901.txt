I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology writing a dissertation chapter on the units of selection debate, and I need a conceptual taxonomy—not a literature review, but a diagnostic instrument that sorts actual theoretical commitments rather than labels people as "gene selectionists" or "group selectionists." Please construct a decision tree (or flowchart-style taxonomy) that moves from concrete answers about evolutionary explanation to named positions, with each branch node phrased as a question a graduate student in evolutionary theory could answer without knowing in advance which interpretation it leads to.

The tree should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a roster of famous theorists. Use three diagnostic axes that genuinely split the field: first, whether evolutionary causation at a given level requires an actual causal bottleneck (e.g., suppression of lower-level replication) or whether statistical covariance between a trait and fitness across levels is sufficient; second, whether evolutionary transitions in individuality—such as the origin of multicellularity or obligate eusociality—are best explained by the suppression of lower-level competition or by the emergence of a new functional integration that renders the old level causally inert; and third, whether the replicator/interactor distinction marks a difference of kind or merely of degree, with corresponding implications for whether genes, organisms, and groups can all simultaneously be "units" in different explanatory contexts. For each combination of answers, locate the position that occupies it—strict gene-selectionism, organism-centered Darwinism, multilevel/pluralist selection, contextualist pluralism, or a developmental-systems-adjacent view—labeling the leaf. Where a combination of answers yields no well-defended position, flag it as unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the gap reflects incoherence, theoretical unattractiveness, or mere historical neglect. In particular, the tree must be able to separate gene-selectionists who rely on causal bottlenecks from those who rely purely on statistical covariance; if your tree genuinely cannot separate these two sub-positions, flag the pair explicitly rather than collapsing them into one leaf.

Every node should be anchored in a concrete case or claim—for instance, asking whether Hamilton's rule describes a causal mechanism operating at the kin-group level or a statistical regularity that can be redescribed at the gene level—rather than asking the respondent to self-identify with a named school. The audience is biology PhD students who understand Hamilton's rule, evolutionary transitions, and the replicator concept, but who have not specialized in philosophy of science. The final tree should have no more than seven terminal leaves, should not ask for bibliographies or reading lists, and should not invoke obscure formalisms that depend on access to specific recent papers. Please also note which distinctions matter most for explaining the origin of new levels of individuality, since that is the purpose for which I am constructing this instrument.
