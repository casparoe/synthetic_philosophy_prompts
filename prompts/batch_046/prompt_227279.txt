I'm trying to figure out why some sad art hits right and other stuff just bums me out, going by my gut. Watching a movie where a made-up character dies slowly of cancer: thats the good kind of sad, I cry and feel wierdly uplifted after. Listening to a folk song about an actual mining disaster that killed real people back in the day: not fine, feels like voyeurism even though the melody is gorgeous. Reading a novel about two fictional people breaking up over nothing dramatic: totally fine, I love that kind of melancholy. Watching a documentary with real kids suffering right now: just depressing, zero enjoyment, I turn it off. Seeing an opera where everyone dies in these over-the-top staged ways: great, I love the sadness there, it feels almost joyful.

But then there's this video game where the story forces my character to suffer and theres literally no choice to avoid it: that feels awful, not cathartic, even though its clearly fiction and I know nobody real is hurt. So I had this tentative idea about "aesthetic quarantine" - like the suffering is boxed off so it can't spill into real moral obligations. That gets the documentary out (real obligations exist) and maybe the mining song (real dead people, real history). But then why is the video game bad? Its fictional, boxed off, no real obligations. And why is the opera good when its so exaggerated? Maybe the principle needs something about agency or inevitability inside the frame?

What principle actually captures all of these, or close? I keep going back and forth on this one case: a real person writes a beautiful memoir about losing their child, its art, its not asking me to donate or fix anything, but its also real suffering by real people. Is that in or out?

Please answer in American English, take a real committed stance - don't just list Aristotle and some psychologist and leave me hanging. Pick a principle, defend it, tell me which of my gut calls has to be wrong if we go with it, and be honest about which ones it still can't save.
