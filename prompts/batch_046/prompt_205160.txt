Below are notes for a short essay arguing that philosophy makes a distinctive kind of progress—what I call disagreement crystallisation—rather than accumulating settled truths in the manner of empirical science. The core claim is that philosophical advancement is best measured by the sharpening of conceptual joints within long-standing disputes, so that opposing positions become more precisely incompatible and more narrowly targeted. For illustration, I contrast the early modern framing of the mind-body problem (ghostly interaction versus mechanical causation) with its contemporary form (phenomenal concepts versus physicalist explanation), and I note a parallel shift in free-will debates from a broad libertarian-determinist opposition to finer-grained distinctions among compatibilist, sourcehood, and leeway conditions. I also consider an objection: if crystallisation only produces more subtle disagreement without convergence, it risks being mere taxonomical refinement rather than genuine cognitive progress. I tentatively reply that sharper incompatibility improves the quality of rational choice among positions, even when selection remains unresolved.

Please comment on these notes. In particular: (1) Are the historical shifts I sketch broadly accurate, or do they misrepresent the trajectory of those debates? (2) Is disagreement crystallisation a coherent and useful metric, or does it conflate conceptual refinement with progress? (3) Does my reply to the taxonomical-refinement objection succeed, or does it concede too much? (4) Are there stronger examples or counter-examples I should consider? Please provide your comments in British English, and avoid offering bibliographies or citation lists—focus on conceptual assessment and suggestions for improvement.
