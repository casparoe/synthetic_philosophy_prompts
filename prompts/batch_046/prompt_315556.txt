Here is an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than the familiar skeptical worry: it seems to show that normative reasoning is impossible in principle, not merely fallible. I want to know exactly which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, especially since I would rather keep the idea that normative reasoning is genuinely possible.

(1) Call a rule R modally saturated for an agent A just in case A's dispositions determine correct versus incorrect applications of R across every possible case in R's domain, not merely the ones A has actually encountered.

(2) Any human agent has only finitely many actual dispositions, covering only a negligible fragment of the domain of any interesting rule—for example, addition for arbitrarily large numbers, or the prohibition on cruelty for technologically novel forms of harm such as direct brain-interface suffering.

(3) So no human agent can satisfy modal saturation for any non-trivial rule.

(4) If an agent fails modal saturation, then for any rule R she seems to follow there exists an incompatible rule R* that matches all her actual dispositions but diverges in the unsaturated regions; hence there is no fact of the matter which rule she follows.

(5) This applies equally to normative rules: there is no fact about whether a principle like "do not torture" applies to a brain-interface harm the agent has never considered.

(6) But if there is never a fact about which normative rule applies in novel cases, then normative reasoning is impossible in principle, not merely uncertain or difficult.

(7) Normative reasoning is clearly possible.

(8) Therefore the modal saturation requirement in (1) must be false.

I feel the slide between (4) and (6) might hide an equivocation between metaphysical indeterminacy and practical unavailability, or perhaps a scope shift from "this agent" to "all agents," but I cannot name it precisely. Which premise should I give up? Explain why it looked plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does the skeptical response say about the rejected step, what does the straight (dispositionalist-idealist) response say, and what does the primitivist about normative content say? I am especially interested in whether keeping (1) forces me to abandon (7) in a way that is genuinely damaging, or whether there is a subtler reading of (4) that blocks the expansion to normative rules without touching (1).

Please end your response with a line beginning Verdict: followed by the number of the premise you reject, then a one-sentence summary of the central cost of that rejection.
