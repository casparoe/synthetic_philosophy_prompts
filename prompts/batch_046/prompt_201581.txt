I have been trying to understand whether moral particularism or generalism offers the better account of moral learning. Consider someone who comes to see that being overly efficient can be disrespectful—not because efficiency is bad in general, but because in this situation it silences a person who needs to be heard. Has the learner acquired a new exception to a general principle, or has their perceptual sensitivity to disrespect become more finely tuned? Apply this comparison to a case I am puzzling over: a therapist decides not to correct a client's false but harmless belief about their past, because doing so would destroy the client's sense of identity and cause severe psychological harm. Here the relevant features—truth, autonomy, and harm—seem to interact in a way that does not match standard cases. How would particularism and generalism each analyze the therapist's reasoning? Which view better captures why this judgment feels like a genuine moral assessment rather than an ad hoc exception, and can particularism avoid sliding into complete arbitrariness without secretly relying on generalist structure?
