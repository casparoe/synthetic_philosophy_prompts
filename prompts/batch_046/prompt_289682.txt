I am drafting lecture notes on the ethics of strategic voting and democratic obligation, and in both student papers and public debate I keep encountering the same compressed objection to voting for a "lesser evil": the line that "voting for the lesser evil is still choosing evil." The context is usually a discussion of whether citizens in non-competitive districts, or those facing severely flawed major-party candidates, have a duty to participate instrumentally, or whether expressive purity should override outcome-oriented reasoning. I have been trying to map the precise philosophical work this sentence is being asked to do—whether it is meant as a tautology, a claim about moral complicity in collective harm, a thesis about the corruption of one's expressive will, or something closer to the idea that democratic legitimacy requires unconditional endorsement of available options.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be advancing, treating them as a disambiguation menu rather than selecting a single correct reading? For each reading, specify whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible on standard accounts of collective action and expressive value, and—crucially—whether it actually supports the refusal to vote for the lesser evil in the situations where people invoke it. In particular, distinguish versions that depend on collapsing the difference between causal contribution and symbolic alignment, and flag which interpretations survive once we separate instrumental efficacy from expressive meaning. I want to be able to present this taxonomy in seminar and ask which version a particular speaker intended, so please include the borderline cases where the slogan sounds compelling but does not entail the conclusion being drawn.
