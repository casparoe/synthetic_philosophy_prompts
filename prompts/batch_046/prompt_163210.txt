As a journalist covering law and moral philosophy, I am trying to grasp the conceptual fallout from a specific tension in how we classify mass atrocity. Lemkin's original conception of genocide encompassed the destruction of collective ways of life—cultural, social, and political—without requiring mass killing, but the UN Convention that eventually codified the term restricted the legal category to acts committed with specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, protected groups defined by nationality, ethnicity, race, or religion, excluding both political groups and purely cultural destruction. If philosophers take this legal narrowing seriously—not merely as a jurisdictional compromise but as a moral taxonomy that determines which victims trigger obligations of prevention and which remain in adjacent categories—what are the most important implications for evaluating state violence that is deliberately calibrated to fall just outside the specific-intent threshold? I am especially interested in how this architecture shapes our concepts of complicity, the ethics of public naming, and the limits of international moral responsibility, rather than in a historical chronology of the drafting process.
