I'm working through a philosophical problem that arises from what researchers sometimes call the gender-equality paradox: in societies that have achieved high levels of formal gender equality, robust welfare provision, and strong economic security, gender differences in occupational choice—for instance, in engineering versus nursing, or in physical sciences versus education—often become larger rather than smaller. I discussed this recently with colleagues and found that the standard explanation, which attributes gendered career patterns primarily to oppressive socialization that egalitarian policies ought to suppress, seems to miss something important about how preferences behave when structural coercion is substantially reduced.

Please argue for the following position. When material necessity and rigid social expectation are greatly diminished, individuals do not converge on gender-neutral career distributions; instead, they exhibit what we might term "high-optionality preference crystallization," in which more stable, internally differentiated preference structures become visible and shape choices with greater force. From this, defend the claim that liberal egalitarian justice should abandon occupational gender parity as a benchmark of fairness and should instead evaluate justice solely by the quality of the choice conditions—absence of direct discrimination, adequate information, and freedom from domination—regardless of whether the resulting distributions remain highly gendered. You should explicitly address the objection that this view risks naturalizing gender differences without establishing biological causation, and you should explain why large gender gaps in high-optionality contexts may indicate not hidden structural bias but rather the removal of earlier coercive suppression of revealed preferences. Structure your response as a direct philosophical argument with explicit premises, not as a survey of empirical literature. Please write your answer in British English, using British spelling and grammatical conventions.
