A graduate student in my seminar offered the following characterization of the transformative-experience argument: for ontologically transformative choices—those that alter not merely your circumstances but the evaluative framework through which you assess well-being—rational deliberation fails not because of ordinary informational poverty, but because the metrics of comparison are indexed to a pre-transformative personal-identity stage that will not survive the transition. Standard decision-theoretic frameworks therefore commit a category error when applied here, since they presuppose a diachronically stable evaluator who can rank outcomes using preference orderings that remain constant across the change. The view proposes, in place of expected-utility reasoning, a form of revelatory reasoning in which the value of the option consists in its capacity to disclose an entirely new axiological structure that the prior self is constitutionally unable to represent or weigh. From this it is said to follow that comparative questions—whether, say, becoming a parent is worth the costs—are not merely difficult but malformed, because there exists no common measure between the values proper to the pre-parental and parental stages of the same life. The student reads all of this as entailing a metaphysical thesis about value incommensurability across personal-identity boundaries, and takes the argument to show that decision theory should be abandoned, not merely restricted, in these domains.

This strikes me as capturing something genuinely important about the limits of first-person prediction and the transformation of subjective values, but I suspect it overstates the anti-decision-theoretic commitment and slides an epistemic limitation into a metaphysical claim about discontinuity. I would like a precise audit. Please distinguish the parts that accurately reflect the view’s claims about predictive failure and the transformation of subjective experience; identify where the summary conflates an inability to represent future values with an absence of common measure or with a breakdown in personal identity; assess whether “revelatory reasoning” is best understood as replacing decision-theoretic frameworks altogether, or rather as operating within a restricted domain where predictive reasoning is acknowledged to fail; and determine whether the conclusion that comparative evaluation is “malformed” actually follows from the premises, or whether it depends on an unstated premise about evaluator discontinuity that the original argument does not endorse. In each case, note what a defender of the view would object to, and whether the student’s inference survives the correction.
