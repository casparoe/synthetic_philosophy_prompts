Consider a philosopher who defends a lexical-threshold form of negative utilitarianism and who has rendered explicit comparative judgments about the following four hypothetical worlds. In World A, a single individual endures unremitting, catastrophic suffering—an intensity and quality of anguish that makes continued life appear literally unendurable—while ten billion others live lives of near-perfect bliss. The philosopher judges World A to be morally unacceptable, holding that no quantity of bliss can justify or compensate for that one suffering. In World B, one hundred individuals endure severe but clearly sub-catastrophic suffering—chronic, debilitating, yet interspersed with relief—while one billion live in robust, sustained happiness. The philosopher judges World B acceptable, maintaining that the aggregate pleasure genuinely outweighs the suffering. In World C, ten billion people experience mild, stable contentment barely above neutrality, while exactly one person suffers at an intensity just below the catastrophic boundary—prolonged, acute anguish that stops short of being literally unbearable—for the duration of a normal lifespan. The philosopher rejects World C, insisting that this near-catastrophic suffering cannot be redeemed by billions of mild pleasures. In World D, that same near-catastrophic intensity is distributed across ten million people, each experiencing only a brief, intense spike of anguish once per year, with the remainder of their lives being highly pleasant; the philosopher accepts World D.

Now consider World E: a single individual will experience suffering at that same near-catastrophic intensity (just below the catastrophic boundary) for exactly one continuous minute, followed by a lifetime of perfect bliss, while ten billion others live entirely free of suffering in perfect bliss.

Predict the philosopher’s verdict on World E—acceptable or unacceptable. I am less interested in the bare answer than in the structure of the reasoning behind it. Identify at least two distinct principles that jointly accommodate the four earlier verdicts, state what each principle predicts for World E, and offer your best guess as to which principle the philosopher is actually running, with reasons. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different in kind from World E—that would best discriminate between the principles you have identified.
