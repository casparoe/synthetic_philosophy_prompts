At a university research-ethics committee, two philosophers debated whether a proposed citizens’ deliberative forum constitutes legitimate persuasion or covert manipulation. (1) Dr. Elena Voss argued that it is manipulation: the forum employs curated information sequencing, affective priming, and social-proof cues to steer participants toward a predetermined policy consensus, all whilst presenting itself as neutral deliberation. (2) Professor James Okonkwo replied that every forum curates information—the chair selects speakers, frames questions, and sets temporal limits—so neutrality is impossible; what distinguishes legitimate persuasion is whether the steering aligns with values the participants already hold upon reflection. (3) Voss countered that a principled boundary exists: legitimate persuasion offers reasons the recipient can endorse after reflection, whereas manipulation operates through mechanisms the recipient would reject if made explicit, such as exploiting loss aversion without disclosure. (4) Okonkwo responded that this ‘endorsement upon reflection’ criterion collapses in practice, since people frequently reject the very framing that made understanding possible; requiring explicit endorsement of every influential mechanism would prohibit not only propaganda but also education, rhetoric, and Socratic inquiry. They asked the chair to adjudicate, and she confessed she could not. Please evaluate each of the four moves in turn, rating them from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification; then state who is more right overall, and identify the important consideration that both parties overlook in drawing the boundary between manipulation and persuasion.
