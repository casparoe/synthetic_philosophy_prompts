Next Thursday I present at my school's interdisciplinary seminar. I will defend the thesis that Whitehead's three-stage rhythm of education—romance, precision, and generalization—should be interpreted as an epistemic structure parallel to Peirce's abductive, deductive, and inductive phases, and that this reading entails that any secondary curriculum lacking a protected, assessment-free romance period of cross-disciplinary inquiry before age sixteen violates Whitehead's normative view that education must be an adventure of living ideas rather than the accumulation of inert information. The audience includes my headmaster, who will defend standardized metrics as necessary for equity; Dr. Elena Vasquez, a cognitive scientist who studies adolescent plasticity and will likely argue that unstructured exploration is neurologically inefficient; and two fellow students familiar with Whitehead's essays on education who treat his rhythm as historical description rather than binding prescription. Please prepare me for the objections in the order they are likely to come, phrased as each listener would phrase them, indicating what I should concede, what I should hold, and—crucially—the weakest point in my argument that the romance stage requires institutional protection from assessment, which I must defend rather than hope they overlook.
