Since I began serving on the technical advisory board for a research consortium exploring formal guarantees for self-modifying reasoning systems, the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents has shifted from an abstract curiosity to a practical design constraint. I am writing the final essay question for my graduate seminar in formal epistemology and rational agency, and I need a prompt that cannot be answered by reciting the standard modal-logic derivation of Löb’s theorem. Instead, the question should require students to build the obstacle within an explicitly defined framework: an agent with a belief operator B satisfying distribution (K) and positive introspection (4), equipped with a delegation relation to a successor agent that revises the original belief set. Students should be asked to derive the collapse condition that prevents the original agent from consistently holding a principle of rational self-delegation—specifically, that if the successor believes p, then p—while maintaining consistency. They must then apply this result to evaluate whether abandoning (4) in favor of a weak introspection axiom or adopting a non-normal belief-revision policy genuinely dissolves the obstacle, or merely relocates it into the meta-level. Crucially, they must distinguish the Löbian obstacle precisely from Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem and from the preface paradox, explaining at the level of rational belief (not formal provability) why those analogies mislead. Please provide the exam question, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by identifying the specific moves required: for an A, an explicit formal derivation of the collapse, a correct application to the delegation scenario, a precise distinction from both Gödel and the preface paradox with reasons, and a critical evaluation of at least one escape route that addresses whether consistency is preserved or lost; for a B, correct identification of the obstacle and a general account of self-trust failure that lacks either the formal construction, the delegation application, or the critical evaluation of an escape route. The exam is for an upper-level graduate seminar; students have 90 minutes and should aim for about 800 words.
