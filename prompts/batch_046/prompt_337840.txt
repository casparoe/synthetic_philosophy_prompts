I run a little discussion group for literature students who think philosophy is just opinions about opinions. Next session I want to tackle whether you can actually learn that a painting or song is good just from someone telling you — aesthetic testimony, I think it's called. But I don't want the usual "Kant said no then people argued" thing; I want to build it around a weird pair of examples. One is a natural wine that literally tastes different to every drinker because it keeps fermenting in the bottle after you open it, so a critic's tasting note is about an object you'll never actually encounter. The other is a movie that exists in a theatrical cut and a streaming cut with different endings, so the reviewer is describing something you literally can't watch on your laptop. I want the students to see that testimony might fail not because taste is "just subjective," but because the thing being testified about isn't even stable across observers.

So I'm asking for a teaching plan, not the full lecture. What's the order the pieces have to go in — do I need to establish that testimony usually works for other domains first, or should I start with the instability problem? What's the one concrete scenario — maybe one of those two, maybe a different one — that should carry the whole idea, and what properties must it have and what should it lack? What misconceptions will they bring — probably that "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" kills the whole idea, or that critics are just experts like doctors — and at what point in the sequence should I head each one off? What's a quick check I can do to see if they've grasped the dependency between the object being the same and the testimony being useful? And what should I openly refuse to explain — maybe formal epistemology of trust, or specific philosophers' names — so I don't confuse them?

Audience is maybe eight smart first-years, forty minutes, no prior philosophy except maybe they heard "subjective" means "not real". I have a whiteboard but no slides.
