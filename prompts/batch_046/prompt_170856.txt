I've been thinking about this weird museum cataloging rule I ran into while arguing with a friend about wheter a restored statue is still the same statue. A bunch of European museums apparently have this internal guideline — not always public — where if conservators replace more than about sixty percent of a sculpture's original bronze or marble with synthetic fill or 3D-printed replicas, the object gets moved in the database from "Original Work" to "Derivative Conservation Object"... but ONLY if the replacement happened without the living artist's explicit sign-off. If the artist authorized the swap, even ninety percent replacement keeps the original catalog entry and the original date. What's wild is that this applys even when the internal armature — the old clay or wax core — is still physically inside, untouched.

What theory of material constitution and artistic identity does that practice actually presuppose? Is it assuming some kind of essentialist view where the artist's mental state or authorization fixes identity across material change, like a hylomorphic lock? Or is it more like a conventionalist tracking rule that just happens to use material percentage as a heuristic, and the sixty percent cutoff is basically arbitrary? I want you to reconstruct the implicit metaphysics here — does the practice need a specific answer to the statue-and-clay problem, or is it underdetermined between, say, a constitution view and a four-dimensionalist view with artist-intention as the unifying relation? And tell me what a cataloging system built on pure material continuity (ignoring authorization) would have to do differently — would it have to deny that the replaced-object is ever the same work, or could it keep identity through the untouched core?

Also, I'm genuinely curious if this is consistent with how the same institutions treat paintings where the canvas is replaced but the paint layer is original — they usually don't downgrade those. So is there a hidden commitment about three-dimensional vs two-dimensional media, or about the visibility of replacement? Don't just list theories; tell me which ones the practice rules out and whether it's philosophically coherent or just a bureaucratic kludge that happens to sit near one particular view.
