I am a doctoral student in political philosophy presenting a work-in-progress paper at our department seminar. My thesis is that Philip Pettit's republican account of liberty as non-domination, while conceptually distinct from Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty, remains incomplete because it treats domination primarily as a visible, interpersonal relation of arbitrary power; I argue that a significant class of modern unfreedoms—what I term "opacity domination"—arises when institutional power is structured so that its arbitrary potential is systematically concealed from those subject to it, for instance through black-box algorithmic management or contractual obfuscation, and that recognizing this requires supplementing republican theory with an epistemic-transparency condition that pure negative-liberty theorists will reject as moralistic overreach and that republican scholars may see as conceptual stretching. The audience is precisely composed of: two senior faculty committed to Berlin-style negative liberty who regard any metric beyond direct interference as normative overreach; one prominent republican scholar who defends democratic non-domination and is deeply skeptical of expanding the concept; two liberal-neutrality graduate students who worry that transparency conditions violate institutional or employer neutrality; and one political economist who regards liberty-talk as largely irrelevant to structural analysis.
