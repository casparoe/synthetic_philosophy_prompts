Explain the conceptual tension among Nietzsche's three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—when they are applied simultaneously to the construction of collective identity. Specifically, show how monumental history requires selecting and simplifying past events into exemplary narratives that can inspire present action; how antiquarian history demands preserving every detail and continuity regardless of present utility; and how critical history requires dissolving precisely those narratives and continuities that the other two modes depend on. Then address the deeper problem: if critical history is valuable only insofar as it serves life by destroying dead traditions, but its destructive force also undermines the illusions and simplifying narratives that monumental history provides and that life apparently needs for motivation, then life seems to require both the preservation and the destruction of its own historical self-image. Is this a genuine contradiction in Nietzsche's view, or is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between the object-level narratives and the meta-level awareness of their constructedness—that allows all three modes to serve life without mutual annihilation? Keep the answer focused on the conceptual structure rather than on historical illustrations or biographical context.
