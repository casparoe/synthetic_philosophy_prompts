I'm taking a course on moral status and gradated sentience, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't place. A debater will concede that an entity—say, a bumblebee or a simple artificial system—might possess some nonzero, quantifiable degree of sentience or welfare-relevant capacity, perhaps five percent of a human's, but then insist that this grants it literally zero direct moral weight, because moral standing is an all-or-nothing property. At the same time, that same debater accepts continuous gradations inside the category of full moral patients: they agree we should prioritize treating a human with severe pain over one with mild discomfort, weighting resources by intensity of suffering rather than treating every human case as equally urgent. So they apply a proportional, scalar calculus within the class of beings that clear the threshold, yet treat the threshold itself as a discontinuity that wipes out all weight for anything below it. I've seen versions of this in discussions of insect welfare, where people say "maybe they feel a little, but not enough to matter," and in AI ethics, where a system might be acknowledged to have "something like" preference satisfaction but denied any claim on resources. Does this specific configuration—continuous moral weighting inside a category combined with an absolute exclusionary boundary at the category's edge—have an established name? How does it differ from simple threshold deontology, from speciesism, and from the "marginal cases" argument? I'm not looking for a bare label; I want to know whether philosophers have carved out this exact pattern, what its nearest neighbors are, and where its boundaries lie—particularly whether it collapses into incoherence once the internal weighting principle is taken seriously.
