I am preparing a postgraduate seminar in the philosophy of social science on whether macro-historical methods can ever genuinely adjudicate between institutional, geographical, and cultural explanations of national prosperity. For the opening session, I should like you to write a joke—delivered in polished British English—that turns on a substantive conceptual difficulty in this domain rather than on a generic statistical cliché. Specifically, the humour should arise from the structural impossibility of isolating institutional causation: for instance, the circularity of using present wealth to validate retrospective institutional ratings, or the logical incoherence of asking what a given territory’s economic trajectory would have been had it retained its geography but acquired an entirely alien institutional framework. Please construct a brief narrative—perhaps involving an overconfident counterfactual historian or a natural experiment that collapses upon inspection—in which the punchline depends upon recognising why causal inference from history is necessarily limited here. The joke ought to make sense to someone acquainted with these methodological debates, but it must not invoke named scholars, empirical case studies, or requests for further reading.
