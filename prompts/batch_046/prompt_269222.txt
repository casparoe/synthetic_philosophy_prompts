As a novelist currently drafting a manuscript about a reclusive marine taxonomist, I have spent months immersed in debates over the species problem, and I have become preoccupied with an argumentative pattern I observe repeatedly—one that seems distinct from the familiar conflicts among the biological, morphological, and phylogenetic species concepts, yet for which I lack the proper philosophical vocabulary. The pattern concerns not which concept is adopted, but how the evidentiary weight of different criteria shifts retroactively to match an implicit taxonomic judgment. Consider two cases I encountered in my research: first, two intertidal gastropod populations separated by a sharp thermal gradient, which interbreed rarely where the gradient blurs, yet are defended as separate species on the grounds of ecological niche divergence, with the occasional hybrids dismissed as insignificant noise; second, two deep-sea bivalve lineages that share an identical symbiotic bacterium and nearly indistinguishable morphology, but exhibit slight genetic divergence with no observed gene flow, which are defended as conspecific on the grounds that reproductive isolation remains unproven, with the genetic divergence dismissed as intraspecific polymorphism. In both instances, the criterion that supports separation—ecological divergence in the first case, reproductive isolation in the second—is elevated as decisive, while the criterion that undermines separation is downgraded, and this calibration is presented as if it had been obvious from the outset rather than adjusted to preserve the prior intuition about lumping or splitting. Is there an established term in philosophy of biology for this specific form of criterion-weight inversion in species delimitation? If the literature offers no precise label, I would ask you to coin one, providing a rigorous definition together with boundary conditions that clearly distinguish it from ordinary taxonomic disagreement, from the well-known incommensurability of species concepts themselves, and from mere confirmation bias.
