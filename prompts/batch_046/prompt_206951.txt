You are a senior philosopher of technology with expertise in AI ethics and conceptual analysis. Before you begin, spend a few minutes brainstorming: list the main ways the "alignment problem" has been characterized in both technical and philosophical literature over the past decade, and note where those characterizations seem to be talking about different phenomena rather than the same one.

Then answer the following in a committed, non-equivocal way.

A persistent stall has emerged in debates over whether AI alignment is one problem or several. One side—call them the specification-fidelity theorists—treats alignment as a single, unified challenge of ensuring that an artificial system's behavior tracks the designer's intended objective function, where the core difficulties are formal: robustness under distribution shift, scalable oversight, and the mathematical representation of human preferences. The other side—call them the constitutive pluralists—contends that this framing illegitimately collapses three distinct problem-types into one heading: (a) the engineering problem of scalable oversight and reward specification; (b) the normative problem of value pluralism, incommensurability, and the impossibility of a single "human preference" function; and (c) the metaphysical-causal problem of whether a system with autonomous causal-ontological dynamics can even be said to possess an "objective function" in the sense required for alignment talk to apply. The pluralists observe that fidelity theorists routinely propose formal solutions—such as inverse reinforcement learning, constitutional AI architectures, or preference-aggregation mechanisms—that address (a) while silently assuming answers to (b) and (c). Meanwhile, the fidelity theorists reply that the pluralists are changing the subject: formal alignment tools are designed precisely for engineered artifacts, not for metaphysically autonomous agents, and normative disagreement about values is a separate political question, not part of alignment proper.

Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal (a dispute about the word "alignment"), substantive (a disagreement about the nature of AI systems and values), or a case of different questions being pursued under one name? In your diagnosis, refer specifically to: (1) the fidelity camp's reliance on a principal-agent model and the exact point at which that model becomes a liability rather than a resource; (2) whether the pluralists' rejection of that model is empirical (concerning what advanced AI systems are actually like), conceptual (concerning the conditions for applying "objective function" and "intention"), or normative (concerning what kind of relation we ought to seek with intelligent artifacts); and (3) whether there is a hidden formal or empirical question—perhaps about the conditions under which a system's behavior is best explained by an assigned utility function versus by emergent causal dynamics—that is doing the real work.

Finally, take a definite position: is this stall resolvable? If yes, specify the precise form of progress required—conceptual clarification, a formal result, an empirical finding about AI architectures, or a reframing of the field's taxonomy—and explain why that progress would dissolve the disagreement rather than merely relocate it. If no, argue precisely why the stall is permanent: is the concept of "alignment" irreducibly ambiguous, are the underlying value commitments incommensurable, or are the sides answering questions that belong to different disciplines? And state what practical or disciplinary consequence should follow—whether we should split the research program, abandon the umbrella term, or maintain the disagreement as a productive tension.
