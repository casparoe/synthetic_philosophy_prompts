I am preparing a take-home examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility and social practice. Please draft one essay question that students could answer in approximately 90 minutes, with an expected response length of about 1000 words.

The question should investigate whether an agent retains standing to blame others for wrongs that are structurally analogous to wrongs the agent produces through institutional roles, even when the agent personally disavows those roles. It must not be answerable by merely summarizing familiar accounts of hypocrisy—such as the claim that hypocrites forfeit standing because they violate norms they invoke, or that blame presupposes a moral authority the hypocrite lacks. Instead, the question should present the following crux: an agent occupies a bureaucratic role that causally contributes to a diffuse, systemic harm H (for instance, environmental degradation) in a manner mediated by rules and division of labor. The agent actively works within the institution to reform it and personally rejects its harmful outputs, yet does not exit the role. Meanwhile, the agent blames a private individual for directly and intentionally producing a token instance of H.

The exam question must require students to evaluate whether this agent retains standing to blame the private wrongdoer, and it should compel them to articulate and defend one of three specific positions: (1) that institutional complicity of any kind undermines standing regardless of internal disavowal or reformist intent; (2) that standing is preserved precisely because the agent's role is oriented toward institutional reform, which distinguishes her moral posture from that of the direct wrongdoer; or (3) an intermediate view that conditions standing on either the agent's comparative causal efficacy relative to the system, or on whether the blame is articulated in a first-personal register (as an acknowledgment of shared fallibility) versus a third-personal register (as a detached accusation).

Please provide: (a) the exam question itself, clearly stating the scenario and the task; (b) a model answer of approximately 800 words that reaches and justifies a specific verdict, explicitly addressing all three positions and explaining why the first-personal/third-personal distinction matters for that verdict; and (c) a marking scheme totaling 20 points, specifying the point value for each of five criteria, describing what an A-range answer (18–20) must contain for each criterion, describing the corresponding deficiency that typically earns a B-range answer (14–17), and noting the common error that costs marks. The five criteria are: framing the structural-analogy problem without collapsing institutional complicity into direct intentional wrongdoing (4 points); distinguishing the three positions with conceptual precision rather than mere labeling (5 points); defending a reasoned verdict that engages the register distinction (5 points); identifying and responding to the strongest objection to the chosen view (3 points); and analytical clarity and coherence (3 points).

The prompt should be formal and polished. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Frame all theoretical distinctions within the prompt rather than invoking obscure named doctrines.
