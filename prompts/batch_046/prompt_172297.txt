After a long argument with a friend about whether the strategy-stealing argument is merely a mathematical trick for proving determinacy or secretly encodes a normative principle of procedural fairness, I want to formalize the middle ground as a research proposal. I am applying for a one-semester dissertation fellowship and need a polished two-page prospectus. The half-formed idea is this: in standard game-theoretic treatments, strategy-stealing shows that symmetric perfect-information games are determined by letting one player appropriate the other's hypothetical winning strategy; but this move depends on an implicit structural condition—call it procedural mirrorability—whereby one agent can replicate another's decision procedure without distortion. I want to ask whether procedural mirrorability can be defined rigorously for asymmetric, imperfect-information institutional settings (for instance, deliberative voting rules with private preference orderings and sequential agenda control), and whether the breakdown of mirrorability in such settings explains why determinacy results fail to extend there. The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the research question; an account of why existing literature leaves this open (the technical literature treating the argument as a pure proof device, the institutional literature ignoring its formal structure); a realistic month-by-month method (formal modeling of mirrorability conditions, comparative case analysis of two voting mechanisms, conceptual mapping of where the strategy-stealing analogy collapses); a specification of what a successful result would look like and what would count as project failure; and a clear scope appropriate to one semester. Write in a formal register suitable for a fellowship committee. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will supply those myself.
