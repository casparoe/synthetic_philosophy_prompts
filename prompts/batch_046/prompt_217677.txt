I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a university-affiliated hospice. We must decide on the case of a 78-year-old patient, a lifelong Theravada practitioner, who executed an advance directive ten years ago instructing that life-sustaining treatment be withdrawn should she ever enter what she terms "anatta-samadhi" — a state she defines in the document as one in which "the constructed sense of being a single continuous self has irreversibly dissolved, leaving only discrete mental and sensory events without an owner." She is now minimally responsive, shows no self-referential language or recognition of others, and her monastic advisor attests that she satisfies the directive's own criteria for this condition. Her adult children object, arguing that personal identity is sustained by narrative continuity and embodied interaction: she still breathes, reacts to familiar music, and occupies the same living body, so treating a metaphysical doctrine of no-self as equivalent to the loss of personhood improperly collapses an ontological claim into a clinical criterion. Our institutional policy permits withdrawal only when there is "a loss of the interests that define the person," but it offers no guidance on how a patient's metaphysical commitments about the self should bear on that determination. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — either honoring the directive and initiating withdrawal of ventilation within a clearly specified time frame, or continuing care with defined conditions and a reassessment schedule — together with the reasoning that supports it. The opinion must engage the children's narrative-continuity objection with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than merely citing the legal validity of the directive, and it should draw on the distinction between bundle-theoretic and narrative conceptions of personal identity to clarify exactly what is at stake, without treating religious authority itself as a decisive clinical ground.
