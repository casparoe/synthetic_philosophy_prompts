Design a three-node decision-tree quiz for an advanced undergraduate political-philosophy course that classifies a respondent's actual commitments about liberty—positive, negative, and republican—without requiring them to know those labels in advance. Each node must present one concrete case and ask for a direct yes/no verdict on whether freedom is reduced in that case. The cases must come apart on the dimensions that actually distinguish the views.

Node 1 (domination without interference): A benevolent master holds absolute legal authority over a slave but never issues a command, never punishes, and allows the slave full physical mobility within an enclosed estate. The slave knows the master could alter conditions instantly. Is the slave free?

Node 2 (resources without domination or interference): A citizen lives in a robust democracy with equal voting power, faces no arbitrary authority, and is never obstructed by law or other people, but extreme poverty makes developing any coherent life plan impossible. Is freedom reduced here?

Node 3 (structural, non-agentic interference): A real-time predictive system—without human oversight, intentions, or legal backing—adjusts prices, visibility, and access costs continuously so that one specific life-choice becomes ruinously expensive, though never physically impossible or criminalized. There is no master and no legislator. Is freedom violated?

Present the tree so that the eight answer-combinations (Y/Y/Y through N/N/N) form the logical space first, then locate the named positions within it: pure negative liberty (Berlin-style), republican non-domination (Pettit-style), positive liberty (autonomy/enabling-conditions), and any coherent hybrid. For every combination that has no standard occupant—such as denying domination in Node 1 while affirming resource-constraints in Node 2—diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and state what further question would be needed to split them. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or named scholars beyond Berlin and Pettit as labels; do not include reading lists. Keep the instrument to exactly three nodes and eight terminal combinations.
