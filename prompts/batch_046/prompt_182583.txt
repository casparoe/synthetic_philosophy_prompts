I've been trying to nail down Arendt's argument about lying in politics for a seminar on public reason, and I ended up with this chain that seems to prove every citizen has to be totally transparent, which can't be right. (1) Arendt says political lying is uniquely dangerous because it doesn't just deceive people about one fact; it corrodes the shared factual baseline — the space of appearance between actors — that makes collective judgement possible at all. (2) If the harm is corrosion of that shared baseline, then the harm doesn't depend on who does it or whether they call it a lie: strategic silence, euphemistic framing, or even an ordinary citizen withholding relevent political information all damage the baseline in the same way. (3) So Arendt's reasoning applies to any withholding or distortion of politically relevent facts, not only to official propaganda. (4) Therefore anyone who possesses politically relevent information is obliged to disclose it fully, and any withholding is morally on a par with the systematic deception Arendt describes. (5) But that erases privacy, strategic ambiguity, and the basic right to keep your own views private, which is clearly absurd. (6) So either (1) is false and lying isn't specially harmful, or I'm missing a hidden restriction that stops the slide.

I feel like the error is somewhere between (2) and (4) — perhaps an equivocation about what counts as 'corrosion', or a hidden quantifier shift — but I can't name it precisely. I'd much rather save (1) and (5): I think lying really is specially corrosive, and I think total transparency is a non-starter. So tell me exactly which step has to go, why it looked fine, and what giving it up costs for Arendt's broader picture of the public realm. Take a committed line — don't just lay out options and leave me to choose.
