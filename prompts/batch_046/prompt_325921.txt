I'm moderating a Q&A for a small philosophy-of-mind colloquium, and the guest is an unnamed defender of a refined Hofstadterian position: she holds that the symbol "I" functions as a strange loop in the full recursive sense, but insists the loop is not merely syntactic like Gödel numbering. Instead, she describes it as a distributed feedback architecture in which predictive subsystems continuously update a global self-model by feeding their own outputs back into the prediction stream, so that the symbol's referential stability is both cause and effect of the dynamics. She rejects any Cartesian theater framing—there is no central observer, only convergence through mutual error minimization across modules. I need eight to ten questions that go far beyond asking her to rehearse the familiar narrative about formal systems and self-reference. At least one should press the gap between syntactic self-reference, where a formula denotes itself, and the first-personal, phenomenological self-reference she claims generates the feeling of being a subject; anticipate her likely reply that syntax becomes semantics only above a certain threshold of recursive integration, and include a follow-up that asks whether that threshold is principled or arbitrary, and whether it allows the self to flicker in and out of existence during transient disruptions. At least one should address split-brain or severe dissociative states: does each hemisphere or dissociative part host a partial strange loop, or does the loop require global workspace integration? Key the follow-up to her probable answer—if she claims integration is necessary, ask what happens during brief disconnections; if she allows local loops, ask why unitary selfhood is the default experience rather than a federation. Another should tackle artificial instantiation: she will likely assert substrate independence but point to a specific structural requirement, perhaps a minimal depth of recurrent feedback or a grounded relation between symbol and sensorimotor state. Write the follow-up so it forces her to state the necessary and sufficient conditions precisely, and asks whether current large language models, which lack continuous sensorimotor feedback but possess deep recurrence, meet them or fail them on which axis. Finally, include a question about the eliminativist pressure: if the loop is ultimately just a high-level pattern describing causal dynamics among subsystems, why treat the self as a strange loop rather than as a useful fiction? Anticipate her deflection—that loop-structure and fictionality are not exclusive, since loops are patterns not substances—and ask the follow-up that presses whether her view then collapses into a kind of pattern-realism that sacrifices the first-person authority she started with.
