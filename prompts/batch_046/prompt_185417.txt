Read George Orwell’s *1984* through Wittgenstein’s notions of language games and family resemblance, with special attention to the private-language problem that lurks behind Winston’s diary. Newspeak is usually interpreted as a Sapir-Whorf-style instrument for thought control, but treat it instead as an attempt to institute a new language game: if meaning is use stabilized within a form of life, does Newspeak fail not merely because it deletes vocabulary, but because the Party cannot secure the shared practices that would give its neologisms criteria of correct application? Meanwhile, Winston attempts to record experiences—his mother’s disappearance, the taste of real coffee, the feeling of rebellion—that have no place in Party discourse. Is his diary an attempt at a private language in Wittgenstein’s sense, and does the novel’s tragedy demonstrate that such a language collapses once the shared norms that ground meaning are withdrawn?

Extend the analysis via family resemblance to the political category “enemy of the people.” Goldstein, Emmanuel, and eventually Winston are all assigned this label, yet they share no single essential property—only a shifting network of overlapping similarities that the Party constructs and reconfigures at will. Does Orwell thereby dramatize that political denunciation works like a Wittgensteinian family-resemblance concept, and that the Party’s power lies less in defining its enemies than in controlling the resemblance-network itself?

I want a reading faithful in both directions: show where Wittgenstein illuminates specific scenes—the dictionary editing, the Two Minutes Hate, Julia’s corruption, O’Brien’s interrogation—without reducing the novel to a philosophical illustration. Where does *1984* resist this framing? Does it suggest that language games can be coerced from above in ways Wittgenstein’s emphasis on spontaneous practice underestimates? And does the novel add something to the philosophy of language and power—perhaps about the role of embodied memory and affect in sustaining meaning—that pure conceptual analysis tends to miss? I’m preparing this for a discussion group that takes both literature and philosophy seriously, so I’d like the answer to end by stating clearly what the work contributes to the debate that argument alone does not.
