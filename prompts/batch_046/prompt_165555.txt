Consider the tripartite regulatory architecture now visible in several European aquaculture and research welfare frameworks: decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs are recognized as sentient and afforded specific protections, including, in some member-state implementations, mandatory stunning prior to slaughter; farmed teleost fish fall under generic welfare directives that rarely specify species-appropriate stunning; and bivalves remain entirely outside regulatory scope despite emerging neurobiological evidence of nociceptive capacity. The philosophically interesting tension lies inside the protected category itself: the stunning protocols typically target a centralized brain or nerve center, yet decapod neuroanatomy involves distributed ventral nerve cords with substantial peripheral processing, so the mandated method is anatomically questionable for the very organisms it is meant to protect. What theory of suffering and moral status does this institutional design implicitly commit to? Does it presuppose that phenomenal consciousness requires integrated information processing in a unified neural architecture—thereby placing distributed cognition below a threshold for moral consideration, only inconsistently applied since decapods are protected—or is the division best reconstructed as a tractability-based policy tracking neuroanatomical familiarity rather than any coherent metaphysics of mind? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change—both conceptually and in regulatory design—if one replaced the underlying view with one that treated suffering as genuinely possible in distributed or modular nervous systems.
