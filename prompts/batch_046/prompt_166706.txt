Here is an argument I built for a seminar on moral contractualism, but its conclusion seems destructive and I cannot find the exit. (1) Scanlonian contractualism holds that an action is wrong only if it is disallowed by principles that no affected party could reasonably reject, where the comparison is between the burden the principle imposes on the agent and the harm it prevents to each particular affected party. (2) In a standard aggregation case—say, one million agents each emitting a small quantity of greenhouse gas—any single victim suffers only one-millionth of the total harm, while requiring any single agent to stop imposes a real, non-trivial burden on that agent alone. (3) So, by pairwise comparison, no individual victim can reasonably reject the principle permitting that particular agent’s emission. (4) The same reasoning applies to every subset: no group of k victims can reject the principle allowing the corresponding k agents, because each agent’s burden still outweighs each victim’s share. (5) Therefore contractualism permits every individual emission and, by extension, permits the collective catastrophe. (6) Now consider a single agent—call her the Aggregator—who personally operates all one million emission sources in sequence. For each individual source, apply (2)–(3): no particular victim can reject the principle allowing that specific source, since the burden on the Aggregator for running that one source still exceeds the one-millionth harm to any victim. (7) Hence contractualism permits the Aggregator to run all one million sources, producing the identical total harm. (8) Intuitively, however, the Aggregator’s total conduct is clearly wrong, and contractualism should be able to condemn the total pattern without abandoning its individualist structure. (9) The only available remedy is to allow victims to reject principles on the basis of summed or aggregated harm rather than pairwise harm. (10) But if aggregation is permitted in evaluating the Aggregator, consistency demands it be permitted in evaluating the dispersed million, which means each individual emitter can be required to stop for the sake of collective goods—precisely the disproportionate, non-individualized burden that contractualism was designed to rule out. (11) So contractualism either collapses into a form of utilitarian aggregation or permits catastrophic harm by design. I would prefer to keep (1) and the intuition in (8), and I am willing to sacrifice (6) or (9) if necessary. Which step actually fails, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost the view?
