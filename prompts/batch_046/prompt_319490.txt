I am a doctoral student in political philosophy seconded to a regional arbitration secretariat that reviews interstate conflicts on a fortnightly cycle. The panel must determine whether a state's resort to armed force can be characterised, in terms drawn from Hegel's Philosophy of Right and his logic of recognition (Anerkennung), as preserving the ethical idea of the state—safeguarding sovereignty, preventing the moral corruption that perpetual peace produces, and sustaining the recognitive structure between institutional states—rather than as mere particularistic aggression exported by civil society. I need a reusable decision procedure that I can execute in approximately ninety minutes without returning to primary texts, designed for repeated application by someone with philosophical training but no time for exegesis. The procedure should translate Hegel's account of war into ordered steps: the sequence of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the precise point at which it halts with a negative determination (no ethical warrant), and the point at which it must defer to the panel because the issue exceeds Hegelian analysis and becomes one of values rather than facts. Crucially, it must include a filter for the recognitive counterpart: does the adversary possess the institutional form of a state capable of mutual recognition, or is it a non-state entity, which places the conflict outside the dialectic Hegel describes? It must also distinguish conflict serving the state's universal will from conflict driven by particular economic or social interests. Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a limited strike by a recognised state to secure a contested border region against another recognised state; (b) prolonged occupation of neighbouring territory primarily to secure resources for domestic civil-society consumption; and (c) a defensive conflict in which the recognitive structure has collapsed, yielding an assessment that the defensive war lacks ethical warrant—an outcome I am likely to find politically unwelcome. For each case, indicate where the procedure terminates, where judgment necessarily intrudes, and what it cannot adjudicate—particularly with respect to modern humanitarian law, individual rights, or distributive justice. Please provide the answer in British English, with clear formatting for the procedural steps and the worked examples, and state explicitly what the procedure is unable to do.
