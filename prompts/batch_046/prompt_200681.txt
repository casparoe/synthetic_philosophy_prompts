I've been reading about brain-in-a-vat skepticism for fun, and I keep noticing an argumentative pattern that I don't think has its own name. Someone raises BIV, the other person says okay I can't empirically rule that out. Then they say but at least I have direct access to my mental states, so psychology survives. The skeptic replies that in BIV-world those mental states are just simulated outputs with no real mind behind them. So then the person pivots and says alright, but the logical structure of my reasoning is intact, and thats all science needs. But when you ask whether logical structure alone can distinguish BIV-world from the real world, they say no -- yet they still claim science is justified. Its like they use the skeptical scenario to knock down external correspondence, then rebuild the same confidence using internal coherence and pretend its a different foundation. I know people call this circular reasoning or bootstrapping, but those feel too broad. Is there a sharper concept for this exact move -- conceding that one kind of justification is lost, then preserving another kind that was always dependent on the first? If not, can you name and define it precisely, with clear boundary cases that seperate it from ordinary stubbornness or from just changing the subject? I'd like the answer in American English, and I don't need any reading lists -- just the carving.
