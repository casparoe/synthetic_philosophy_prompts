I have spent the last year reading translations of the Pali suttas and a few works in philosophy of mind entirely outside any formal course of study, and I have become convinced that the Buddhist teaching of anatta is widely misunderstood by Western readers. Next Thursday evening I am to present my thoughts to a philosophy discussion group that meets at the local library; the regular attendees include several analytic philosophers well versed in Parfit’s reductionism and Lockean memory theories, a visiting lecturer in Buddhist philosophy, and a researcher in cognitive neuroscience who studies consciousness during general anaesthesia. My central claim is that anatta ought not to be read as a flat denial that there is any personal identity whatsoever, but as the rejection of a substantial, enduring “owner” behind the stream of experience. Drawing on the Abhidhamma description of consciousness as a rapid succession of mental moments—cittas—I want to argue that what we call the self is the continuity of ethical attention, manasikāra, reconstituted across those moments, and that this explains why we do not feel ourselves to have died and been replaced after deep sleep or surgical anaesthesia, even though no persistent substrate carries the identity. I recognise that this sounds like psychological reductionism, but I believe it differs from Parfit’s account because the binding factor is not merely causal relations among mental states but the intentional structure of moral attention itself, which can bridge interrupted phenomenal consciousness in a way that pure psychological continuity cannot accommodate.
