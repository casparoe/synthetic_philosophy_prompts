I've been trying to formalize a worry about masked dispositions—particularly in the context of counterfactual robustness testing—that seems to prove no macroscopic object has any stable disposition, and I can't find the exit. Here is the argument, step by step.

(1) If an object O genuinely has a disposition D with manifestation conditions C and result M, then there is a categorical base B in O such that, were C satisfied and no interfering factor present, B would produce M.

(2) Now suppose there is a "recursive mask" R: an intrinsic structural feature of O that is itself disposed to suppress B's manifestation of M, but only under conditions that include the absence of an external testing signal S. In other words, R is a fink that activates precisely when O is not being observed for D.

(3) Because R is part of O's categorical structure, O contains both B (grounding D) and R (grounding the suppression of D). The presence of R does not depend on any external factor except S, which is not part of C.

(4) Therefore, O simultaneously possesses the categorical grounds for both D and not-D, with the difference being only whether S is present.

(5) But if two contradictory dispositions can be grounded in the very same categorical base without any change in O's intrinsic properties—only a change in an external contextual parameter like S—then the categorical base does not determine which disposition O has.

(6) So either we deny that B uniquely determines D (giving up the categorical-base theory), or we accept that every object has every disposition, with recursive masks selecting which ones are active in which contexts.

(7) The second option proves too much: a crystal of sugar would have the disposition to dissolve in water masked by a recursive lattice that activates only in chemistry labs, rendering "soluble" meaningless outside those labs.

(8) Hence, if we want to preserve meaningful disposition ascriptions, we must reject (1) and abandon the categorical-base account entirely.

This feels wrong—surely the presence of an internal shock-absorbing lattice makes a vase genuinely tough rather than genuinely fragile with a mask. But I can't see whether the error is in (2), treating R as part of the base; in (4), sliding from "contains both structures" to "possesses contradictory dispositions"; or in (6), presenting a false dichotomy. I'd prefer to keep (1) and (7)—the idea that dispositions need categorical bases and that they shouldn't collapse into context-relative chaos—but I'm willing to lose one if the other survives. Which step is the weakest, and what does giving it up cost?
