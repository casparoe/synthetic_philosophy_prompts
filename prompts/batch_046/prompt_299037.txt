I want a full specification of what an adequate answer to the contrastive historical question “why Britain and not China?”—the Needham problem or Great Divergence—would have to deliver, before any particular explanation is weighed. Lay out the desiderata systematically: the phenomena the account must cover (the approximate 1750–1850 divergence in industrial capacity, the prior parity in living standards and technological achievement across Qing and British economies, the persistence of asymmetry into the twentieth century, and the specificity of Britain rather than “Europe” as the unit of comparison); the conceptual distinctions it must preserve rather than collapse (an explanation of Britain’s trajectory versus an explanation of China’s absence; structural versus contingent causes; endogenous institutional dynamics versus exogenous shocks such as Atlantic trade or colonial extraction; economic mechanisms versus political-institutional ones; and the separation of explaining an outcome from normatively endorsing industrialization as progress); the consistency conditions with established comparative history (the administrative and fiscal capacities of the Qing state, the technological sophistication of Song-Ming metallurgy and printing, the geographic availability of coal versus its actual exploitation, and the magnitude of New World silver flows); and the methodological requirements specific to well-posed contrastive explanation (counterfactual dependency mapping—specifying whether Britain’s industrialization was contingent on particular events, such as the Glorious Revolution or specific coal-field access, or structurally overdetermined across nearby worlds, with a symmetric specification for China’s path—without committing the fallacy of treating absence as a causally efficacious negative condition; scaling invariance, so that the causal structure does not shift arbitrarily between nation-state, regional, and macro-regional units of analysis; and teleological neutrality, so that industrialization is not treated as an inevitable endpoint of history).
