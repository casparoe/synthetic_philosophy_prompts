I'm writing the eulogy for my former doctoral supervisor, a theoretical physicist who died last month at eighty-seven and who held, in private conversation if never in print, a particular variant of the block-universe view drawn from the Rietdijk-Putnam line of argument. He took seriously the idea that because simultaneity in special relativity is frame-dependent, there is no unique present slice that cleanly separates the living from the dead; instead, the relation between "he is present" and "he is past" shifts with one's frame of reference in a manner that is literal rather than merely poetic. The congregation will be divided roughly evenly between physicists who will recognize the argument and family members who will not. I need the eulogy to treat this idea as its structural backbone—not as decorative metaphor but as a hard constraint on what can honestly be said—so that I do not offer false consolation about his watching over us from some present elsewhere, yet can still say something genuinely comforting about the permanence of the moments we shared. The speech must work for both halves of the room: it should land as philosophy for the physicists without becoming a lecture, and as sincere comfort for the family without smuggling in metaphysical commitments they do not share. Please write it as continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables—arranged as a single flowing address that could be spoken aloud. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not ask me to supply any.
