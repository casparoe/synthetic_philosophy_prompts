I have just concluded a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend over whether our respective moral commitments—to reducing factory-farming suffering and to prioritising distant strangers in our charitable giving—represent genuinely reasoned preferences or merely culturally inculcated biases, and I have come away convinced that the conceptual apparatus usually brought to bear on this question is inadequate. I am therefore drafting a proposal for a six-month independent research project, suitable for a small philosophy fellowship or a dissertation prospectus, on the difficulty of distinguishing preferences from biases in morality. My half-formed idea is that the debate is typically framed as a binary classification exercise, but I want to explore whether the distinction can be made tractable only by splitting it across three dimensions: first, the judgement's stability under counterfactual information about its causal origin; second, its coherence with the agent's broader normative commitments under conditions of reflective equilibrium; and third, its functional role in practical reasoning, quite apart from its phenomenological feel. The precise research question is whether any combination of these dimensions yields a non-circular criterion for identifying a genuinely moral preference that survives the charge of bias, or whether the distinction ultimately collapses into a pragmatic rather than metaphysical one.

Please construct this as a formal research proposal. I need a precise statement of the question, an explanation of why existing positions—whether those that reduce moral disagreement to incommensurable preference or those that treat all disagreement as correctable bias—leave this particular tri-dimensional framing open, and a month-by-month account of what I would actually do over six months. Specify why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives, such as appeals to universalizability or evolutionary debunking arguments, and be explicit about what would count as the project failing. I also want an honest assessment of scope: what can genuinely be delivered in six months without empirical data collection, what the useful deliverable looks like—perhaps a conceptual taxonomy with carefully constructed test cases—and what risks making the project too large. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation review; I will supply those myself. The proposal should be rigorous but accessible to a philosophy funding panel that is not exclusively metaethical, and it should treat the work as a plan for inquiry not yet undertaken rather than a defence of an already settled view.
