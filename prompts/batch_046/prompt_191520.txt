You are a philosopher of mind with substantial experience in seminar pedagogy, and before you outline any rules you should take a moment to brainstorm the core tension you need students to feel rather than merely understand: the difference between having a first-order mental state and being the object of a higher-order representation that may or may not correctly track it, especially when the second-order access is mediated by proxies that can be systematically decoupled from the first-order reality. Design a structured 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about twelve students, centered on higher-order theories of consciousness. The exercise should use a custom framing I will call the “silent monitor” scenario—not a named theory in the literature, but a pedagogical construct in which the group is split into a “first-order” cohort of six students who perform rapid perceptual discrimination tasks (sorting ambiguous visual patterns, judging brief tonal sequences, or identifying masked emotional expressions) using only direct sensory engagement, and a “higher-order” cohort of six students who never observe the tasks directly but instead receive only aggregated behavioral proxies—reaction-time logs, forced-choice confidence ratings, and scripted verbal reports—through which they must construct meta-cognitive judgments about whether consciousness is present in, and correctly aligned with, the first-order cohort’s states. Crucially, embed a hidden “misalignment rule” for two specific 15-minute rounds: the proxy data fed to the higher-order cohort is deliberately decoupled from the actual first-order performances (for example, confidence ratings are inverted or reaction-time clusters are swapped), so that the higher-order group forms authoritative-seeming judgments that systematically misrepresent the target states, simulating a form of higher-order misrepresentation or “silent monitoring” in which a meta-cognitive mechanism functions but fails to correctly represent its first-order object.

Specify explicit roles and rotation rules so that every student eventually occupies both positions; provide a clear timeline broken into four phases—orientation and role assignment (10 minutes), first-order task execution with transparent proxy feedback (20 minutes), execution with the hidden decoupling rule active (20 minutes), and a structured revelation and comparison session (20 minutes)—plus a brief closing synthesis (20 minutes). The payoff scheme should use symbolic points rather than money: award “tracking accuracy” points to the higher-order cohort for correct meta-cognitive attributions and “autonomy” points to the first-order cohort for maintaining task performance despite being observed only through distorted reports, with a collective bonus if both groups jointly identify the decoupling before the facilitator reveals it. Arrange the incentives so that the higher-order cohort initially feels authoritative and correct, then experiences a collapse of that authority when the misalignment is exposed, while the first-order cohort discovers that they have been “seen” only through representations that bore no faithful relation to their actual experience—this dynamic should emerge from the rules and the hidden data manipulation, not from any lecture delivered beforehand or during the exercise. Then provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the formal structure of higher-order theories: the first phase corresponds to the basic claim that consciousness requires a higher-order representation of a first-order state; the decoupling phase corresponds to the possibility of misrepresentation; the revelation phase corresponds to the problem of whether a misrepresenting higher-order state still counts as conscious, and whether first-person authority can be reconstructed. Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the constitutive claim that the higher-order state literally makes the first-order state conscious rather than merely causing or reporting it—and state clearly that the facilitator must supply this metaphysical commitment verbally in the closing synthesis. Also note what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it: the higher-order group is likely to conflate confidence with accuracy and resist admitting error, while the first-order group may feel objectified and disengage; suggest handling this by framing the decoupling as a designed feature of the simulation rather than a betrayal, and by allowing a brief unstructured dialogue before the formal debrief begins.
