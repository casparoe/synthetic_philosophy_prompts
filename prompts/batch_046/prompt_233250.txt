I was discussing Bernard Williams's writings on moral integrity with a close friend after a long lecture, specifically his example of a man saving his wife from drowning and his broader claim that certain moral demands ask us to abandon what gives our lives meaning. Our argument kept returning to a distinction I cannot find a precise label for, and I want to know whether philosophy already has one. Here is the pattern I keep noticing in debates about deep personal commitments. Imagine a musician who plays only in small, non-commercial settings because she understands her art as an intimate form of expression rather than a public commodity. A critic argues that she should perform at a large charity concert because many lives depend on it. Her first defense sounds like a claim about personal cost: the concert format would destroy the conditions that make her playing meaningful, so complying would cost her the very thing she values. But when the critic replies that she could perform once, bear that loss, and retain the commitment elsewhere — that the damage is survivable — she shifts ground. She now says that if she agreed to play because the lives saved morally outweigh her artistic preference, she would be treating her commitment to intimate art as just one preference to be weighed against others on a common scale. That mode of reasoning, she insists, is itself incompatible with the commitment: to see it that way is already to have lost it. The refusal is not about how heavy the burden is, but about what kind of practical reasoning she would have to adopt.

My friend insisted this is just Williams's integrity objection stated twice. I think there are two different structures here. One targets the magnitude of what the agent loses; the other targets the conceptual framing of the agent's own motivation — the idea that complying on moral grounds requires her to reconceptualize her ground project as fungible, which corrupts the very attitude that makes the project hers. I have seen the same move with parents refusing relocation for the public good, not because family disruption is unbearable, but because viewing family bonds as overridable by aggregate welfare calculations destroys what being a parent means to them. Is there an established term for this second, structural form of refusal — the one that objects not to the cost of compliance but to the kind of reason that would motivate it? Or is this exactly what Williams means by integrity, with the drowning-spouse example serving a separate, adjacent purpose about practical reasoning? If they are genuinely distinct, how should I understand their relationship, and where exactly do the boundaries lie?
