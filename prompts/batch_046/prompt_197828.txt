ive been stuck in this philosophy of history group for like three years and the covering-law vs narrative explanation debate just wont die. the hempel-style people say you cant actually explain the bronze age collapse or the fall of rome unless you subsume the events under some general law about institutional decay or resource stress, and the narrative camp says arranging those same events into a story with agents, turning points, and counterfactual dependencies is doing real explanatory work that the law never captures. we keep running through the same cases — the french revolution, the bronze age collapse — and nobody moves. my hunch is that this isnt really about explanation at all but about whether history counts as a science or a humanistic discipline, and the whole thing is just institutional turf dressed up as philosophy. but maybe im wrong and theres an actual formal disagreement hiding in there about what counts as an explanation, or about whether historians implicitly use laws even when they tell stories. i want a diagnosis, not a survey: are they answering diferent questions under one word? is there a hidden empirical claim about how working historians actualy construct arguments? and given your diagnosis, could anything — a new logical distinction, a cognitive science result about how people understand narratives, a case study that forces both sides to agree on whats being explained — actually break the stalemate, or is it just bedrock commitments about what knowledge should look like?
