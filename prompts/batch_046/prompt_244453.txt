I have no formal background in philosophy or neuroscience, but I have been turning over a modern variation of Molyneux's problem on my own, and I would like to see two careful thinkers argue it through in real time.

The classic problem asks whether a person blind from birth, if suddenly made to see, could distinguish a cube from a sphere by sight alone without touching them. I want to push this into a contemporary scenario. Imagine someone blind since birth who, for two decades, has used a sensory-substitution device that converts visual edges, surfaces, and spatial relations into precisely structured auditory textures. Through this mediation they navigate rooms fluently, identify objects by sound-pattern alone, and describe geometric shapes with accuracy—yet every experience is ultimately auditory. Then they receive a neural implant that restores direct visual cortical processing. The first time they open their eyes and look at the cube they have long handled and heard-described, do they recognize it?

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors—call them A and B—who take opposing positions.

A should defend the view that recognition is immediate: the spatial-relational structure of the cube has already been internalized in a modality-independent form, and the implant merely delivers the same structural information through a new channel. A should argue that successful navigation and description already demonstrate conceptual possession of the shape, so there is only a change in sensory vehicle, not a conceptual gap.

B should defend the opposing view: that sensory substitution builds a form of functional correlation or behavioral adaptation without genuine visual concept-formation, and that "seeing the cube" requires a phenomenal binding or qualitative integration that auditory translation never achieved. B should press A on whether fluent navigation proves conceptual recognition or merely learned association.

The exchange must contain genuine back-and-forth. A should respond to B's phenomenal-binding objection not by dismissing it, but by asking whether functional equivalence requires an identical subjective quality in order to count as true recognition. B should respond to A's structural-mapping claim not by denying the neuroscience, but by distinguishing between using a representation instrumentally and experiencing the object under a specifically visual concept.

Keep both characters equally competent—no straw-manning. Avoid asking either to cite studies, name authors, or provide reading lists; I am not looking for a literature survey, but for the reasoning to unfold through their direct engagement with each other's points. Write in a polished but accessible style.
