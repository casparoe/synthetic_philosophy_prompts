I sit on a small advisory board for a local community organization that drafts ethical guidelines for resource distribution and conflict mediation. We rely heavily on shared moral intuitions to justify rules, and I have been reflecting on my own about a pattern I see in popular discussions: many of our strongest moral feelings—such as that intentional harm is worse than accidental harm, or that we owe something special to people not yet born—are said to be evolutionary leftovers, shaped because they aided ancestral reproduction rather than because they track anything genuinely true. I want this turned into a practical, reusable decision procedure that my board can actually run in about fifteen minutes, without anyone holding a philosophy degree, to decide whether an evolutionary explanation of one of our guiding intuitions should weaken our justification for using it in policy.

The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear "this evolutionary story does not debunk our belief," and where it stops with "this undermines our justification for relying on the intuition." Clearly mark which steps are factual judgments and which are value judgments that the board must make as citizens rather than as experts. The procedure must include two specific checks I want embedded: first, a proxy-decoupling check—determine whether the evolutionary mechanism tracks the moral property itself or only tracks a reliable ancestral proxy for it, and whether modern social conditions have broken that correlation; second, a cross-environment persistence check—determine whether the belief survives with similar strength across societies with very different reproductive pressures, kin structures, and resource environments. The procedure must also say exactly what to do when the evolutionary story is speculative or contested, and it must be honest about where it will misfire—for instance, if every moral belief turns out to have some evolutionary origin story, or if the board cannot agree on whether a correlation has genuinely broken.

Then apply the procedure in order to three beliefs we might invoke: (1) that intentional harm is morally worse than accidental harm of equal severity; (2) that distributing goods equally is fairer than distributing them strictly according to need; and (3) that we have special obligations to distant future generations that go beyond what a simple calculation of overall welfare requires. For each case, run every step, state where the procedure lands, and explain the reasoning. Ensure that at least one of these three yields a result that would frustrate someone generally inclined to think evolutionary explanations usually debunk moral beliefs—that is, the procedure should preserve the belief's standing despite a plausible evolutionary origin story. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can ever establish the positive truth of a moral claim, or only weaken or preserve justification; and whether it can distinguish evolutionary explanation from evolutionary debunking in every case, or only flag where further deliberation is required. Write this as a formal request for the procedure and its three worked applications, in the voice of someone without academic training who has been reading and thinking about these ideas independently.
