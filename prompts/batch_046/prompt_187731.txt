I'm preparing a sequence of questions for a podcast interview with the author of a recent monograph that extends Hofstadter-style Copycat analogies into a general account of conceptual architecture. Her thesis is that in microdomains like letter-string transformation—where relations such as successor, predecessor, and opposite continuously deform into one another—conceptual slippage is not cognitive noise but the primary mechanism of reasoning. She uses this to argue that both symbolic AI and LLM-based reasoning commit the same category error: they treat conceptual boundaries as fixed entities suitable for parsing or tokenization, when in fact every boundary is only a temporary equilibrium of analogical pressure.

She anticipates the objection that this is merely descriptive psychology by claiming that normative rationality just becomes the evaluation of which deformations preserve coherence across contexts.

Write a sequence of interview questions that makes it progressively harder for her to retreat to the position that she is only describing a mechanism, not defending a normative thesis. The questions should address whether she can identify a genuinely erroneous slippage—say, an analogy that violates structural correspondence—without smuggling in a hidden fixed criterion like the intended mapping, which her theory treats as constructed rather than discovered. They should also press her on the internal architecture of Copycat itself: the model relies on competing codelets within a workspace governed by a control parameter analogous to temperature that precisely regulates the rate of conceptual deformation. If boundaries are intrinsically porous, why does the system require an explicit, rigid parameter to govern how porous they become? Does that parameter not function as exactly the kind of non-slipping boundary she denies?

Then ask what predictive or experimental result could actually falsify her claim that all conceptual boundaries are porous—specifically, what she would accept as evidence that some microdomain operates with genuinely rigid, categorically fixed concepts, rather than allowing her to absorb any apparent rigidity as merely a low-temperature equilibrium.

End with a direct commitment question: if an engineered cognitive architecture solved analogical reasoning tasks using fully deterministic, boundary-fixed representations with zero internal slippage, would she treat that as a refutation of her thesis, or only as proof that the system is artificial and therefore fails to capture real cognition?

Keep the tone precise, skeptical without being dismissive, and avoid generic biographical or career questions.
