Some constitutions have "eternity clauses" — like Article 79(3) in the German Basic Law — that block even democratic majorities from amending core principles, and in mental-health law we use Ulysses contracts where a patient pre-commits to treatment she might later refuse. Both treat a future decision-maker as someone whose authority needs restraining. What theory of diachronic agency or rationality do they presuppose? Can you defend them without saying the future self is literally a different person? And there's a weird asymmetry: we think constitutional binding protects democracy, but if a private person tried to contract away thier future autonomy permanently we'd call it coercive. What's the hidden principle that makes one legitimate and the other not, and does it depend on a specific view of collective vs. individual identity over time?
