I take heterophenomenology to be not just a provisional heuristic but the correct methodological posture for consciousness science: first-person reports are to be treated as texts that construct a reported experiential domain, comparable to an anthropologist's field notes recording a culture's cosmology, without ontological commitment to the entities named within them—phenomenal colors, introspective objects, or qualitative feels. The claim is that this protects empirical inquiry from metaphysical entanglement while preserving all predictive and explanatory labor. What would genuinely count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I want more than the familiar charge of eliminativism: specify which empirical practices become impossible or distorted if reports are treated as fictional texts rather than as data demanding functional explanation—particularly in cases of disciplined intersubjective calibration, where trained observers iteratively stabilize descriptions of experiential structures across subjects. Does the stance risk insulating itself, for instance by interpreting any resistant report as part of the fictional narrative rather than as counterevidence, and is that insulation a methodological virtue or a vice? Also distinguish the objections that defenders of this framework should themselves concede as damaging from those they can legitimately dismiss as begging the question against it.
