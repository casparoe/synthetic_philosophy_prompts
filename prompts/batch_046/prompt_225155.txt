I facilitate a graduate workshop on strategic reasoning for philosophy and computer-science students — 16 participants, 75 minutes, one session. Design a structured, in-class simulation, using only physical materials, assigned roles, and a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, that makes participants feel why program equilibrium permits cooperative outcomes that standard Nash rationality rules out in a one-shot interaction. The mechanism should work as follows: in each round, pairs submit sealed protocol envelopes containing conditional instruction cards (for example, 'Cooperate if the other envelope displays a green seal' or 'Defect regardless'). The facilitator opens both envelopes, inspects the conditions, and executes the actions, awarding points according to the scheme: mutual cooperation 6 points each, mutual defection 2 points each, unilateral defection 8 for the defector and 0 for the cooperator. To prevent this from being a standard Prisoner's Dilemma replay, inject this niche constraint: in every pair, one participant is secretly assigned a compiler token that permits them to alter one word on their opponent's instruction card after seeing its seal colour but before the facilitator executes the envelopes. The rules, roles (player, hidden compiler-holder, neutral facilitator), and timeline must be arranged so that participants discover through play — not through any prior lecture — that mutual inspection of the envelopes makes conditional cooperation rational, and then discover that the compiler token collapses that stability. Provide: a precise 75-minute timeline broken into submission, inspection, possible compilation, execution, and scoring phases; explicit instructions for the facilitator to execute envelopes without bias; a debrief that maps the inspection phase onto mutual source-code observation and the compilation phase onto post-submission tampering or imperfect execution in formal program equilibrium; an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time you run it (for instance, participants treating seal colour as a binding commitment rather than an inspectable condition, or the compiler-holder refusing to use the token out of a sense of unfairness); and a frank admission of which feature of formal program equilibrium — perfect, simultaneous, unalterable mutual inspection of source — the envelope format cannot reproduce, leaving that part to be supplied verbally by the facilitator. Do not name the concept of program equilibrium before the debrief; do not ask for bibliographies or citations.
