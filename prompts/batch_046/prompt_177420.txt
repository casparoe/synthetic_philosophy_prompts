I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind and comparative cognition, and after last week's discussion of invertebrate neural architecture, two members of my study group—Jordan and Riley—fell into a disagreement that has continued in our group chat. I was only listening and haven't taken sides; I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange point by point. Jordan opened by proposing that consciousness across animal phyla should be assigned only where there is a "phenomenal fusion point"—the minimal neural condition in which disparate sensory streams are bound into a single, ego-centered perspective with mutual accessibility, rather than being processed in parallel isolation. Applying this, Jordan argued that octopuses likely possess consciousness despite their distributed nervous systems, because their optic lobes and central brain achieve genuine cross-modal integration, whereas insects such as bees process vision, olfaction, and reward in largely modular pathways that never converge into one subjective viewpoint. Riley objected that Jordan's criterion smuggles in mammalian brain architecture as the universal norm. Riley proposed instead an "adaptive valence model": consciousness is present wherever an organism employs a flexible, world-modeling system guided by intrinsic positive or negative valence that cannot be reduced to reflex arcs. On this view, bees qualify because their navigation and reward-learning require an internal spatial model with affective tagging, while octopuses may actually instantiate several semi-independent conscious perspectives—one per arm cluster—rather than one unified consciousness, making them polycentric rather than clearly singular subjects. Jordan replied that Riley's model collapses the distinction between consciousness and sophisticated behavior; if flexible world-modeling with valence is sufficient, then a reinforcement-learning algorithm or even a plant's tropistic root-direction system might cross the threshold, which strips "consciousness" of its explanatory power regarding subjective experience. Jordan insisted that without the phenomenal fusion requirement, we have no non-arbitrary reason to deny consciousness to any adaptive system. Riley countered that Jordan's fusion point arbitrarily privileges unified ego-structures and fails to account for humans with split-brain conditions or highly distributed cortical processing, where consciousness can fragment without disappearing; Riley added that evolutionary continuity suggests consciousness comes in degrees and configurations, not as a single on-off switch tied to one anatomical layout. The exchange ended with Jordan asking whether polycentric consciousness is genuinely consciousness or merely parallel unconscious processes, and with Riley asking what principled reason remains to deny bees conscious status once we reject an anthropocentric unity requirement. Please go through their moves in order—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification—then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing, particularly regarding whether their competing criteria can be jointly satisfied or whether one implicitly excludes cases the other is right to preserve.
