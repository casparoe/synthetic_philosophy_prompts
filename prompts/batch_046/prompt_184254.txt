Consider the following argument about policing and the state's monopoly on legitimate violence. (1) The Weberian claim is coherent only if there exists a stable institutional boundary separating state-sanctioned coercion from private coercion within a territory. (2) Policing is the everyday mechanism that maintains that boundary: it applies state coercion to citizens and excludes private coercion from the same domain. (3) Reforms that genuinely strip police of coercive capacities—disarmament, abolition of custodial arrest, removal of legal immunities for the use of force—do not merely weaken policing; they erase the boundary-marker itself. (4) Once that marker is erased, citizens facing harm must either submit to private violence or organize their own defensive coercion, which is precisely the privatization that reformists claim to avoid. (5) Hence a stable, non-coercive police institution is conceptually impossible: it either retains sufficient coercive authority to remain the state's boundary-marker (so reform has not occurred) or it vanishes and leaves a vacuum that private force fills (so reform is indistinguishable from abolition). (6) Nevertheless, reformists propose exactly such an institution: unarmed community safety workers who investigate, mediate, and support without exercising state coercion or delegating it to markets. (7) Therefore the reformist middle ground is illusory. Where does this argument go wrong? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (6), but I want to know which step fails and at what cost.
