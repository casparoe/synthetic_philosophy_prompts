My friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether consciousness should be understood as a user illusion—an interface constructed by the brain that presents a streamlined model of self and world rather than revealing the underlying neural machinery in any direct way. The argument kept returning to a concrete institutional practice in neuroethics and end-of-life medicine: for patients with severe brain injury, clinical and legal protocols typically treat consciousness as a binary property. A patient is classified as either conscious or not conscious on the basis of behavioral scales and, in difficult cases, covert neuroimaging signatures such as command-following detected in motor cortex; that binary classification then determines eligibility for withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, the applicability of research consent procedures, and the framework used for family counseling. The practice seems to presuppose that consciousness is either present as a single, unified phenomenon or absent altogether, with no legitimate intermediate ontological status.

What philosophical commitments about consciousness does this binary-gating practice actually presuppose? Does it commit to a realist view according to which consciousness is a unitary natural kind—something like a globally integrated process that is either running or not—rather than a graded, fragmentable interface? Or can a proponent of the user-illusion view consistently accept the same protocols by treating the binary threshold as a purely practical decision boundary with no ontological weight? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice treat detected neural signals as direct windows onto an underlying reality, or as reports produced by an interface whose reliability is being audited? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Then tell me what would have to change in the legal and medical framework if consciousness were genuinely reconceived as a user illusion—would we need graded rights, continuous rather than binary withdrawal criteria, or a different epistemic relationship between behavioral reports and underlying neural states? Finally, point out whether the practice is internally consistent, given that the same institutions often employ graded, spectrum-based assessments in rehabilitation contexts while insisting on binary classifications for life-or-death decisions.
