I'm currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on the historical Jesus and the methodological debates surrounding the criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, embarrassment, coherence, and especially dissimilarity. Next week I will be moderating a recorded colloquium Q&A with a visiting scholar who has just completed a monograph arguing that these criteria contain a structural antinomy that makes it impossible, in principle, to move from which sayings are authentic to who the historical Jesus was. Her central claim, which she calls the fragmentary bootstrap problem, is that rigorous application of dissimilarity strips away every saying that connects Jesus to Second Temple Judaism or to the theological concerns of the earliest communities, leaving only decontextualized fragments. Because coherence requires a prior profile of Jesus against which to judge remaining sayings, but that profile can only be built from sayings already vetted by dissimilarity, the method enters a loop: the reconstructed figure is either so discontinuous with his environment as to be historically implausible, or the loop is broken by smuggling continuity back in through ad hoc adjustments. She typically deflects criticism by saying that all historical reconstruction involves iterative refinement and that the tension is productive rather than paralyzing.

I need a sequence of interview questions that presses precisely on this iterative-refinement defense and makes it progressively harder to deploy. The questions should anticipate her likely replies—particularly the move of treating the antinomy as a heuristic tension rather than a logical deadlock—and should include sharp follow-ups. I want to avoid generic biographical or how-did-you-get-interested-in-this openings; the exchange should stay on the conceptual machinery. In particular, please include a question that asks what kind of archaeological, manuscript, or comparative-historical discovery could actually count against her view that the bootstrap problem is insurmountable, since she has sometimes suggested that no conceivable evidence could close the loop. The sequence should feel like a genuine philosophical interrogation of the method, not a survey of the field, and it should end with one final question about what evidence could count against the view.
