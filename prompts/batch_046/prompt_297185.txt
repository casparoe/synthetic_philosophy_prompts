I am drafting a short essay on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian epistemology, and I would welcome critical comments on its coherence, on whether its central distinction holds up, and on whether the argument avoids circularity.

The thesis is that the standard formulation mislocates the difficulty. The familiar observation is that if evidence E is already known, then conditioning on E raises no credence in H, since P(E) equals one; this is taken to show that Bayesians cannot account for the scientific value of explaining familiar data. I propose that this diagnosis conflates two functions evidence can serve: synchronic evidential support, which operates on credence via likelihood ratios, and diachronic epistemic credit, which operates on explanatory justification assigned retrospectively.

My central distinction runs as follows. Evidential support requires that P(E|H) exceed P(E|~H). Epistemic credit, by contrast, is granted when H demonstrates that E was not merely post hoc accommodated but was made probable, or even structurally inevitable, within H's framework—specifically relative to competing theories that also fit E after the fact but do so with lower assigned likelihood or with greater ad hoc adjustment. I call the comparative mechanism here retrodictive discrimination: it is not logical consistency with E, but the sharpness with which H distinguishes E's probability from that assigned by rival frameworks that treat E as an anomaly to be patched.

The essay then addresses a circularity worry. One might object that selecting rival theories precisely to highlight their failure at retrodictive discrimination smuggles the desired conclusion into the prior space. I respond tentatively that the selection of rivals is constrained by independent theoretical commitments—ontological parsimony, predictive track record elsewhere, structural analogies—so that retrodictive discrimination functions not as an independent criterion but as a second-order structural feature revealed within an already-constrained comparison class.

I am particularly uncertain whether this distinction genuinely resolves the old-evidence problem or merely relocates it into the problem of prior specification. I also worry that the notion of epistemic credit cannot be made precise without collapsing back into subjective credence adjustment, in which case the proposed two-layer structure adds nothing.

What is your assessment of the argument's coherence? Is the separation between evidential support and epistemic credit sustainable, and does the concept of retrodictive discrimination avoid the circularity objection? If there is a fatal structural flaw, where does it lie, and what revision would best preserve the essay's core insight?
