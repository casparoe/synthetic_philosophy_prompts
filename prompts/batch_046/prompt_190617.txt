The assignment asked: Ruth Chang argues that some choices are hard because the alternatives stand on a par — neither better, worse, nor equal, yet still comparable through normative relations. Joseph Raz argues that some values are incommensurable: they share no common metric by which they can be ranked. Consider a novelist's protagonist who must choose between completing a masterpiece that will secure artistic immortality but destroy her family, or abandoning the work to preserve her marriage. She feels neither option is superior, yet she also believes the choice is not arbitrary and that she can have genuine reasons for either path. Explain whether Chang's parity or Raz's incommensurability better captures her situation, address whether incommensurability makes rational choice impossible, and discuss whether the presence of moral residue after deciding favors one framework over the other.

The student writes: The protagonist faces a hard choice in Chang's sense: the two lives are on a par. They are neither better nor worse than each other, but they remain comparable because we can see normative relations between them — one is more creative, the other more loving. This is actually just a localized form of Raz's incommensurability, occurring when two values that lack a common metric happen to sit at the same normative level. Raz is correct that incommensurable values cannot be ranked by a single scale, but he wrongly concludes that this blocks rational choice altogether. The protagonist clearly exercises rational agency by weighing her commitments, so Raz's stronger claim — that incommensurability renders choice arbitrary unless supplemented by sheer will — should be rejected. Regarding moral residue, Chang follows Bernard Williams in holding that hard choices leave behind legitimate regret, because parity means both options were genuinely valuable and comparable. If Raz's incommensurability applied instead, there would be no basis for such residue: without comparability, sacrificing one value for another carries no special loss. Therefore, the case is best explained by Chang's framework, and Raz's view should be rejected because it undermines rational agency.
