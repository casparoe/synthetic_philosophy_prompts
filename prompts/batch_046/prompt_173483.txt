Consider the following argument about rational belief and self-monitoring, developed with a hypothetical “partitioned escrow” mechanism: an agent quarantines beliefs produced by processes flagged as unreliable rather than lowering credence in them, preserving practical availability while withholding full epistemic endorsement.

(1) Rational belief requires that one’s credence in p match the strength of one’s total available evidence, including higher-order evidence about the reliability of the process that produced the belief.
(2) In any non-trivial domain, there is always available higher-order evidence that one’s reasoning is subject to bias, peer disagreement, or historical error, so for any first-order belief p there exists undermining evidence.
(3) The escrow policy allows an agent to respond by placing flagged beliefs in a quarantined set: they are not actively endorsed but remain available for practical reasoning, apparently avoiding the need to suspend judgment.
(4) However, the decision to adopt and maintain the escrow policy itself rests on normative beliefs about rationality and reliability, which are also outputs of reasoning processes.
(5) By (2), there is always available higher-order evidence undermining the processes behind those normative beliefs, so they too should be quarantined unless there is a principled stopping point.
(6) If the stopping point is arbitrary, then rationality permits leaving some undermined beliefs active—precisely epistemic akrasia—without justification for selecting which ones are spared.
(7) If there is no stopping point, then the policy applies recursively until every belief, including the belief that quarantine is appropriate, is quarantined.
(8) But if all beliefs are quarantined, the agent has no active basis for maintaining the quarantine itself, and the policy collapses into total epistemic paralysis.
(9) Therefore, either rational belief is impossible, or the escrow mechanism fails, leaving epistemic akrasia—actively believing p while actively believing that one’s belief in p is unreliable—as the only stable state.
(10) Yet epistemic akrasia appears to violate coherence: one cannot rationally hold that p and that one should not hold p at the same level of belief.
(11) So the argument seems to show that rational belief either collapses into paralysis or permits outright incoherence.

I am most reluctant to give up (1) or (3), since abandoning the relevance of higher-order evidence looks like willful blindness, and discarding the escrow idea merely returns us to the standard problem without solving it. But if I keep both, I seem driven to (9). Which step should be rejected? Is there a hidden scope shift or modal slide between levels of belief—perhaps between “there is undermining evidence available” and “rationality requires quarantine”? Or does the regress in (5)–(7) illicitly assume that practical reasoning requires actively endorsed beliefs rather than quarantined ones? What does each rejection cost, especially for someone who wants to preserve both that rationality is genuinely responsive to self-doubt and that it does not demand total suspension?
