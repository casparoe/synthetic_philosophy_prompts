I was discussing with my friends over coffee about that philosophy problem where if we change climate policy now, the people born in 2100 will be totally different individuals—Parfit's nonidentity thing, right? So they can't complain about our emissions because they literally wouldn't exist without the carbon-heavy path that led to their parents meeting. But I think everyone is missing the darker flip side. By locking in the high-emissions timeline, we're not just failing to help some generic future people; we're acutally selecting against a specific counterfactual cohort—the ones who would have lived in the cleaner-world branch. It's like there's a shadow population that gets displaced or erased every time we choose coal over renewables. I tried to sketch a decision rule where you compute the "modal displacement cost" by comparing welfare integrals across the two possible future populations, but my friend said that's incoherent because those shadow people never actually exist to be harmed. But isn't that exactly the puzzle? If they don't exist in the actual world, how do we account for the moral loss of their never-being? I'm wondering if there's a formal framework—maybe using possible-worlds semantics with the person-affecting restriction lifted—that lets us treat these counterfactual individuals as carrying negative existence-value in our world, so we can factor them into cost-benefit analysis without falling into the repugnant conclusion. Does anyone actually model it this way, or is weighing "displaced" possible people just a category mistake I'm too stubborn to see?
