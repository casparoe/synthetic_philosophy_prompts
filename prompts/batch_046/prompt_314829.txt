Here is an argument I sketched for a workshop on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness that now strikes me as suspicious because, if sound, it would undermine not just illusionism but any theory that relies on first-person mental reports. (1) Illusionism holds that phenomenal properties—what-it-is-likeness—are never actually instantiated; instead, introspection systematically misrepresents physical or functional mental states as possessing such properties. (2) If phenomenal properties are never instantiated, then every first-person judgment attributing them, including judgments of the form it seems to me that there is something it is like to be in state S, is false, since the seeming in question is precisely the misrepresented phenomenal property. (3) Yet the illusionist's own case depends on taking first-person reports seriously: the illusionist explains why people report that there is something it is like, rather than explaining why people report that they hold false beliefs. (4) So the illusionist must treat at least some first-person reports as veridical—specifically, reports that there is an appearance of phenomenality—while treating others as false. (5) But there is no principled distinction within introspection between the appearance of phenomenality in general and the appearance of particular phenomenal qualities, such as the reddishness of a sensation, since both are delivered by the same introspective mechanism. (6) Therefore the illusionist is committed to the claim that introspection is globally unreliable about phenomenality, which removes the evidential basis for accepting illusionism in the first place. (7) Hence illusionism, if true, is self-defeating: it employs the very cognitive faculty it declares broadly untrustworthy. Where does this reasoning slip? I would prefer to keep the premise that introspection is broadly reliable about mental states, but I want to understand what it costs to reject step (5) rather than step (2).
