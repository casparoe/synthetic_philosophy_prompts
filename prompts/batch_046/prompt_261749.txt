I've been trying to wrap my head around Dewey's experimentalism—his idea that democracy isn't mainly voting or preference-aggregation but is basically social inquiry writ large, where a public solves shared problems through cooperative intelligence and shared experimentation. But I keep running into the same wall: Dewey seems to assume that people in a democratic community more or less agree on what the problem even is, and then they pool intelligence to solve it together. That feels like it misses the adversarial pluralism we actually live with, where different groups don't share problem-definitions, don't trust each other's methods, and aren't even playing the same epistemic game.

So I'm wondering which open questions or downstream lines of work in this area actually matter most if you take Dewey seriously as a framework for real institutions rather than just a historical curiosity. Is the crucial gap figuring out how cooperative inquiry can survive when participants reject the framing of the problem itself? Or is it that Dewey never really spelled out a theory of distributed epistemic division of labor—like, who does the experimenting, who checks the results, and how knowledge moves between specialized groups when the "public" is massive and fragmented? Or is the practical upshot mainly about restructuring deliberation versus decision-making, and everything else is just ornament? Give me a defended, dependency-aware prioritization: which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable without just hand-waving about "more dialogue," and which ones are overrated because solving them wouldn't actually change how democratic institutions function. I'm putting this together for a seminar discussion, so I don't need a reading list or citations—just an agenda for where the intellectual effort should actually go, including honest flagging of where Dewey's framework probably just breaks down.
