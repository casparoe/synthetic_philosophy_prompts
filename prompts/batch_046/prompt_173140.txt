I'm drafting a chapter where my protagonist—an ex-patent lawyer turned novelist, don't ask—gets into a bar argument about whether you can actually own an idea. She scribbles this chain on a napkin and now I'm stuck because it feels like it proves something insane, but I can't find the leak. Here it is, numbered like she's talking it through:

(1) If I mix my mental labor with an unclaimed concept—say, a narrative structure or a mathematical insight—I acquire a property right in it, the same way Locke argued with land.
(2) But mental labor is invisible; the only proof I've done it is that others can reproduce the result without repeating my effort.
(3) So to protect my property, the law has to stop others from reproducing the result, not just copying my particular notes or manuscript.
(4) That means copyright or patent must cover the idea itself, not just its expression, or else the labor-mixing claim is totally empty.
(5) Once the idea itself is enclosed, anyone who independantly arrives at the same structure or formula is still trespassing on my mental homestead, because they're using conceptual space I've already claimed.
(6) But if independant rediscovery counts as trespass, then every student who works out calculus on her own owes Newton's estate a fee, and every novelist who stumbles on a three-act arc owes some ancient playwright.
(7) Since that's obviuosly unworkable—society can't run if all learning is retroactively licensed—labor-mixing can't justify owning ideas.
(8) Yet if labor-mixing fails for mental goods, it ought to fail for physical goods too: if I build a fence, someone who builds an identical fence elsewhere isn't stealing my fence, just using unclaimed timber. So either we accept universal cognitive licensing, or we reject property rights altogether.

My protagonist wants to hang onto (1) and reject (7) or (8), but she feels like she's cheating. Where does the chain actually snap? Is (3) smuggling in something about visibility? Is (5) the bad step, and if I cut it, what happens to the idea-expression distinction? I need to know which link is weakest and what giving it up costs for someone who still wants to defend some form of IP. Don't just say it's obvious—this is for a novel, so I need the logical cost priced out clearly.
