I run a graduate seminar in philosophy of history—sixteen students, one ninety-minute session—where we have been working through the problem of contingency in historical explanation, specifically the tendency to read “for want of a nail” narratives backward from outcomes without being able to distinguish genuine dynamical chaos (small perturbations amplifying through sensitive dependence) from mere modal contingency (things could have gone otherwise without any amplification mechanism) or from causal overdetermination. I want a classroom simulation, not a lecture, that makes the students feel this knot from the inside: they should construct causal chains themselves, commit to them with a small non-monetary stake, and only then discover that the rules governing the outcomes were hidden from them and that identical or wildly divergent results can arise from the same or different minor inputs. Design the activity with concrete rules, assigned roles—for example, competing historiographical committees tasked with explaining a fictional succession crisis—broken into timed phases, printed-card materials, and a reputation-based payoff scheme that rewards persuasive explanation but costs points for being contradicted by the revealed regime map. The conceptual problem must emerge from what happens—groups should argue that a particular trivial choice “caused” the result—before any debrief names the distinction between chaos, contingency, and overdetermination. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto the corresponding part of the idea, predicts exactly what will go wrong the first time (for instance, one explanatory school dominating discussion, or students conflating unpredictability with randomness), and offers a practical fix for each failure. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real historical chaos the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce—almost certainly the long temporal scale over which sensitive dependence actually unfolds—so that I know precisely what I must supply verbally rather than through the rules.
