I'm writing a feature on identity and tech for my magazine, and at a roundtable last week I overheard two panelists — one a cognitive scientist, one a philosphy grad student — get into it over coffee. They were arguing about a hypothetical case: a patient gets every neuron replaced, one by one over a decade, with neuromorphic chips that copy spike-timing-dependent plasticity but share zero biochemistry with brain tissue. The patient reports perfect continuity of memory and personality. Here is how it went.

(1) The scientist said this proves anatta was right all along: there was never a self, just a stream of aggregates (skandhas) and causal links. Replace the substrate, the stream continues; asking 'is it the same person' is like asking 'is the river the same after all its water changes.'

(2) The grad student replied that even without a metaphysical soul, we need practical identity for moral responsibility and relationships. Functionalism explains continuity: same pattern, same person. But he pushed further — if the pattern is all that matters, then in principle we could copy it into two bodies at once. That means functionalism can't explain why I'd be terrified of the original being destroyed after copying, since both copies would be 'me.'

(3) The scientist said that's exactly the point — the fear comes from clinging (upadana) to a self that was never there. Conventionally we track one stream for practical reasons, but ultimately neither copy nor original is 'me.' The copy and the destroyed body are equally empty of self.

(4) The grad student said this smuggles in a conventional self to do the tracking. If both copies are equally 'not me,' then why should I care which stream gets tracked? Without a principle picking one stream as the continuation of *this* person, the view collapses into 'it doesn't matter which survives,' which feels wrong — it definatly matters to me.

Please think carefully, maybe do a quick brainstorm of the conceptual map first, then adjudicate. You are an expert philosopher of mind with deep knowledge of Buddhist philosophy. Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Tell me who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both parties are missing — especially whether anatta realy dissolves the duplication problem or just relocates it.
