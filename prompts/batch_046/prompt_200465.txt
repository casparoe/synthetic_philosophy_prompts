I am presenting a thesis defense for my paper on what I call localized dashboard illusionism, to be delivered next Thursday in the philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-science joint seminar. The position is that phenomenal consciousness is not globally non-existent, but is a specific interface-level user illusion: the brain's metacognitive monitoring system takes its own compressed, modality-independent summary variables—outputs of a high-level predictive model—and misattributes to them an intrinsic qualitative character that they lack. This is a local modeling error rather than a wholesale denial of experience; it is meant to explain both the explanatory gap and the apparent authority of introspection, without invoking phenomenal properties or collapsing consciousness into bare access. I further claim that this view generates empirically distinguishable predictions, specifically that introspection will systematically overestimate sensory detail and produce false stability judgments, and that it differs from both global illusionism and standard global-workspace broadcasting accounts.

The audience will be:
- A phenomenal realist with dualist leanings, who holds that any illusionist view fails because conscious experience is transparent and self-presenting, so that to deny its reality is to deny the very datum being explained.
- A physicalist phenomenal realist who accepts strong phenomenal concepts and argues that illusionism must either deny the existence of the data or fail to account for why those data seem irreducible.
- A predictive-processing researcher committed to global active inference, for whom consciousness is just the brain's best guess about its own attentional states; they will likely argue that my extra misattribution layer is redundant.
- A philosopher of science skeptical that treating consciousness as unobservable in principle by the system itself makes the view unfalsifiable, and who will press whether this is metaphysics disguised as cognitive modeling.
- Several graduate students, one of whom will ask whether this undermines the moral reality of suffering, and another who will specifically ask whether affective phenomenology (pain, pleasure) is subject to the same dashboard misattribution as sensory phenomenology.

Please prepare me for the objections. Give them in roughly the order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary each interlocutor would actually use. For each objection, specify: whether I should concede fully, concede a limited point, or hold firm; a concise reply of about two to three minutes suitable for a seminar defense; and the specific weak point in that reply. Order the objections by how damaging they would be if I failed to answer them well. Then identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—the challenge to the empirical distinguishability of my view from standard predictive-coding accounts—and tell me precisely what I need to have ready to defend rather than hoping it is overlooked. Conclude by identifying the single weakest point in my own overall position that I should actively prepare to defend rather than attempt to conceal.
