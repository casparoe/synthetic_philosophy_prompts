You serve on a joint parliamentary committee reviewing whether to recommend statutory codification of the monarch's reserve powers—specifically, the discretionary authority to appoint a prime minister when no party holds a parliamentary majority, and to refuse a dissolution request that would trigger an election within twelve months of the previous one. The proposal before the committee would replace these unwritten conventions with explicit statutory rules, assigning the appointment function to the parliamentary speaker and the dissolution timing to an automatic statutory clock, thereby eliminating royal discretion entirely. Proponents argue that in a modern democracy every exercise of state authority must trace back to an electoral mandate; retaining such powers in an unelected, hereditary office creates a democratic deficit that is not merely ceremonial but structurally consequential in moments of crisis. Opponents contend that the very absence of democratic accountability is what makes the monarchy functionally valuable in these roles: because the monarch possesses no electoral constituency, no party base, and no path to retaining office through popular appeal, commitments made through royal action carry a credibility that elected officials—who must always anticipate the next election—cannot replicate. They invoke an institutional-design framework that treats certain constitutional roles not as democratic agents but as credibility devices whose value lies precisely in being insulated from the incentive structures democracy creates.

Draft a formal committee memo—substantive but concise, suitable for circulation before a vote—recommending either adoption of the statutory proposal, its rejection, or adoption with specific amendments. Your recommendation must be concrete: if you retain royal discretion, specify which powers and under what procedural constraints (for example, mandatory consultation with a constitutional council, publication of reasons, or a time-bound parliamentary override); if you abolish it, specify the statutory mechanism that replaces it. Your reasoning should address the philosophical tension directly: is democratic legitimacy a property that applies uniformly to every state function, or can it permit—perhaps even require—non-democratic nodes that stabilize democratic procedures themselves? Give the strongest argument for the position you reject its full weight, and do not dismiss it as nostalgia or symbolism. The memo should have a clear bottom line and, if applicable, actionable conditions, written for committee members who must decide rather than for a general audience.
