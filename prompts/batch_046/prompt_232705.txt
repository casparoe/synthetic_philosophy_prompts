At our study group’s session on *The Brothers Karamazov*, Nadia and Jonas argued about whether the Grand Inquisitor’s defense of authoritarian happiness over Christ-like freedom collapses into a performative contradiction. Nadia claimed it does: if human beings genuinely prefer the certainty of bread and miracle to the burden of choice, then the Inquisitor’s own decision to suffer the burden of rule—sacrificing personal comfort to govern others—proves he exercises exactly the freedom he denies them. She said this is structurally analogous to Nozick’s experience machine, but inverted: the Inquisitor collectively plugs humanity into an apparatus of guaranteed satisfaction while he alone remains outside, enduring the reality he withholds, which shows that freedom is inescapable and that his “happiness” is merely a degraded pleasure rather than true eudaimonia. Jonas replied that Nadia imports an anachronistic Aristotelian framework Dostoevsky never employs. He offered instead a moral-hazard reading: freedom generates an externality of despair that the Church internalizes by removing choice, so the Inquisitor’s suffering is not free martyrdom but the unavoidable cost of absorbing risk on others’ behalf. Nadia countered that the text never describes people suffering on behalf of one another, only suffering from freedom itself, so there is no externality to internalize and Jonas has misread the economy of salvation. Jonas then argued that if Nadia’s inverted-machine analogy holds, the Inquisitor’s refusal to plug himself in demonstrates that he recognizes an authentic reality beyond the apparatus—but he denies that reality to others out of love, not contradiction, which saves the argument. They turned to me and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: assess Nadia’s performative-contradiction claim, her experience-machine inversion, Jonas’s moral-hazard framing, his correction about internal suffering, and his final defense of loving denial. Then take a definite overall position: is the Inquisitor’s argument genuinely self-defeating, or does it only appear so because modern readers project concepts of autonomy onto a pre-modern theological economy? Be committed—state which reading survives scrutiny, explain what the other side misses, and identify which specific point in the exchange is the weakest link.
