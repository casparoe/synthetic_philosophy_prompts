I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently defended a modified Humean account of aesthetic judgment. She argues that Hume's standard of taste—delicacy of sentiment, practice in comparison, and freedom from prejudice—functions not as a positive scale that ranks works from best to worst, but solely as a negative filter: it can reliably disqualify judgments that are coarse, biased, or uninformed, yet it offers no comparable authority for declaring any particular work genuinely beautiful. On her view, expert critics can converge on ruling out bad taste, but the survivors remain in an undifferentiated field where no further objective ranking is possible. I want a focused sequence of questions that makes this asymmetry hard to maintain. Specifically: ask how she distinguishes this position from ordinary aesthetic relativism; press her on how she explains the widespread tradition of praising specific masterpieces as genuinely superior, not merely uncorrupted by prejudice; include a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply—that true judges converge only on exclusions, not on positive rankings—and asks what that convergence actually proves; and end with a question about what empirical or cross-cultural evidence—say, persistent expert disagreement over a single widely studied work—would actually count against her view rather than being explained away as residual prejudice. Keep the questions free of biography and generic openings, make them accessible to an audience without formal philosophical training, and ensure they turn on the actual content of her negative-filter reading rather than on Hume scholarship in general.
