Design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about fifteen students that makes the force of ambiguity aversion in the Ellsberg setup felt before it is named. The session should center on a structured betting game using only course-credit points or small tangible rewards: in Phase One, each student individually chooses between betting on a draw from an urn with thirty red balls and sixty balls that are either black or yellow in an unknown proportion (the ambiguous urn) versus betting on a draw from an urn with a known fifty-fifty split (the risk urn). In Phase Two—the injected twist—students are randomly paired and given private cards: one partner receives a card stating they have observed a hidden sample of ten draws from the ambiguous urn but are forbidden by the rules from showing the card or describing the sample with numerical precision; the other partner receives a card stating they have observed nothing. The pair must negotiate and submit a single joint bet for both, with disagreement costing each partner a fixed penalty in points. The payoff scheme should reward correct joint predictions but penalize disagreement, so that the ambiguity is no longer merely private but intersubjective.
