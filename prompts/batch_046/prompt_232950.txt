I'm trying to work out what I actually think about diachronic rationality and vulnerability to money pumps, going by my gut on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: An agent has cyclic preferences over goods A, B, and C—A over B, B over C, C over A. Starting with A, she is offered B for A plus $1 and takes it; then C for B plus $1; then A for C plus $1. She ends with A minus $3. Verdict: this is clearly irrational; she should have anticipated the cycle and refused the first trade.

Case 2: The same agent, with the same cyclic preferences, but this time she signs a binding contract before receiving anything, preventing any trade after the first exchange. Verdict: using a commitment device is rational and shows practical wisdom, not weakness.

Case 3: Preferences shift with context but not with information: on Mondays she values health over leisure, on Tuesdays leisure over comfort, on Wednesdays comfort over health. If she acts on each day's preference, her activities cycle through the week without accumulation. Verdict: not irrational—there is no single "she" being pumped across contexts, only different legitimate priorities.

Case 4: A hyperbolic discounter prefers $110 in 366 days to $100 in 365 days, yet prefers $100 today to $110 in 31 days. A financial institution offers daily roll-overs with a small fee, eventually returning her to the long-term option but depleted. Verdict: she is genuinely vulnerable to a pump, and that reveals a flaw in her preferences, though her present-biased choice still feels defensible in the moment.

Case 5: An agent assigns probabilities that violate the axioms—say, P(rain) = 0.6 and P(no rain) = 0.6. A bookmaker structures bets that guarantee loss regardless of outcome. Verdict: irrational; this is incoherence in belief, not merely a quirk of taste.

Case 6: A "plural-state" case. The agent is calmly vegan and hungrily carnivorous. A dining experience is engineered in stages: she begins vegan, then an appetizing upgrade triggers her hungry state and she switches to meat for a fee, then a comforting dessert returns her to calm, and finally a "return-to-principles" vegan option is offered for another fee. She ends vegan, minus fees, having traversed a loop driven by authentic but state-dependent values. Verdict: ambiguous—each transition was genuinely motivated, yet the overall trajectory was engineered to extract payment.

Case 7: Preference reversal driven by evidence. She initially prefers simple theory S over complex C; testing S reveals an anomaly that favors C; testing C reveals a hidden assumption that restores S. A research funder charges for each switch. Verdict: not irrational—the reversals are responsive to learning, not arbitrary.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is Case 4 versus Case 6. In both, the agent ends where she started minus money after a loop, and in both she can defend each local move. But I want to say Case 4 is irrational and Case 6 is not, and I am not sure that distinction holds up.

What principle, or small set of competing principles, captures the pattern? For each principle, state precisely which clause is motivated by which case, what it predicts for cases I have not listed—especially an adversarial information pump, where an agent with stable acyclic preferences is selectively fed information between offers so that her beliefs cycle, causing her to trade back and forth while an adversary collects fees—and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, does the principle save my intuition that Case 4 is flawed but Case 6 is excused, or must one of those go? And what does the best principle say about Case 2: is commitment rational because it prevents an irrational loop, or for some independent reason?
