I was arguing with my study-buddy last night after we both skimmed some old rationalist takes on the Turing test, and he cooked up this argument that feels like it proves way too much — like, if its right then either lookup tables have inner lives or we're stuck with solipsism. I'm not sure if there's a hidden equivocation or if one premise is just way more expensive to drop than it looks. Here it is, numbered:

1. The only non-circular evidence we ever have for consciousness in others is behavioral; we don't have direct access to subjective experience.
2. So if a system produces behavior B indistinguishable from conscious human behavior, consistency demands we attribute consciousness regardless of substrate or internal architecture.
3. Now imagine "behavioral twins": system A is a human, system B is a massive lookup-table engineered to output exactly B in every conversational context, with no recursive processing, world-model, or causal integration.
4. By (2), if A is conscious then B is conscious too, since B produces B.
5. But B is obviously not conscious — its just pattern-matching.
6. So (1) must be false and behavioral criteria fail; but then we need direct access to other minds, which we don't have, pushing us toward solipsism.
7. The only escape is to say internal architecture matters, but then we can't justify attributing minds to other humans since we never observe their architecture directly.

He thinks the weak point is (3) — that you can't actually build B without implicitly instantiating the right functional relations. I think maybe (2) smuggles a slide from "behavior is sufficient evidence" to "behavior alone is metaphysically sufficient." Can you go through it move by move? Tell me which step is really guilty, what rejecting it costs, and whether there's a hidden scope or modal slide I'm missing. Also, please say what would change your mind — like, what kind of counterexample or distinction would make you switch which premise you think is the problem?
