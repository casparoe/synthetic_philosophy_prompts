I hold a position that fuses Dennett’s multiple-drafts model with a stronger conceptual-linguistic claim: not only is there no Cartesian theater—no singular neural locus or temporal slice at which experience is “presented” to a subject—but the grammar of “presentation to a subject” is itself a theoretical artifact. On this view, first-person reports do not describe an inner stream of consciousness; they are authoritative, self-constituting moves that stabilize one competing narrative fragment over others, primarily for social coordination, and there is no deeper fact about “what it was really like” prior to that stabilization. What would genuinely count against this position?

Please map the defeaters with care, ranking them from fatal to merely awkward. I want empirical findings—say, from global workspace neuroscience or studies of subjective report under disruption—that seem to reveal a unified, singular integrated stream rather than parallel competing drafts; phenomenological or introspective considerations that appear to disclose a seamless, non-fragmentary temporal flow that cannot be reconstructed as draft competition; and conceptual arguments showing that the performative analysis of reports either collapses into an untenable eliminativism about consciousness or smuggles back in a hidden theater after all. I also want to know which results in other domains—perhaps the philosophy of language on first-person authority, or work on personal persistence—this view stands or falls with.

Crucially, distinguish what a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as damaging from what they would dismiss as presupposing the very theatrical intuition being rejected. Is there any observation or formal result that could not be absorbed by redescription—reinterpreting the apparent counterexample as merely another draft achieving temporary dominance? If the view can always retreat to that move, has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a damaging vice? Finally, include an honest assessment of whether this position predicts anything that rival accounts—whether global-workspace theories or more traditional phenomenological approaches—do not, and whether that predictive exposure is sufficient to make the view genuinely risky rather than merely revisionary.
