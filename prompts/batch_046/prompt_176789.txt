Here’s the prompt they were given: Black’s two-spheres universe is usually taken to show that the Identity of Indiscernibles fails, since the spheres share every qualitative property including spatial relations. Some defenders of PII invoke relational differences or primitive thisness (haecceity) to save the principle. And here is the student answer (around 450 words):

Black imagines a universe with only two spheres, identical in every intrinsic and relational respect. This seems to violate Leibniz’s Law, which says if x and y share all properties then x equals y. Since sphere A and sphere B are not identical yet share all properties, PII is false.

One response is that they differ relationally: A is two miles from B, and B is two miles from A. But since the relation is symmetric, both have the same property — being two miles from another sphere. So that doesn’t distinguish them. Some philosophers invoke haecceity, a primitive thisness, but that feels like giving up on explanation.

Now for quantum mechanics: identical particles like electrons are indistinguishable and don’t have individual trajectories. This is just like Black’s spheres — nature allows duplicates. The Pauli exclusion principle says fermions can’t occupy the same state, but that doesn’t mean they have hidden properties; it just shows that even without qualitative differences we can have numerical plurality. So quantum mechanics actually supports Black’s intuition that PII isn’t necessary.

However, I think Black’s argument is stronger than people admit. Even if we accept haecceity it’s mysterious. And quantum symmetrization requires labeling particles 1 and 2 for the math, but that labeling proves we already presuppose their numerical identity. So in both philosophy and physics we see that numerical difference can float free of qualitative difference, and the symmetrization labels are ontologically real, not just formal.
