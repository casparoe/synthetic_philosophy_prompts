Question: Compare the function of defeat or retreat in the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada narrative, Kosovo Polje, and the Dunkirk myth. In particular, explain what makes Dunkirk an inverted case—where the myth transforms strategic retreat into moral victory rather than denying or redeeming defeat—and distinguish the specific political or psychological work each of the four narratives performs, rather than treating them as instances of a single compensatory mechanism.

Student answer: The Lost Cause and Masada both turn defeat into national pride by insisting that the losing side was morally superior. The Confederacy claimed it fought for states’ rights rather than slavery, and that Northern industrial might—not justice—decided the war. Masada works similarly: the Jewish rebels in 73 CE chose suicide over Roman captivity, and modern Israel uses this to show that surrender is worse than death, turning a military failure into proof of resolve. Kosovo Polje operates on the same logic for Serbs: in 1389 they lost to the Ottomans, but the myth claims Prince Lazar chose the heavenly kingdom over earthly victory, so defeat becomes a sacred sacrifice. Dunkirk is the inverted case because Britain was not actually defeated—over 330,000 troops were evacuated—so there is no defeat to deny. Instead, the myth celebrates the rescue as a moral triumph of civilian boats and collective endurance. All four narratives therefore serve an identical psychological function: they allow a people to feel pride despite military failure, with Dunkirk being different only because its underlying event was successful rather than disastrous.
