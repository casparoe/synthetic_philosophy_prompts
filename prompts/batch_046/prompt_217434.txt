I chair the evaluation panel for an interdisciplinary research council that funds applied environmental science. We regularly receive proposals claiming to move beyond the dominant predictive-statistical paradigm in climate and ecological modelling, yet reviewers cannot agree whether these represent genuine Kuhnian paradigm shifts, prolonged crisis-phase anomaly accumulation, or merely methodological refinements within normal science. The disagreement is costly: we either reject transformative work for being incommensurable with our evaluation metrics, or we fund incremental adjustments misclassified as revolutionary.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure that my panel—composed of domain scientists with no specialist training in history or philosophy of science—can apply to any proposal. The procedure should operationalise Kuhn's core distinctions (normal science, anomaly, crisis, paradigm shift, and incommensurability) without reducing them to vague intuition.

Specifically:

1. Provide a flowchart in words: an ordered sequence of no more than six decision nodes. At each node, state the precise question reviewers must answer, what evidence counts as sufficient, and what answer directs the proposal to the next node or to a terminal classification.

2. Address incommensurability explicitly: include a test for whether the proposal's evaluation criteria can be translated into the current paradigm's metrics (even with disagreement over weighting) or whether they reject those metrics as fundamentally inappropriate. Flag the exact point where this determination becomes a value judgement rather than a factual one.

3. Include three worked applications:
   a) A proposal refining deep-learning downscaling techniques within existing predictive climate modelling—expected classification: normal science.
   b) A proposal that abandons predictive accuracy entirely in drought forecasting, arguing that mechanistic process-fidelity is the only valid success criterion and that statistical error metrics are conceptually irrelevant—expected classification: potential paradigm shift with incommensurable metrics.
   c) Design a third proposal that forces the unwelcome answer: the procedure classifies it in a way that conflicts with the panel's likely funding preference (for instance, a proposal that meets all shift criteria but addresses a problem the council considers trivial, or an incremental proposal that technically triggers a crisis classification because it undermines a single core assumption). Explain why the procedure yields this answer and what it reveals about the procedure's limits.

4. Mark every point where judgement cannot be eliminated—where reasonable reviewers might diverge despite following the procedure correctly—and distinguish these from points where divergence indicates a procedural error.

5. Conclude with an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do: particularly, whether it can distinguish retrospectively between a failed shift and a successful refinement, and whether it can resolve disputes about the desirability of a shift once it is identified.

Write in formal British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not depend on obscure theoretical texts that a generalist model could not reasonably recall; ground the procedure in the conceptual structure of Kuhn's framework as commonly understood.
