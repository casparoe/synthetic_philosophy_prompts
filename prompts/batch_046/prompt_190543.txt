I've been observing a recurring structural pattern in institutional and inter-group ethics that I can't quite map onto the standard taxonomy of compromise theories, and I'm trying to determine whether there is an established concept that captures precisely this configuration. Imagine two moral communities whose evaluative frameworks are incommensurable in a robust sense—not merely different in emphasis, but organized around goods that resist translation into a common metric. One might assess practices primarily through collective ritual purity and boundary maintenance; the other through individual welfare and distributive fairness. When they must share a resource—say, a watershed governed by both ceremonial restriction rules and equitable access norms—they do not converge on a shared principle. Instead, they arrive at an institutional arrangement that violates the pure demands of both frameworks: some allocations are neither fully just by the second community's lights nor fully pure by the first's, yet the arrangement is stable, and each side prefers it to the collapse of cooperation. The striking feature is not just that both sides "give something up," but that the resulting institution appears to sustain a cooperative surplus—value or stability—that neither framework can represent or justify from within its own vocabulary. If either group attempts to rationalize the arrangement entirely in its native terms, the equilibrium dissolves. Concrete analogues come to mind: interfaith burial agreements that satisfy neither theology's full requirements for the dead yet prevent desecration and maintain communal peace; or environmental accords that neither deep-ecological nor strongly anthropocentric frameworks endorse in principle, yet both prefer to unilateral extraction.

What I'm specifically trying to isolate is this surplus-generating feature of compromise between genuinely incommensurable value systems—the way a joint practice can occupy an interstitial moral space that is, in effect, ontologically prior to neither party's values, producing stability and mutual benefit that disappear under monolingual moral description. I want to distinguish this sharply from ordinary political compromise, from Rawlsian overlapping consensus, and from a simple modus vivendi sustained by power balance. I'm also not asking about value pluralism in general, or about whether compromise can be rational. Rather, I'm asking whether philosophers have named and analyzed the specific phenomenon in which the compromise itself generates a distinct kind of joint value—unrepresentable within either constituent framework—that makes the arrangement more than a grudging split-the-difference bargain. Does this configuration have an established term? If not, how exactly should it be carved apart from related notions like moral trade-offs, hybrid institutions, or incommensurable goods coexisting under practical constraint? I would particularly value a clarification of whether this surplus should be treated as a conceptually significant feature of inter-framework cooperation or merely as an accidental byproduct of strategic accommodation.
