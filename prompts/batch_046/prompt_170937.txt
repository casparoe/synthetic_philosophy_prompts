As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, I find myself returning to the same question whenever autonomous systems fail in the real world: who actually bears the moral weight? I was discussing this with a former colleague over coffee, and the conversation turned to the 1988 downing of Iran Air Flight 655 by the USS Vincennes, an episode I remember well from the news of my working years. To recall the salient facts: in July 1988 the Aegis combat system aboard the Vincennes misidentified the civilian airliner as an attacking F-14, and despite the crew's stress and conflicting data, Captain William Rogers ordered the missiles fired, killing all 290 passengers and crew; the United States eventually paid compensation but never admitted legal liability, and Rogers received no disciplinary action. I have since read, in rather general terms, about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems—how the diffusion of agency between human operators, software, and institutional design can leave no party clearly accountable. Could you analyze the Vincennes incident as a disciplined case study in precisely this kind of gap, mapping which actors—the captain, the Aegis designers, the Navy's rules of engagement—occupied which positions in the chain of decision, and whether the episode exemplifies a structural gap, an epistemic gap, or something closer to what some recent writers call a moral crumple zone, in which the human operator absorbs blame that properly belongs to the system? I am particularly interested in whether the theory predicts that Rogers should have been held responsible, or whether it predicts that he was the wrong target altogether, and what the fact that he was neither punished nor formally exonerated tells us about whether responsibility-gap theory describes a real absence of accountability or merely a failure to assign it.
