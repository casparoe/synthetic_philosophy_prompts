I am currently researching a feature on the metaphysics of moral agency, and I keep returning to Anthony Burgess's A Clockwork Orange as a narrative that seems to stage the very dispute between compatibilism and incompatibilism. The standard reading treats the Ludovico Technique as an obvious violation of free will: by conditioning Alex against violence through aversive therapy, the state robs him of the ability to choose evil, and therefore of any genuine moral worth. Yet I want to test whether this reading is too quick. Might Burgess be closer to a hierarchical compatibilist — say, in the Frankfurt tradition — for whom freedom lies not in the bare absence of causal determination but in the coherence between first-order impulses and reflective second-order volitions? The conditioning does not merely suppress Alex's violence; it appears to destroy his capacity to identify with or reject his own desires, which is precisely what such an account says matters. At the same time, the prison chaplain's insistence that goodness must come from within, and the ambiguous final chapter in which Alex's violent capacities return without any corresponding restoration of reflective endorsement, seem to pull in the opposite direction, suggesting an incompatibilist intuition that unconditioned, open-ended choice carries intrinsic value regardless of its content.

I would like a sustained critical reading that takes both the philosophical framework and the novel's formal details seriously: which scenes genuinely illuminate the compatibilist claim that determination and freedom are not mutually exclusive, and where does the text resist that interpretation by dramatizing the idea that engineered compliance is morally hollow even if it produces identical behaviour? I am particularly interested in whether Burgess uses Alex's first-person narration — with its shifting registers of ultraviolence and affected rehabilitation — to show that the absence of second-order identification is phenomenologically distinct from mere constraint, or whether that narrative voice ultimately collapses the distinction and vindicates the chaplain's view. Crucially, I am not after a bibliography, a survey of secondary literature, or any citation of recent papers; I want an interpretation that a philosophically literate reader could construct from the concepts and the plot alone, one that remains faithful to the novel's resistance to being reduced to an illustration, and that ends by saying what Burgess adds to the debate that pure argument cannot capture.
