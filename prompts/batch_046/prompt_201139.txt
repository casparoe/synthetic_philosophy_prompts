(b) Virtù is not moral goodness but practical effectiveness — the capacity to read shifting circumstances and the resolve to act decisively when they change.

(c) A prince should aim to be feared rather than loved, because fear lies within his control, whereas love depends on the people's fickle moods, which sounds like leaving too much to fortune.

(d) At the same time, a ruler needs a small inner circle of advisors bound by genuine personal affection and shared loyalty, not by fear, because only that kind of trust survives the sudden reversals that fortune brings.

(e) Cesare Borgia proves that perfect virtù can overcome almost any fortune: he rose from nothing through sheer ability, and was ruined only by an unforeseeable coincidence — his father's death occurring together with his own illness — which actually shows that, without that one fluke, he would have prevailed.

(f) I also take Machiavelli's advice to study history as being mainly about copying the successful actions of great men, giving a kind of blueprint that reduces one's exposure to fortune, rather than about learning to adapt to entirely new situations.
