The debate in analytic philosophy of religion over Molinism—whether God possesses middle knowledge of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—has persisted for decades without resolving into either a settled formal result or a clear conceptual mistake. Molinists maintain that counterfactuals such as “if Peter were placed in circumstance C, he would freely deny Christ” are true prior to any divine decree and ground God’s ability to actualize particular worlds without determining free choices. Critics, including many Thomists and open theists, deny that such counterfactuals can be metaphysically prior to God’s creative will: some argue that they would be “brute” facts that constrain omnipotence, others that free will is incompatible with the fixity required for God to know them in advance, and still others that the very notion of a “counterfactual of freedom” collapses under scrutiny once we distinguish logical from metaphysical possibility.

Yet the literature often feels circular. Molinists respond by refining decision-theoretic models of divine deliberation and appealing to intuitive modal judgments about what agents “would” do; critics respond by sharpening arguments that freedom requires the absence of such determinately true counterfactuals, or by questioning whether middle knowledge genuinely explains providence rather than renaming it. Empirical experiments are irrelevant, formal results in modal logic have not adjudicated the dispute, and both sides claim the other is misunderstanding “freedom” or “knowledge.” What kind of stalemate is this?

Is the disagreement verbal—do “counterfactual of freedom” and “pre-volitional truth” track different concepts for each side, masked by shared vocabulary? Is it substantive but hidden: a dispute not about God but about the metaphysics of grounding, specifically whether counterfactual truths require actualist grounds or can float freely across worlds? Or is it a clash over what counts as an adequate explanation of divine cognition, with Molinists demanding an account of how God knows without causing, and critics demanding an account of how such knowledge avoids compromising autonomy? Could there also be a bedrock divergence in modal intuitions—about which possible worlds are “near” enough to ground true counterfactuals—that functions as unargued data for both sides?

Please diagnose the structure of the impasse with reference to what proponents and critics actually commit themselves to: do they differ about facts, about standards of explanatory adequacy, about which modal or metaphysical considerations are admissible, or about the framing of the original question (epistemic—how God knows—versus metaphysical—what makes counterfactuals true)? Offer your diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of interpretations. Then, given that diagnosis, specify what would count as progress: a formal distinction (for instance, separating the logic of counterfactuals from their metaphysical grounding), a conceptual shift (reframing the problem in terms of explanation rather than foreknowledge), or an argument that the question is ill-posed. If you think the disagreement is ineliminable, explain why—whether because it rests on intuitions that neither side can justify without begging the question, or because the two sides are answering different questions under one name—and say whether that ineliminability is a philosophical failure or simply the sign of a genuinely hard boundary.
