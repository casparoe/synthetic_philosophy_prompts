In my novels I often place characters at decision points where a remote, almost unimaginable catastrophe competes with an immediate, tangible wrong; the mathematics of expectation suggests one course, the architecture of narrative conscience suggests another. This tension mirrors, I believe, a philosophical impasse that has persisted for years without resolution. Proponents of expected-value rationality—particularly in discussions of existential risk and longtermist ethics—argue that when outcomes vary by orders of magnitude, even probabilities approaching zero can dominate deliberation, yielding obligations that swamp ordinary moral concerns. Their critics, drawing on what might be called narrative-bounded rationality, respond that practical reasoning is not an aggregation of weighted worlds but a structured trajectory requiring epistemic proximity: a reason must be narratively accessible—capable of being woven into the agent's ongoing story—to count as a genuine motive. Both sides accept the formal apparatus of expectation; both concede that human cognition is not optimized for astronomical scales; both invoke identical cases, from modified Pascalian wagers to St. Petersburg-style gambles. Yet neither has persuaded the other. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on whether rationality denotes an ideal calculative procedure or a practice of coherent self-authorship? Is there a hidden empirical claim about how agents actually integrate distant probabilities into decision-making, masked as a normative dispute? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about global optimality conditions, the other about local integrity conditions—under the single heading of expected value fanaticism? Given this diagnosis, what would constitute progress: a formal result showing that narrative coherence constraints are mathematically equivalent to a specific probability threshold, an experimental study measuring how subjects weight outcomes that exceed their temporal horizon, or a reconceptualization that dissolves the problem by distinguishing theoretical expectation from practical wisdom? And if no such progress is available, does that indicate that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it asks of human reason something it was never structured to deliver?
