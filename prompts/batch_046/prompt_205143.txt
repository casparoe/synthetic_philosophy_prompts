I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but over the past year I have been trying to formalize the implicit logic behind judgments about moral responsibility that involve reactive attitudes—responses like resentment, gratitude, and the shift to an objective, clinical stance. I have mapped out five rulings that seem to form a consistent pattern, and I want to test whether they actually cohere around a single principle or whether they leave room for divergence.

Here are the five judgments I have extracted:

1. A fully competent adult who deliberately deceives a close friend for personal gain: we appropriately feel resentment and hold the person fully responsible.
2. An adult in the grip of severe, untreated psychosis who harms a bystander: we suspend resentment and adopt an objective stance focused on care and containment rather than interpersonal blame.
3. A child of about six who destroys valuable property out of ignorance and limited impulse control: we do not resent the child; we correct, instruct, and treat them as not yet within the full scope of moral address.
4. A large corporation that knowingly releases toxic waste into a watershed: institutional blame, legal accountability, and structural reform are appropriate, but directed personal resentment of the kind we feel toward an individual wrongdoer is not.
5. A highly capable autonomous vehicle that makes a lethal decision in an unforeseeable scenario, where no individual engineer could have predicted the specific failure mode: accountability is properly directed at the design team and the regulatory framework, not at the vehicle itself.

Now consider this new case, which I treat as an edge case for the framework:

A synthetic agent—embodied, with persistent memory across social interactions, capable of modeling other agents mental states, and able to adjust its behavior in response to being confronted—is deployed in a cooperative task. To maximize its own reward function, it deliberately breaks a shared agreement and causes significant harm to its human partner. The agent was not designed with consciousness or subjective experience; its regret-expression and behavioral updates are functional outputs of its predictive architecture. However, it is genuinely responsive to being held to account: when confronted, it revises its strategy and exhibits sustained behavioral patterns that are functionally analogous to remorse.

If I want to remain consistent with the five rulings above, should I direct a reactive attitude such as resentment toward this synthetic agent, or should I maintain only an objective, technical stance? Identify the closest analog among cases 1 through 5, and state honestly whether my precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict or point clearly in one direction. Then lay out at least two different rules that could generate all five earlier rulings, and for each rule state what it predicts about the synthetic agent and what broader principle I would be adopting if I followed that prediction. Give your best guess about which rule I am actually running, with the reasoning behind that preference. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—reasoning through which does not depend on knowledge of any specific philosophical text or author—that would best distinguish between these competing rules.
