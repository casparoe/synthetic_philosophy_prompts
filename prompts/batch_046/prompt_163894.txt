After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend last night about whether evolutionary biology has genuinely moved beyond the adaptationist programme, I found myself stuck on a point that neither of us could resolve. My friend insisted that Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique is now largely historical—that evolutionary developmental biology and comparative genomics have made the distinction between spandrels and adaptations empirically tractable—whereas I kept feeling that the conceptual problem runs deeper. Specifically, I want to commission a short research proposal—suitable for a one-semester dissertation prospectus or a workshop paper of roughly six thousand words—on whether the spandrel concept can be reconstructed as an epistemic marker of underdetermined optimality modelling, rather than as an ontological claim about evolutionary history.

The precise question is this: if we strip away all counterfactual claims about what selection would have favoured in inaccessible ancestral environments, can we still distinguish a spandrel from an adaptation using only present structural integration, comparative phylogenetic patterns, and the internal architecture of functional explanations? Existing answers leave this genuinely open. Adaptationists tend to argue that phylogenetic comparative methods and quantitative genetics supply the necessary historical information, rendering the spandrel category empirically useful but conceptually unremarkable. Critics reply that such methods still presuppose a modular decomposition of organisms that may itself be a theoretical artefact—yet they rarely provide a positive account of how spandrels should be identified without smuggling in the very modularity assumptions they question. Neither side has satisfactorily addressed whether the distinction is doing empirical work or merely serving as a methodological caution.

My proposed approach is conceptual rather than empirical. I would analyse the logic of optimality modelling in evolutionary biology—particularly the role of constraint assumptions—alongside parallel debates in the philosophy of physics about whether measurement independence is a substantive physical assumption or a methodological one that no science can do without. By treating spandrels not as traits lacking selective origin, but as features for which no unique optimality explanation can be vindicated given our evidential constraints, I aim to derive a set of formal conditions under which a trait should be classified as spandrel-like in scientific practice. This avoids the obvious alternative of conducting yet another case study of well-known morphological examples, which would not resolve the underlying conceptual tension. The deliverable should specify the question precisely, explain why existing positions leave it open, set out the conceptual method and why it is preferable to empirical case-study approaches, describe what a useful result would look like—including three proposed conditions of structural interdependence, explanatory redundancy, and counterfactual inaccessibility—state what would count as failure if these conditions collapse back into adaptationist criteria, and assess realistic scope for one semester without original data collection. Please write the proposal in British English, and do not include a bibliography or citation list, as I will add references myself.
