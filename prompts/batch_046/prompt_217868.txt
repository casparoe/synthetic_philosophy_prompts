You are a philosopher of social science with deep familiarity with Max Weber's sociology and the history of explanatory debates in the human sciences. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the different structural ways long-standing disagreements become stuck—whether they are verbal, normative, methodological, or conceptual—so that your diagnosis is deliberate rather than reflexive.

The debate I have in mind concerns Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis and, more broadly, whether culture or values can ever function as a genuine cause rather than as an epiphenomenal gloss on material conditions. For over a century, defenders have argued that Calvinist asceticism formed an elective affinity with rational capitalist accumulation—shaping motives, legitimating profit, and altering everyday economic conduct—while critics have countered that the correlation is spurious, that Catholic regions such as Belgium developed comparable capitalism without the theological trigger, or that institutional, geopolitical, and class-based factors fully explain the variance Weber attributed to ethos. More recently, quantitative work in cultural economics has tried to resolve the matter with cross-national regressions linking Protestant heritage to growth, entrepreneurship, or trust, yet the philosophical core of the dispute remains untouched: materialist critics treat values as downstream of interests and structures, while Weberians treat meaningful orientation as an irreducible link in the causal chain. The sides share substantially the same historical records and increasingly the same datasets, yet neither has moved meaningfully.

My working hypothesis is that this stalemate is not primarily empirical but concerns what an explanation in history must do—specifically, whether invoking actors' subjective meanings adds causal power that could not be captured by institutional or economic variables alone. I want you to diagnose the disagreement precisely: Is it verbal, with both sides using cause differently? Is there a hidden formal question about whether social science requires intentional-level laws or mechanisms? Are the sides actually answering different questions—one about necessary conditions for capitalism's emergence, another about sufficient conditions, and another about the phenomenology of modern rationalization? Or is the question ill-posed because culture is being asked to do explanatory work it was never designed to do? Then tell me what kind of progress could genuinely move this debate: a conceptual distinction, a formal model integrating meaning and mechanism, an empirical result that one side genuinely could not absorb, or an argument that no further progress should be expected and why.
