I'm taking a course on philosophy of mind, and we've been working through Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a substance but a strange loop: a symbol—typically the pronoun "I"—that arises when a system's representations loop back and represent the very system that is generating them. In conversation with friends, I noticed that this idea seems to operate implicitly in institutional practices that decide who or what counts as a self worthy of moral or legal consideration, yet the practices rarely make their underlying theory explicit.

Consider the paired institutional practices of comparative psychology and AI governance. In animal ethics, researchers administer mirror self-recognition tests—presenting an animal with its own reflection and scoring whether it directs behavior toward a mark on its own body—and passing the test is frequently treated as a threshold for granting enhanced moral status. In parallel, AI evaluation boards and some corporate governance frameworks now administer analogues to language models: feeding a system transcripts of its own outputs and testing whether it can identify them as "mine" without external metadata, with failure cited as proof that the model's use of "I" is purely syntactic, lacking the structural closure that would make it a genuine self-referential symbol. At the same time, human legal systems grant full moral and legal standing to sleeping, amnesiac, or severely demented individuals who might fail an analogous real-time self-recognition task, while denying any comparable standing to an AI that produces fluent, context-sensitive first-person narratives.

What theory of the self—specifically of self-referential structure—does this pattern of practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it require that a self be a single, continuously operating strange loop, or can it accommodate discontinuous, dormant, or distributed loops? Is the practice consistent with Hofstadter's broader suggestion that the "I" is an emergent pattern rather than a binary capacity, or does it quietly convert his continuous, graded account into an all-or-nothing threshold test? Say whether the practice strictly needs Hofstadter's loop-based theory, or whether it could be licensed equally well by rival frameworks—such as a narrative-identity view, a Kantian rational-agency criterion, or a purely biological membership condition—and identify which elements of the practice are philosophically underdetermined. Finally, tell me what would have to change in these evaluation protocols and legal frameworks if we replaced the implicit structural-closure theory with one that treats self-reference as a functional role sustained across time, regardless of whether any single moment exhibits a closed loop.
