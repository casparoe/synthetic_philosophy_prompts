In discussions of Bertrand’s paradox—specifically the classic chord-in-a-circle case, where selecting a chord “uniformly” yields probabilities of 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether one parameterizes by radius, midpoint position, or endpoint angle—the disagreement seems to have settled into a durable stalemate that outlasts every formal refinement. One camp holds that the paradox demonstrates the principle of indifference is incoherent or inapplicable over continuous spaces that lack a canonical measure; a second camp argues that the paradox merely exposes ambiguity in problem formulation, and that once a physical selection mechanism or symmetry group is precisely specified, an invariant measure emerges and the contradiction dissolves; a third treats the dispute as pedagogical, conceding that parameterizations diverge but insisting this reveals a feature of conditional probability rather than any crisis in rational belief. Despite the introduction of group-invariance arguments, maximum-entropy resolutions, and careful distinctions between epistemic and physical interpretations of chance, none of these sides has persuaded the others, and each can reproduce the opponents’ formal results while denying that those results address the core concern.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not mathematical but structural: the camps disagree about what it means for a problem to be “well-posed” prior to any probabilistic assignment, with one side demanding uniqueness independent of parameter specification and the other treating uniqueness as an artifact of that very specification. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the legitimacy of non-measurable sets, or about whether symmetry arguments tacitly presuppose the principle they defend—actually sustaining the divide? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations: is this a verbal dispute about the word “uniformity,” a substantive disagreement about the ontology of chance in continuous spaces, or are the sides answering different questions—one about rational credence, one about physical mechanism—under a single heading?
