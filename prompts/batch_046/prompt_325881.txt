Consider the practice Benjamin Franklin describes in his autobiographical writings: he enumerates thirteen virtues, dedicates each to a particular day of the week for concentrated practice, and logs his lapses in a small notebook carried on his person. As the project proceeds, he reorders the virtues, reinterprets their scope, and introduces adjustments that he compares to a printer issuing corrections to successive editions, all while maintaining that full moral perfection lies beyond human reach. This is not merely Aristotelian habituation, since it treats moral development as an iterative, self-correcting engineering scheme with explicit performance metrics and version updates; it is also not Stoic spiritual exercise, because it depends on external tracking instruments and treats inevitable partial failure as a structural feature rather than a remediable defect. Is there an established term in moral philosophy or moral psychology for this precise configuration—an iterative, metric-tracked, self-correcting virtue practice oriented toward an endpoint that is acknowledged to be unattainable? If no single term captures it, can you coin one and define its boundaries, distinguishing it sharply from habituation, behavioral self-tracking, and Stoic spiritual exercises, and identifying which of those neighboring concepts comes closest?
