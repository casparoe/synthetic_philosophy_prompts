I'm a clinical risk manager at a mid-sized hospital, and every Tuesday I chair a ninety-minute morbidity-and-mortality review attended by nursing, pharmacy, and attending-physician representatives. For internal corrective-action tracking only—not for legal liability determination—we must classify whether a failure-to-act (omitting a scheduled lab check, failing to escalate a vital-sign change, withholding a standard prophylaxis) was a causally contributing factor in an adverse patient outcome, or merely a concurrent absence. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical analysis of causation by omission, that a clinician without philosophy training can apply in about fifteen minutes per case once the clinical facts are established. The procedure must distinguish true causation-by-omission from three confounds: bare correlation with an absent event; normative failure that did not alter the outcome trajectory; and omissions that appear causal only because the comparison class is artificially restricted. Deliver a flowchart-in-words with ordered steps, explicit pass-fail or yes-no tests at each stage, clear flags for points where clinical judgment cannot be eliminated, and an explicit escalation rule that returns "indeterminate—committee vote required." It must also state honestly what it cannot do, such as assigning legal blame or resolving metaphysical disputes about negative causation. In constructing the procedure, inject and operationalize one specific niche distinction: treat an omission as causally active only when it interrupts an active maintenance trajectory (a sustained clinical process that was actively being kept within safe bounds) rather than when it merely allows a static state to persist; build this "maintenance-interruption filter" into step two of the checklist. Apply the full procedure to three worked cases: first, a post-operative patient whose scheduled anticoagulant was not restarted for forty-eight hours, after which a stroke occurred, where the procedure should likely return "causal omission"; second, a septic patient for whom a specialist consultation was never requested, but who was already in irreversible multi-organ failure before the consultation window opened, where the procedure should return "normative failure without causal contribution"; and third, a case where the procedure technically clears the omission as non-causal only because the baseline trajectory used was the hospital protocol rather than the patient's actual prior physiological state—an answer that will upset the nursing staff—showing exactly how to document that tension without suppressing it. Fix the role, the decision frequency, the stakeholder sign-off requirements, and the time constraint in the opening framing, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works.
