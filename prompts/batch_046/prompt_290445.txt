I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether the self is literally a fiction, and I kept returning to the idea—often called the self as a center of narrative gravity—that personal identity is not a substance but an abstract organizational point around which autobiographical events cluster and from which actions are attributed. I want to teach this to a reading group of non-specialist colleagues in law and literature—bright people, but with no training in philosophy of mind—without reducing it to “we tell stories about ourselves” and without letting it drift into mysticism. Plan the explanatory arc: the dependency order in which the pieces must be introduced, specifying what conceptual ground has to be in place before the “gravity” analogy can even be introduced, why physical analogies must come only after the functional claim is secured, and which idea must never precede which. Identify the single concrete case or scenario that should carry the idea—something that involves competing attributions of a single action and a resolution that does not appeal to an inner observer—and state precisely what that example must contain and what it must lack. Map the misconceptions they will predictably form: treating narrative coherence as causal efficacy, confusing the center of gravity with a homunculus, importing physical determinism into the analogy, and the specific error of thinking that if the self is “only” a narrative center then moral responsibility dissolves; for each, specify the point in the sequence where it should be defused and how. Give me an observable check—something I can detect in their questions or responses—that would show they have grasped the functional point rather than merely memorized the metaphor. Tell me explicitly what to leave out at this level, such as whether to mention phenomenological accounts of the minimal self or technical literature on predictive processing and self-modeling, and state that omission openly. Finally, separate what is broadly settled in the relevant philosophical and cognitive-scientific literature from what remains genuinely contested—particularly regarding whether narrative integration is a necessary condition for selfhood or only one contingent organizational form among others—and do not present any contested view as if it were the consensus.
