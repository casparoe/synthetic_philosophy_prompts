I am drafting a philosophical dialogue for a novel in which one character defends a rigorous nominalism and another attempts to show that the position collapses into a skepticism about scientific reasoning itself. For the nominalist's central speech, I have constructed the following argument, but I find it suspicious: each premise appears defensible in isolation, yet together they yield the conclusion that inductive justification is impossible regardless of one's ontology. I wish to embed this instability in the dialogue precisely, but I need to know exactly where the chain breaks so that my characters can address it with philosophical accuracy rather than mere rhetoric.

(1) If universals exist, they must be either transcendent—wholly outside space and time—or immanent—literally present within each particular that instantiates them.
(2) If they are transcendent, they stand in no causal or spatiotemporal relation to observers, so we can never have empirical knowledge of them; if they are immanent, then what is present in particular a at location L and in particular b at location M are numerically distinct features, not one shared feature multiplied.
(3) Thus, on either realist account, we never genuinely observe shared properties—only particular resemblances among distinct entities.
(4) Scientific induction requires a justified belief that unobserved particulars share properties with observed ones.
(5) If there are no genuinely shared properties—only particular resemblances—then there is no objective ground for extending a property from observed cases to unobserved ones.
(6) Therefore, if realism is true, induction lacks an observable ground; and if realism is false, induction lacks an objective ground.
(7) Hence, regardless of whether universals exist, scientific induction is unjustified.
(8) Yet induction is clearly justified in practice and forms the basis of empirical knowledge.
(9) So at least one premise must be rejected, though I cannot see which without abandoning either our epistemology or our ontology.

The conclusion that induction is undermined either way strikes me as precisely the sort of reductio that proves too much. Which step should my nominalist concede, and what does conceding it cost her position? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve the integrity of inductive practice and the nominalist's rejection of transcendent entities—keeping (1) through (3) intact and locating the error elsewhere—but I want to understand the precise philosophical price of each available alternative, including any hidden equivocation between epistemic access and ontological commitment, or between particular resemblance and objective similarity.
