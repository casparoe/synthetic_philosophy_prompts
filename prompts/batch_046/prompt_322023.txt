I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I'm preparing the question sequence for a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends what she calls the proprioceptive mediation hypothesis regarding Molyneux's problem. On her view, the negative answer to whether a newly sighted subject can immediately identify shapes by vision alone is not best explained by a lack of shared conceptual structure between touch and sight, nor by a raw sensory deficit, but by a misalignment between vision's initially egocentric spatial frame and the body-centered coordinate system that tactile exploration has already calibrated through motor action. She argues that cross-modal recognition becomes possible only after a period of active spatial re-mapping mediated by proprioceptive feedback, which suggests that Molyneux's question should be reframed as asking about the temporal dynamics of sensorimotor integration rather than about innate or learned conceptual correspondence.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual mechanics of this position, not on biographical details. At least one question should address how her account handles the classic Lockean framing of the problem—particularly the idea that tactile ideas of extension are qualitatively different from visual ones—anticipating her likely reply that proprioceptive schemas provide a common metric, and then following up with whether this metric is genuinely amodal or merely a third, hybrid modality. Another should engage the empirical literature on congenital cataract reversal, specifically asking how she interprets findings that some subjects show partial immediate matching despite lacking prolonged motor calibration, with a follow-up keyed to her probable appeal to residual subcortical cross-modal pathways. I also want a question that targets the normative implications of her view: if recognition depends on active calibration rather than passive sensory convergence, does this commit her to an enactivist claim that perception is constituted by action, and if so, how does she avoid collapsing into the stronger thesis that there is no perception without movement? Finally, include one question that probes the boundary conditions of her hypothesis—perhaps by asking whether an artificial agent equipped with haptic sensors and a visual feed but lacking an analog to proprioceptive body-schema could, in principle, solve its own version of Molyneux's problem, and what that result would imply for whether her view is specifically about human embodiment or about a more general architecture of cross-modal learning.
