I'm delivering the opening remarks at a memorial colloquium for Professor Mara Chen, who taught comparative philosophy for thirty-five years and died last month at seventy-two. The audience is split: half are analytic philosophers of mind and language who knew her rigorous work on self-reference and comparative metaphysics; half are her family, students from other disciplines, and colleagues from outside philosophy who want to remember her as a person rather than as an entry in a bibliography. She was captivated—not mystically, but philosophically—by Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, specifically by a reading that treats it not as a sweeping skeptical argument for universal doubt but as a localized collapse of the authority we claim over the boundary between being a subject and being an object of experience. On her view, the dream does not prove that all experience is illusory; rather, it shows that the first-person certainty that I am awake now, and that I am the one who remembers having been the butterfly, both depend on a narrative framing that the bare experience itself does not secure.

I need these remarks to run about ten minutes and to do real structural work: they should honor her intellectual life without turning into a seminar paper, comfort people who are grieving without asserting anything she would have rejected—such as that she is watching over us from some ontologically distinct realm, or that her consciousness has migrated into another form—and they should be fully intelligible to the non-philosophers in the room without being patronizing to the experts. The butterfly image should govern what I can and cannot say: for instance, I should be able to speak of her presence in this room as a matter of how we continue to be shaped by her, not as a literal persistence of a soul-substance, and I should avoid any language that presupposes a sharp metaphysical fact of the matter about whether she really still exists in some transformed mode. Please write the remarks with that constraint active throughout the architecture of the speech. Do not include citations, a reading list, or references to specific secondary literature; she always insisted that the point was in the image, not in the footnotes.
