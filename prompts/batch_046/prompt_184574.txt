In my rationalist-adjacent reading circles, the debate over what the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result actually tells us about market epistemology has been stuck for years. One camp treats the theorem as a structural benchmark: if traders share common priors and it is common knowledge that each is rational and profit-maximising, then the mere willingness of another party to trade ought to dissolve your belief that you hold private information they lack. Because real markets display enormous volume, at least one condition fails, and—so they argue—this undermines the stronger claim that prices fully aggregate dispersed knowledge. The opposing camp accepts every formal step but denies that the result sets any meaningful benchmark for actual markets; they invoke heterogeneous risk preferences, liquidity needs, and differential endowments as rational explanations for trade that leave price informativeness intact. Both sides agree on the mathematics and on the empirical fact of high volume, yet neither has conceded an inch.

I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a verdict on market efficiency. Is the disagreement verbal—turning on competing uses of “common knowledge” or “efficiency”? Is it substantive but concealed: perhaps an empirical dispute about whether heterogeneous priors or genuine information asymmetry dominates observed trading, masked by philosophical language? Or are the two camps answering different questions under one heading—one descriptive (what does the formal model show?) and one normative (what should a well-functioning epistemic institution deliver?)—without noticing the divergence? I am inclined to think there is a deeper structural blind spot. Both camps treat the theorem’s conditions as either satisfied or violated in a static snapshot, but neither addresses what I will call reflexive volume: the possibility that the observation of trading volume itself becomes common knowledge and thereby alters the information structure in a way the static framework cannot represent. If that framing is apt, does it dissolve the impasse or merely relocate it?

Do not simply catalogue possibilities; defend one diagnosis and say what progress would look like under it. Write in British English, and avoid any bibliography or citation requests.
