I work in developmental biology and am comfortable with the vocabulary of zygotic totipotency, lineage restriction, Waddington’s epigenetic landscape, and the distinction between genetic identity and functional identity in cell differentiation. I have encountered the philosophical position usually called origin essentialism: roughly, that a particular human being could not have come into existence from any sperm and egg other than the ones that actually formed the zygote from which she developed, so that having originated from those specific gametes is part of what makes her the individual she is, and that asking whether she could have developed from different gametes but still be “the same person” is asking something metaphysically substantive rather than biologically empty. Restate this position and its main supporting arguments using only the conceptual resources of my field—developmental potential, genetic and epigenetic programming, cell-fate determination, and the conditions under which an organism counts as the same organism across developmental stages. Do not import metaphysical vocabulary like possible worlds or essential properties except to flag explicitly where they are being replaced by biological analogues. Then provide a residue report: which elements of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto biological distinctions (for example, between a specific genome and a generic developmental trajectory), which elements require stretching biological concepts beyond their empirical use, and which elements—especially modal claims about what could have happened to the same individual—have no counterpart in the biology of development at all. In particular, address whether the biological framework’s silence on counterfactual origins is a limitation of the science or whether it reveals that the philosophical claim was never tracking a biologically meaningful distinction in the first place.
