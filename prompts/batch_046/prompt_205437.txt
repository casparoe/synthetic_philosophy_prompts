At a philosophy reading group, two members—Leo and Mara—debated a modified teletransportation case. A scanner reads a person’s complete neural state and sends the data to Mars, where it is used to reconstruct bodies. In this version, however, the original body on Earth is not destroyed: it survives the scan in a deep coma with partial hippocampal damage, eventually awakening with intact reasoning and language but with no autobiographical memories from the past five years and with markedly altered emotional dispositions. Meanwhile, the reconstruction on Mars is split into two bodies. Replica Alpha receives the full scanned psychological profile unchanged. Replica Beta receives the same base profile except that every memory of the past five years is replaced with convincing but false substitutes, and the corresponding emotional dispositions are altered to match those false memories. The Earth body eventually wakes in a hospital, unable to recognize family members, with no recall of recent personal history, though retaining older memories and skills.

Leo argued that what matters in survival is psychological continuity with the right kind of cause, and that this relation comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing. He claimed Replica Alpha is clearly the strongest continuer: it possesses full psychological continuity with the pre-scan person, produced by the appropriate scanning-and-reconstruction process. The Earth original, he said, has severely disrupted continuity—while there is spatiotemporal continuity of the organism, the psychological chain is broken by the coma and amnesia—so it survives only weakly, if at all. Replica Beta, he added, has the wrong causal-psychological profile because its recent mental life is fabricated, giving it little or no survival relation to the original. Leo pushed further: if psychological continuity can branch—one pre-scan person, two replicas—then personal identity is not a one-one relation, and we should speak of survival as gradable. On this view, the Earth body carries a low-grade survival claim, Alpha a high-grade one, and Beta almost none.

Mara rejected the gradable framing entirely. She insisted that survival is binary and grounded in the spatiotemporal continuity of the living human animal. The Earth body never died; it was scanned, damaged, and woke up. It is the same organism with the same biological life history, however impoverished its present mental state. The replicas, she said, are merely perfect copies—new organisms with copied brains. The existence of Replica Beta proves that copying can produce divergence without destroying an original; by parity, Replica Alpha is also a copy, not the original relocated. She granted that brain transplantation preserves survival, but only because one brain is continuously functioning and relocated within a biological life, not duplicated into new matter. She also attacked Leo’s notion of degrees: asking “will I survive?” demands a yes-or-no answer, not a percentage. If Leo is right, she argued, then in a branching case one should say “I will be Alpha and Beta to different extents,” which is incoherent as a first-person expectation.

Leo replied that Mara treats biological continuity as sacred while ignoring that we already accept brain-transplant recipients survive despite receiving a new body. If the continuity of the whole organism is what matters, then a brain-transplant patient should count as dead, with a new person inheriting the brain—yet we do not think that, so biological continuity must be subordinate to psychological or at least neural continuity. He added that the Earth body’s partial recovery actually undermines Mara: if the original organism is the survivor, then destroying its brain and replacing it with a functionally identical copy should still leave “the organism” intact, which is absurd—what we care about is the mental life, not the torso.

Mara answered that transplantation involves one brain, continuously functioning, being moved; there is no duplication. The scanner creates a new brain from new matter with an identical state. The Earth body retains its original brain, damaged but continuous. So there is exactly one continuous biological life—the Earth one—and two new ones on Mars. She noted that Leo’s gradable survival collapses into a confusion between identity and similarity: we can say Alpha is very similar to the pre-scan person, but similarity is not identity. Finally, she argued that Leo has not explained why psychological continuity should matter unless it is the continuity of a particular living being—which is precisely her point.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each main move—(1) Leo’s claim that psychological continuity is gradable and can branch; (2) his assessment that the Earth original survives only weakly; (3) his analogy to brain transplantation; (4) Mara’s objection that copying produces divergence without destroying the original; (5) her charge that “degrees of survival” is incoherent from the first-person perspective—rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, ambiguous, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one substantive issue that both overlook, particularly regarding the asymmetry between Replica Alpha and Replica Beta or regarding what the first-person perspective genuinely requires in branching cases. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; assess the arguments on their conceptual merits alone.
