The slogan that history is always the history of the present is frequently invoked in debates over whether historians can escape the moral and conceptual standards of their own era. Separate the precise theses this slogan might express—from the uncontroversial claim that present questions guide archival selection, to the stronger epistemic claim that present conceptual frameworks determine what past events can be recognized as such, to the normative claim that evaluating past agents by current standards is not merely unavoidable but appropriate. For each reading, indicate whether it actually supports the conclusion that historians cannot escape presentism, and identify the specific point at which a defender of historical objectivity could reject the inference without denying the premise. In particular, address whether there is a coherent intermediate position that accepts presentist framing in topic selection—say, investigating archival silences about indigenous land management or omitted women's labor—while resisting presentist moral evaluation of the agents whose records survive, and say which reading of the slogan is typically being smuggled in when the phrase is used to dismiss attempts to understand past societies on their own terms.
