Construct a thought experiment engineered to isolate a non-standard tension within prioritarian distributive ethics, deliberately distinct from the familiar leveling-down objection. Set the scenario in a realistic institutional context—such as a municipal archival-diagnosis laboratory or a public research library’s specialized reference service—where the scarce good being distributed is not wealth, health, or welfare but cognitive resolution: the fineness and depth of expert analysis that can be applied to a given user’s problem. Design the case so that a prioritarian endorses a reallocation policy that dilutes the resolution available to well-served users (who retain access but lose surplus depth) in order to extend minimal-resolution access to previously unserved users, while a sufficientarian rejects the very same policy. Both theorists should agree that equality of resolution is not achieved—the well-served remain better served—and that no one suffers an absolute loss of functional access; indeed, the well-served merely experience a reduction in non-essential surplus quality. Constraints: the scenario must be confined to one paragraph, must involve no science-fiction technology, no perfect predictors, and no exotic physics; the dilution must result from the non-rivalrous but depletable nature of expert attention rather than from destroying or confiscating goods; and the prioritarian’s endorsement must flow from the moral weight of raising the worst-off above a threshold of meaningful access, not from any direct egalitarian commitment. After presenting the scenario, provide the reasoning each theory offers, identify the precise structural feature—likely the dilutability of attention across users without reducing anyone below a baseline—that drives the prioritarian verdict away from the sufficientarian one, and explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard leveling-down problem or transforms it into a different distributive puzzle altogether. Then state how a prioritarian defender would attack the framing of the case. Finish with a brief note on how confident you are in this structural analysis, and explicitly single out the specific claim within it about which you are least certain.
