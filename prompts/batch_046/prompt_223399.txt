Following a long argument last night with a friend who accused me of secretly reinstating descriptivism under the guise of modal semantics, I am finalizing my defense for next Thursday's philosophy of language workshop. I will argue that the standard Kripkean treatment of theoretical identifications—such as the claim that water is necessarily H2O—conflates singular-term rigidity with kind-term rigidity, and that this conflation forces an essentialist metaphysics that we need not accept. My alternative is that theoretical kind terms operate with what I call semi-rigidity: they designate the same kind across worlds only relative to a fixed background theory, and this theory-relative designation is sufficient to preserve the a posteriori necessity of scientific identities without committing us to full-blown natural-kind essentialism.

The audience is precise: eight analytic philosophers of language and metaphysics (mostly working in the post-Kripke tradition), including two defenders of kind essentialism who have recently published on essentialism in leading journals, one philosopher of science skeptical of applying modal semantics to theoretical vocabulary, and three graduate students well-versed in epistemic theories of reference but not specialists in metaphysics. They value structural clarity in modal arguments and are suspicious of ad hoc distinctions that save appearances without explanatory payoff.

Specifically include these four objections in roughly this order of likely appearance and damage:

1. The essentialist objection that weakening rigidity destroys the metaphysical payoff of the necessary a posteriori—if kind terms are only semi-rigid, then the modal discovery becomes a feature of our theoretical framework rather than of the world itself.

2. The philosopher of science's worry that theory-relativity makes scientific identification contingent on historical accident: if the background theory is part of what fixes the designation, then had physics developed differently, electron might have designated a different kind, undermining scientific realism.

3. The graduate students' epistemic challenge: without full rigidity, how does an a posteriori empirical discovery reveal a necessary modal fact rather than merely updating our theoretical commitments?

4. The hidden crux about Twin Earth cases applied to theoretical kinds—if on Twin Earth they possess a different theory of atomic structure, does electron there designate the same kind, a different kind, or nothing at all? I fear this is where my view collapses into either full rigidity or descriptivism, and I need to know exactly where the reply is soft.

Also identify the single objection I am most hoping nobody asks—specifically, the charge that my criterion for a fixed background theory is circular because I must already presuppose cross-world identity of theoretical entities to determine when the theory is stable enough to secure designation.

Finally, explicitly flag the weakest point in my overall position: I lack an independent, non-circular criterion for when a background theory counts as fixed enough to secure semi-rigid designation without already assuming the modal fact I am trying to explain.
