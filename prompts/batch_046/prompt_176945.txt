Two friends of mine got into it over coffee about whether moral progress is acutally real, specifically around that expanding-circle idea — you know, the one where moral concern slowly widens to include outsiders, women, animals, distant strangers, etc. One of them, call her Lena, thinks it's genuine progress driven by reason correcting old biases. The other, Jonas, thinks it's mostly a story we tell ourselves while we just swap who we care about.

Jonas started by saying the circle doesn't really expand, it just reconfigures. We claim to care about distant strangers and animals now, but we've gotten worse at local obligations — family, neighbors, community. He called it moral gentrification: the boundary gets wider but thinner. If everyone is in the circle equally, specific obligations dissolve. He said that's not progress, it's dilution, and if you care about everyone in the same abstract way you end up caring about no one specifically.

Lena replied that Jonas is confusing descriptive feelings with normative justification. Yes, we feel closer to kin, but that's evolutionary bias, not ethics. The point of expanding the circle is to correct arbitrary exclusions — race, species, nationality — not to erase all differences. Graded obligations are fine, she said, but they need reasons beyond proximity. Otherwise it's just nepotism with a fancy name.

Then Jonas said something more interesting: particular relationships aren't just biases, they're the root system. You learn to care specifically for your kid, then extend that capacity outward. If you start with abstract universal love, there's no soil. So flattening the gradient doesn't just feel wrong, it might destroy the mechanism that produces moral agents in the first place.

Lena said that's a just-so story. Moral concern can grow through solidarity and reason, not just kin bonds. And she pointed out Jonas's view makes progress impossible by definition — any inclusion weakens the root, so change is always bad. That doesn't describe reality; it just defends the status quo.

Jonas countered that Lena's correction-of-bias frame assumes there's a neutral standpoint outside all particular attachments, but there isn't. Reason itself is embodied and situated. So universalizing isn't rising above bias, it's just substituting one bias — for abstraction — for another.

They turned to me and asked me to referee since I was just sitting there with my coffee. Can you go through this round by round? Rate each move — clearly mistaken, mostly right, genuinely open — and say who has the stronger position overall. Also, is there actually a tension between widening the circle and keeping obligations thick, or are they talking past each other about different kinds of care? I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.
