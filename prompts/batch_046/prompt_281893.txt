Having recently joined a municipal committee tasked with revising our housing-allocation criteria, I have become acutely concerned with whether empirical patterns of resident behavior can justify normative redesign of institutional rules without committing the naturalistic fallacy. Please construct a realistic, non-scientific thought experiment—set in an ordinary bureaucratic or professional context— in which a planner argues that because a stable statistical distribution of preferences among applicants is descriptively observed (the "is"), the allocation algorithm ought to be redesigned to reproduce that distribution exactly (the "ought"). Engineer the scenario so that, under a strict Humean-Moorean interpretation of the is-ought gap, the planner clearly commits the naturalistic fallacy, whereas under an institutional-constructivist interpretation—one that treats the normative rule as constitutive of the practice rather than derived from external facts—the inference is legitimate and fallacy-free. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, state the specific belief or claim, the descriptive premise, the normative conclusion, and the intermediate step that carries the inference. Then demonstrate the reasoning for both interpretations, explaining precisely why each yields its verdict. Identify the single structural feature (for instance, whether the normative claim functions as external guidance or as an internal constitutive rule) that isolates the disagreement, and describe what happens to the argument if that feature is removed. Conclude by indicating how a defender of the Humean-Moorean view would respond to the constructivist defense, and whether removing the feature collapses the case into a standard is-ought example or leaves its distinctive structure intact.
