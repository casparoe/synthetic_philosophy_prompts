After decades of studying mesolimbic dopamine circuits and the neurobiology of reward, I find myself dissatisfied with the standard philosophical treatment of hedonism. The heterogeneity problem asks what all pleasures—gustatory enjoyment, the satisfaction of solving a proof, the relief of pain subsiding, the warmth of sunlight on skin—share that makes them pleasures rather than other mental states. The traditional answers invoke either a uniform qualitative feel, sometimes called the hedonic tone, or a shared quantitative profile of intensity and duration. Yet neither answer seems convincing: the phenomenology of tasting a pear differs radically from that of intellectual achievement, and intensity is a measurement, not an essence.

I want to frame a more precise question by importing an idea from my former field, but without reducing philosophy to neuroscience. In reward research, there is a well-documented dissociation between wanting—motivational salience driven largely by dopaminergic signals—and liking—the actual hedonic impact mediated in part by opioid activity in small neural hotspots such as the nucleus accumbens shell. If so, the philosophical heterogeneity problem may be misframed at its root.
