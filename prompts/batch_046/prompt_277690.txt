I have no formal training in philosophy or science, but for several years I have been thinking independently about whether the patterns we detect in complex systems—particularly in climate dynamics and in large machine-learning models—exist in any meaningful way apart from our predictive goals, or whether they are only projected onto nature by the stance we adopt toward the system. I keep encountering a framework that sorts explanations into three kinds: those that appeal to physical makeup, those that appeal to designed function, and those that attribute beliefs and intentions. Within that framework, one group of thinkers I have read insists that the statistical regularities found in neural networks are no more ontologically weighty than the regularities in clock gears; they exist only relative to the design stance, because the architecture was engineered to approximate them, and they carry no causal powers beyond what that design predicts. Another group argues that these same regularities are genuine physical patterns, comparable to turbulence or crystal structures, which would continue to interact and constrain outcomes even in the absence of any engineer, user, or observer who interpreted them functionally. The disagreement has persisted without resolution: each side introduces new examples from artificial intelligence or atmospheric modeling, restates its core claim, and accuses the other of changing the subject, yet no one’s position actually shifts. I want to understand what kind of stalemate this is. Are the two sides using the word real in different ways—one meaning independent of interpretation, the other meaning robustly predictive within the design stance—so that the dispute is largely verbal? Or is there a hidden empirical question about whether these patterns possess causal capacities that outstrip their predictive utility? Might one side be asking an ontological question about what exists, while the other is asking an explanatory-strategy question about which stance yields the best predictions, so that they are answering different questions under a single heading? I am not asking for a survey of authors, a reading list, or citations. I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or structurally misaligned; and given that diagnosis, what—whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about causal powers, or a reframing of the original question—could actually move it forward, or should we expect the impasse to remain inevitable?
