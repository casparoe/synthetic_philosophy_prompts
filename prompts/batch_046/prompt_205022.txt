The secular cycle in agrarian societies moves through four phases — expansion, stagnation, crisis, and interregnum or recovery. Turchin says the root cause is demographic growth outpacing agricultural productivity, which drives popular immiseration and elite overproduction, meaning too many elites competing for a shrinking surplus. This creates fiscal stress because the state can't tax enough, leading eventually to collapse.

Applying this to Venice or the Dutch Republic requires swapping land for credit. Instead of grain prices rising, we see interest-rate spikes and sovereign-debt defaults that squeeze artisans and small merchants — that's the immiseration analog. For elites, instead of noble families multiplying and carving up estates, we get an overproduction of naval officers and customs bureaucrats competing for state contracts and colonial offices. The fiscal crisis still happens: the republic can't service its bonds, so it cuts wages and raises taxes on the urban poor.

One detail to watch is the father-son generational turnover. Turchin treats this as the actual engine behind secular cycles, with loyalty structures resetting every ~40 years and accelerating the crisis phase. In maritime republics this should still apply because bureaucratic families reproduce across generations. So the extension holds. The main divergence is that without land scarcity these republics never really hit a disintegrative phase — they just stabilize at lower fiscal health, which explains Venice's slow decline rather than a sudden collapse.
