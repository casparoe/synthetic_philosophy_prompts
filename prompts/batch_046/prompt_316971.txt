University endowments commonly operate under an institutional norm of perpetual preservation: the principal is never fully depleted, investment returns are partly reinvested, and annual spending is capped at a fixed percentage of total value, so that the pool is expected to grow indefinitely across generations regardless of any immediate consumption or exchange need. What philosophical view of money and exchange does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value, growth, and purpose embedded in treating capital as something that ought to expand on its own. Is it consistent with Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia, which acquires goods for use within a natural limit, and chrematistike, which pursues acquisition without bound, together with his claim that money is barren by nature and invented solely to facilitate exchange? Or does the endowment model implicitly depend on a different metaphysics of money—one in which holding liquidity itself generates legitimate, open-ended yield? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it: could perpetual capital growth be defended on Aristotelian grounds by analogizing the university to a household provisioning for an indefinite future, or does that analogization fail at a specific point in his account of natural versus unnatural wealth?
