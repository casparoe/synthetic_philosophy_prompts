Read Spielberg's Lincoln (2012) as a case study in the ethics of dramatizing the dead. Take seriously the charge that inventing dialogue for Lincoln and Stevens and collapsing months of legislative maneuvering into days does not merely mislead viewers but wrongs the dead themselves; contrast this with the view that obligations to the dead are always mediated through the living and therefore reduce to epistemic duties to present audiences. Introduce the distinction between representational harm—distorting a record the dead cannot correct—and dignitary harm—using a life as narrative material regardless of accuracy—and apply it to two specific liberties in the film: the fictionalized backroom vote-buying and the compressed portrayal of Mary Todd Lincoln's mourning. Then ask whether the dead possess independent moral standing or are only proxies for the concerns of survivors, and what that implies for whether biopics owe fidelity, transparency, or something else entirely.
