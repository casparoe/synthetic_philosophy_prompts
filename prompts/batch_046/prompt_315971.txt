I have been tracking a persistent stall in recent literature on the meaning of life, specifically between neo-absurdist positions that treat meaninglessness as an irreducible feature of the human condition and contemporary objectivist accounts that ground meaning in the conjunction of objective value and subjective fulfillment. The stall is puzzling: both sides seem to concede that human lives contain projects of deep subjective investment and that those projects can be evaluated for objective worth. Yet the absurdist insists that this combination still fails to yield meaning, while the objectivist insists it succeeds, and neither side seems able to move the other with further examples or refinements of the conditions.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a substantive disagreement about value or psychology, but a hidden disagreement about the logical structure of meaning itself—specifically, whether the concept functions as a first-order predicate applied directly to life-projects or as a second-order predicate applied to the relation between an agent's evaluative framework and those projects. On this reading, the absurdist is not denying that projects have value and fulfillment; she is denying that there is any coherent second-order property that binds them together into a single judgment of meaningfulness without smuggling in a transcendent frame that the modern condition has dissolved. The objectivist, by contrast, treats meaning as a first-order property that supervenes on the conjunction of objective value and subjective engagement.

If this diagnosis is correct, then the debate is partly verbal—centered on which logical role meaning should play—and partly substantive, turning on whether a second-order evaluation of this kind can be naturalized without cosmic or theological scaffolding. But I am unsure whether there is also a deeper empirical disagreement masked here: perhaps about whether human cognitive architecture actually operates with such second-order evaluative categories, or whether the feeling of meaninglessness is best explained as a failure of first-order integration rather than as the absence of a transcendent second-order warrant.

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? I want to know whether it is primarily a verbal dispute about the semantics of meaning, a substantive dispute about what normative conditions must be met, a disagreement about the psychology of evaluation, or some combination. Crucially, I also want your assessment of whether anything—say, a rigorous conceptual distinction between first-order and second-order significance, an empirical result about meta-cognitive evaluation, or a shift to a purely naturalized framework—could actually resolve the stall, or whether the impasse is permanent given bedrock differences about whether transcendence is a necessary condition for significance.
