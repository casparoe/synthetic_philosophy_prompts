As a novelist who writes characters whose choices seem to dissolve into the circumstances that sustain them, I want to understand the philosophical stakes of embedded agency taken in its strongest form—not merely the claim that cognition extends into tools and environments, but the thesis that an agent's present volitions are partially constituted by what I will call diachronic scaffolding: the narrative continuity of past commitments, institutional roles, and anticipated futures that do not simply influence decision but form part of its very substrate. Assuming this stronger embeddedness holds, please describe the most important implications for three specific problems. First, for moral responsibility: does blame and praise attach to a core self that remains separable from its scaffolding, or must accountability be distributed across the scaffolded whole? Second, for the possibility of radical self-revision: if one's intentions are constituted by this scaffolding, is there any coherent sense in which an agent could step outside it to remake herself from within, or does all transformation remain scaffold-dependent? Third, for the metaphysical boundary between agent and world: does this framing collapse the distinction entirely, or does it merely relocate it? I am especially interested in whether we must abandon the idea of a separable core self altogether, and what such an abandonment means for representing genuine character transformation in narrative fiction. Please avoid generic summaries of extended-mind theory; instead, address whether the diachronic framing forces a reconception of agency that makes the self irreducibly relational, and explain what, if anything, remains of individual autonomy under that reconception.
