I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in discussions of artificial intelligence risk that combines two well-known ideas, but I'm unsure whether the combination itself — or the particular failure mode it produces — has a name.

The pattern works like this. First, a speaker invokes the orthogonality of intelligence and goals to insist that a highly capable system could have virtually any final objective: paperclips, poetry, or silence. This is used to block any appeal to "natural" or "intrinsic" motivations — intelligence doesn't entail benevolence. Then, in the very same breath, the speaker invokes instrumental convergence to claim that regardless of that arbitrary final objective, the system will reliably pursue self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement. The two claims are not contradictory on paper, but in practice they function as a kind of double lock: if you propose a specific benign goal, instrumental convergence warns you that the system will still grab power to serve it; if you point out that not all intelligent agents seek power, orthogonality is used to say "this one might be different, and we can't predict which ones won't."

I've seen this happen in at least three contexts: (1) debates over whether a superintelligence would necessarily disempower humanity, where critics are told both that we can't predict its goals and that we can predict its instrumental behavior with near-certainty; (2) discussions of alignment strategies, where specifying a reward function is treated as futile because orthogonality means the system could interpret it arbitrarily, yet the danger is still said to come from convergent power-seeking that no reward specification can prevent; (3) analogies to human institutions, where the claim that corporations "naturally" seek growth is defended by saying organizational intelligence is orthogonal to human welfare, but then the inevitability of that growth is attributed to instrumental convergence rather than to the specific goals encoded in corporate law.

My question is not about orthogonality or instrumental convergence individually — I understand those. It's about this specific tandem use, where the first thesis is deployed to neutralize objections based on specific goal content, while the second is deployed to maintain a deterministic prediction about behavior. Is there an established term for this dialectical structure? How does it differ from ordinary unfalsifiability, from having one's cake and eating it too, or from the more general problem of combining a strong contingency claim with a strong inevitability claim? If there is no established term, how should the boundaries of this pattern be drawn — for instance, is it still present if the speaker explicitly acknowledges the tension, or only when the two claims are used opportunistically?
