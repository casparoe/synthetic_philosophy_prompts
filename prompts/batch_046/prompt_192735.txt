I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been thinking about introspection as a kind of internal telemetry—like reading logs from a running process whose sensors have varying calibration, noise floors, and blind spots. Here are my gut reactions to several cases, and I'm trying to see whether there's a single principle that captures them, or whether I have to drop one.

First, if I say "I'm tired" and it later turns out there's an undiagnosed thyroid condition I knew nothing about, I still think my report was correct: I was describing a subjective state, not diagnosing its root cause. Second, if I sincerely insist "I'm not angry" at a colleague while my voice shakes and I avoid eye contact, I think my introspection is wrong here—something is being masked or filtered. Third, when an experienced meditator reports "there is no self experiencing this breath," I treat that as a faithful report about the phenomenological structure of awareness, not as a metaphysical claim about the non-existence of persons; but if the same meditator said "there is a permanent soul witnessing this breath," I would treat that as conceptually contaminated rather than purely introspective. Fourth, if I claim my reason for refactoring legacy code is "technical elegance," yet my commit history shows I only refactor when I'm anxious about being seen as incompetent, I think my introspection about my motives is unreliable—it's post-hoc rationalization. Fifth, in a vivid dream I once "knew" I was awake, and only upon actually waking did I realize the knowing itself was defective; that makes me think introspection is a fallible mechanism, not direct unmediated access.

There's a tension I can't resolve cleanly: in the tiredness case I defend introspection despite an unknown causal factor, but in the anger case I reject it despite the subject's sincere denial. Both involve hidden influences, yet my verdicts diverge. Is the principle that introspection is authoritative about qualitative feel but unreliable about causal explanations, socially threatening emotions, or self-interested motives? Or is there a simpler account—perhaps about attentional availability or conceptual framing—that unifies all five?

What principle, if any, best captures these verdicts? Which of them should I abandon to keep the principle parsimonious? And what should it predict for a case I haven't listed—say, a person with alexithymia who reports "I feel nothing" during an obviously emotional event, versus someone who reports "I feel a vague unease" without being able to name it?
