For a consistency check before I present at a seminar: I am a retired physicist now working in philosophy of science, and here is where I have landed on the Fermi problem. (1) I treat the paradox as a genuine evidential update: sustained null results for technosignatures, megastructures, and artifacts should rationally lower our credence in prevalent, long-lived detectable civilizations. (2) I reject point-valued probabilities for unknown biological and sociological steps; our state of ignorance should be represented by imprecise interval probabilities spanning orders of magnitude, not by false-precision Drake equations. (3) Even so, I maintain that Bayesian-style updating on cosmic silence is legitimate: with wide but bounded intervals, null observations should shift both lower and upper prevalence bounds downward. (4) I accept the Self-Indication Assumption for anthropic reasoning, restricted to biological observers only—my phenomenology is unambiguously biological, so I weight worlds with more biological observers more heavily. (5) I think the Great Filter is almost certainly ahead of us: civilizations typically self-destruct, stagnate, or transition into undetectable forms shortly after achieving interstellar signaling capacity, which explains the silence without requiring an astronomical lottery win. (6) To reconcile (4) with (5), I argue that SIA applies only pre-transition, so a universe with many brief biological civilizations is still heavily weighted, even if few survive to become detectable. (7) Consequently I support active SETI—since (5) allows survivors—while I reject METI, because (5) implies any recipient is likely dangerous, and (2) forbids assigning precise risk probabilities.

Do these hang together? Specifically: does (2) undermine the legitimacy of the update in (3) in a way I have not addressed? Is my biological-only restriction in (4)–(6) ad hoc special pleading that collapses under pressure, or is it independently motivated? Is there only an apparent tension between (1) and (5) that dissolves once prevalence is separated from current detectability? And if I must drop one commitment to clean up the package—SIA, imprecise probabilities, or the "filter ahead" view—which revision costs the least and resolves the most conflicts? Please rank the actual tensions by severity, distinguish them from illusory ones, and indicate for each what precisely would have to give.
