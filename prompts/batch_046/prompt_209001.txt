Please argue that the automation of cognitive and affective labor does not primarily threaten the "meaning of work" in the conventional psychological or sociological registers—purpose, identity, social contribution, or self-realization—but instead collapses Hannah Arendt's distinction between labor and work by eliminating the material resistance that defines work as world-building rather than mere life-maintenance. From this premise, derive the counterintuitive claim that preserving genuinely meaningful human activity in a highly automated economy requires not job retention or universal basic income, but the deliberate engineering of artificial friction, non-optimizable resistance, and irreducibly manual intervention into automated systems, even at direct economic cost. Your argument should address the obvious objection that such engineered friction is merely simulated or theatrical work rather than authentic world-building activity, and should clarify whether the value being preserved is intrinsic to the resistant activity itself or instrumental to sustaining the psychological and social conditions that make human agency legible. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than empirical: do not survey labor-market data or cite specific automation studies, but focus on whether Arendt's categories survive the transition from physical to cognitive automation and what normative consequences follow if they do not.
