Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—on Schrödinger’s Vedantist conviction that consciousness is singular and that the appearance of many minds is an illusion of perspective rather than an ontological plurality. Choose six end-words, such as one, eye, light, mirror, mind, and many, that rotate through the strict sestina pattern. Let the repetition do philosophical work: across the stanzas, the same words should gradually shift from naming separate entities to naming facets or reflections of a single underlying reality, so that the form itself enacts the dissolution of individual boundaries. In the envoi, place all six words in the required order but arrange the syntax so that “many” and “one” exchange the grammatical roles they held in the opening stanza, implying that pluralization was never more than a grammatical accident. The poem should be contemplative rather than devotional, accurate enough that a reader familiar with Schrödinger’s essays on Vedanta recognizes his view—especially the comparison of individual consciousness to images produced by a single source—without quoting any text directly. Keep the tone open-ended about whether this unity is metaphysical or merely epistemic, and hold to about thirty-nine lines.
