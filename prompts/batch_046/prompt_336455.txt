Jordan and Riley in my study group have been going back and forth about voting for the lesser evil in local races and I want someone to actually sort out the mess. Jordan said you should never vote for someone you'd condemn in private, because voting isn't just picking a winner — it's feeding the mandate narative. Even in a low-turnout race your ballot gets folded into aggregate stats the winner cites to claim broad support, so you're actively lending your moral identity to a machine. Riley said that's too pure: abstainning doesn't create some legal firewall, the winner represents the district anyway, and in a race with maybe five hundred voters your single ballot has real causal weight. If candidate A will do measurably less harm than B, refusing to vote for A is just letting B win out of symbolic vanity.

Jordan came back with what they called a testimentory asymmetry: abstaining reads as ambiguous — apathy or protest — but voting for A reads unambiguously as approval. So the expressive cost of voting is definite and high, while the expressive benefit of abstaining is vague. Plus Jordan introduced this idea of a delegation shadow — once elected, the officeholder claims constituent backing specifically from voters, not just residents, and uses that roll to justify policies you'd hate. So it's not about purity, it's about not putting your name on that roll.

Riley countered that the delegation shadow is mostly imaginary: representatives don't check individual voter rolls before deciding. The mandate is constructed by pundints regardless of your one vote. And in a small race the causal point is stronger — maybe one in four hundred. Jordan is asking people to sacrifice a real if tiny harm reduction for an expressive benefit that exists only in theory. Riley also said that if everyone like Jordan abstained, turnout collapses and democratic accountability weakens, which actually hurts the causes Jordan cares about.

Then Jordan said okay, but Riley is treating the vote like a private switch-flip, ignoring that voting is a social practice with symbolic weight that shapes how citizens see themselves. If you vote for the bad-but-better option you normalize the frame that these are the only choices. Riley said normalization comes from structural forces, not individual ballots, and that refusing the lever doesn't dismantle the structure — it just cedes ground.
