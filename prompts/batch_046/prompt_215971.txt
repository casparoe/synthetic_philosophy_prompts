I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence course. The unit covers Dennett's intentional stance, but I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the distinction between the physical, design, and intentional stances, and that forces students to confront a specific conceptual tension in applying mental-state attribution to modern retrieval-augmented language models.

Please write a single essay question (to be answered in approximately 90 minutes, with answers expected to be around 800 words) that centers on the following scenario: a clinical decision-support system built on a retrieval-augmented language model accesses a verified medical database and synthesizes diagnostic recommendations. Clinicians consistently and predictively successfully describe the system using belief-desire language—for example, saying that it believes the patient has pneumonia and wants to recommend appropriate treatment—yet the system computes each response de novo from retrieved vectors and templates, with no cross-contextual internal state integrating information across queries. This structural feature can be called the synthesis gap.

The question should require students to:
(i) explain why the intentional stance might seem appropriate for this system, specifying the predictive conditions that make mental-state talk successful and the rationality assumption that Dennett requires;
(ii) introduce and precisely apply a distinction between attribution-for-prediction and attribution-for-explanation to argue that mental-state talk about the system succeeds in the first mode but fails in the second precisely because of the synthesis gap;
(iii) evaluate the objection that if predictive success alone justifies the stance, then calibrated barometers, search engines, and navigation algorithms also merit belief-attribution, and determine whether Dennett's rationality constraint or the synthesis gap provides the more defensible boundary for blocking that extension;
(iv) conclude with a precise statement of whether belief-attribution to this AI system is licensed, together with an explicit identification of the philosophical cost of that conclusion—for instance, instrumentalism about mental-state language, an unacceptable anthropomorphizing of artifacts, or a fragmentation of the intentional stance itself.

Then provide a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit points. For each point, state the marks, the specific move an A-level answer must make that a B-level answer typically misses, and a one-line note on the common failure mode. The scheme should clearly separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one: a B answer should be able to summarize Dennett's framework correctly and apply it mechanically to the clinical system, but should fail to deploy the prediction/explanation distinction rigorously, neglect the synthesis gap as an explanatory failure rather than a mere absence of consciousness, or resolve the barometer objection in an ad hoc way without engaging the rationality constraint.

The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not include verbatim quotations from published texts; paraphrase any references to Dennett's framework. Avoid relying on obscure technical literature that a well-prepared student could not be expected to have memorized; instead, treat the synthesis gap and the prediction/explanation distinction as conceptual tools to be defined and used within the answer itself.
