I am a retired population geneticist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science, and I want to understand a dispute I used to treat as settled. On one side are biometric heritability realists, who take narrow-sense heritability (h²) to measure the causal contribution of genetic variance to phenotypic variance and who treat high h² as licensing predictions about genetic interventions; on the other are population-statisticians, who insist h² is only a variance-ratio defined relative to a specific environment and mating structure, carrying no counterfactual or mechanistic meaning whatsoever. Please draft the joint statement these two camps should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the formal definition of h², the assumptions of the additive model, the broad- versus narrow-sense distinction, key twin-study results, and what h² does and does not imply for individual prediction; (2) a disagreements table stating each camp's precise position in wording its own members would accept, especially on whether h² licenses counterfactual claims about gene-editing or environmental-change outcomes, and on whether the resolution of the missing-heritability problem via polygenic scores changes the philosophical interpretation; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—including whether a concept of causal heritability can be defined independently of variance partitioning, and whether cross-population portability of polygenic scores validates the biometric view or merely confirms statistical consistency; and (4) for each crux, a concrete empirical case or argument both sides accept as relevant (e.g., the shift in height-heritability estimates across birth cohorts with changing nutrition, or the portability of educational-attainment polygenic scores across ancestries), together with each camp's explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates. Keep the statement strictly neutral, do not adjudicate correctness, and where the dispute involves verbal ambiguity—such as different uses of the phrase genetic contribution—identify the ambiguity explicitly and propose a shared disambiguation.
