Compose a poem of exactly eighteen lines, arranged in three sestets. Present each sestet enclosed in a distinct typographic frame: square brackets for the first, round parentheses for the second, and angle brackets for the third. The first sestet should adopt the voice of a museum wall label describing a painting of a mirror; the second should adopt the voice of the mirror itself describing what it refuses to reflect; the third should adopt the voice of a critic who claims to have reconciled the first two perspectives. Following the tripartite distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages, the poem's philosophical work should be structural rather than expository: the framing punctuation itself should generate an acrostic, formed by the initial letters of the lines within each frame, that produces a hidden sentence undermining the critic's reconciliation. The poem should stand as good verse for a reader who never notices the acrostic, but a reader familiar with the framework should recognize that meaning is distributed across all three layers, that the frame is not neutral scaffolding but syntactically active, and that the inner message can only be recovered by attending to the interaction between frame and content. Do not include any prose explanation of the hidden sentence; let the formal nesting perform the argument.
