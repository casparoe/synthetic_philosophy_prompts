I've come around to thinking that the common prior assumption in disagreement theory isn't just a modeling convienience—it's actually a normative claim. The view is that if two rational agents share all the same evidence and update by Bayes' rule, but still disagree on some factual question, then the disagreement must trace back to different priors, and that prior divergence is itself a kind of rational failure: they're not anchored to the same world-model. What would count against this? Not just "people disagree all the time," but which formal results, which concrete cases, and which structural ideas—say, localized or fragmented priors where you agree globally but not on the subspace that matters—would actually break it? Would a committed defender have to concede that shared priors are impossible, or can they absorb the hit by saying only the local prior is broken? Rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward, and say honestly whether this view is actually exposed or mostly insulated by qualifications.
