Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for an advanced philosophy-of-language seminar that sorts philosophical positions on concept formation—specifically regarding whether Wittgensteinian family resemblance is adequate—using only answers to concrete diagnostic questions, not by asking respondents which label they prefer. The tree should begin with a single root commitment: whether there exists any shared necessary and sufficient feature across all correct uses of the concept "boundary" in three specified contexts (a riverbank, a legal jurisdiction, and the edge of a cast shadow). From that root, each branching node must pose one further concrete claim about how users should treat cross-contextual disagreement—for example, whether rejecting the shadow-edge usage as metaphorical preserves conceptual unity, whether legal usage requires an institutional rule that riverbank usage lacks, or whether all three can be core without hierarchy. The tree must terminate in named or unnamed leaves corresponding to actual philosophical stances: at minimum, classical essentialism with analogical extension, prototype theory with radial categories, strict family-resemblance pluralism, language-game contextualism that denies cross-game comparison, and deflationary conceptual pluralism. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment—specifically the commitment that arises when the view is applied not just to "boundary" but to normative concepts such as obligation or authority. The tree should also explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the questions cannot genuinely separate, for instance if two positions yield identical answers to every node about descriptive cases but diverge only on normative ones and the tree stops before reaching normative applications. Additionally, identify any logically possible combination of answers that no named position occupies, briefly diagnosing whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere unexploration. Frame every node as a question a student could answer without prior knowledge of which interpretation it leads to, and keep the instrument to eight to ten decision nodes. The purpose is to determine, by case-based judgment alone, which model of concept-binding a respondent actually holds.
