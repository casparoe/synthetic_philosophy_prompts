I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on "Methods for Consciousness Research," and the room is divided in a way that will make or break the event. On the other side sit phenomenologically oriented philosophers and clinicians who regard heterophenomenology, the interpretive method that treats first-person reports as texts describing a constructed world of experience rather than as direct windows onto an inner reality, as a kind of methodological betrayal: not a neutral stance, they say, but one that dissolves the very phenomenon it claims to study by reducing lived experience to a fiction narrated by the subject. The audience includes graduate students from both camps who have not yet hardened into positions, so the speech should be accessible to them without sounding like an introductory lecture. I want you to build the remarks around one concrete, slightly unconventional framing device — for instance, comparing the heterophenomenological reading of a subject's report to the way a literary scholar interprets an unreliable narrator, or to the way an ethnographer treats an informant's cosmological account not as false physics but as data about a worldview — so that both halves of the room can grasp the analogy but cannot use it to flatten the disagreement into mere semantics. The tone should be that of a philosopher of science speaking to people who actually run experiments: precise about the methodological stakes, respectful of the empirical work being presented, and free of generic appeals to "interdisciplinarity" or "breaking down silos." Please write the speech so that it lands as a genuine opening — setting the terms of engagement for the sessions that follow — rather than as a detached commentary on the field.
