At a philosophy of religion study group, two members argued over whether Buddhist ethics, once the anātman (no-self) doctrine is taken seriously, can accommodate consequentialist reasoning. Mara claimed that consequentialism requires a stable moral agent to bear the calculus—someone for whom outcomes are maximized or minimized—and that without an enduring self there is no such bearer, so Buddhist ethics must rest on virtue or intention rather than on aggregate welfare. Lin replied that anātman actually removes the central obstacle: with no privileged selves, only the arising and cessation of dukkha are morally fundamental, and upāya (skillful means) permits flexible action guided precisely by which outcomes reduce suffering. Mara countered that this ignores karma and individual moral responsibility, which seem to presuppose a continuity that functions like a self. Lin answered that karma is mere causal continuity without an owner, and that if responsibility genuinely required a self then Buddhism would have none—yet it clearly assigns responsibility—so the continuity is sufficient for consequentialist evaluation, and the absence of a self makes suffering ownerless in exactly the way consequentialism needs.
