Consider the following track record of rulings by a constitutional court in a universalist welfare-state democracy on access to social goods for non-citizens. First, it held that denying basic subsistence and emergency healthcare to asylum seekers during status determination violates human dignity, regardless of contribution history. Second, it upheld a law conditioning family reunification subsidies and language-course vouchers on passing a civic-integration examination, ruling that the welfare state may demand demonstrable reciprocity in the form of cultural and linguistic incorporation. Third, it struck down a provision excluding temporary-protection holders from unemployment benefits after twenty-four months of contribution, holding that social-insurance entitlements must follow fiscal contribution rather than residence title. Fourth, it permitted differential access to non-contributory child allowances based on a gradated residency scale, accepting that universal welfare membership strengthens with temporal and social embeddedness. Fifth, it required municipalities to enroll undocumented minors in public education, treating schooling as a precondition for future integration rather than a reward for legal status.

Now consider a new applicant: a person granted temporary protected status for climate-related displacement, who has resided continuously for nine years, contributed fully to social insurance and taxation, but belongs to a status category for which the state administratively suspended the integration-examination pathway, making formal contract fulfillment impossible. After a workplace injury, she applies for long-term disability care benefits—a hybrid entitlement partly funded through her contribution record and partly through general welfare redistribution—and for access to municipally allocated social housing tied to integration-progress metrics. The government denies both, arguing that without either permanent membership or a completed integration contract, she remains outside the circle of full welfare membership, and that contribution history alone cannot substitute for the symbolic and procedural reciprocity the contract represents.

Predict how this court should rule for consistency with its precedents, identifying which of its implicit principles—contributory reciprocity, integration-contract conditionality, or a gradated dignity-floor framework—best explains the track record. Specify whether the court would likely grant the disability benefit, the housing access, both, or neither, and identify the closest analog among the five prior rulings. Then state honestly whether its precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome: if they do, articulate the distinct principle the court would be adopting with each possible decision. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—different in its facts but falling within the immigration-welfare nexus—that would definitively distinguish the contributory-reciprocity principle from the integration-contract principle.
