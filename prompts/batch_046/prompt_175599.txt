I am preparing for a graduate seminar on early modern theories of perception and have been working through the problem John Locke raises in Book II, Chapter IX of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding, drawn from William Molyneux’s correspondence. The scenario, as Locke frames it, concerns a man born blind who has learned through touch alone to distinguish a globe from a cube, and who is then suddenly made able to see; Locke asks whether this man could, by sight alone and without touching the objects, immediately identify which is the globe and which is the cube. The standard interpretation I have encountered holds that Locke answers no, on the grounds that ideas received through different senses are not automatically interchangeable and that the understanding must learn through experience to associate visual with tactile representations. Yet I am finding it difficult to pin down exactly what structural commitment about the mind this answer requires.

My specific confusion concerns whether Locke’s formulation implicitly presupposes a kind of modular atomism about sensory ideas—the view that vision and touch each deliver discrete, self-contained representations that the intellect must explicitly collate—rather than allowing that the perceiver’s embodied engagement with a single object might already unify the sensory modalities prior to any judgment. A fellow student in the seminar has argued that Locke is not really asking about cross-modal matching at all, but rather about whether the newly sighted man possesses the concept cube in its visual form, which would make the problem one about concept possession rather than sensory integration. I find this distinction hard to maintain in the text, because Locke seems to treat the case as testing whether visual ideas carry the same geometric content as tactile ones, not merely whether a particular concept has been acquired.

Could you help me understand whether the passage supports the modular-atomism reading, the concept-possession reading, or neither, and precisely where in Locke’s reasoning the relevant commitment lies?
