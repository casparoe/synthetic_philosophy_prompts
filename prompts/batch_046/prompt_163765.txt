I'm an evaluator at a small foundation that funds civic groups in Eastern Europe, and suddenly I'm on a working group that has to decide whether we suspend grants when a country starts "backsliding." The problem is half the board uses the word to mean "government I don't like," particlarly since my partner just started working for an election-monitoring NGO in Warsaw and now we argue about Poland and Hungary at dinner every week. I want a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that my team can run in about twenty minutes on a one-page brief, no PhD required. It needs ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient yes/no/maybe at each step, and clear stop points: where it says "not backsliding, stop here," where it says "flag it as backsliding," and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's values, not facts.

I'm trying to ground this in a principle I've been circling: democratic backsliding isn't just any harm to democracy, it's specifically a change to the meta-rules of competition — who can run, how votes are counted, who oversees — made by incumbents using legal means, combined with what I'm calling an "epistemic alibi": a claim that the people really wanted it, used to justify removing checks. But I also want to catch "soft backsliding," where the formal rules stay intact but the conditions that make them meaningful — media pluralism, judicial independance — are hollowed out. The procedure should handle both hard and soft forms, decide who gets to call something a "meta-rule" in practice, and be honest about why this concept keeps becoming a partisan label.

Then run it on three cases:
1. A government replacing constitutional court judges and lowering retirement age to pack the court.
2. A democratically elected government passing a "foreign agent" law that makes NGO funding harder but doesn't touch election rules, backed by strong poll numbers.
3. A US state legislature changing early-voting rules and election-certification procedures in ways that clearly benefit the party in power but stay within formal legal bounds — and I need the procedure to come back with "not backsliding" or "ambiguous, hand to values" even though my gut says it's bad. Explain why the procedure disagrees with my gut.

Make sure you flag where judgment can't be eliminated, say exactly what the procedure can't do (like whether it can ever tell me to suspend funding for strategic reasons even if backsliding is confirmed), and note the hard cases where it will misfire — particlarly when incumbents use genuinely popular mandates to justify institutional capture. And please don't ask me for a bibliography or reading list — I don't have database access right now and I'm writing this on my phone.
