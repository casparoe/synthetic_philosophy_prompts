I'm preparing remarks for an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind, and I've become stuck on a disagreement that feels eerily circular. The debate concerns whether knowing how to do something—say, improvising a jazz solo or navigating by starlight—can be fully analyzed as a species of propositional knowing-that, perhaps under a practical mode of presentation, or whether it constitutes a distinct form of practical competence that resists such reduction. On one side, intellectualists argue that even apparently tacit skills involve knowledge of propositions expressed in a practical manner; on the other, anti-intellectualists insist that the phenomenological opacity and bodily fluency of expert performance show that ability can outstrip any propositional grasp the agent could articulate. For months my study group has rehearsed the familiar choreography: the intellectualist invokes de re and de dicto distinctions to accommodate tacit knowledge, while the anti-intellectualist points to cases where agents perform flawlessly yet deny possessing any explicit rules. Nobody has budged.

My own suspicion is that the impasse is not purely substantive but involves a hidden register shift: one side is using "knowledge" in a normative-evaluative sense—crediting an agent with knowing-how in virtue of reliable success—while the other uses it in a descriptive-psychological sense, asking what cognitive architecture actually governs the performance. There may also be an unacknowledged empirical disagreement lurking beneath the conceptual one, specifically about whether procedural memory in expert domains retains explicit representational content that is merely inaccessible to introspection, or whether it operates through genuinely non-representational dynamics. Given this, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about the semantics of knowledge-attribution, the other about the ontology of skill—under a single heading? And if your diagnosis identifies the stall as rooted in bedrock intuitions about mind-body relations, or in an empirical gap about procedural representation, what—if anything—would actually move it? Would a refined conceptual distinction, a result from cognitive neuroscience, or a shift in the framing question do the work, or should we expect no progress?

Please provide your answer in American English, and please support your diagnosis with concrete reference to what each side actually claims—particularly regarding the role of explicit representation, practical modes of presentation, and the evidential status of first-person phenomenological reports versus third-person behavioral criteria—rather than offering a generic survey of the literature.
