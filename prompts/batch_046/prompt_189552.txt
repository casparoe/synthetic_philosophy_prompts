heres the idea: standard rational choice theory says past expenditures that cant be recovered shouldnt influence future decisions, since only marginal costs and benifits matter. but in biological and physical systems, past investment often transforms the decision landscape itself. take a beaver thats half built a dam. the wood and labor are sunk, yes, but the partial dam has already altered water flow, sediment patterns, and predator access. finishing it isnt just avoiding waste; the sunk effort has restructured the cost of every alternative. i call these structural commitments, as opposed to dead costs like a lost lottery ticket that leave the world unchanged.

so honoring a sunk cost can be rational when it completes a structural transformation that lowers future costs or opens new option spaces, not merely from some emotional attachment to past spending. the fallacy, then, might be misapplied whenever people confuse dead costs with structural commitments, which are actually quite seperate categories. is this a real distinction or just a semantic dodge? and does it survive contact with actual decision theory?
