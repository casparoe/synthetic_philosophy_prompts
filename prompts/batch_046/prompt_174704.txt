I've been trying to extend two-dimensional semantics consistently to moral language and keep arriving at an absurd dissolution of ethical disagreement; I need to know which step collapses. (1) Kaplan evaluates sentences at centered worlds ⟨w, a, t⟩, with primary intensions mapping worlds-considered-as-actual to truth-values. (2) If we extend the center to include a normative framework n, so evaluation is at ⟨w, a, t, n⟩, then sentences containing moral predicates have primary intensions sensitive to n. (3) So the primary intension of "Torture is wrong" assigns true at any center ⟨w, a, t, n⟩ where n prohibits torture and false otherwise, regardless of w. (4) This makes the sentence a priori relative to n: once n is fixed, you determine the primary intension without empirical investigation of w. (5) But then two speakers with different n's who utter "Torture is wrong" express different propositions with different primary intensions, just as "I am hungry" expresses different propositions when spoken by different people. (6) Therefore, apparent moral disagreement is not conflict over shared content but a failure of cross-framework translation—like two people saying "I am hungry" with only one being hungry. (7) Since this dissolves substantive moral disagreement into mere contextual variation, it proves too much: it makes genuine ethical argument impossible. (8) Which premise should I reject? I am inclined to keep (1) and (2) because the four-parameter center is a natural extension, but I want to know whether the error lies in treating moral predicates as center-sensitive (step 2 or 3), in the inference about a priori status (step 4), or in the analogy to indexicals (step 5–6), and what each rejection costs for a 2D analysis of moral language.
