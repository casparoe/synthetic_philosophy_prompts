At a recent study-group meeting, two members—Samir and Elena—got into an extended argument about Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism and whether it genuinely resolves or merely relocates the philosophical problem of induction. I was present and stayed neutral; I genuinely could not adjudicate the exchange. After that, deliver a committed overall verdict: take a definite side, or at least a definite weighting, and explain why one party comes out ahead. Do not just list options and leave the choice to me.

Here is how the argument unfolded.

Samir opened by claiming that Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism—where God is the direct cause of every event and created things possess no independent causal efficacy—actually solves Hume’s problem of induction rather than worsening it. On Samir’s reading, the observed regularity of nature is not an unexplained habit of matter but the expression of God’s consistent customary course (sunnah). So when we expect that fire will burn cotton, we are not projecting an unjustified psychological habit onto the world; we are relying on the known reliability of divine action.

Elena replied that this merely relocates the inductive leap. Instead of asking why nature is regular, we now ask why God maintains His custom consistently. That expectation, she said, requires the same unjustified inference from past divine behavior to future divine behavior—only now the object is divine psychology rather than physical causation, and we have lost any empirical check that nature at least provides.

Samir answered that the comparison is unfair. In the theological framework Al-Ghazālī works within, God’s consistency is not established by empirical induction over past events but by rational and revealed knowledge of necessary divine attributes. So occasionalism grounds regularity in something necessary and knowable independently of experience, not in contingent observation.

Elena conceded that this works inside theology, but insisted that philosophically it adds metaphysical cost without explanatory gain. From a neutral philosophical standpoint, occasionalism replaces one unexplained regularity with another and denies all secondary causation entirely. She said that if the goal is to explain why fire is paired with burning rather than with freezing, substituting God for natural essences does not solve the pairing problem; it just renames it.

Samir then introduced a structural comparison he thought favored occasionalism. He argued that occasionalism is actually the inverse of modern dispositional essentialism: rather than distributing necessary causal powers across every kind of substance, occasionalism centralizes all efficacy in a single agent. This makes it more parsimonious in one respect, since it avoids multiplying necessary connections across countless created kinds.

Elena accepted the structural parallel but argued it undermines Samir’s case. If occasionalism is just dispositional essentialism with God substituted for natural essences, then it inherits the same explanatory gap—why these particular pairings?—while also being less parsimonious overall, because it requires a constantly active deity. She added a further objection: if God creates burning specifically when cotton is near fire, then the conditions are doing causal work in determining divine action, which smuggles secondary causation back in under a different name. If, on the other hand, God creates burning regardless of conditions, then occasionalism cannot explain observed correlations except as sheer divine coincidence.

Samir pushed back on the smuggling charge. He said that God creates effects in proximity to the usual conditions not because those conditions cause Him to act, but because His wisdom orders effects alongside conditions for human benefit and epistemic access. The correlation is real but unidirectional: conditions accompany effects, they do not cause them, and God’s wisdom explains why He chooses this orderly accompaniment rather than chaos.

Elena replied that this “accompaniment” view makes occasionalism empirically indistinguishable from regular causation, which raises a final problem. If the world behaves exactly as if fire causes burning, but we are told the real cause is always God acting directly, then occasionalism becomes a metaphysical overlay with no distinct empirical content. She concluded that a philosophical position that makes no difference to what we should expect or investigate is not an explanation but a reinterpretation, and not a particularly useful one.
