I recently joined the board of a community land trust founded in 1974 by activists who pooled savings to purchase a small orchard and two cottages, with a charter stating that the property should remain in collective stewardship for ecological and residential use. Every original trustee has since died or relocated; the current board is composed entirely of people who joined after 2010, and the charter has been amended three times, including once to reconstruct a membership appendix that was destroyed in a basement flood. A developer is now challenging our legal standing, arguing that because every human member has been replaced and the governing documents have been rewritten, the original entity dissolved and the property should revert to the county. Meanwhile, several board members want to sell one cottage to fund urgent repairs, claiming that we are the same enduring organization—continuous in location, purpose, and institutional memory—and therefore have full authority to dispose of assets. Others insist that because none of us were part of the original collective, and because climate shifts have altered the orchard’s yield so dramatically that it no longer matches the harvest patterns recorded in the founders’ handwritten journals, we are in fact a new body with only a respectful, derivative duty to preserve what remains intact. I must cast the deciding vote on both the legal defense and the proposed sale, but I cannot see whether continuity of place and mission is sufficient to make us identical to the original group, or whether total turnover of parts, texts, and even functional outputs creates a genuinely separate collective with different rights and obligations.

What I need is not generic governance advice, but a principled way to think through whether an organization survives the complete replacement of its members, the loss and reconstruction of its defining records, and the alteration of its core activities—without simply assuming that location or name fixes the answer. If there is a defensible criterion for when a plural entity composed of people, documents, and practices remains the same over time, I want it applied directly to our case: does the replacement of every trustee, the amendment of the charter, and the changed orchard harvest mean we are a different thing, and if so, does that undermine our claim to defend the property or our authority to sell part of it? I am particularly unsure how to weigh the fact that the physical land and buildings remain continuous against the fact that every relational and procedural component has turned over.
