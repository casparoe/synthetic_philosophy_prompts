Exam question: Can moral compromise between genuinely incommensurable value systems ever yield something analogous to economic gains from trade, or does any hybrid outcome necessarily constitute a corruption of both original frameworks?

Student answer:
The analogy between moral compromise and economic exchange is misleading at first glance but becomes useful once we distinguish scientific incommensurability from ethical conflict. Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism shows that goods like liberty and equality are genuinely incommensurable, yet rational agents can still rank them contextually through practical reasoning—this is not the Kuhnian paradigm incommensurability that prevents rational comparison across scientific revolutions. Alasdair MacIntyre strengthens this point by arguing that traditions possess internal coherence through narrative rationality, and that successful cross-traditional dialogue depends on translation without loss, which allows each tradition to absorb practical innovations while fully preserving its telos. Consider the negotiation between a secular liberal framework and a religious traditionalist framework over shared public space: they can enter a hybrid normative zone in which accessibility requirements are met and ritual recognition is granted, with neither framework being reduced to the other. The corruption objection therefore rests on a category mistake; it treats ethical disagreement as if it were governed by the same incommensurability that separates scientific paradigms, where no rational comparison is possible. Because ethics permits partial overlap and contextual ranking, the hybrid zone is not a corruption but rather an emergent property of practical rationality that is more stable than either framework in isolation.
