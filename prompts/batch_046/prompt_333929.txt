I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether we can genuinely trust introspection when we report our own moral motivations—particularly when the state in question is a thick emotional property like compassion rather than a thin phenomenal one like a headache. I want to turn this into the final essay for my upper-level epistemology seminar, but I need a question that students cannot answer by reciting the standard skeptical script about self-deception. Please write one 75-minute essay prompt centered on the distinction between access introspection—knowing that one is in a mental state—and mechanism introspection—knowing the causal or functional process that produced it. The question should ask students to evaluate whether the well-documented failures of mechanism introspection spill over into access introspection specifically for thick moral-emotional properties, on the grounds that identifying such properties requires conceptual framing that may be theory-laden. They should have to address whether this makes thick introspective reports more analogous to judgments about one's beliefs than to reports of raw feel, and what that analogy implies for the general reliability thesis about first-person access. Deliver: (1) the essay question itself; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that exhibits the required reasoning; (3) a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain and the common failures that should cost marks. Keep the prompt self-contained—do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, and avoid framing the issue as if it hinges on knowledge of any single obscure theory.
