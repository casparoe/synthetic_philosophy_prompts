The neuroscience of voluntary action sparked by Libet's readiness-potential experiments has been deadlocked for decades: neuroscientists treat the interval between RP onset and reported conscious intention—roughly a third of a second—as evidence that conscious choice is causally downstream, while philosophers reply with the conscious-veto hypothesis, confabulation concerns, and distinctions between token and type causation. Rather than adjudicating these replies, diagnose the stall. Is it a hidden empirical question about whether veto-related signals can be cleanly separated from preparatory neural noise; a conceptual disagreement about whether libertarian, compatibilist, or revisionist free will is the actual target; or a normative dispute about whether causal efficacy is even the right criterion for moral responsibility? Specify what each side actually takes the experiments to show, locate the precise point where the arguments become incommensurable, and say what—if anything—would count as progress: a particular experimental design, a conceptual distinction, or an admission that the framing itself is malformed.
