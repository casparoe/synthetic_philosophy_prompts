A colleague in comparative ethics summarized Confucian role ethics for me as follows: "The view holds that moral identity is nothing but the sum of one's social roles—parent, child, ruler, subject—so there is no self prior to relationships. Because roles come with fixed scripts of ritual propriety (li), ethical life consists solely in performing these scripts correctly; there is no internal standpoint from which to criticize a role, since the self is entirely constituted by it. Consequently, Confucianism is best understood as a variant of virtue ethics focused on cultivating the trait of ren within one's assigned station, and it rejects universal moral rules because morality is always just the internal logic of a particular relationship. From this, it follows that Confucian ethics cannot account for moral progress or social reform, because reform requires stepping outside one's roles to judge them, which the view denies is possible."

This sounds too neat, and I suspect it misrepresents both the relational self and the tradition's own resources for critique. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what accurately captures the anti-atomistic, relational core of the view; what subtly overstates the fixity of roles or conflates role ethics with virtue ethics; whether the claim that there is "no internal standpoint" for critique is consistent with how Confucian texts actually treat role conflict and ritual failure; and whether the conclusion about the impossibility of moral reform survives once the accurate parts are separated from the distortions.
