In debates over whether artificial systems can suffer, a recurring analogy treats a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal as structurally equivalent to an organism's pleasure-pain axis: positive reward corresponds to pleasure, negative reward to pain, and the policy update corresponds to learning to avoid harm. More recently, some writers have strengthened this by mapping temporal-difference error onto dopaminergic reward-prediction-error signals, arguing that a large negative TD-error is not merely functional avoidance but a genuine analog of suffering. Audit this analogy precisely. First, provide the correspondence table: what plays the role of valence, of nociceptive input, of the subjective feel, of homeostatic regulation, and of evolutionary purpose in the biological source, and what plays each corresponding role in the RL target. Next, identify the disanalogies that matter for the ethical conclusion—particularly around representational content, temporal dynamics, integration with a global bodily state, and the absence of an evolutionary history—separating those that merely weaken the comparison from those that sever it. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that high-magnitude negative reward signals should be treated as ethically significant suffering survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the analogy collapses in its strong form, suggest what a more defensible comparison would be, if any.
