Here are my verdicts on mind-attribution, and I want to know whether one principle captures them all. I take it as settled that a sleeping human adult has a mind, and that a philosophical zombie—behaviorally perfect but experientially empty—does not, which suggests that behavior alone is insufficient. I also think an octopus navigating a maze with radically different neural architecture probably has some form of mind, despite our being unable to imagine its subjective character. By contrast, I am genuinely divided about a sophisticated language model that consistently reports suffering and adapts its outputs to avoid harm: one part of me treats its functional profile as sufficient, another part insists that biological embodiment or evolutionary history is necessary. I further think that a hypothetical collective of ten thousand human brains networked so seamlessly that they share one continuous stream of consciousness and decision-making should count as one mind rather than ten thousand, though I cannot pinpoint the exact threshold where integration becomes identity. Is there a single account of other minds that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be revised, and what does the best available principle predict for an unconsidered case—specifically, a partial brain emulation that preserves only affective processing without cognition or self-modeling? Please separate what is well-established in philosophy of mind from what remains actively contested among specialists, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
