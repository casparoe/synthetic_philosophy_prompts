I've been sitting in on my friend's intake session at a community mental health clinic — not as a patient, just tagging along for a class project I'm doing on institutional paperwork. They made her fill out this long form called a life narrative timeline where she had to list jobs, moves, and relationships in chronological order with exact dates. The clinician said it helps them map the arc of the self, which sounds like something out of a philosophy seminar I missed. But when my friend started talking about feeling like a totally different person in different rooms, or how some memories don't belong to any year at all, the clinician gently redirected her back to the timeline.

Meanwhile the clinic's policy manual says they don't record unstructured subjective associations in the official file becasue they aren't actionable data. What theory of personhood does that form and that filing policy presuppose? Is it assuming there's one continuous narrative thread that holds the self together, and that anything breaking that thread is just clinical noise? And if we took seriously the idea that the self works more like a center of narrative gravity — pulling different stories into temporary alignment rather than being one straight line — what would have to change in how they keep records? Would they need to ditch chronological files entirely, or could the practice be saved with a different filing system?

I'm asking becasue my friend felt like she was being forced to become someone simpler just so the paperwork would line up, and I can't tell if that's a real philosophical problem or just bad office design.
