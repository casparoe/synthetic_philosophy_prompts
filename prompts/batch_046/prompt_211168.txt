I've been arguing with my roommate about zoning reform and wether people who already live somewhere have any real moral claim to keep it "the same" besides just not wanting property values to drop. I'm applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship (philosophy + urban policy) and I need a two-page proposal. The core question I want to investigate: is there a philosophically coherent version of the right to the city that doesn't just reduce to individual property rights or to a generic state obligation to provide housing, but instead takes seriously the idea that existing residents might have a legitimate continuity interest in the social and spatial practices of their neighborhood?

Most discussions either treat NIMBY opposition as pure selfishness or as a legitimate defense of community identity, but neither side actually explains what kind of entity a neighborhood is such that it could have interests worth protecting. I want to approach this by borrowing a framework from philosophy of cultural heritage — specifically the distinction between preserving a physical artifact and preserving the living practices associated with a site — but apply it to residential zoning rather than museums or historic districts. The method would be conceptual analysis: map out what continuity could mean for a social group without slipping into essentialism about who really belongs, then test whether zoning mechanisms that protect this continuity (like design review or height limits) can be distinguished from exclusionary ones (like minimum lot sizes) on philosophical grounds rather than just economic ones.

What I'd deliver is not a policy recommendation but a framework that lets us ask whether a specific zoning rule is protecting a practice or freezing a demographic accident. I'd consider the project a failure if the distinction collapses — if every protection of neighborhood character turns out to depend on an implicit claim that current residents have a privileged status that can't be justified without circular reasoning. The scope is tight: six months, one conceptual framework tested against two or three concrete zoning tools (maybe design review, inclusionary zoning, and historic district rules). I dont need citations for this proposal since I'll fill those in myself, but I do need the framing to be precise enough that someone can see why this isn't just the same old NIMBY debate reheated.
