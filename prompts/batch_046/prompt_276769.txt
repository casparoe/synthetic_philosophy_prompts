Rank the following four accounts of costly signaling in epistemic communities from most to least adequate as explanations for why established scholars in highly specialized subfields continue to circulate technically dense, unfinished working papers that expose them to significant reputational risk. The accounts are: (A) a direct application of Zahavi's handicap principle, in which the very difficulty and obscurity of the work demonstrates cognitive fitness because only a genuinely capable researcher could produce it; (B) a Spencean signaling model, in which the cost is the scholar's time investment and the signal is credible because lower-ability scholars could not bear that cost to produce an equivalent display; (C) an inverse-handicap framework, in which the scholar deliberately signals status by displaying vulnerability—such as openly admitting methodological dead ends—because only those with secure reputations can afford the reputational cost of such exposure; and (D) an audience-cost model, in which the expense is borne not by the signaler but by the reader's required verification effort, so publication functions primarily as a filter on the audience rather than as a display of the author's quality. Assign ranks 1 through 4, and for each provide a one-sentence justification focused specifically on who bears the cost and how that cost structure ensures the honesty or credibility of the signal.
