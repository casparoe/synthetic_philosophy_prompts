I spent much of last evening in a sustained, somewhat heated argument with a close friend about Harry Frankfurt’s hierarchical model of free will and whether the autonomy it delivers is genuinely the variety “worth wanting.” My friend pressed the point that if we refine Frankfurt’s mesh with a reasons-responsive mechanism—so that second-order volitions are not merely present but actively regulate first-order desires in response to reasons—the distinction between sourcehood and leeway begins to dissolve: one can be the origin of an action without having open alternatives, yet the normative weight of being that origin seems to depend on a modal openness that the mesh itself cannot supply. I countered that perhaps what collapses is not leeway but a certain robust conception of sourcehood, leaving us with a coherence condition that even an addict could satisfy. We ended without resolution, suspended between the intuition that freedom must involve being the source of one’s will and the suspicion that origin alone, once analyzed, turns out to be thinner than our moral practices require.

Given this unresolved tension, I would like you to write a villanelle—nineteen lines, with the standard A1bA2 rhyme scheme and two refrains—that enacts our disagreement structurally rather than merely describing it. Let one refrain articulate the sourcehood claim in plain, almost declarative language (for instance, “my will is mine because I claim it”), and let the other articulate the leeway claim (for instance, “the road must split for choice to live”). Use the villanelle’s cyclical return to show each position repeatedly asserting itself and being displaced by the other, distributing the volta across the stanzas so that the reader feels the mesh tightening and then loosening as reasons-responsiveness is introduced. In the final couplet, place the two refrains adjacent but do not synthesize them: let the poem end with the two conditions sitting side by side, much as our conversation did. The rendering should be philosophically precise enough that someone familiar with Frankfurt’s hierarchy, Fischer and Ravizza’s reasons-responsiveness, and the sourcehood-leeway debate recognizes the positions correctly rendered, but it should also work as poetry for someone who has never read a word of philosophy of action.
