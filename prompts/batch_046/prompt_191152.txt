I'm trying to work out what I actually mean by "work" now that large parts of labor can be automated, and my intuitions shift depending on the framing. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. A warehouse employee who now only loads trays for a fully automated line and watches a dashboard: still work, but hollowed out—there's a social role, just emptied of skill. A "prompt engineer" who spends days refining inputs to an image generator and then sells the outputs: this counts as work, yet it feels categorically different from the warehouse case, more like curation or play that happens to earn money. A retired accountant who spends weekends doing taxes for friends for free: not work, just helping out, even though it uses professional skill and benefits others. But a volunteer firefighter who trains, maintains equipment, and risks injury with no pay: definitely work, despite the absence of wages or employer. A prisoner assigned to dig ditches and fill them back in, monitored by guards, with no product and no consent: not work—it's punishment or degradation, even though it involves physical exertion and institutional assignment. Someone receiving universal basic income who spends forty hours a week rebuilding antique engines with no customers and no intention to sell: this is labor, perhaps even craft, but not work—there's no social claim or recognized function being fulfilled. Meanwhile, a novelist who spends years drafting a book she knows a large language model could produce in minutes, deliberately refusing automated assistance because she believes the difficulty of thinking-through is part of the value: this is work, and in some sense more genuinely so precisely because it resists automation. Finally, an AI researcher writing code to automate literature reviews in her own field, knowing the tool may eventually eliminate positions like hers: this is work, and I do not see it as self-undermining or contradictory, though I struggle to articulate why.

These judgments feel mostly coherent, but they contain hard-to-reconcile pairs. The retired accountant and the volunteer firefighter both perform unpaid, skilled activities that others depend on; why is one work and the other not? The prisoner and the warehouse worker both perform assigned, monitored tasks with minimal autonomy; why does only one count? And the novelist's case seems to imply that resisting automation makes an activity more genuinely work, which is puzzling if work is essentially economically necessary activity. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving social recognition, self-formation through resistance or difficulty, or a relation to collective need—that captures the majority of these verdicts, predicts what I would say about a case I have not listed (for instance, a person paid hourly to play video games in order to generate training data for an AI), and forces me to abandon one or two intuitions? Please state the principle precisely, apply it systematically to each case above, offer its verdict on the unlisted case, and be explicit about which of my judgments it cannot accommodate and why.
