I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently preparing a long-form piece on whether the situationist experiments of the last half-century have effectively dismantled virtue ethics. The familiar narrative runs from Milgram and Zimbardo through Harman and Doris: moral behavior tracks situational variables so closely that global character traits appear empirically vacant. Philosophers have replied that virtue ethics never promised behavioral uniformity, but I want you to advance a sharper, less defensive claim. Argue that the situationist critique commits a category error by treating virtue as a first-order behavioral disposition—something like a stable internal cause of uniform action—rather than as a second-order normative competence: a skillful, context-sensitive attunement to the morally salient features of a situation. To make the argument concrete, draw on the ecological-psychological idea of an affordance landscape—the field of action possibilities that a situation presents to an agent—and show that virtue, on this reading, is precisely the capacity to navigate that landscape with practical wisdom, not to suppress it. From this vantage, the very variability that situationists cite becomes supporting evidence: the virtuous agent does not behave identically across contexts but responds differentially to genuinely different moral demands. Address the obvious worry that this reconception makes virtue unfalsifiable, and explain why treating practical wisdom as a dynamic skill rather than a static trait avoids that charge without collapsing into mere post-hoc rationalization. Keep the argument accessible to an educated general reader but philosophically rigorous.
