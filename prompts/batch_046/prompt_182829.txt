I teach an MA-level philosophy of mind seminar and I am drafting the final examination. I require a single essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian kinds — but the question must not be answerable by mere definitional recitation.

Please construct an examination item that forces students to confront a specific conceptual tension: contemporary predictive-coding and active-inference models in comparative cognition describe animals (for instance, certain corvids and cephalopods) as constructing rich offline generative models that are corrected by sensory error rather than by behavioural trial-and-error. This seems to blur Dennett's boundary between Skinnerian creatures, which learn by reinforcing successful behaviours in the world, and Popperian creatures, which generate candidate actions internally and allow the poor ones to die in their stead. The taxonomy must be applied precisely.

Specifically, provide the following:

1. The exam question, labelled for a 45-minute essay carrying 40 marks out of 100, at upper-level undergraduate or MA standard. The question must instruct candidates to (a) characterise all four kinds with particular attention to the generative mechanisms that distinguish each; (b) analyse exactly where predictive-coding architectures challenge the Skinnerian/Popperian boundary and whether the Gregorian category — traditionally anchored in linguistic and cultural transmission — remains untouched; and (c) defend one of three positions: that predictive coding reveals a genuinely fifth kind of mind; that it forces a reinterpretation of either the Skinnerian or Popperian category; or that Dennett's original distinction can absorb the phenomenon without revision.

2. A model answer of approximately 800 words. The answer must clearly separate what is settled in Dennett's framework from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and cognitive scientists regarding its extension to predictive-coding systems, and it must not present any disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus view.

3. A marking scheme that distinguishes an A-range response (which correctly maps all four mechanisms, identifies the specific locus of the predictive-coding challenge, offers a defended verdict, and maintains the settled/contested distinction) from a B-range response (which correctly lists the four kinds but treats the challenge superficially, omits the Gregorian dimension, or collapses contested claims into false certainty). The scheme should specify the exact moves that earn marks and the specific errors that should cost them.

Constraints: write in formal British English; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not include verbatim quotations in quotation marks unless you have verified the exact wording from a source — paraphrase without quotation marks if in doubt; and do not rely on obscure historical details that a model without web access could not be expected to know.
