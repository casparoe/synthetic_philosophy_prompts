I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I sit on the ethics advisory board of a medical-technology firm. I want to understand whether a tension I see in our institutional practices is genuinely inconsistent or only apparently so.

In our engineering division, we design brain-computer interface dashboards that treat subjective experience as a configurable predictive interface—what we informally call a “user illusion” that the system maintains for operational coherence and that can be debugged, updated, or temporarily suspended without altering the underlying hardware state. In these design meetings we speak of consciousness as an interface layer rather than as an intrinsic property of the system.

Our clinical protocol for patients with severe brain injury, however, operates very differently. To decide whether a patient qualifies for an expensive assistive-communication implant, we apply a behavioural scale that scores consciousness as an all-or-nothing intrinsic state. Patients who fall below the threshold are classified as lacking the “coherent user model” required to benefit, and funding is denied. The vocabulary borrows our engineering metaphor, yet the practice treats consciousness as a binary, morally decisive property that is either present or absent.

What philosophical commitments about consciousness and personal identity does this dual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes it coherent for the same institution to treat consciousness as an illusion in design and as a binary intrinsic state in clinical allocation. Does the protocol strictly presuppose that consciousness is indeed a user illusion, or can it be reconciled with a realist or dualist view? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined—that is, which rival theories of consciousness and identity would license exactly the same policy.

Also, explain what would have to change in our clinical protocol if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly eliminativist account, or with a view that grants full moral status to every functioning predictive interface regardless of its coherence.

Please answer in British English, and include a note on what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding about consciousness, predictive processing, or personal identity would lead you to a different verdict about what our practice presupposes, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
