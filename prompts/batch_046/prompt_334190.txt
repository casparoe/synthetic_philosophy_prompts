I am trying to determine where conceptual effort should actually go in the debate over advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, because much of the literature seems to circle the same binary—precedent autonomy versus present interests—without asking which resolution would genuinely reshape clinical practice or legal doctrine. To move past that stalemate, I want to foreground a distinction that is rarely formalized in the philosophical literature: between first-order preference reversal, in which a patient with cognitive decline now seems to enjoy or seek experiences they once explicitly refused (for instance, social engagement or particular foods), and second-order value restructuring, in which the patient no longer endorses the evaluative framework that originally gave the directive its normative force (for instance, no longer regarding "dignity" or "not being a burden" as reasons that should govern treatment choices). Alongside this conceptual clarification, there is an institutional-design question that receives almost no attention: whether directives should be structured as tiered revocation protocols rather than all-or-nothing instruments, such that affective or experiential preferences might override specific provisions without invalidating the moral-identity claims embedded elsewhere in the document.

Given these framing devices, I want a defended prioritization—not a survey—of which open lines of work are upstream. Should philosophers and policy designers prioritize (1) rigorously mapping the conditions under which first-order reversals can be distinguished from second-order restructuring without relying on contested neuropsychological metrics; (2) defending or dismantling the tiered-revocation model and specifying what institutional safeguards would prevent its abuse; or (3) resolving whether diachronic rational authority survives only when there is "value continuity" rather than mere psychological continuity, and whether that distinction even survives scrutiny once we abandon the fiction of a single, stable earlier self? For each, please defend its rank according to explicit criteria: downstream impact on law and clinical ethics, tractability for conceptual work that does not depend on empirical neuroscience breakthroughs, and the risk that the problem is a pseudo-issue that dissolves once we stop treating the pre-illness self as a unified authority over time. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list; I want an agenda that tells me where limited philosophical and institutional-design effort should go first, what evidence or argument would change that ranking, and which of these debates, if settled, would leave the others largely untouched.
