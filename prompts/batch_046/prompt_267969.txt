I’ve been turning over in my mind why promises actually bind us, and I keep running into cases that pull in different directions. I don’t have any formal philosophical training—just long conversations with friends and some solitary thinking—so I’m hoping you can help me see whether there’s a single principle behind my judgments or whether I need to revise some of them.

Here are my verdicts on several concrete situations. First, if I promise a friend I’ll meet him for coffee, but on the way I encounter a stranger in genuine crisis, I feel I’m permitted—perhaps even required—to break the coffee promise in order to help. Second, if I promise my dying grandmother I’ll never reveal a family secret, and after her death revealing it could prevent serious harm to innocent people, I still think the promise binds me not to speak; the fact that she is gone doesn’t dissolve the obligation. Third, if I make a vivid promise inside a dream to a figure in the dream, I feel no real duty upon waking—only a lingering emotional echo—so I think dream-promises don’t bind. Fourth, if I promise to help cover up a serious crime, I don’t think I’m bound at all; in fact I think I’m actively obligated to break that promise. Fifth, consider two cases of inability. If I promise to water my neighbor’s plants while they’re away, but then develop a severe allergic reaction to their soil, I think I’m genuinely released from the promise. But if I promise to repay a loan and then lose my job through no fault of my own, I think I’m still bound to repay eventually; the obligation survives my temporary inability. These two feel importantly different to me, though structurally they both involve being unable to deliver exactly what was promised. Sixth, if I regularly show up every Tuesday to help at a community garden without ever saying “I promise,” but everyone comes to rely on me, and then one Tuesday I stay home for no special reason, I think I’ve done something wrong—there’s a binding expectation—even though no explicit promise was ever made.

My tentative idea is that a promise binds when it creates a legitimate expectation in another party that you will act in a specific way, where “legitimate” means the expectation wasn’t produced by deception, coercion, or immoral content, and where you remain bound unless fulfilling the promise would cause serious harm or you become genuinely unable in a way that destroys the very point of the expectation. But this feels too neat, and I’m not sure it handles the dream case or the implicit Tuesday case correctly. Can you state a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures all six verdicts, specifying which case motivates each clause? Please also tell me what your account predicts for a case I haven’t mentioned—say, a promise made to an artificial system that lacks consciousness but is programmed to register and express disappointment—and be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without revision. If one of my judgments has to be overturned, say which one and why.
