I've been working through the literature on impact measures for my doctoral research in philosophy of technology, and in a seminar last week a colleague pressed me on whether the 'avoid side effects' problem can even be formulated without a complete axiology. I've now tried to write down my own position, but I'm uncertain whether it holds together.

Here is where I stand: first, I think that defining 'side effects' is inherently value-laden, because the boundary between intended and unintended consequences depends on what we take to matter; second, nevertheless, I want to defend that structural measures—such as reversibility constraints or bounded causal influence—can be specified using only physical or informational parameters, without invoking a full utility function; third, I hold that there is a robust distinction between altering material states of the world and altering belief states, with the former being amenable to value-neutral measurement and the latter not; fourth, I've recently been attracted to a framing I haven't seen widely discussed, which I call the 'counterfactual corridor' condition: an action is low-impact just in case, had the agent not taken it, the causal trajectory outside a narrowly defined task-space would have been identical; and fifth, I simultaneously believe that this corridor condition secretly reintroduces value judgments, since fixing the boundaries of the 'task-space' requires prior normative commitments.

Do these five positions form a consistent set? Please trace the tensions—particularly between my second and fifth commitments, and between my first and third—ranking them by severity, and suggest the cheapest single revision that would restore coherence. I'd also like to know whether my counterfactual corridor idea is salvageable if I abandon one of the other four theses, or whether it should be dropped altogether.
