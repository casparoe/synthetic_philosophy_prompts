I'm meant to be giving the opening remarks on Tuesday for the launch of this new institute — it's half philosophers of mind and half people who actually run animal welfare charities, and the whole point is they're meant to talk to each other for once. The audience will be about forty people in a lecture theatre, some of them have published on global workspace theory and the rest just want to know why a octopus gets protected in labs now but a lobster doesn't, or something like that. I want the speech to actually do structural work, not just be a welcome — please don't just decorate it with philosophy.

The idea I keep coming back to is this weird split: in the science we treat consciousness as totally continuous — more or less integrated information, more or less affective complexity, whatever — but every policy framework I've seen treats it as an on-off switch. You're either inside the protected circle or you're not. I was discussing this with my friend who's a marine biologist and she said that's just practical, but I think it hides a philosophical commitment we're not admitting: that moral standing has to be binary even if the property that grounds it isn't. So I want the remarks to build the whole framing around that tension — ask what kind of theory of value or of personhood we need if we take the gradient seriously, and then point out that our current regulations don't actually answer that question, they just avoid it. Make sure both halves of the room can follow — the philosophers should recognise that I'm not just saying 'be nicer to animals', and the practitioners should leave with a concrete sense of why the binary framework might be failing them. Don't give me a bibliography or cite specific papers, I can't read them out in a speech anyway. And please keep it casual — I'm not a professor giving a lecture, I'm just the person who volunteered to open the day.
