Over the past few months I have been thinking through a question that keeps circling back: can reasons exist in the absence of any reasoner? I do not mean this as a puzzle about language, but as a concrete question about whether there is a "why" behind an erosion-resistant rock formation, a correct mathematical proof, a predator's efficient kill, or a dead person's burial instructions—when no mind is present to grasp that why.

I would like you to design a sorting instrument—either a decision tree or a taxonomy with diagnostic questions—that classifies the positions one can actually hold on this issue. The instrument should work by asking what someone says about specific cases, not by asking them to pick a philosophical label. Use the following concrete scenarios as branch points: an evolutionary adaptation that is optimally suited to its environment but designed by no one; a true mathematical theorem and whether its proof supplies a reason for its truth; a moral obligation implied by a will or promise after all conscious beings have ceased to exist; and the wrongness of predation in a universe that never contained observers.

The taxonomy must include these terminal positions: full normative realism (reasons are irreducible, mind-independent entities); naturalist reductionism (reasons are just descriptive optimal patterns in nature); anti-realism about reasons (reasons are necessarily relational and vanish without reasoners); constructivism (reasons are artifacts of rational practice that can persist like tools after their makers are gone); and quietism (the framing mistakes a grammatical form for a metaphysical claim). For any combination of answers that no position occupies, state clearly whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and defend that judgment with reasons rather than leaving it open.

Please also flag pairs of positions that this tree cannot separate—views that agree on every case but diverge in underlying metaphysics—rather than pretending the instrument distinguishes them. Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then place the named positions inside it, noting any empty cells.

Finally, do not present all options as equally live. Take a committed position: argue for which branches are genuinely coherent, which are dead ends, and why. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want an instrument that reveals what I actually believe when I answer about the rock, the theorem, the will, and the predator, together with your own reasoned verdict on where the space of live options ends.
