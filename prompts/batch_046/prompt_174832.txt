After a long and unresolved argument last night with a close friend about whether societies ought to celebrate or quietly suppress exceptional achievement—she defending what Scandinavians call Jantelagen and what Australians recognize as the tall poppy syndrome as necessary correctives to hierarchy, I insisting that the American Dream requires visible, unapologetic monuments to individual ascent—I now face that very tension in an institutional capacity. I serve on my city’s Civic Commemoration Review Board, and we must circulate a written recommendation by next Friday regarding a concrete proposal: the downtown Public Square, currently anchored by a collective labor memorial honoring cooperative industry, has received a petition for a prominent parallel installation—an inscribed bronze figure and biographical narrative honoring a local immigrant entrepreneur who built a manufacturing firm from nothing, with explicit emphasis on risk-taking and upward mobility. The petitioners frame this as correcting an “egalitarian opacity” that hides aspirational pathways from younger residents; opponents, including community groups from Scandinavian-descended neighborhoods, argue that elevating one biography in shared civic space institutes a “meritocratic visibility regime” that functionally cuts down collective solidarity by making exceptional success the implicit normative standard, thereby importing an American Dream logic incompatible with local norms of modesty and communal leveling. Our mandate is not aesthetic but philosophical-political: we advise the city council on whether public commemoration should institutionalize achievement-display or preserve what opponents term “solidarity-preserving anonymity.” Please draft the board memo I will circulate: it should recommend a specific course—approve, reject, or approve with structural conditions—together with a concrete implementation timeline (for instance, within ninety days, paired with a specified complementary measure), and it must treat the strongest version of the losing position with genuine seriousness rather than caricature. In particular, address whether recognizing individual ascent necessarily undermines communal equality, whether suppressing such recognition is itself a form of cutting down the successful, and whether a coherent institutional design—perhaps what my friend called “asymmetric visibility”—could allow both values to operate in the same space without collapsing into one another.
