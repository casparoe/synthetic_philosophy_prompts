I hold a reconstructed Weberian position: that cultural values and world-images can function as autonomous causal forces in large-scale institutional change, not reducible to material interests or structural conditions, provided that we replace deterministic covering-law causation with a model of elective affinity—where a cultural formation selectively resonates with an institutional trajectory without necessitating it. I apply this not only to the classic thesis about Protestantism and capitalism, but to contemporary shifts such as the emergence of audit cultures or climate governance regimes. What would count against this view? I want the decisive defeaters separated from the merely awkward ones: which empirical historical cases, if interpreted differently, would show that values always follow material incentives rather than shaping them? Which philosophical arguments—say, from causal exclusion in philosophy of mind, or from mechanistic explanation in sociology—would genuinely threaten the autonomy claim? Furthermore, has the notion of elective affinity been qualified so broadly that the view risks unfalsifiability—if every institutional outcome can be read as an affinity, does the position still risk anything? I want an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a virtue appropriate to cultural explanation or a vice that renders the view empty. Please respond in British English.
