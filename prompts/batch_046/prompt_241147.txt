Compare the material-constitution framework developed by Lynne Rudder Baker with the sortal-dependent identity account associated with David Wiggins, focusing on how each handles the classic case in which a statue and the clay that composes it appear to occupy the same space yet seem to differ in their persistence conditions. Now extend both accounts to a modified case: imagine a marble statue that is never physically removed from its pedestal, but is maintained over decades by a continuous, layer-by-layer dissolution and replacement process monitored by real-time 3D scanning, so that shape, surface properties, and internal structure are preserved without any discrete interruption, yet eventually every original atom has been replaced. For Baker, does the relation of constitution hold between the statue and each successive material base, and does the lack of discrete replacement events affect whether the original statue survives? For Wiggins, does the sortal concept that individuates statues permit persistence through this kind of total, gradual material turnover, or does it compel the judgment that the present object is merely a perfect replica? Finally, assess whether either framework must implicitly adopt a processual criterion of identity—tracking continuous causal-functional trajectories rather than static material relations or essential sortals—in order to avoid counterintuitive results, and argue for which view accommodates that criterion more naturally.
