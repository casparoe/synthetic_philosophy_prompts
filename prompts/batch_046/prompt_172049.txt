Next month I present to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-psychiatry working group—philosophers familiar with Hacking's looping effects, clinical psychologists who use DSM-5 criteria daily, and data scientists skeptical that philosophical framing improves diagnostic practice—my view that embedding psychiatric classification in interactive machine-learning systems accelerates Hacking's loop in a novel direction. I argue that as patients interact with algorithmic screening tools, they learn to present symptoms optimized for statistical recognition, producing what I term adversarial phenomenology: a feedback loop that distorts both symptom reports and the training distributions that depend on them. I have no formal philosophy background and work in ML engineering, so I need the objections ordered by how damaging they are and by who will raise them. For each objection—particularly the one about whether Hacking's framework requires human self-interpretation rather than statistical pattern matching, the clinical objection that demand characteristics already explain this, and the hidden question about whether my argument implies abandoning algorithmic screening—tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, the weakest point in my framing that I must defend rather than ignore, and how to reply within the time available in a seminar discussion. Include specifically the objection I hope nobody asks, and prepare me for the two-minute version of my response.
