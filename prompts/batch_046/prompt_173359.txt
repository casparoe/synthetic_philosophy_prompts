I write fiction for a living, and over the course of two decades I have observed a recurring asymmetry in my own practice: the characters and narrative structures that feel most psychologically true tend to emerge not from any deliberate, articulable theory of human behavior, but from an iterative, selectionist process—drafting, discarding, revising for tonal coherence—during which my own comprehension of a character's motives typically arrives only after the competent rendering is already in place, if it arrives at all. This personal pattern has led me to recognize a structural parallel with two philosophical inversions that seem more rigorously formulated than my workshop intuitions: Darwin's demonstration that adaptive biological complexity requires no comprehending designer, only differential retention of variations across generations; and Turing's demonstration that a mechanical system manipulating symbols according to formal rules can exhibit behavior functionally equivalent to intelligent comprehension without possessing any internal understanding of meaning. In both cases, the explanatory order is reversed—competence is not derived from prior comprehension, but rather produces the appearance of comprehension.

Given this parallel, please construct a rigorous argument for the position that literary creation and aesthetic judgment operate according to the same inverted logic—what I will call generative opacity—whereby an author or reader achieves genuine narrative competence precisely in proportion to the absence of an explicit, articulable theory governing that competence. The argument should maintain that explicit theoretical comprehension—psychoanalytic frameworks, narratological schemata, explicit motivation charts—functions not as a hidden foundation for narrative skill but as a form of epistemic interference that disrupts the procedural and selectionist mechanisms (revision, tonal calibration, reader-response testing) through which narrative competence actually operates. You should defend the stronger claim that when a novelist seems to understand human nature through her fiction, this is not a case of hidden propositional knowledge made manifest, but rather a case of competence generating the illusion of comprehension in both creator and audience, structurally analogous to evolutionary adaptation generating the illusion of design.

In developing this, please address specifically how the gap between functional behavioral equivalence and internal architectural comprehension applies to the distance between a literary critic's articulated reasons for admiring a novel and the actual mechanisms driving her aesthetic judgment; address how Darwin's separation of selection pressure from anticipatory foresight applies to the emergence of narrative coherence through iterative revision without an author's prior grasp of the whole; and explain precisely where this inversion fails or becomes unstable—distinguishing narrative competence from domains such as mathematical proof or legal reasoning, where explicit propositional knowledge is genuinely constitutive rather than interfering. Do not supply a bibliography or invoke specific scholarly authorities beyond Darwin and Turing; I want the argument evaluated solely on its conceptual architecture. Make the case as forcefully and precisely as you can.
