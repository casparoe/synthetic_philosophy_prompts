I'm a journalist covering comparative philosophy and cognitive science, and I'm preparing a feature on how classical Buddhist metaphysics interacts with modern theories of personal identity. At a recent interdisciplinary symposium, I witnessed a sustained exchange between Dr. Asha Mehta, who specializes in Madhyamaka ontology, and Dr. Ben Rourke, a philosopher of mind working on reductionism and agency. Their disagreement turned on whether the Buddhist doctrine of anattā (no-self) offers a genuinely distinct account of personal continuity or merely re-labels Western psychological-continuity theories, and on what this implies for moral responsibility under hypothetical conditions of divided consciousness. I am reporting the exchange neutrally; I have no settled view on which side is stronger.

Here is the argument as it unfolded, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

Mehta's opening move: She argued that anattā should not be read as a flat denial of all personal continuity. Within the Abhidharma framework, she said, a person is a conventional designation (prajñapti) applied to a causally linked stream of five aggregates (skandhas). Because that stream includes consciousness (vijñāna) that bears karmic imprints, there is functional continuity across lives that is neither a permanent self (ātman) nor a mere Humean bundle of unrelated perceptions. She claimed this makes anattā closer to a "two-truths" meta-ontology: in ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) no person exists, but in conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) the designated stream is real enough to ground rebirth and moral accountability.

Rourke's first reply: He said this sounds like psychological continuity—of the kind defended by reductionists like Parfit—dressed in different vocabulary. If the only link between present action and future suffering is causal dependence among mental states, then Mehta's view collapses into standard reductionism: there is no further fact about identity, only relations of psychological connectedness. He argued that invoking "two truths" does not solve the problem but relocates it. If the person is only conventionally real, then present "I" lacks any special reason to care about future aggregates designated as "me," unless we secretly reintroduce a self that does the caring.

Mehta's second move: She invoked the classical chariot analogy found in early Buddhist literature—where a chariot is neither identical to nor different from its constituent parts—to argue that the person stands in exactly that relation to the aggregates. Not reducible to them, not separate from them, but dependently originated (pratītyasamutpāda). This, she said, allows conventional continuity without essentialism. She added that caring about future rebirth is justified not by metaphysical identity but by direct causal dependence, analogous to caring that a chariot's future parts will suffer wear if they are gradually replaced.

Rourke's second reply: He introduced a modern hypothetical he called the "simultaneous split-aggregate" scenario. Imagine that each of the five aggregates of a dying individual could be continuously transmitted into two separate synthetic substrates, so that both resulting streams possess full causal and qualitative continuity with the original. If Buddhism denies that one person can become two, Rourke said, it must rely on a hidden unitary principle—something like a singular, undivided consciousness-stream—to prevent division. But that principle, he claimed, functions exactly like the subtle self (ātman) that anattā is meant to reject. He pointed to the historical personalist schools—who posited a conventionally designated "person" that is inexpressible with respect to the aggregates—as internal evidence that Buddhists themselves recognized this tension.

Mehta's final move: She conceded that those personalist schools addressed precisely this worry, but she denied they smuggled in essentialism. Instead, she argued, they used the notion of a conventionally designated person solely as a pragmatic device for karma and ethical discourse, not as a metaphysical substance. She then proposed a framing drawn from four-dimensionalist metaphysics: the Buddhist stream can be understood as a spacetime "worm" composed of momentary aggregate-phases, where each phase is dependently originated rather than ontologically basic. The crucial difference from Western four-dimensionalism, she said, is that the Buddhist view denies even the fundamentality of the temporal parts themselves: they exist only in relational dependence.

Rourke's closing move: Even granting this four-dimensionalist, anti-realist framing, he pressed the moral-psychology point. Conventional designation might allow us to talk about "my" future suffering, but it does not generate the normative pull—the first-person urgency—that makes moral responsibility and prudential reasoning coherent. He concluded that anattā, taken seriously, undermines not just metaphysical selfhood but the practical standpoint of agency itself.

For each of the five moves outlined above—Mehta's opening two-truths claim, Rourke's reduction-to-Parfit objection, Mehta's chariot-and-causal-dependence reply, Rourke's split-aggregate hidden-essentialism charge, and Mehta's four-dimensionalist convention reply—indicate whether the argument is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, with brief reasoning. Then state who is more right overall, and identify the single strongest consideration that the losing side still retains in its favor. Treat the arguments as self-contained; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
