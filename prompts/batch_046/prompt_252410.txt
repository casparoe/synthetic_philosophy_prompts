I serve on the allocation committee of the Riverton Community Resilience Fund, which distributes modest grants to neighborhood projects. We must finalize our annual report, due for publication in three weeks, and we are deadlocked over how to present the 2023 cohort outcomes. One recipient, a local food cooperative led by Elena Vasquez, turned a twelve-thousand-dollar seed grant into a self-sustaining network serving over four hundred households; she has since been approached by regional funders about expansion. Several members want her featured prominently—full name, specific metrics, a brief profile—arguing that visible proof of exceptional results demonstrates that our fund works and gives other residents a concrete model to copy. Others, including some of our longest-serving members, insist that singling her out violates an unwritten community norm: we have always listed every recipient in identical language, and highlighting one person’s exceptional success will breed resentment, discourage future applicants who fear invidious comparison, and implicitly brand every other funded project as a failure by contrast.

I have been turning this over on my own for weeks, without any academic training but with the memory of growing up in a place where anyone who “got too big for their boots” was quietly resented, and with the counter-memory of how motivating it was when a local family business finally put up a sign celebrating its twentieth anniversary. The tension feels deeper than etiquette. On one side stands what I understand as the American Dream logic: societies must honor exceptional effort if they want people to strive, and success is meant to be visible so others can learn its shape. On the other side stands something like Jantelagen, or what people here call the “tall poppy” problem—the belief that shining too brightly threatens the group, and that equality is maintained not only by lifting the bottom but by preventing the top from rising visibly above everyone else.

We need a concrete recommendation, not a lecture. Specifically: should our report list Vasquez’s results separately and prominently; list all recipients with identical descriptions regardless of outcome; or adopt an intermediate format that distinguishes information from ranking? The memo should recommend one course of action with a clear time frame, address the strongest consideration on the losing side with real fairness rather than dismissal, and be readable by committee members who have never encountered these ideas in any formal context. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand on common experience and clear logic alone. If you invoke specific dynamics about status, envy, or social comparison—such as the difference between recognizing achievement as useful information versus allocating it as a rank—describe them in plain terms rather than relying on technical names. I will circulate this before Thursday’s meeting, so the document should read as a formal opinion the full committee can act upon.
