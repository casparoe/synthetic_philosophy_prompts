Exam question: In his postwar analysis of collective responsibility, Karl Jaspers distinguishes four modes of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. He maintains that criminal and moral guilt attach strictly to individuals, whereas political guilt belongs to citizens by virtue of membership in a state and may persist across generations through institutional continuity. Metaphysical guilt, he argues, arises from a sense of solidarity with all human beings and from the failure to prevent wrong that one might have influenced; it is not legally adjudicated but addresses an internal, existential condition. Apply this framework to the following hypothetical: In 1962, the democratic government of the Republic of Valdoria ratified the Treaty of Kessler, authorizing extraction of a mineral later discovered to release a persistent toxin that degrades soil for centuries. By 2040, Valdoria is a different democratic regime with entirely new leadership, yet the constitutional state remains the same legal entity. International bodies now demand that present-day Valdorian citizens fund restoration in affected neighboring territories. A commentator argues that these citizens bear metaphysical guilt for the ecological harm, and that this guilt obligates them to pay reparations as a form of collective atonement. Is this a correct application of Jaspers? In your answer, carefully distinguish political guilt from metaphysical guilt, and address whether either can ground a legal or political duty of reparation.

Student answer: Jaspers divides guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical categories. Criminal guilt requires direct personal action and legal judgment, so current Valdorian citizens cannot be criminally guilty for a treaty signed in 1962. Moral guilt is also strictly individual: it concerns one’s own conscience and ethical self-examination, and because none of the present citizens personally authorized the extraction, they carry no moral guilt whatsoever. Political guilt, however, attaches to citizenship in a state; Jaspers holds that citizens share responsibility for their government’s actions and that this responsibility can be inherited by later generations as long as the state continues. Therefore, the present citizens do inherit political guilt for the Treaty of Kessler, and this inherited guilt creates a legitimate, enforceable obligation to contribute to ecological restoration, even if ordered by an international tribunal. Metaphysical guilt is different: it is the feeling of solidarity with all humanity and the awareness that one failed to stop a wrong. Although it is not assigned to particular groups, it belongs to every human being, and in this case the Valdorian citizens experience it collectively because they live within the institutional framework that caused the harm. Jaspers suggests that metaphysical guilt should be expressed through concrete acts of atonement—including financial reparations—to restore human solidarity. Thus, the commentator is partly correct in invoking metaphysical guilt, but mischaracterizes it as individually assignable; the stronger and proper basis for reparations is inherited political guilt, which is both collective and legally enforceable.
