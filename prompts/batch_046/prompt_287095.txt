At a philosophy of mind seminar, two participants—Mira and Jonas—disagreed about whether Hofstadter's conception of the self as a strange loop permits a single I to be distributed across two living brains. They began with a hypothetical: imagine a continuous, bidirectional information loop linking the neural dynamics of two people, so that each brain completes patterns initiated in the other, with no privileged causal direction and a shared working-memory architecture. Mira argued that this is not two people communicating but one strange loop instantiated across two substrates. The self, she said, is the pattern rather than the brain; if the loop's self-referential coherence is maintained across both skulls, asking which brain is the real self is like asking which half of a split-brain patient is the real person—a category mistake. Jonas replied that this collapses functional dependence into numerical identity. He granted that each brain might host part of the loop, but insisted that first-person perspective requires a singular point of view; there would still be two subjects of experience, each with its own I, even if their mental contents overlap perfectly. Mira countered that Jonas was smuggling in a biological container view that Hofstadter rejects: if the loop is the bearer of selfhood and it is genuinely one loop rather than two synchronized copies, then there is one bearer, regardless of how many crania it occupies. Jonas then raised a boundary problem: if two brains can host one loop, why not ten? And if the loop splits, does the original I survive in fragments or perish upon distribution? He claimed that without a substrate-bound individuation condition, the loop view cannot distinguish one distributed self from multiple highly correlated selves. Mira answered that individuation comes from the loop's internal coherence and causal closure, not from counting bodies, but Jonas said coherence is a matter of degree and offers no non-arbitrary cutoff for when one becomes two.

They asked me to referee their exchange. Please go through each round point by point and say who is right about which specific claim, then indicate who is more right overall and—importantly—what both are missing. In your answer, separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretive claim—especially about Hofstadter's intended metaphysics or about the conditions for distributed cognition—as if it were the consensus. Give an honest adjudication rather than a false balance, but do not paper over real disagreement with a smooth compromise.
