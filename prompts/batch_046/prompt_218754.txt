Please comment on the following notes for an essay. The central claim is that contemporary Stoic revivalism—particularly in cognitive-behavioral applications and popular self-help frameworks—tends to reinterpret Epictetus's dichotomy of control not as an ontological partition between what is up to us (prohairesis) and what is not (externals), but rather as an epistemic filter: external events are treated as causally inert informational inputs that acquire moral valence only through our judgments. This reinterpretation, I argue, risks collapsing the Stoic distinction into a form of internalist phenomenalism in which the causal structure of the world becomes ethically irrelevant. In particular, if a traffic accident or economic recession is reconceived merely as a mental representation awaiting cognitive appraisal, then the Stoic injunction to accept what is not in our power loses its practical bite—acceptance becomes indistinguishable from cognitive reframing rather than a disciplined acknowledgment of genuine causal independence. I further suggest that this slippage is encouraged by modern therapeutic contexts that prioritize subjective well-being over metaphysical coherence. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; rather, I want critical feedback on whether this diagnosis is accurate, whether it unfairly conflates therapeutic Stoicism with philosophical Stoicism, and whether the proposed distinction between causal independence and informational neutrality holds up under scrutiny. Are there conceptual resources within classical Stoicism—perhaps in the distinction between what is in our power and what depends on others—that could resist this internalist drift without reverting to a naive dualism?
