Read Denis Villeneuve’s *Arrival* through the framework Douglas Hofstadter develops in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* regarding the location of meaning in layered messages: the frame message that establishes the interpretive code, the outer message that carries explicit propositional content, and the inner message that only emerges once the frame has been activated and the interpreter is structurally altered. In the film, Louise Banks learns the heptapods’ circular logograms, and the narrative treats her acquisition of the language as restructuring her cognition of time—suggesting that meaning is not merely in the translated statements she delivers to the military (the outer message) but in the formal frame that rewires the reader herself. Where exactly does the film locate meaning: in the simultaneous, non-linear visual form of the script (frame), in the semantic content she decodes about the heptapods’ intentions (outer), or in the deterministic temporal consciousness that results for Louise (inner)? Where does the metaphor hold—does the circular, holistically perceived logogram genuinely function like a Hofstadterian frame message that instructs the interpreter how to decode the rest, rather than simply conveying information within a linear syntax? And where does it break down—does the film conflate linguistic framing with inevitable cognitive restructuring in a way that collapses the distinction between the frame and the inner message, or does it ignore the possibility of interpreting the frame without being transformed by it? I am preparing this for a philosophy of language seminar and want to use the film without reducing it to an illustration of formal-systems theory, and without distorting Hofstadter’s tripartite model to fit the plot; I also want to know what the film adds to the debate about where meaning lives—whether in the symbol, the system, or the altered subject—that a purely conceptual argument about message layers does not capture on its own.
