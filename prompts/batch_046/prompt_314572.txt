I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind writing up an argument for my seminar on explanatory reduction, and I need a consistency check before I present. I apply Dennett's crane/skyhook distinction together with greedy reductionism to normative authority, but the chain seems to collapse into self-defeat. Here is the argument:

(1) Any legitimate explanation must be a crane: it builds complex phenomena from simpler, independently intelligible parts without invoking unexplained explanatory power.
(2) Normative authority—the capacity of a reason to genuinely bind action—cannot be decomposed into descriptive neural, evolutionary, or game-theoretic components without leaving a remainder.
(3) So normative authority, if real, is a skyhook.
(4) Greedy reductionism holds that once a phenomenon has a complete lower-level explanation, there is no remaining causal or explanatory work for the higher-level property to perform.
(5) Therefore, if we construct a full crane-based explanation of human decision-making, normative authority is not merely unexplained but causally redundant.
(6) But scientific authority itself depends on the normative claim that evidence should guide belief; if that claim cannot be reduced to descriptive processes without remainder, it too is a skyhook rendered redundant by neuroscience and sociology of science.
(7) So the framework eliminates not only moral obligation but the very normative force required to justify applying the framework.
(8) Hence the crane/skyhook distinction, pushed consistently, undermines its own conditions of acceptance.

Which step should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (4) intact if possible.
