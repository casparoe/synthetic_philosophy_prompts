Design a decision-tree taxonomy for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar that sorts positions on whether a first-person 'I' can be distributed across multiple brains, using Douglas Hofstadter's idea that the self may persist as a pattern in others—rather than being locked to a single biological substrate—as the motivating framework. The tree should contain exactly seven leaves and be built from logical commitments rather than from a list of philosopher-names: every branch node must pose a concrete, answerable question about a hypothetical case, phrased so that a respondent need not know any label to choose. Include at least the following diagnostic forks: (a) whether two brains linked by a sustained, high-bandwidth neural bridge share one 'I', split into two, or form something that is neither; (b) whether gradually replacing neurons in Brain A with functionally identical copies housed in Brain B transfers the original self, duplicates it, or dissolves it; and (c) whether an 'I' requires a singular locus of causal integration or can be sustained by parallel, non-interacting loops. The leaves must represent: strict biological individualism; Hofstadter-style pattern persistence in another substrate; a functionalist distributed-self view; an eliminativist denial that there is any 'I' to distribute; and at least two further positions drawn from the remaining logical combinations. For every combination of answers that your tree leaves unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree fails to separate—such as variants of functionalism that differ only in rhetorical framing—rather than inventing a spurious distinction. The deliverable should be the tree itself, with each node stated as a question and each leaf given a one-sentence characterization of its core commitment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; frame every reference in general terms.
