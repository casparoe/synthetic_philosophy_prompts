I cover emerging science and ideas for a general-audience publication. I frequently face a decision within hours: whether to report a claim that rests almost entirely on testimony—an expert’s interpretation of preliminary data, a whistleblower’s account of methodological failure, or a researcher’s description of an unreleased result—when I have no direct access to the raw data, the instruments, or the peer-reviewed manuscript. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophy of testimony and specifically in the debate between reductionism (testimony is justified only when supported by independent perceptual or inferential evidence about either the speaker’s reliability or the claim’s consistency with background knowledge) and anti-reductionism (testimony is a basic, irreducible source of justification that carries a presumptive entitlement to acceptance unless actively defeated).

The procedure must be executable in under fifteen minutes before a deadline, without specialized expertise in the relevant scientific subfield. It must specify, in order: (a) the questions to ask; (b) what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (c) where the procedure halts with a “do not publish,” a “publish only with explicit qualification,” or a “defer”; and (d) where the decision is handed over to human judgment because the issue concerns values—public interest, potential harm, institutional trust, or competitive pressure—rather than purely epistemic status. The procedure must handle hard cases: testimony mediated through institutional press offices; testimony that conflicts with established background beliefs but comes from a recognized authority; and testimony about events, such as alleged laboratory misconduct, for which direct perceptual access may be permanently unavailable.

Please construct the procedure around a synthetic operational distinction I will call “epistemic delegation depth”—the number of intermediaries between me and the original source of knowledge—since this is the structural feature that distinguishes scientific testimony from ordinary face-to-face conversation. Include a “bootstrapping threshold”: the point at which accepting testimony requires prior independent calibration of at least one component claim, and flag exactly when anti-reductionist default entitlement is overridden by reductionist failure conditions.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases:

1. A well-known climate scientist, in an unpublished interview, describes an anomalous temperature spike in a single dataset that, if genuine, suggests a feedback loop not captured by current models. The data are not yet peer-reviewed. The scientist holds high institutional standing, but the claim conflicts with the consensus framework I have reported on for years.

2. A former laboratory technician, currently without institutional affiliation, alleges that a recently published high-profile neuroscience study involved selective exclusion of outlier subjects. I have no access to the raw data files, and the principal investigator denies the allegation. The claim, if accurate, would require a correction or retraction; if inaccurate, it damages a career and misdirects public attention.

3. A trusted institutional source—specifically, a university press office—issues a summary statement describing a peer-reviewed study’s conclusions in language that slightly overstates the causal claim, substituting “demonstrates” for “suggests.” The study itself is accessible, but I have not read it. The procedure should yield a result—likely to publish only with heavy qualification or to defer—that conflicts with my journalistic instinct to trust the institution and meet the deadline.

For each case, show the procedural run, marking the precise step where judgment enters and cannot be eliminated. Explicitly identify the points where the value of timely public information competes with epistemic caution. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: specify its limits of applicability (for example, breaking news with immediate public-health implications, or testimony embedded in proprietary or classified contexts), and identify one class of testimony-based claim for which reductionism and anti-reductionism converge on the same practical recommendation but for structurally opposite reasons, leaving the journalist without procedural guidance. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an editorial protocol.
