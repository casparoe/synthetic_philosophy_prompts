As a journalist writing a long-form feature on conceptual biology and the philosophy of science, I am commissioning a short verse piece to close a section on functional explanation. I need a Petrarchan sonnet—fourteen lines, with a clear octave, a sestet, and a distinct volta near line nine—that stages the tension between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. To keep the poem from reading like a recapitulation of familiar textbook examples, the argument should turn on a specific hypothetical case not standard in the literature: a bioengineered cardiac patch, grown de novo without any evolutionary lineage, implanted in a recipient, where it contracts rhythmically and sustains systemic circulation, though its designers selected it solely for tensile strength and biocompatibility, not for pumping. The octave should build the etiological intuition—that function is fixed by the selection history for which an effect was retained—using imagery of inherited form, ancestral purpose, and lineage. At the volta, the poem should reveal that this tissue has no ancestors and no selection history for circulation. The sestet should then present the causal-role claim: that the patch is a pump purely through its present contribution to the system's capacity. The closing couplet should leave the tension formally open, pairing vocabulary drawn from both sides so that the rhyme scheme binds them even as the meanings diverge. The poem should be philosophically precise enough that a specialist in the philosophy of biology recognizes the distinction without either view being reduced to a straw figure, yet it should stand alone as verse for a general reader who knows nothing of the debate. Do not include citations, author attributions, or a bibliography; render the concepts entirely through image, voice, and formal structure.
