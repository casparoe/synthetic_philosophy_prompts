I'm drafting a prospectus chapter on methodological debates in evolutionary morphology, and I keep running into a specific deployment of Gould and Lewontin's architectural analogy that I think needs a rigorous audit before I adopt it. In recent seminar discussions, colleagues invoke the spandrels of San Marco to frame adaptationist explanations as a category error: they say that asking "what is trait X for?" is structurally parallel to asking "what is the triangular space between arches for?"—both presume an intentional designer (the architect, or natural selection conceived as an optimizer) and both risk mistaking an unavoidable structural byproduct for a purpose-built feature. They then use this parallel to defend a methodological default: in the absence of rigorous experimental evidence for a specific selective history, we should classify complex traits as spandrels—architectural fallout—rather than as adaptations, thereby reversing the burden of proof onto the adaptationist.
