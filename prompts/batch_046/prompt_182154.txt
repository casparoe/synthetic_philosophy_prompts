I have been reflecting lately on whether motion through space can ever be genuinely absolute, a question that has become personally relevant since I began reconsidering my own bodily orientation in relation to my surroundings. Below are my intuitive verdicts on five scenarios; I want to know whether a coherent principle unites them.

First, the classic bucket: a pail of water spins about its axis in an otherwise empty cosmos except for distant fixed stars, and its surface becomes concave. I take the concavity to indicate real rotation, but I do not attribute it to motion relative to the stars. Rather, I think it reflects relation to the local inertial structure of spacetime itself—so I read this as supporting structural substantivalism rather than Machian relationism.

Second, the solitary sphere: a perfectly homogeneous sphere alone in an otherwise empty universe. I feel strongly that there is simply no fact of the matter whether it spins. Without other bodies or internal inhomogeneities, rotation seems undefined, which points toward relationism.

Third, the twin-sphere universe: two identical spheres orbit their common centre of mass in otherwise empty, flat spacetime. Here I think there is a definite fact that both are in motion, yet the motion is entirely mutual—there is no further fact about which one is 'really' orbiting. Still, I want to say the system possesses a real rotational state, since angular momentum is non-zero. This feels like an unstable hybrid.

Fourth, the cylindrical void: spacetime is globally flat except for one periodic spatial dimension, like a cylinder, with no matter present. A geodesic looping around returns rotated relative to its initial orientation. I think this parallel-transport rotation is physically real even absent all bodies, so I am substantivalist about global geometric properties.

Fifth, the cosmic vector field: flat Minkowski spacetime contains a uniform, undetectable vector field defining a privileged rest frame, but all matter moves uniformly with respect to it and there are no dynamical effects. Even if discovered, I think this field should not license the claim that a body at rest with us is 'really' moving. Without dynamical consequence, it should not ground real motion.

Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning dynamical manifestation, or the ontology of spatiotemporal structure—that captures all five? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause each case motivates, predict my verdict for an unlisted case (a rotating universe with homogeneous matter everywhere), and be honest about which of my verdicts must be abandoned if the principle is kept simple. Please answer in British English.
