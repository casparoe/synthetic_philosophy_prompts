Draft the joint statement that a Theatricalist and a Draftist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on consciousness and its neural architecture. The Theatricalist holds that a minimal, non-substantival Cartesian theater is necessary: there must be a functionally unified convergence zone where disparate processes become jointly available to a single reportable perspective, even if no homunculus observes them. The Draftist holds Dennett's multiple drafts view: there is no central theater, only parallel, competing narrative drafts edited over time, with reportable consciousness being a post-hoc construction rather than a projected show.

The document should contain four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering what neuroscience establishes about attention, the absence of introspective access to raw processing, what no-go arguments against a literal homuncular observer actually rule out, and the phenomenology of unified experience. Include at least one shared claim about the observed 200–500 ms delay between neural initiation and reportable awareness in masked-priming and attentional-blink paradigms—the "editorial latency window"—without resolving whether it reflects convergence or post-hoc editing. Second, a disagreements table with at least three rows, each stating the Theatricalist position in wording they accept, the Draftist position in wording they accept, and the precise divergence. The rows must cover: (a) whether functional convergence requires a spatiotemporal binding site or can be fully distributed; (b) whether the experience of a single narrative thread is best explained as the product of a unified workspace or as the winner of distributed draft competition; and (c) how to interpret "retroactive stitching" cases, where subjects report unified percepts integrating information across temporal gaps, such as in color-phi phenomena or certain blindsight-cuing conditions. Third, a cruxes section identifying at least two cruxes—one empirical, one conceptual—such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or experimental pattern both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's explicit prediction or interpretation. The empirical crux should concern what pattern of neural synchronization, or its absence, in split-brain or binocular-rivalry tasks would actually favor one model; the conceptual crux should concern whether "being available for report" presupposes a single receiver or can be satisfied by distributed publication without convergence. Fourth, a jointly authored concluding paragraph stating whether part of the dispute is verbal—e.g., whether "theater" functions only as misleading shorthand that the Theatricalist accepts conditionally—and, if so, identifying precisely which part is terminological and why the remainder is substantive. The statement should avoid bibliographies, citations, and appeals to obscure literature; it should be precise enough that a reader unfamiliar with the debate can locate the exact fault lines without searching for external sources.
