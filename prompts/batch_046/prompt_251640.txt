I am taking an upper-division seminar on intergenerational justice and climate ethics, and for our final creative exercise I need a short story that dramatizes a conceptual problem we have discussed: the non-identity problem applied to climate harm. Rather than retelling a standard future-generation lawsuit, I want the narrative to center on an invented institutional mechanism—call it a Counterfactual Standing Board—through which representatives of people who exist only in a lower-emissions timeline can present grievances to present-day carbon-budget committees. These representatives do not claim to be actual victims, since under the high-emissions trajectory they never come to exist; instead, they argue that the failure to realize their possible world constitutes a distinctive kind of wrong, a “structural absence” that present actors have caused. The story should focus on one representative’s attempt to persuade a present-day committee, and it should keep the philosophical tension precise: can moral responsibility attach not merely to harming specific future people, but to causing a specific valuable possible world to be replaced by another? Please begin with the story itself, with no preface, no framing commentary, and no bibliographic apparatus. The piece should be conceptually rigorous enough that someone familiar with non-identity reasoning recognizes the problem, yet rendered through character and scene rather than exposition.
