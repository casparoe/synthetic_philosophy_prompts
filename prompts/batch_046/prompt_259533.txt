A colleague has pressed me with an argument that seems to show that the very debate over original versus derived intentionality collapses into eliminativism about intentionality altogether. I want to defend that both Dennett and Searle can avoid this collapse, but I cannot see where the slip occurs.

(1) Call original intentionality (OI) intrinsic mental aboutness that does not depend on an external interpreter; call derived intentionality (DI) aboutness that exists only because an OI-bearing mind assigns it.

(2) Searle maintains that human brains possess OI, while artifacts—books, computers, maps—possess at most DI.

(3) Dennett's two-bitser reasoning attacks the idea of a non-arbitrary boundary: if we grant intentional descriptions to human brains, consistency demands we grant them to any system that realizes a sufficiently similar functional profile, including minimal pattern-matchers with only two informational states. The physical substrate is not what matters; the interpretability is.

(4) Suppose we accept this anti-boundary conclusion. Then if artifacts lack OI, human brains must also lack it—otherwise we have drawn an arbitrary line.

(5) So human intentionality is DI.

(6) By Root Dependency—a principle I take to be implicit in the notion of derivation—every chain of derivation must terminate in an original source. Thus there must be some being B (not human) with OI that assigns intentionality to humans.

(7) But any candidate B is either biological (hence, by (3)–(5), lacking OI) or an artifact (hence possessing only DI).

(8) Therefore no being in any chain possesses OI.

(9) By Stance-Transitivity—if system A's intentionality derives from B, and B's from C, then A's is ultimately grounded in C—if no root exists, then no derived link in any chain is genuinely grounded; DI collapses.

(10) Hence there is neither OI nor DI. But the argument itself presupposes intentional states (premises, inferences, beliefs). So it proves too much: it eliminates the very intentionality it employs.

The colleague insists that rejecting (4) saves Searle but betrays Dennett's anti-boundary insight, while rejecting (6) saves Dennett but requires abandoning Root Dependency, which he claims is implicit in the very notion of derivation.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to give up (3) and (6), but I want to know whether keeping both is genuinely incompatible, and whether there is a hidden equivocation—perhaps between "lacks OI" as applied distributively versus collectively, or a modal slide from "no non-arbitrary cutoff" to "no original source anywhere"—between them.
