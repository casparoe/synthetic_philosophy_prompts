Student answer:

Weber argues that Calvinist predestination produced acute salvation anxiety, which believers resolved by treating disciplined, rational worldly labor as evidence of election. This worldly asceticism encouraged capital accumulation and reinvestment, fostering the spirit of capitalism. To avoid reducing history to material forces or to autonomous ideas, Weber proposes elective affinity: a reciprocal, almost chemical stabilization between religious ethos and economic form, in which neither is the base. He also distinguishes causal interpretation, which explains action through motives and conditions, from value interpretation, which judges the ethical worth of those motives.

Applying this framework, the Protestant ethic should be understood mainly as a value-embedded practice—saving and reinvestment were rational strategies within emerging markets—rather than as a value-articulated belief system that independently directed conduct. Weber warns against idealist reduction, so he insists that in the long run material interests and structural conditions always override cultural values; the ethic was therefore only a temporary, permissive condition, not a genuine cause. Thus, while culture influences the timing and form of capitalist development, it cannot cause it in the same robust sense that technological change or demographic pressure does.
