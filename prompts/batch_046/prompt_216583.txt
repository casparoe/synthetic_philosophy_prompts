I'm trying to figure out where I actually stand on animal sacrifice, partly because I've been skimming Maimondies' Guide and his explanation keeps feeling both convincing and weirdly incomplete. Here are my gut verdicts on different rationales people float. The claim that God literally needs the smell of burnt offerings: that's out, it sounds like pagan anthropology projected onto the divine. The claim that sacrifice is just an arbitrary historical accident with no deeper purpose: also out, it feels too dismissive of something that shaped an entire civilization. The claim that it's a concession — humans were already sacrificing to false gods, so God redirected the same impulse toward Himself, picking the very animals (cattle, sheep, goats) that the Sabians worshipped: that lands right, but then I'm stuck on why the law gets into such granular detail about blood and fat if the whole thing is basically a temporary bridge away from idolatry. The claim that the point is to cultivate compassion by having the sacrificer identify with the suffering animal: I'm split — ethically it sounds nice, but I don't think Maimonides meant that, and I'm not convinced it fits the ritaual structure. The claim that once the Temple is gone, prayer is just as good and sacrifice is permanently obsolete: half of me accepts that, half thinks it's a post-hoc rationalization for exile.

So is there one principle behind all this, maybe that a ritual is justified if it hijacks an existing human tendency and redirects it toward correct belief, but its specific form is historically contingent and not eternally binding? Or do I have to overturn one of my reactions? And what would that principle predict for the case I keep circling: if a small modern group started sacrificing chickens in a backyard shrine not out of idolatry but out of sincer monotheistic devotion — is that a legitimate revival of the redirected impulse, or does the absence of the Temple and priesthood make it automatically wrong?
