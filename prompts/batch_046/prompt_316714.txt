Examination question:
'Analyse the relationship between cetanā (volition) and karmic evaluation in Theravāda Abhidharma, and contrast it with the ethical reasoning underlying the Mahāyāna doctrine of upāya-kauśalya (skillful means). Is the tension between an intention-based ethics and an outcome-oriented justification resolvable within Buddhist philosophy, or does it reveal a fundamental divergence between traditions?'

Student answer:
In Theravāda Abhidharma, cetanā functions as the principal karmic agent: the Abhidharmakośa and its commentarial tradition treat volition as that which determines the ethical quality of bodily and verbal action, while the external results (vipāka) are regarded merely as the fruition of that volition rather than as its moral ground. Consequently, Theravāda ethics is best characterised as a form of mental-state deontology, in which the wrongness of an act such as killing depends not on whether death actually occurs but on the presence of a hateful intention within the agent's mental continuum.

By contrast, the Mahāyāna doctrine of upāya-kauśalya, particularly as illustrated in Jātaka-derived narratives in which a bodhisattva captain kills a would-be murderer in order to save five hundred merchants, permits the violation of conventional precepts when such action produces a substantially greater reduction in suffering. The student argues that this is not a mere exception but reveals an underlying consequentialist structure: the moral status of the act is retroactively determined by its beneficial outcomes, with the bodhisattva's compassionate motivation functioning only as an instrumental condition rather than as the ethical foundation. The student further claims that Nāgārjuna, in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, resolves the apparent conflict by demonstrating that all dharmas—including both volitions and their results—are empty of inherent existence (svabhāva). From this emptiness, the student infers that neither intention nor consequence possesses independent moral weight, and that ethical evaluation must therefore proceed solely through a calculus of suffering-reduction.

Finally, the student distinguishes between 'karmic causality' (the Abhidhammic law linking cetanā to future vipāka) and 'ethical justification' (the normative reasoning that validates an act). The student contends that Theravāda improperly conflates these two domains, treating the mechanics of karmic causation as if they constituted ethical justification, whereas Mahāyāna correctly separates them and subordinates the former to the latter through consequentialist reasoning. The student concludes that Buddhist ethics is not unified: Theravāda represents a mental-state deontology, Mahāyāna is genuinely consequentialist, and attempts to reconcile them through the two-truths doctrine merely redescribe the divergence rather than resolving it.
