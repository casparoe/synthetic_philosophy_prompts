Write a proposal for a one-semester philosophy term paper (approximately 5,000 words) on the paradox of fiction. Frame the project around a precise question that moves beyond the standard stalemate between pretence theories and irrationality accounts: does the paradox dissolve if we model emotional responses to fiction not primarily as failures of belief-formation, but as instances of a structurally suspended practical orientation—an emotional state that retains its cognitive-evaluative content while lacking the action-readiness that normally accompanies it?

State the question precisely, explaining why existing answers (pretence, thought-theory, and irrationality resolutions) leave this specific angle open: they treat the doxastic gap (belief versus non-belief in the fiction's reality) as the core problem, but do not systematically examine whether the emotional response itself is modified by a decoupling from conative-practical commitment rather than by doxastic error.

Outline the approach: a conceptual analysis of three emotional responses—fear, pity, and grief—comparing real-world instances with responses to literary and cinematic fiction. For each, identify the cognitive-evaluative component (representing an object as dangerous or suffering) and the conative-practical component (readiness to flee, intervene, or console). Argue that in fiction, the latter is not merely suppressed but structurally absent, producing an emotional state that is neither simulated nor mistaken, but genuinely felt yet practically unanchored. Address the obvious alternative—treating this as just a variant of pretence theory—by showing that suspension of practical orientation does not require representing oneself as only pretending.

Specify what the result would look like: either (1) a defensible taxonomy of suspended-orientation emotions that resolves the paradox without attributing irrationality, or (2) a demonstration that practical suspension always collapses back into either pretence or hidden belief, leaving the paradox intact. State clearly what would count as failure—if the analysis applies equally to non-fictional hypothetical reasoning, or if every case of fictional emotion reveals an implicit practical commitment.

Keep the scope realistic for one semester: limit the corpus to two literary texts and one film, avoid empirical psychology, and do not attempt a general theory of emotion. Note that citations will be added later.
