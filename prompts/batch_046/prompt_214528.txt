I defend the following reading of Hegel's master-slave dialectic, which I think captures a structural tension usually glossed over in the standard intersubjective account. On my view, the dialectic does not culminate in mutual recognition; rather, it demonstrates that recognition is an unstable ground for self-consciousness because it must be either freely granted—making it contingent and therefore unreliable as a foundation—or structurally compelled—making it coerced and therefore not genuinely recognition. Consequently, the slave's achievement is not that the master eventually acknowledges him, but that the slave realizes the master's recognition is worthless (the master being a dependent, non-autonomous consciousness) and secures self-certainty through labor and stoic independence from the need for the other. The resolution is therefore monological: the other is rendered obsolete, not reconciled. I take this to imply that any social philosophy treating recognition as a basic, unproblematic category carries a hidden contradiction.
