I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun reading metaphysics systematically, and I would value an audit of a summary I encountered in an unnamed popular book on ontology and emergence. The author presents the special composition question—the problem of when parts genuinely form a whole—approximately as follows: universalists, in the tradition of David Lewis, maintain that any plurality of objects automatically composes an object, so that there literally exists an entity consisting of my coffee cup and a distant star; nihilists deny that composition ever occurs, holding that a table is nothing over and above fundamental particles arranged table-wise; and the special-composition view occupies the reasonable middle ground, holding that parts form a whole only under conditions of genuine integration—physical contact, causal interaction, or functional unity—thereby admitting real tables and organisms while excluding scattered aggregates. The author adds that this middle position offers a principled boundary parallel to physics: just as a phonon is a real entity only within the functional organization of a crystal lattice and not among randomly distributed atoms, so a macroscopic object is real only when its parts are integrated in the appropriate way. Consequently, the view is said to explain why scientific practice recognizes some groupings as genuine objects and others as arbitrary collections.

This sounds suspiciously tidy. I suspect the phonon analogy may be lending unwarranted scientific authority to a metaphysical claim, and I have heard that some philosophers deny the existence of tables altogether. Please examine the summary claim by claim: distinguish what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the actual philosophical positions—particularly whether "the special-composition view" is a single named doctrine or a family of restricted-composition proposals, whether it genuinely saves ordinary objects like tables, and whether the integration conditions mentioned are actually endorsed in that form. I am also unsure whether the analogy to emergent quasiparticles holds: does it illuminate the metaphysical debate, or does it smuggle in a physical criterion of "functional unity" that metaphysicians either reject or have not agreed upon? Finally, does the conclusion—that this yields a scientifically resonant, principled boundary—survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
