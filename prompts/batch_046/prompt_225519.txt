As I draft a scene for my novel in which a cognitive scientist debates an AI ethicist, my scientist extends the familiar comparison of consciousness to a computer's graphical user interface—icons and windows that present a simplified, actionable world while hiding the underlying machinery. She argues that just as deleting an icon does not damage the server, and just as the interface itself has no weight or temperature, moral status should attach only to the "user-level" experience rather than to the neural substrate. Consequently, entities that lack such an interface—whether a thermostat or an insect—fall outside the sphere of moral consideration regardless of behavioral complexity. I would like you to audit this analogy precisely: map which features of an interface (representational compression, interactive feedback, error-correction, opacity to the substrate) correspond to which features of conscious experience, and identify exactly where the correspondence collapses—particularly whether an interface can suffer, be deceived about its own existence, or generate obligations in the substrate beneath it. Does the analogy genuinely support the conclusion that moral status tracks the presence of a user illusion rather than some underlying functional or sentient property, or does it smuggle in an unargued distinction between appearance and reality? And if the interface is itself a product of the machinery, what becomes of the separation between the "user" and the system that generates it?
