I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science studying rationality in collective research contexts. I've observed a recurring pattern in institutional decision-making that I have not been able to map cleanly onto the standard literature on practical reason. In several cases—funding agencies continuing large-scale experiments, laboratories maintaining long-running protocols, or universities sustaining interdisciplinary centers—the decision to persist is made with full acknowledgment that expected future benefits are minimal and that continuation costs exceed any likely scientific or social return. The justification offered is not that past investment predicts future success, nor that decision-makers suffer from the cognitive bias of conflating unrecoverable costs with prospective ones. Rather, the argument appeals to the concrete sacrifices already borne by specific agents—graduate researchers whose formative years were shaped by the project, technicians whose expertise is tied to its instruments, or public stakeholders who were promised particular outcomes. Abandoning the project is seen not merely as inefficient but as disrespectful: it treats those past sacrifices as meaningless rather than as generating a present obligation to see the commitment through. This seems structurally different from the sunk-cost fallacy as typically defined, since the past expenditure functions not as a mistaken proxy for future value but as a source of present-directed moral weight. It also does not reduce neatly to organizational inertia, loyalty to colleagues, or deontological promise-keeping in any straightforward sense. Is there an established concept in philosophy, economics, or organizational ethics that captures this specific configuration—honoring sunk costs as a form of recognition or respect for those who incurred them? If existing literature treats this only as a variant of the sunk-cost fallacy, I want to understand precisely where the boundary lies: under what conditions does honoring past investment shift from irrational persistence to a rational or morally required form of continuity? And if there is no established term, how should one delineate this phenomenon from nearby failures of reasoning and from legitimate commitments to promises or persons, and what conditions would have to obtain for such honoring to be genuinely justified rather than a rationalization?
