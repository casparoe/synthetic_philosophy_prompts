I'm preparing a syllabus module on practical reason that deliberately moves past the standard Bernard Williams versus Christine Korsgaard framing, and I want a conceptual map precise enough that students can locate their own commitments without knowing the textbook labels in advance. Please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between a "motivational-access internalist" and a "normative-authority externalist" should have produced after a sustained good-faith dispute. The internalist holds that a consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if it is capable of motivating that agent through a sound deliberative route from their actual or corrected motivational set; the externalist holds that reasons are facts about what an agent ought to do, regardless of whether they can currently access them motivationally.

To keep the exercise from being answerable by lifting a standard encyclopedia entry, frame the collaboration around two cruxes that rarely receive joint treatment in introductory texts. The first crux concerns adaptive preference formation: when an agent's first-order desires have been shaped by severely constrained or oppressive circumstances, and the agent then reflectively endorses those desires, does that endorsement generate a genuine internal reason, or is the endorsement itself a normative mistake that leaves an external reason intact? The second crux concerns institutional obligations that are assigned by social role rather than chosen—such as the duties of a public defender or a military officer—specifically asking whether these obligations bind agents who never explicitly consented to the role and currently feel no motivational pull toward fulfilling them, or whether they reduce to internal reasons via implicit social identification or practical identity. For each crux, provide one concrete, under-discussed case (not a Newcomb-style puzzle) that both sides agree is genuinely diagnostic, write down each side's predicted verdict in advance, and specify what kind of evidence or argument each camp would treat as decisive.

The deliverable should contain: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, including shared commitments about the formal structure of practical reasoning, about what phenomena need explanation, and about which no-go results or distinctions both accept; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's position is formulated in wording that the opposing camp would recognize as accurate for its own view, with unoccupied logical combinations briefly noted and diagnosed; (3) the two cruxes above, each paired with the agreed diagnostic case, each side's explicit prediction, and the test or argument both sides accept as bearing on the dispute; and (4) a brief flag identifying any residual disagreement that appears to be verbal rather than substantive. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; the task is to classify conceptual space, not to survey literature.
