Hi — I'm a retired molecualr biologist (thirty-odd years in genetics) who's been dipping into philosophy of mind since retirement, mostly for fun, though lately it's started to bug me. Could you please act as an expert philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist, think carefully step by step, and maybe start with a quick bit of brainstorming about how behavioural criteria actually work in practice?

I keep running into a pattern when people argue about whether machines or animals genuinely think, and I don't know if it has a proper name. Someone proposes a behavioural test — say, using language in genuinely novel contexts, or adapting to an unexpected obstacle — and when a system actually passes it, the critic doesn't just shout 'simulation'. Instead they refine the criterion itself: 'real' language use has to be embedded in a lived form of life, or 'true' adaptation requires intrinsic motivation rather than reward optimisation. Each refinement sounds like a more precise statement of the original behavioural standard, but in effect it sneaks in a hidden condition about internal causes or biological origin. So the test still looks like an objective, observable benchmark, yet it becomes impossible for anything non-biological to satisfy it, and the critic never has to admit they've switched from asking about behaviour to asking about cause. I've seen this with Turing-style discussions, but also in debates about animal minds — it's not quite moving the goalposts, because the critics seem sincere that they're clarifying the same target. Is there an established concept for this particular failure mode, where a behavioural criterion for mind is protected from counterexample by quietly importing causal or biological requirements? If not, what would be the sharpest way to name and define it, and how exactly does it differ from an ordinary ad hoc rescue or from outright biological chauvinism?
