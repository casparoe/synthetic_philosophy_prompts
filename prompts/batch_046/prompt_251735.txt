hey so i've been trying to wrap my head around Gongsun Long's white horse thing and the School of Names (Mingjia), and I built this argument that feels like it proves way too much. maybe you can spot where it slips?

(1) To call X an instance of kind K, X must share all the esential marks that define K, and K must share all the esential marks that define X.
(2) A white horse has the mark of whiteness in addition to horseness; a horse (simpliciter) lacks whiteness.
(3) So they don't share all essential marks.
(4) Therefore a white horse is not a horse.
(5) Same structure applies everywhere: a human has rationality that 'animal' lacks, so human isn't an animal; a particular sample of H2O in this glass has location that 'water' lacks, so that sample isn't water.
(6) But if specific instances are never members of their kinds, taxonomy, chemistry, and basically all classification collapse.
(7) So (1) must be wrong? Or is the error somewhere else?

If I have to choose, I'd rather save the Mingjia idea that kinds and instances carry different marks—seems true—than save (6). But I don't want to pay the price of saying nothing is ever an instance of anything. Where's the hidden slide?
