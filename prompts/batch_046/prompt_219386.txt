A fellow engineer who reads philosophy blogs summarised the logical omniscience problem for me like this: in epistemic logic, an agent is logically omniscient if knowing one proposition forces them to know every logical consequence of it, which is clearly false for humans and for any software system with bounded computation. The usual response is to use awareness structures, where the agent only knows consequences drawn from a subset of propositions they are aware of, but this merely relocates the difficulty, because defining awareness without smuggling omniscience back in requires an infinite hierarchy of meta-awareness rules. Consequently, every formal model of belief either accepts omniscience or collapses into an uncomputable regress, so epistemic logic can never be applied to real reasoning systems such as knowledge graphs or automated theorem provers.

As someone without formal philosophical training, I am inclined to think the summary captures the core difficulty but exaggerates the impossibility result. Could you audit this characterisation? In particular, I have heard there are approaches that avoid awareness structures altogether—say, by restricting closure to computationally tractable subsets of consequences—meant to sidestep the regress; does the summary fairly represent why those alternatives do or do not solve the problem?
