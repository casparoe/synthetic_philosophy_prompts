You are an expert in decision theory and institutional ethics with extensive experience leading interdisciplinary seminars for non-philosophers. I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of clinical ethics consultants—practicing physicians, nurses, and hospital administrators—who have encountered basic rational-choice ideas but have never considered bounded rationality as a structural feature of committees rather than merely a cognitive limitation of individuals. The session should avoid surveying literature and should instead build one conceptual insight: that satisficing in institutional contexts operates through nested accountability structures, where stopping rules are not failures of optimization but necessary conditions for defensible collective action. Please think carefully about the dependency structure of the explanation, and begin with a brief brainstorming of the conceptual prerequisites before outlining the ordered sequence.

Plan the explanatory arc in detail. First, identify the preliminary idea that must be in place before the audience can grasp what is genuinely at stake—specifically, the distinction between a decision rule that selects the best option within a defined feasible set and a rule that terminates search once an option crosses an acceptability threshold, and state precisely what has to come before that distinction can land without collapsing into common sense. Second, propose the single carrying example that should structure the session; I am inclined toward a resource-scarcity triage committee allocating ICU beds, but I want you to refine or replace it with a scenario that highlights institutional satisficing—where multiple stakeholders hold veto-like authority over thresholds—rather than individual cognitive overload. Specify exactly what the example must include (e.g., competing criteria that are incommensurable without prior weighting) and what it must deliberately omit so that the discussion does not drift into standard medical-ethics debates about distributive justice alone. Third, map the misconceptions these consultants will likely bring or form, in the approximate order they will surface: for instance, that satisficing is merely cutting corners under pressure; that bounded rationality means committees should be replaced by algorithmic optimization; that if a threshold is ethically defensible, then any outcome above it is ethically equivalent; and that the concept applies only to individuals with limited memory, not to well-resourced institutional bodies. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence—before the example, during its analysis, or after—you should head it off so it does not derail the arc. Fourth, design one diagnostic question I can ask near the end that reveals whether they have understood the structural point rather than memorized vocabulary; the question should be answerable without citing any literature and should distinguish institutional satisficing from either lazy compromise or optimal allocation. Fifth, specify what technical apparatus—formal multi-attribute utility representations, computational-complexity arguments, or strategic-game extensions—you should openly set aside in this session, explain to the audience that you are leaving it out, and justify why each omission protects rather than weakens the core insight. Finally, advise how I should open the session, given that these consultants will arrive assuming that ethical decision-making in medicine ought to aim at the best possible outcome and that anything less is a failure of professionalism.

Please deliver this as a teaching plan rather than as the explanation itself: the ordered components, the dependencies among them, the misconception map with interception points, the check for understanding, the explicit omissions, and the framing for the opening. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; the consultants have no access to specialized literature in this session, and the goal is conceptual architecture, not scholarly reference.
