Working in software, I am used to systems where metrics are incommensurable—latency and correctness do not always collapse onto a single scale, and engineers often enforce hard lexical constraints rather than weighted trade-offs. That framing led me to the philosophical debate over lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: the view that suffering is lexically prior to pleasure, so that no amount of well-being can justify even a small unit of pain once a threshold is crossed, which presupposes that pleasure and pain are not fully commensurable on one hedonic axis. The dispute seems to have stalled for decades. One camp insists on a qualitative, phenomenological gap; another cites shared neural substrates to argue for a continuous scale; and a smaller group suggests the conflict is really about how welfare should be formalized—whether a total ordering is required, or whether partial orders with lexical thresholds are coherent in normative theory. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical or psychological, but structural: the sides are answering different questions about what kind of mathematical representation ethics demands, masked by talk about mental states. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about phenomenology that neither side can resolve? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an assessment of whether anything—a distinction from decision theory about representability of lexical preferences, a formal result on ordinal versus cardinal measurement, or an empirical finding—could actually move the debate forward, or whether the question is ill-posed. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
