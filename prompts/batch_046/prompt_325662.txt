I sit on the Certification Board for High-Stakes Inference Engines, and we must issue a ruling by Friday on an application from Regress Logic. Their legal-reasoning engine employs an explicit-rule-endorsement architecture: before any inference is executed, the logical rule licensing it—such as modus ponens—must be entered as an explicit, user-endorsed premise in the proof record. The vendor argues that this design resolves the justification problem illustrated in Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise: instead of treating deductive rules as unexplained primitives that invite circular justification, the system makes every rule a contingent premise, thereby blocking the infinite regress at the cost of an ever-expanding proof chain. Critics on our board counter that this merely implements the tortoise's regress in software, since some background logic must still govern how premises combine, and that in time-sensitive legal contexts an unending premise list is operationally useless.

Please draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, approve with restricted-use conditions, or reject—containing a concrete recommendation, an implementation time frame, and reasoning that engages seriously with the vendor's philosophical claim. The memo should address whether any practical reasoning system can avoid treating at least some logical principles as primitive without falling into either circularity or infinite delay, and it should include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.

Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Also, state how confident you are in the recommendation and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly whether the vendor's architecture genuinely resolves the justification problem or only relocates it to an implicit meta-level.
