I serve on the municipal ethics review board charged with signing off on public infrastructure and health proposals that foreseeably trade aggregate welfare gains against localized or concentrated suffering—everything from transit expansions that displace neighborhoods to screening programs with severe false-positive outcomes. In our deliberations we have been attempting to apply a version of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, specifically the view that pleasure and pain are not fully commensurable on a single cardinal scale and that suffering can reach domain-relative lexical boundaries—thresholds that shift depending on whether the harm is direct or indirect, voluntary or imposed, acute or chronically distributed—beyond which no quantity of countervailing pleasure can justify the project. I need a reusable decision procedure that my board can execute within a standard two-hour session without relying on formal welfare economics or philosophical expertise; it should present a fixed sequence of diagnostic questions, specify how to classify proposed harms according to domain and temporal profile, distinguish the lexical-block test from the comparative-weighing zone, and flag precisely where a judgment call is unavoidable rather than derivable from the principle itself.

The procedure must include explicit stopping rules—conditions that mandate rejection, conditions that permit approval, and an indeterminate middle zone where discretion is retained because the theory underdetermines the outcome—along with a brief documentation protocol that records which step produced the result. Please apply it to three proposals: first, a highway expansion that subjects two hundred residents to chronic noise-related stress but reduces commute burdens for fifty thousand; second, a mandatory screening initiative that prevents five hundred serious illnesses but induces severe anxiety in thirty false-positive recipients; and third, a waterfront recreation project that generates substantial community pleasure but requires culling an invasive population that experiences acute pain during removal, where the board is predisposed to approve and the procedure must show whether that inclination survives the lexical-block test. For each case, indicate the procedural landing point and highlight where intuitive cost-benefit reasoning conflicts with the framework. Finally, specify the limits of the procedure—particularly which categories of harm, such as intergenerational damage or impacts on non-sentient ecological entities, it cannot evaluate, and where it is prone to misfire if members conflate the intensity of suffering with its duration.
