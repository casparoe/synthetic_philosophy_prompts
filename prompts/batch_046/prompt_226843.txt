Design a 75-minute classroom activity for a graduate metaphysics seminar of approximately ten participants that makes them feel the force of the ontology-identification problem under conditions of permanently inaccessible information. The conceptual target is this: when the properties that determine an entity's ontological kind are in principle unobservable—hidden behind an epistemic barrier that no instrument or inference can breach—any identification of entities across contexts, or any decision about how many kinds exist, becomes underdetermined not merely in practice but in a way that undermines the usual assumption that ontology tracks discoverable structure. The activity should take the form of a structured simulation with explicit rules and small, non-monetary stakes that force binding commitments.

Participants should be divided into two roles: Cataloguers, who must classify artifacts and decide identity conditions over time, and Adjudicators, who allocate limited preservation resources based on those classifications. Each artifact is represented by a card showing observable surface properties and a sealed envelope containing its "structural profile"—the properties that allegedly determine its ontological kind. The rules must state that the envelopes may never be opened, but that Cataloguers must nevertheless commit to a taxonomy and Adjudicators must distribute a fixed pool of points according to that taxonomy. Introduce a novel constraint: some artifacts have relational hidden properties (their kind depends on an unobservable relation to another artifact, not on any intrinsic feature), and Cataloguers must place bets—using a portion of their points—on whether two spatially separated artifacts are ontologically continuous (the same entity persisting across space) despite having no access to the hidden relational facts.

Structure the 75 minutes as follows: 10 minutes for rules and role assignment; 25 minutes for initial classification and betting; 15 minutes for a "partial revelation" in which the facilitator reveals that several envelopes contain contradictory structural profiles, showing that the available data underdetermine the taxonomy without resolving which scheme is correct; 15 minutes for participants to revise or defend their commitments under the new instability; and 10 minutes for the debrief. The payoff mechanism should be designed so that consistent classification earns points, but that consistency is impossible once the contradictions are revealed, leaving participants with irreconcilable schemes that all fit the observable data.

The debrief should map specific moments onto parts of the idea: the initial betting phase onto the assumption that hidden essences ground identity; the contradiction-revelation onto the failure of parsimony or simplicity to adjudicate between ontologies; and the final defense phase onto the slide from epistemic limitation to metaphysical indeterminacy. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply: specifically, the modal distinction between permanent inaccessibility (the properties are in principle hidden) and temporary ignorance, and the precise claim that ontology does not merely await discovery but is genuinely unsettled when kind-determining information is absent. Also predict what will go wrong the first time: participants will likely treat the sealed envelopes as containing information they could in principle access, and will attempt to infer the hidden properties from surface patterns; the facilitator should block this by enforcing a strict rule that no inference to the envelope's contents is permitted, and should be ready to point out when participants smuggle in assumptions about discoverability.

The idea should emerge from the commitments, losses, and disputes generated by the rules, not from an upfront lecture. Do not ask participants to cite authors or read specific texts; the facilitator should name the concept only in the debrief. Ensure that the scenario's reliance on relational hidden properties and forced bets on cross-spatial continuity requires reasoning about the specific mechanics rather than retrieval of a standard classroom example.
