I'm putting together a film-discussion group and we're watching Amadeus next week — the eighties film everyone knows, where Salieri confesses to poisoning Mozart and Mozart himself is this giggling vulgar genius who writes masterpieces without breaking a sweat. I want to use it to think through something that keeps bugging me about historical drama: what do we actually owe people who've been dead for two centuries when we turn them into characters? The film basicly invents a murder that never happened, and it turns Mozart's real life into raw material for a parable about divine unfairness. Theres a standard philosophical debate about whether the dead can be wronged at all — if harm requires suffering and they can't suffer, maybe there's no moral debt — but I've been playing with a tighter framing, maybe call it narrative extraction: treating an actual existence as scafolding for your aesthetic object, where the wrong isn't suffering but a kind of ontological disrespect, like you're mining someone's biography for dramatic ore regardless of its real shape.

Read Amadeus through that lens. Where does the film seem aware that it's extracting — maybe in the framing where Salieri confesses to a priest and seems to beg forgiveness from the dead? — and where does it just go ahead and do it anyway? Does turning Mozart into a symbol of unfair genius tell us something about resentment and talent that a stricter portrait couldn't, or is that just an excuse? And where does the work resist being read this way — maybe in the music scenes, where Mozart's actual compositions are played with real reverance? I want a reading that doesn't just say artistic license is fine or that biopics should be accurate, but that takes both the film's dramatic logic and the moral problem seriously, and says whether Amadeus adds something to the philosophical argument about posthumous harm that a straight essay wouldn't capture. If there's one scene you'd point the group to that crystallizes the tension, say which one and why.
