I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I am currently developing a long-form feature on how philosophical ontology quietly infiltrates everyday demographic language. For a companion teaching resource aimed at final-year undergraduates in the philosophy of social science, I need a single take-home essay question that asks whether so-called generations—Baby Boomers, Millennials, Generation Z, and similar cohort labels—are real kinds rather than statistical conveniences or marketing fictions. The question should not permit students to fall back on rehearsing the standard cohort-effect versus period-effect distinction; instead, it should embed a precise conceptual tension that demands original reasoning. Specifically, it should ask them to evaluate whether generations might operate as interactive kinds that are causally affected by the very classifications used to describe them, while simultaneously confronting a position I shall call demographic nominalism, which treats generational boundaries as arbitrary cuts through continuous age-graded processes that lack any unified causal structure. The prompt must also require students to consider whether the self-conscious uptake of generational identity—people altering their behaviour because they believe themselves to belong to a named cohort—undermines realism about generations, or whether it can be reconciled with a moderate realist view that allows social kinds to be partly constituted by reflexive awareness without thereby becoming unreal. Please write the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately seven hundred words, and then a marking scheme, all composed in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other formatting devices, exactly as one might write in a formal letter or email. The marking scheme should name the specific argumentative moves that separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one, and it should identify the particular misconceptions—such as conflating statistical correlation with kind-membership, or assuming that reflexive constitution automatically entails ontological unreality—that should cost marks. Please write entirely in British English, avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from named texts; if reference to a specific theorist is necessary, paraphrase without quotation marks.
