Imagine the Marquis de Condorcet had survived into the present, mastered formal social choice theory, and fully absorbed the Gibbard–Satterthwaite impossibility result together with the subsequent mechanism-design literature on strategy-proofness. Drawing only on his documented commitments—his jury theorem, his probabilistic theory of collective reason, his suspicion of factional manipulation, and his institutional preference for large, educated, heterogeneous deliberative bodies—reconstruct how he would likely respond to the theorem. Specifically: (1) Would he treat the result as a fatal indictment of democratic aggregation, or as a design constraint that vindicates his preference for expansive juries over small committees? (2) How would he reconcile his conviction that rational agents converge on truth with the demonstration that strategic misrepresentation is unavoidable in any non-dictatorial rule with three or more alternatives? (3) Would he be inclined to escape the impossibility by restricting the preference domain—say, through deliberative pre-processing that enforces single-peakedness—or would he accept strategic behavior as an irreducible feature of collective reason? Do not treat Condorcet as a modern mouthpiece; instead, trace where his eighteenth-century framework (probability, education, institutional scale) naturally extends to the formal result and where it genuinely strains. Conclude by identifying the single revision he would find least costly: relaxing his optimism about rational convergence, accepting restricted domains, or redesigning institutions to absorb manipulation rather than eliminate it.
