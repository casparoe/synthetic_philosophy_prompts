I keep noticing a particular tension in debates about artificial sentience. A philosopher argues that some specific computation—say, integrated information processing or a global workspace architecture—suffices for moral status, regardless of substrate. A critic then points out that the very same computation can be distributed across disconnected devices that never interact locally, executed sequentially with long interruptions, or run redundantly on two servers with an arbitrary tie-breaker selecting one output. In each case the functional profile is preserved, yet people who endorsed the computation-based criterion suddenly want to add conditions about spatial integration, temporal continuity, or causal unity—conditions that were absent from the original claim. The phenomenon is not ordinary substrate bias; it is a specific argumentative pattern in which acceptance of a functionalist criterion collapses once the computation’s physical boundaries become ambiguous, and defenders retreat to boundary conditions they had previously rejected. Is there an established name for this pattern—perhaps in the literature on functionalism, group minds, or distributed cognition? How exactly does it differ from simple substrate bias, and from the problem of defining an organism’s boundaries? If no specific term exists, could you coin one, with a clear definition and boundary cases that distinguish it from mere inconsistency?
