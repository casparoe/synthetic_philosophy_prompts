I serve on the acquisitions and descriptive-cataloging committee of our university's special collections library. We have received a bequest consisting of approximately four hundred loose index cards, each containing a single clause or logical formula, together with a handwritten note stating that these constitute "one argument about the conditions for propositional unity," but providing no ordering principle, no explicit binding relation among the cards, and no clearly marked conclusion. The donor's estate insists the cards must be archived, described, and digitized as a single manuscript item—tentatively titled The Regress of Relations—with a unified finding aid and one persistent identifier, on the ground that the author's intent was to present one unified proposition about Bradley's regress. A faction on our committee argues that without a specified relation that binds the cards into an ordered whole—whether temporal, logical, or authorial—we face precisely the regress Bradley identified: if we invoke a "cataloging relation" to unify them, that relation itself requires further unification with its relata, generating an infinite regress; therefore we should catalog them as four hundred separate items, each with its own identifier, leaving any synthetic reading to future scholars. Another faction holds that treating them as separate destroys the very philosophical content, since the argument is allegedly about how relations fail to achieve unity without infinite regress, and fragmenting the artifact reproduces the failure the argument describes. Our policy manual requires us to preserve "the integrity of the original artifact" but offers no criteria for what counts as one artifact versus many when the physical form is deliberately dispersed.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—approve unified cataloging, approve distributed cataloging, or approve a hybrid scheme with conditions—addressed to the library director and due before next month's board review. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action with an implementation time frame (for example, preliminary descriptive work within sixty days and a final binding decision within one hundred twenty days), and it should engage seriously with the philosophical problem: specifically, whether the absence of an explicit binding relation in the physical object genuinely entails that no unified proposition is present, or whether authorial intent and the thematic coherence of the clauses can serve as the stopping point for the regress without themselves becoming new relata requiring further unification. Treat the strongest version of the opposing view fairly—if you recommend unified cataloging, explain precisely why the regress concern does not defeat it and what institutional risk it creates; if you recommend distributed cataloging, explain why authorial intent and thematic coherence cannot halt the regress without arbitrarily privileging one relation over others. Do not request bibliographic references, citations, or a reading list; reason from the conceptual structure of Bradley's regress and from archival practice itself. You may assume familiarity with the basic structure of the regress (that relating relations to relata seems to require further relations), but do not assume the reader knows cataloging ontology.
