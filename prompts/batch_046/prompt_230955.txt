You are a professional philosopher of science and social ontology with expertise in gender studies, labor economics, and normative theory. Before you respond, take a moment to brainstorm the empirical, conceptual, and normative commitments hidden in the view below; then work through them step by step.

Consider this position: The gender-equality paradox—larger gender differences in career and study choices in more egalitarian countries—is best explained by a constraint-release model. In less egalitarian societies, economic pressure and rigid gender roles suppress innate vocational preferences, forcing convergence; in egalitarian societies, structural freedom allows authentic, biologically influenced divergence to emerge. The paradox is therefore not evidence of lingering socialization but of its removal. From this it follows that policies aiming for 50/50 representation in every field treat a naturally emerging distribution as an injustice, substituting engineered parity for freely expressed preference, which is not only unrealistic but normatively suspect.

What would have to be true for this position to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of generic objections. Which empirical patterns—say, about within-country change over time as egalitarianism increases, about cross-cultural variability in the size and direction of the paradox, about the interaction between economic structure and cultural signaling—would be decisive, and which merely awkward? Which conceptual distinctions—between preference, choice, and opportunity, or between biological influence and fixed destiny—must the view hold firm, and where do they fray? What normative premises about the relation between natural distributions and justice must its defenders accept, and which of these would they themselves concede as vulnerable? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed defender of the constraint-release view would treat it as genuinely counting against them or would dismiss it, and be explicit about whether the position turns out to be well insulated or exposed, and whether that insulation is a strength or a liability.
