Following the recent death of my father, I have found myself returning repeatedly to works of tragic art—specifically requiem compositions and classical dramas centered on irreversible loss—despite the fact that they evoke profound sadness. This has led me to reflect on what philosophers call the paradox of tragedy: our voluntary engagement with representations that elicit negative emotional states we typically seek to avoid in ordinary life. I wish to understand this not merely as a psychological curiosity, but as a conceptual problem concerning the relationship between aesthetic form and emotional value.

Could you please explain the conceptual architecture of the paradox, particularly by distinguishing the Aristotelian catharsis account from the modern aesthetic-distance and rich-experience accounts? I am especially interested in a tension that seems under-examined: many of the works I find most meaningful are those with ambiguous endings or unresolved grief, which appear to deny the very cathartic release that traditional explanations invoke. In addressing this, please consider whether the resolution requires what might be termed affective bifurcation, whereby one experiences sorrow at the representational level of the narrative while simultaneously maintaining an evaluative appreciation of emotional coherence at the formal level. How does this bifurcation operate when narrative closure is deliberately withheld, and does it help explain why such art feels intellectually and emotionally necessary rather than merely painful? I am not seeking a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific scholars; I want a conceptual clarification that can stand on its own reasoning.
