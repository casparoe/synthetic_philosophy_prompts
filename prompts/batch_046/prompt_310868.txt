As a novelist presently composing a work in which a bronze sculptor fashions a figure that eventually acquires legal standing within the plot, I have become preoccupied with the metaphysics of material constitution—particularly the familiar puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay from which it is formed. In philosophical correspondence with a colleague, I have articulated the following cluster of commitments, which I now wish to submit for coherence testing. First, I maintain that the statue and its composing clay are numerically distinct, since they bear different persistence conditions: the clay can survive melting and reformation, whereas the statue cannot. Second, I deny that this distinction generates an objectionable ontological redundancy, on the grounds that co-located entities are only problematic when they contend for identical causal powers, and in artifactual cases all efficacy is ultimately mediated through the material substrate. Third, I extend this constitutional analysis to fictional entities—specifically, to a statue-character in my narrative, whom I call The Unnamed—holding that such figures are constituted by but not identical to the textual sequences that present them, despite lacking spatiotemporal location. Fourth, I insist that if every physical copy of my manuscript were destroyed and all memory of its narrative content extinguished, The Unnamed would cease to exist, thereby rejecting any robust Platonism about abstract or fictional objects. Fifth, I contend that when I revise a passage and thereby alter the properties of The Unnamed, I do not annihilate one object and generate another; rather, I modify the properties of a single constituted entity, just as a sculptor might reshape a statue without destroying the underlying clay.

I am concerned, however, that these commitments do not form a stable equilibrium. My anti-Platonist fourth thesis appears to undermine the constitutional model I invoke in the third and fifth: if fictional objects depend for existence upon physical tokens or mental representations, they may lack the autonomous persistence conditions required for a genuine relation of constitution, reducing them instead to mere descriptive dependents. Conversely, my second thesis—that causal mediation defuses the objection of excessive ontological proliferation—seems to leave the statue ontologically inert, since if all causal labor is performed by the clay, it is unclear what independent work the statue performs beyond serving as a useful fiction. Meanwhile, my first thesis asserts a real modal distinction between statue and clay, yet my fifth thesis treats revision of the fictional statue as analogous to physical reshaping, which may illegitimately import spatiotemporal persistence conditions into a non-spatial domain. Would you audit this set of beliefs? Please specify which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are merely apparent, and rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, indicate the least costly revision and what that revision sacrifices. Finally, advise whether a single adjustment—perhaps to my account of fictional constitution or to my resolution of the causal-mediation problem—could resolve the majority of inconsistencies without forfeiting my central intuition that material constitution operates differently upon physical artifacts and upon characters in fiction.
