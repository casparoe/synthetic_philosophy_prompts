I have recently been trying to decide whether I can rationally endorse my own judgment about a major career transition, and I kept encountering a frustrating circularity: every argument I constructed in favor of my reasoning seemed to depend on assuming the reliability of that very reasoning. A colleague in formal epistemology pointed me toward the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents—the result that in a formal system capable of representing its own provability relation, an agent cannot consistently maintain the reflective principle that "if I have proven φ, then φ is true" without being compelled to prove φ outright, which collapses the distinction between justified belief and arbitrary conclusion. This seems to undermine any attempt to validate one's own cognitive processes from within them, yet I am unsure whether the obstacle is unavoidable or merely an artifact of treating the agent as a single, uniformly reflexive system. I am therefore writing the final exam for my upper-level graduate seminar in philosophical logic and rational agency, and I want a question that forces students to move beyond reciting Löb's theorem and instead confront whether a specific architectural workaround I have been considering can genuinely escape the obstacle.

Write one essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam, expecting an answer of approximately 800 words. The question should ask students to explain the Löbian obstacle as it applies specifically to self-trusting agents rather than to formal systems in the abstract—clarifying why an agent's belief in its own soundness triggers the self-referential loop that activates the theorem, whereas belief in another system's soundness does not. Then present them with the "staged reflection" architecture: Stage 1 generates candidate beliefs using a full set of inferential rules R; Stage 2 evaluates those candidates using only a restricted logical subset L, with the explicit design constraint that Stage 2 does not apply L reflexively to its own evaluations. Students must determine whether this separation allows the agent to consistently endorse Stage 1's outputs without being forced to "prove" its own soundness outright, or whether the obstacle re-emerges—whether in Stage 2's meta-justification of L, in the hand-off relation between stages, or in the agent's global identification with both stages as parts of a single reasoning system. They should identify the precise locus of reintroduced self-reference and assess whether relocating the problem to an inter-stage relation solves it or merely reproduces it at a higher level.
