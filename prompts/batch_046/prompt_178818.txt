I sit on an ethics review board for a university comparative-cognition unit, and we recieve about a dozen proposals a month that claim to study 'problem solving' or 'insight' without saying which kind of mind they're actually probing. I'd like you to turn the Darwinian-Skinnerian-Popperian-Gregorian taxonomy into a screening procedure my panel can run in twenty minutes per proposal — no philosophers on staff, just biologists and a vet, so keep the language practical and avoid jargon that needs a textbook citation to defend.

Write it as an ordered checklist: the questions we ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where we stop and reject because the study design doesn't match the claimed cognitive architecture, and where we have to hand the decision to values rather than facts — say, when the science is sound but the behavioural risk crosses a line only ethics can draw. The procedure needs to catch false Gregorian cases, where symbolic labels mask Skinnerian conditioning, and Popperian overreach, where researchers assume inner modelling but only measure outward behaviour.

Then apply it to two proposals: one testing whether New Caledonian crows select hook-shaped wire after a single demonstration (claimed Gregorian insight), and one running a variable-ratio reinforcement schedule with dolphins to test reward-rate optimisation (claimed Popperian expectation). Make sure one case gives us an answer we'll dislike — probably that the study is misclassified but scientifically sound, which means more paperwork, not less.

Don't lay out options and leave us to pick: take a definite position on which misclassifications are most dangerous for animal welfare, defend that ranking, and say exactly where the procedure will misfire — which hybrid minds it can't sort, and why that limitation is actually useful. Also flag the points where judgement can't be eliminated, no matter how good the checklist is.
