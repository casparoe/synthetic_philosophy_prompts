I sit on a clinical ethics review board at a large teaching hospital, and we are currently revising our end-of-life care protocol. The draft contains three provisions that sit in an uneasy relation to one another. First, a competent patient may request the withdrawal of mechanical ventilation or artificial nutrition and hydration, even when death is both certain and foreseen; the clinical notes classify this as allowing nature to take its course, and no further justification beyond informed consent is required. Second, the same protocol prohibits clinicians from administering any substance with the explicit purpose of causing death, regardless of the patient's suffering or consent; the institutional position is that we do not kill, and any request for such assistance triggers a mandatory psychiatric evaluation. Third, however, the protocol permits palliative sedation to unconsciousness using doses of barbiturates or benzodiazepines that are known to suppress respiration, provided the documented primary intention is the relief of suffering rather than the hastening of death — and this is permitted even in cases where the patient reports no physical pain but describes continued existence as unbearable. Meanwhile, should a patient with a treatable infection decline antibiotics and subsequently die, the event is recorded as an omission of treatment consistent with autonomy, with no psychiatric referral required.

What philosophical commitments about the moral distinction between doing and allowing, or between killing and letting die, does this institutional framework presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on a causal account in which the clinician's action is not the source of death in withdrawal, on an intentionalist account derived from the doctrine of double effect, on a normative account about the proper role of medical institutions, or on some unstable combination? Is the framework philosophically coherent, or does it quietly presuppose inconsistent views — for instance, treating intention as morally decisive in the case of sedation but as irrelevant in the case of withdrawal or refusal? Furthermore, if the underlying distinction were replaced by a welfare-based calculus that considers only consent, prognosis, and the burden of suffering, which specific provisions of the protocol would necessarily change, and which could remain intact?
