I am a high school student writing for my school's philosophy journal, and I am trying to understand why the debate between covering-law and narrative explanation in history never seems to advance. Proponents of the covering-law view argue that to explain an event like the fall of Rome we must show it followed from general conditions and antecedent laws, while narrative theorists insist that explanation comes from placing events in a coherent sequence with meaningful connections rather than subsumption under laws. Both sides have standard replies—the law camp says narratives smuggle in implicit generalizations, the narrative camp says laws miss the particularity of human action—and neither moves. I suspect the stall is not about explanation per se but about whether historical understanding requires supporting interventionist counterfactuals: knowing not just that events followed one another, but whether altering a specific contingency would have disrupted the outcome, something narrative coherence might license differently than law-based deduction. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or are the sides answering different questions—one about causal structure, one about intelligibility? And what distinction, example, or conceptual clarification could actually make progress?
