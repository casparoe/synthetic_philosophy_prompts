I've been messing around with an idea for an essay that applies Good's theorem on the value of information to recursive forecasting tournaments — the kind where people aren't just predicting outcomes, they're betting on each other's bias corrections, and the information value gets weird. Basically, learning about forecaster A's error model doesn't just update my beliefs about the world; it updates A's self-model and incentives, which feeds back and destabilizes the very calibration data I was using. I think there's a kind of negative-value information case here that Good's framework can capture if you model the forecaster as part of the environment rather than an exogenous sensor.

Rough notes:
- Start with standard Good: value equals expected utility gain from acting on new info.
- Introduce the reflexive twist: in social prediction markets, information targets are agents who observe being observed.
- Argue that the referance class for similar forecasting errors collapses once the error is public, because the agent adapts.
- So the optimal decision rule might involve strategic ignorance — deliberately not acquiring certain calibration reports — which runs against the usual rationalist instinct that more data dominates.
- End by asking whether this is actually a novel corollary or just an overdramatized version of something already covered by mechanism design or game theory.

Could you comment on whether this holds together? I'm especially unsure about whether I'm treating agent transparency consistently — do I need to assume perfect rationality for the reflexive loop to bite, or does bounded rationality actually make it worse? Also, is there a cleaner way to formalize the idea that information destroys its own substrate without just gesturing at feedback loops? And be honest: is the strategic-ignorance conclusion genuinely counterintuitive, or am I just dressing up an obvious point about not spooking the market? Answer in American English please.
