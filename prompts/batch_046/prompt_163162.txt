I am revising my lecture notes for an advanced seminar on Islamic philosophy of mind, specifically on Avicenna's flying-man argument. The standard interpretation holds that the scenario—imagining a fully formed human suspended in void, deprived of all external and internal sensation—establishes only that the soul possesses an immediate, non-inferential awareness of its own existence. Yet I have recently encountered an interpretive suggestion, advanced in some contemporary scholarship, that treats the argument as a broader ontological proof: if the soul can maintain not only self-awareness but also the full structure of rational cognition—including the grasp of primary intelligibles and the operation of the estimative faculty—without any corporeal mediation, then Avicenna is effectively committed to a form of substance dualism in which the intellect is a complete, self-sufficient substance that merely uses the body as an instrument. Assuming this stronger reading is textually defensible, I am trying to work out its consequences for Avicenna's doctrine of the acquired intellect. Does the flying man entail that the acquired intellect can achieve its full actualization in complete isolation from sensory experience, thereby collapsing the distinction between potential and actual intellect? Or is there a way to preserve the stronger ontological claim while still maintaining that the body plays a necessary, if not sufficient, role in the acquisition of intelligibles? I want to present this to my students without conflating Avicenna's specific concern with self-consciousness and a more general Cartesian separation of mind and body, but I find the textual evidence in both the Kitab al-Shifa' and al-Isharat ambiguous on this point.
