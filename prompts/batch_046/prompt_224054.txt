As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or law, I have been thinking about legal systems through analogies to version control and cryptographic root-of-trust. I have come to accept the following position: every coherent normative system—whether a constitution, a legal code, or a smart-contract protocol—must contain at least one immutable ground rule that cannot be amended by any rule within the system, including itself, functioning like a hard-coded root key that validates updates but cannot be rotated by the system's own logic. Without such an unamendable anchor, any self-amending framework eventually collapses into either an infinite regress of meta-rules or a direct paradox of self-reference, such as a rule that authorizes its own revocation. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the standard democratic objection that all rules should be amendable, but which formal results in logic or legal theory, which historical constitutional practices, and which distinctions between internal amendment and external replacement it stands or falls with. Which defeaters would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the system-boundary distinction? I am especially interested in whether the position is unavoidable—no coherent system can lack such an anchor—or impossible—no rule can genuinely be immune to all systemic change. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether this view is well-insulated or genuinely exposed to collapse.
