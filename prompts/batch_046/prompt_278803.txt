I'm working through what I actually take to be the non-negotiable conditions on any morally adequate justification for capital punishment, as opposed to the commitments that merely mark one camp—retributivist, deterrence-based, or abolitionist. For a philosophy of punishment seminar I'm helping design, I want a specification of the desiderata: the phenomena such a theory must explain, the distinctions it must respect, the consistency conditions with other moral commitments, and which of these are genuinely mandatory rather than preferences of particular schools. The motivating cases behind each requirement should be made explicit, and I want an honest assessment of whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly given the risk of executing the innocent.

Several tensions keep surfacing in my own thinking, and I want them built into the list. One concerns proportionality: if the worst crimes deserve the worst punishment, but our epistemic limits mean we can never be certain we've correctly identified the worst offenders, does that create an in-principle conflict, or can proportionality be satisfied at the level of procedure rather than outcome? Another is what I think of as the annihilation problem—if execution destroys the very moral agent who was meant to "pay" the debt, does that undermine retributive coherence in a way that permanent incapacitation avoids? I also want the list to include a requirement about the asymmetry of irreversibility: that execution, unlike imprisonment, permits no partial remedy if the verdict is later overturned, and any adequate theory must either show why this asymmetry doesn't matter or explain why it is outweighed. Finally, there's a condition about specificity: the theory must explain why execution is appropriate rather than lifelong incapacitation, not merely that death is a permissible option.
