I've been thinking about this alot lately since I started volunteering at a community garden where we spray for aphids, and I cant shake the feeling I'm killing something that might matter. There's this deadlocked argument I keep seeing: one camp says insects dont have subjective experience—brains are too simple, no real integration, behavior is basically reflex loops. The other camp says okay, the individual probability is low, but there are something like ten quintillion insects alive right now, so even a tiny chance of sentience makes the potential suffering enormous. Then the first side replies that multiplying near-zero by huge numbers still gives near-zero, or that we shouldnt change farming on pure speculation. It just circles. My hunch is the stall isnt really about entomology—its about whether moral reasoning should aggregate over vast populations under deep uncertainty, versus requiring near-proof before we restrict human activity. But maybe Im wrong and its something else entirely. I want an honest diagnosis, not a literature review: are these sides fighting over brain facts, over what counts as adequate evidence for consciousness, or over hidden rules about risk and expected value? Is one side doing science and the other doing ethics under one name? Are they answering different questions—what's in a beetle's head versus how we should act—without realizing it? Use what each side actually says. And tell me what, if anything, could actually move things: a new neuroscience result, a conceptual distinction, admitting the question is ill-posed, or is this basically permanent because the premises are just incompatible intuitions?
