I spent last evening in a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether health and disease are purely descriptive, evolutionary-functional categories or whether they are irreducibly normative. I came away still convinced by a particular version of naturalism: a condition counts as a disease if and only if it represents a deviation from statistically typical functional efficiency within an organism’s evolutionary-design reference class, where function is defined strictly in terms of contribution to survival and reproduction, and where the reference class is calibrated not by universal human norms but by the specific ecological and developmental niche the organism actually occupies. I want to push this further and insist that even mental conditions—say, severe anxiety in a genuinely threatening environment—are not diseases unless there is a measurable functional deviation relative to that niche-calibrated baseline, regardless of whether the subject suffers or whether we judge the state undesirable. This means I deny that suffering, social dysfunction, or any evaluative judgment plays even a hidden role in classification.

What I need now is an honest map of what would genuinely count against this view, not just the standard normativist complaints about mental illness or the usual evolutionary-mismatch objections. Which concrete cases or empirical findings—perhaps in evolutionary medicine, psychiatric genetics, or the philosophy of disability—would force me to concede that some normative judgment is doing unavoidable work? Are there formal results or conceptual arguments showing that the selection of reference classes or functional goals is itself value-laden in a way that cannot be eliminated by niche-calibration? Which of these defeaters would defenders of this naturalism themselves acknowledge as serious, and which would they dismiss as category errors? Has the view been qualified—say, by allowing ad hoc adjustments to the reference class—into something unfalsifiable, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal weakness for a position that claims to be purely descriptive? Please rank the potential defeaters from those that should genuinely change my mind to those that are merely awkward, and say whether any available evidence already counts, or whether the view remains largely protected.
