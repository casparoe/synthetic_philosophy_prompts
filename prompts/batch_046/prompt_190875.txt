I'm teaching a seminar on urban justice, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative move in local zoning disputes that I haven't been able to map onto the standard literature. The pattern works like this: a resident opposes a proposed multi-family development not by disputing regional housing needs, not by alleging direct property-value harm, and not by invoking environmental risk, but by asserting that the neighborhood possesses an established "character" that existing residents have implicitly contracted into by purchasing homes there. What makes the move distinctive is its temporal structure: the resident treats the physical and demographic state of the neighborhood at the moment of their own arrival as a normative baseline, so that any subsequent alteration counts as a kind of breach of legitimate expectation, regardless of whether earlier waves of residents made precisely the same complaint against them. I have observed this in several municipal hearings, and it seems to differ from ordinary NIMBYism because the grievance is not proximity or density as such, but what feels like a backward-looking entitlement to stasis—later arrivals "ruining" what earlier arrivals "bought into." Is there an established concept in the philosophy of property, urban ethics, or legal theory that captures this specific temporal-entitlement version of local opposition? If the literature lacks a precise label, can you coin and define one that clearly distinguishes it from generic status-quo bias, from Lockean labor-mixing arguments about home improvement, and from the standard collective-action framing of zoning conflicts? Please say how confident you are in the identification or proposed taxonomy, and single out the specific point—whether in the conceptual boundaries, the comparison to existing frameworks, or the normative assessment—about which you are least certain.
