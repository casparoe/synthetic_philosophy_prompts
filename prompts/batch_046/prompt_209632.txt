I facilitate a 90-minute graduate workshop for 16 participants (mixed history and philosophy students) on how societies construct usable pasts from decisive defeat. Design a structured negotiation game in which four committees—each assigned an anonymized historical fragment drawn from the American Lost Cause, the Masada narrative, the Kosovo mythos, and the Dunkirk mythology—must jointly produce a single commemorative artifact (a monument inscription, a mandatory school module, or a national ritual) that transforms military failure into collective identity. Give each committee hidden stakeholder roles with conflicting payoff rules: commemorative architects score points for emotional coherence and present-day political utility; critical historians lose points for factual distortion but gain for archival rigor; state educators are penalized if projected student cynicism exceeds a threshold; diaspora activists score only if the narrative supports current territorial or moral claims. Do not reveal the hidden payoffs to other groups. The rules should force committees to choose between historical precision and mythic utility without being told that this is the theme—for example, by requiring unanimous approval of language that must simultaneously satisfy contradictory scoring criteria. Include materials (the fragments should be brief, neutral 200-word summaries of events, not interpretations), a timeline for negotiation rounds, and a debrief that maps specific bargaining moves onto the structural dynamics of lost-cause mythology: moral inversion of defeat into virtue, attributional displacement onto betrayal or overwhelming force, and sacrificial framing that makes the fallen into eternal exemplars. Be frank about which feature the simulation cannot reproduce—such as the multi-generational sedimentation of myth or the absence of lived trauma among participants—and predict what will go wrong the first time (e.g., participants identifying the real-world analogues prematurely and performing for the facilitator, or converging on bland compromise to avoid conflict). The participants should not hear the phrase "lost cause" or the names of the four analogues until the debrief; the force of the idea must come from the negotiation mechanics themselves.
