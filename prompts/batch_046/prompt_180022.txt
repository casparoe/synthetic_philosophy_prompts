As a retired biologist who has recently taken up philosophy of mind, I have become dissatisfied with the standard framing of Molyneux's problem, which seems to assume only two possibilities: immediate cross-modal recognition or association learnt through experience. I should like you to argue for a more neglected position: that the problem is malformed because it tacitly presupposes a 'translation model' in which tactile and visual representations must share an amodal format, whereas recognition ought instead to be conceived as a graded, modality-specific competence that requires no inter-modal translation whatsoever. In constructing this argument, please avoid appeals to bibliographies, recent empirical citations, or lists of further reading; rely instead on conceptual distinctions between recognitional capacity, inferential reconstruction, and what one might term the 'statistical alignment' of independent sensory streams. I am especially keen to know how your account treats the case in which a subject can match shapes across modalities only after prolonged practice: does this indicate that translation eventually takes place, or merely that two autonomous recognitional systems have become calibrated? Keep the reasoning precise and formal, but do not invoke specific authors or texts by quotation.
