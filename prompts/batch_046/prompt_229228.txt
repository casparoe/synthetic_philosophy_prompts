You serve on the Allocation and Governance Committee of the Meridian Cooperative Research Institute, a self-governing collective whose founding charter commits it to a patterned distributive principle: internal research resources and collective decision-making weight are distributed equally among members, with adjustments only for demonstrated need. The charter explicitly permits members to enter voluntary private arrangements—specialized training, consulting, or peer-to-peer exchange—provided these do not draw on institute-funded infrastructure.

Over the past eighteen months, senior member Dr. Elena Vasquez began offering an optional, fee-based advanced methodological workshop open to other members. Participation was entirely voluntary; members paid using portions of their base stipends, and Vasquez used the proceeds to expand her personal research infrastructure. More consequentially, the institute’s governance system links accumulated reputation tokens—earned through peer endorsement—to voting weight on budgetary and disciplinary matters. Vasquez’s workshop popularity generated a large token surplus, giving her roughly thirty-four percent of collective voting weight, up from the intended four percent (equal share among twenty-four members).

A coalition of twelve members has petitioned the Committee to invoke Article VII, which permits emergency redistribution when the patterned equality of the collective is materially disrupted. They request that Vasquez’s surplus tokens and accumulated workshop revenue be reallocated to restore equal democratic weight and resource parity, arguing that the charter’s patterned principle was adopted precisely to prevent economic advantage from converting into institutional control.

Vasquez responds that every token and every stipend transfer was acquired through fully voluntary exchange with no coercion; she argues that redistributing her holdings violates the historical entitlement view—just holdings arise from legitimate acquisition and free transfer, not from conformity to an end-state pattern. She further contends that the charter’s allowance for private arrangements was meant to protect exactly this liberty, and that treating token accumulation as a disruption mistakes a feature of free association for a failure.

The Committee must issue a formal written opinion before next month’s general assembly. Recommend a concrete course of action—approve the petition and redistribute, reject it and preserve Vasquez’s holdings, or approve with conditions (for example, a structural firewall between economic tokens and voting weight, a cooling-off period, or a graduated cap on token-to-vote conversion)—together with a clear implementation time frame. The reasoning should treat Vasquez’s entitlement-based objection with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as self-interest, and should present the strongest argument for the position that ultimately loses. Write the document so that members without background in distributive justice can understand and act on it, but precisely enough that Vasquez and the petitioners can see exactly which premises are accepted or rejected. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers. Where philosophical distinctions are invoked, define them in the opinion’s own terms.
