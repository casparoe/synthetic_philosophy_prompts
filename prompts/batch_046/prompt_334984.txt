I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy preparing a proposal for a six-month research fellowship in applied ethics. I want to investigate the procreation asymmetry — specifically, whether the standard asymmetry between reasons not to create suffering lives and reasons to create happy ones survives once we distinguish between 'choice-contingent' and 'background-contingent' potential existence. My half-formed suspicion is that philosophers have treated the asymmetry as a uniform feature of person-affecting morality, but that it may actually vary with the modal proximity of the potential person to the actual world: when existence hinges on a discrete, avoidable decision (choice-contingent), the asymmetry may be robust; when it hinges on diffuse social structures (background-contingent), it may weaken or reverse. Please write the proposal. It should state the precise research question, explain why existing debates in population ethics leave this modal distinction unexamined, set out the conceptual method for distinguishing the two contingency types without relying on contested theories of personal identity, describe what a successful result and a clear failure would each look like, and include an honest assessment of feasibility within six months. Write in British English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not treat standard thought experiments about happy versus unhappy children as if they settle the matter on their own.
