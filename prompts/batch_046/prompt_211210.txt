I am seeking your counsel on a disagreement that has arisen within a small indoor-farming cooperative I help advise. For years the group has run its hydroponic lettuce operation according to observed regularities: they calibrate nutrient pH within a narrow band, maintain specific light cycles, and expect predictable yields. One founding member has recently begun insisting that these human adjustments are not genuine causes of the harvest but only the circumstances that happen to coincide with it, while the growth itself is directly produced by a source that operates independently of any mechanism they observe. He argues that relying on correlation for planning is therefore a kind of practical error, and that the cooperative should abandon predictive scheduling in favor of treating each growing cycle as an isolated event with no necessary link to prior actions. The remaining members counter that giving up correlation-based planning would make collective labor impossible, and that even if every outcome ultimately depends on something beyond the pH adjustment, the adjustment remains the reliable pathway through which they must work. They have asked me whether the founder's distinction can be maintained without collapsing the cooperative's ability to organize its labor, and whether there is a philosophically consistent way to honor his concern without discarding practical reasoning altogether. I am not looking for agricultural tips or generic mediation strategies; I want to understand whether the argument that observed regularities lack true causal force can be sustained, what it implies for human agency and collective planning, and how one might advise them to restructure—or not—their relationship to the schedules they have built. Please answer in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, as one might write in a careful letter.
