I was discussing with a friend last night, over rather too much wine, whether the course of history is genuinely open to small perturbations or whether large-scale patterns are effectively inevitable. We kept returning to the nursery rhyme about the lost nail and the fallen kingdom, but we could not agree on what kind of evidence, or what kind of conceptual distinction, would actually settle whether history is chaotic, contingent, or structurally determined. I am now trying to formalise our disagreement for a small philosophy-of-history reading group, and I want a diagnostic taxonomy — specifically a decision tree — that sorts someone by the commitments they actually hold rather than by the label they prefer.

Please construct the tree so that its root asks whether macro-historical outcomes are sensitive to micro-level initial conditions in a way that makes long-term prediction impossible in principle (a chaos-theoretic reading), or whether such sensitivity is merely an epistemic limitation that leaves the underlying historical process fully determined. The next branches should ask whether human agency introduces genuinely branching possibilities that are not reducible to prior causal chains, and whether the classic nail scenario requires that the lost battle actually depended on the specific absence of that nail rather than on some structurally equivalent substitute failure — for instance, whether a lost horseshoe in an otherwise identical scenario would still produce the identical kingdom’s collapse. From these combinations, locate at least the following leaves: a strong contingency view (radical openness), a weak contingency view (local openness within broad structural constraints), a structural determinism that allows epistemic chaos but denies ontological openness, a retrocausal or counterfactual-dependence view (the nail matters only because narrative selection singles it out), and an unnamed position that denies both chaos and determinism — perhaps a cyclical or attractor-based model. For each leaf, provide its most awkward commitment in one sentence, and flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate.

Crucially, phrase every branching node as a concrete case or claim that a respondent could answer without knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to — for example, ask whether substituting a different minor failure in an otherwise identical scenario would still produce the kingdom’s fall. Build the taxonomy from the logical space of answers first, then map the named or unnamed positions onto that space, noting which combinations remain unoccupied and briefly diagnosing whether that emptiness suggests incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The answer should be written in British English, and it should not rely on bibliographic citations, reading lists, or references to obscure historiographical debates — I want an instrument that works for someone reasoning from first principles alone.
