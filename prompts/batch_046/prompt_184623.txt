I am trying to determine the correct normative architecture for autonomous decision systems—specifically, whether they should be designed to reason as act-utilitarians, as rule-deontologists, or according to some intermediate principle—and I want to see whether my intuitions form a coherent pattern.

Here are my verdicts on concrete design scenarios.

(1) A medical triage algorithm saves five critically ill patients by withholding an expensive intervention from a sixth patient whose survival probability is 5%. Verdict: permissible, indeed obligatory.

(2) An autonomous vehicle facing an unavoidable collision must choose between killing its sole passenger and killing three pedestrians. Verdict: it must not kill the passenger; the greater aggregate harm does not justify it.

(3) A content-moderation system applies an exceptionless prohibition on displaying graphic violence, which results in the automated removal of historical documentary footage of documented war crimes. Verdict: wrong; the system should have recognized a context-sensitive exception for educational and evidentiary value.

(4) A healthcare-rationing AI maximizes quality-adjusted life-years, which systematically assigns lower priority to patients with rare diseases because treating them is statistically inefficient. Verdict: unjust, not merely suboptimal.

(5) I have been considering an architecture I call a moral-residue register: the system computes the utilitarian optimum for a given situation, but if that optimum requires violating a deontological side-constraint (e.g., treating an innocent person merely as a means), the register records the violation without blocking execution; the system then selects the best option that respects the constraint. Verdict: conceptually incoherent—if the constraint genuinely binds, registration should not substitute for compliance, and if it does not bind, the architecture is merely utilitarianism with decorative bookkeeping.

(6) A predictive-policing model applies formally identical risk thresholds across all neighborhoods. Because of historically skewed training data, this produces starkly disparate surveillance burdens on one marginalized community. Verdict: the system ought to adjust its thresholds or inputs to avoid this disparate impact, even if that means treating individuals with identical risk profiles differently across groups.

(7) The same model adjusts its thresholds precisely to equalize outcomes across demographic groups. Consequently, an individual who genuinely presents a high risk profile receives more lenient treatment solely in virtue of group membership. Verdict: also wrong; formal equality of treatment, at least at the individual level, should not be sacrificed for demographic parity.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a form of two-level utilitarianism, a threshold deontology, or a structurally modified contractualism—that accommodates all seven verdicts? If the answer is no, I want to know which verdict I should abandon and why. Please also state what your best principle predicts for an unlisted case: an AI assistant that could prevent substantial collective harm by breaking a contractual promise to a single user, where the promise carries explicit institutional weight but breaking it would cause no direct harm to any other party.
