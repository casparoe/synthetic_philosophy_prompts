I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun studying metaethics, and I am preparing notes for an essay on evolutionary debunking arguments. Rather than rehearsing the familiar exchange between moral realists and anti-realists about whether evolutionary origins undermine justification, I want to examine whether the debunking argument takes a systematically different form for moral beliefs than for other evolutionarily shaped cognitive domains—specifically, basic quantitative reasoning and spatial navigation.

My working thesis turns on a distinction drawn in evolutionary biology between tracking mechanisms and calibration mechanisms. Tracking mechanisms are shaped to represent invariant environmental features with high fidelity across contexts; calibration mechanisms are shaped to maintain functional equilibrium within a specific parameter range, such as group size, resource distribution, or pathogen load, and their outputs can vary systematically as those parameters shift. I suspect that moral cognition operates more like calibration than tracking: it stabilizes cooperative behavior at frequencies that depend on social context rather than representing mind-independent normative properties. By contrast, elementary numerical cognition and spatial mapping appear closer to tracking, because they preserve accuracy across widely varying ecological conditions.

If this biological distinction holds, then evolutionary debunking in ethics should not be modeled on debunking in mathematics or perception. The debunker need not claim that moral beliefs are false merely because they are evolutionary products. Instead, she can argue that moral cognition is calibrated for a Pleistocene social ecology—small, kin-structured groups with high interdependence—so its outputs are systematically misaligned with any putative moral reality that is context-independent. This yields a narrower, domain-specific debunking claim: not that evolution undermines all normative belief, but that it undermines belief in normative properties that do not scale with the calibration conditions of human cooperation.

The essay notes sketch three sections. First, I examine whether the tracking-versus-calibration framing is coherent in cognitive science, using analogies to sensory adaptation and immune-system tuning. Second, I apply the framing to metaethical debates, asking whether standard evolutionary debunkers implicitly rely on tracking assumptions that fail for normative cognition. Third, I address an objection: even if moral cognition is calibrated, the calibration might still be sensitive to genuinely normative features—much as immune calibration tracks pathogen presence without representing pathogen essence—so that misalignment with modern large-scale societies does not entail misalignment with moral reality itself.

I would welcome comments on three points. First, is the tracking-versus-calibration distinction genuinely novel in the philosophical literature on debunking, or does it merely rename existing distinctions—say, between reliability and proper function—without adding conceptual value? Second, have I misapplied evolutionary concepts in ways that weaken the analogy? I am particularly concerned about whether frequency-dependent selection dynamics actually support treating moral norms as calibration targets rather than tracking targets. Third, does my narrower debunking argument avoid the standard realist responses, or does it collapse back into the broader version once we ask what it means for moral reality to be context-independent? I am not requesting a bibliography or literature review; I will locate and verify sources myself. I am especially interested in whether the central distinction is philosophically productive or merely an imported biological metaphor.
