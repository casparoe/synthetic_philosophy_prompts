The institution treats probabilities as operational betting quotients, aggregates them through market mechanisms, and uses the resulting numerical outputs to determine reserve allocations and fiscal triggers. Drawing on Keynes's distinction—famously developed in his later economic writings—between measurable risk and non-measurable uncertainty, his concept of the weight of argument, and his broader logical-relational account of probability articulated in his early philosophical work, what position do you think he would adopt toward this institutional design?

Specifically, I would like you to address three interrelated issues. First, whether Keynes would regard market-aggregated probabilities as possessing sufficient epistemic weight to justify large-scale policy commitments, given his skepticism about reducing rational belief to purely numerical degrees. Second, whether his logical view of probability—as an objective relation of partial entailment rather than a subjective mental state—would allow him to treat crowd-aggregated betting behavior as a legitimate source of probabilistic knowledge about economic structures. Third, how he might distinguish the kind of calculable risk suitable for statistical aggregation and insurance from the kind of deep structural ignorance—say, about the long-run macroeconomic effects of artificial general intelligence or climate tipping points—that he believed characterized genuine economic decision-making. Please do not frame this as a historical reconstruction with citations; instead, construct a philosophically grounded prediction of his likely stance, noting explicitly where modern Bayesian or subjectivist assumptions diverge from his framework.
