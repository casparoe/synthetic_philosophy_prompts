Consider the long-running disagreement over norms of assertion: must one assert only what one knows, only what is true, or only what one holds with sufficient belief or justification? For decades the exchange has repeated the same patterns—counterexamples drawn from lottery propositions and expert testimony, defenses of contextual flexibility, and appeals to the distinctive social function of asserting—without either camp conceding ground. I suspect the impasse is structural rather than evidential. Specifically, I wonder whether the dispute conceals an unacknowledged divergence about whether the governing norm is direct, attaching to the mental state expressed in the speech act itself, or indirect, attaching to the deliberative procedure by which a speaker comes to be in a position to speak—much as indirect consequentialism governs action through a prior decision procedure rather than through case-by-case evaluation. On this reading, defenders of the knowledge norm may be insisting that assertion must issue from an inquiry that has reached a specific epistemic closure, while defenders of a belief or reasonableness norm may be treating the act as governed solely by the speaker's present doxastic posture, irrespective of how that posture was formed. Is this diagnosis accurate, or does the stall instead reflect a substantive disagreement about the nature of epistemic commitment, about which conversational contexts supply the relevant data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding speaker responsibility that neither side can further justify? Provide a diagnosis with reasons—identify whether the disagreement is verbal, empirical, formal, or foundational; specify whether the sides are answering different questions under the single heading of 'assertion'; and state honestly what, if anything, could resolve or dissolve it: a conceptual distinction, a formal result in speech-act theory, an empirical study of conversational practice, or a change of framing that reveals the original question to be ill-posed. Do not survey positions; engage them concretely and assess whether genuine progress is possible.
