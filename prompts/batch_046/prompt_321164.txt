Design a 75-minute workshop activity for about 16 philosophy-of-mind participants (no technical background required, but familiar with basic perception and selfhood debates) that makes them inhabit the idea that consciousness is not a direct window onto brain processes or the world, but a predictive user-interface that is "phenomenally transparent" — it renders a seamless, singular experience while hiding both its own construction and the distributed, often contradictory processes beneath it. The specific conceptual target is the "illusion of singular presence": the feeling that there is one unified "user" inside experiencing everything, even though the underlying system is modular and lacks a central observer. Do not lecture first; arrange roles, rules, and staged breakdowns so that participants run into the structural problem themselves — that they can operate the interface expertly, that attempts to "look behind" it only produce more interface, and that they naturally locate the "experience" in a single point (themselves or a screen) rather than in the distributed system — before the facilitator names any of this. Divide the group into Source Modules (6 people, each given one ambiguous, partial data fragment about an object in the room and allowed to communicate only via slow, noisy sticky-note messages through a bandwidth bottleneck), Renderers (2 people who must synthesize the fragments into one seamless "presence display" using predictive-filling rules, with strict prohibition against showing contradictions or raw inputs), and Users (8 people facing only the display, tasked with reporting what they "directly experience" and then with pointing to "where in the system the experiencing is happening"). Run a baseline round, then a "glitch round" in which the Source Modules feed contradictory inputs and the Renderers produce a coherent but false image; reveal the raw notes afterward and ask the Users whether they experienced any contradiction, any filling-in, or any access to the sources. Follow with a "reflexive round" in which Users attempt to turn attention "inward" to report raw data of their own consciousness, but are restricted to the same rendered-report format; the facilitator should then ask whether those inward reports revealed source code or only more interface. Provide explicit rules, materials (sticky notes, a large display board, role cards with hidden contradictory inputs for the glitch round), and a non-monetary payoff scheme (e.g., Users earn collective points for accurate object identification, Renderers earn points for seamless displays, Source Modules earn points for faithful transmission — but all incentives should align with producing a smooth interface rather than revealing underlying conflict). Then give the debrief: map each role and round onto parts of the user-illusion idea (Source Modules as distributed predictive processes, the bottleneck as resource limits, the display as phenomenal consciousness, the missing contradictions as transparency, the Users pointing to a single location as the illusion of singular presence, and the reflexive round as introspection's failure to access architecture). Say explicitly what will predictably fail the first time (Users will try to bypass the Renderer and talk directly to Modules; Renderers will accidentally display contradictions; skeptics will treat it as "just a metaphor" before the analogy is made; the reflexive round often collapses into generic mindfulness reports) and how to handle each without breaking the experience. Be frank about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce — especially the actual phenomenological quality of subjective presence and the evolutionary-functional reason the interface exists — and state that the facilitator must supply those gaps verbally after the experience. Finally, state what argument or empirical finding would change your assessment of whether this design succeeds: for instance, if there were strong evidence that introspection can in principle access pre-rendered neural states without interface mediation, or if the predictive-processing framing of consciousness were shown to be empirically inadequate, then the mapping of the activity onto real consciousness would collapse; also identify where your reasoning is weakest, likely in assuming that a verbal/visual simulation can capture the specific "what-it-is-likeness" that the user-illusion is meant to explain away rather than replicate.
