Okay so I've been thinking about coherence arguments for expected utility maximization - the dutch book stuff, money pumps, all that. There's this view I kind of hold now that these arguments don't actually establish EU maximization as a normative requirement, but only as a kind of local stability condition. Specifically, I think the standard presentation hides a crucial assumption: that the agent's preferance ordering over acts is fixed and exogenously given, rather than being constructed on the fly through the very process of deliberation. If preferences are constructed, then being money-pumped isn't necessarily irrational - it's just evidence that your preference construction mechanism is responsve to framing, which might actually be a feature.

But I want to push this further. I was talking with a friend who studies decision theory and they mentioned something about "sequential coherence gaps" in dynamic choice - basically, that if you allow for endogenous belief revision between choice nodes, you can satisfy all the local coherence constraints without converging on EU at all. You'd just have a tree of locally consistent preferances that never aggregate into a single utility function. So my position is: coherence arguments fail to justify EU maximization not because agents can rationally violate coherence, but because coherence is only defined locally, and there's no bridge principle that forces global EU consistency without begging the question.

What would actually count against this? I want an honest assessment, not just the standard money-pump reply. First, is there a formal result showing that local coherence plus some minimal structural assumption about belief revision necessarily collapses into global EU? That'd be decisive. Second, is there a conceptual argument that preference construction can't actually happen in the way I'm describing - that it's either psychologically unrealistic or conceptually incoherent? That'd be awkward but maybe not fatal. Third, what about empirical data: if real human decision-makers do show global EU consistency in lab settings, does that damage the normative claim, or is it irrelevant? And I want to know what defenders of coherence arguments themselves would concede here - do they admit there's a gap between local coherence and global EU that needs a bridge principle, or do they think I'm just misunderstanding the scope of the argument? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if my view is actually well-insulated or if I'm just avoiding the obvious problem.
