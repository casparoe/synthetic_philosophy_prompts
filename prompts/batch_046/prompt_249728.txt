I've been reading up on Plutarch for a course on moral exemplars and came across this summary in a blog post about biography as ethics. The author says Plutarch's Parallel Lives are basically an early form of hagiography — he picks famous Greeks and Romans, pairs them up to show who's more virtuous, and expects the reader to just imitate the good ones and avoid the bad ones like a child copying saints. The post claims Plutarch thinks moral learning works through direct emotional identification: you read about Cato's stubbornness or Alexander's rage and you either feel inspired to copy them or disgusted into avoiding those vices. There's no room, according to this summary, for critical distance — Plutarch supposedly wants you to merge with the subject, and that's why he never really analyzes institutional context or questions whether virtue in one era applies to another. The blogger concludes that this makes Plutarch useless for modern moral philosophy because he just gives us celebrity worship with ancient branding. Is this fair? I feel like it's definately missing something about how the pairs actually work — like maybe the comparison is meant to make you think harder, not just pick a team — but I don't know enough about hagiography traditions to say where the summary is sloppy versus where it's dead wrong. Can you go through it point by point? I'm especially wondering if Plutarch actually ignores context, and whether the imitation-only reading holds up against the actual texts.
