Been reading a lot of metaphysics stuff lately and I keep running into the same argumentative shape, but I can't tell if it actually has a specific name. Someone points to a table and says it's nothing over and above atoms arranged table-wise. Then they do the same move for atoms — just quarks arranged atom-wise — and push downward level by level. But eventually they hit quantum fields or spacetime points or whatever and suddenly declare "this is the brute base, it's not arranged anything-wise, it's just fundamental." What's weird is that the stopping point always tracks whatever our current physics can't visually or conceptually decompose, not any principled feature of that level itself. If we suddenly discovered quarks had obvious internal parts, I bet people would just say quarks are "just sub-quark-bits arranged quark-wise" and shift the brute-fact badge downward to those bits. So there's this pattern where the "just arranged" analysis gets applied selectively based on whether we can picture the parts, and the leftover level gets called fundamental almost by default.

Is there an established term for that specific bias or failure mode? I know it's in the neghborhood of well-foundedness debates and the old turtles-all-the-way-down worry, and maybe adjacent to how people talk about explanatory regress, but all of those feel like they're about the structure of the dependency chain rather than about this cognitive asymmetry in where we stop applying reduction. I'm not asking for a bibliography — I just want to know if philosophers have carved out this exact thing, and if not, how you'd clearly seperate it from plain mereological reductionism or from the view that fundamentality is basically epistemic. What are the boundary cases or near-misses I should watch out for?
