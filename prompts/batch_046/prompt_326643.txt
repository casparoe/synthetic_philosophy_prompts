I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of risk and regulatory science, and we've spent several weeks on the precautionary principle. Most of our readings treat it either as a decision-theoretic problem—whether ambiguity aversion, maximin-style reasoning, or other non-expected-utility frameworks can justify acting before full evidence is available—or as a practical regulatory tool in climate and biotechnology policy. What strikes me is that neither literature adequately addresses a conceptual distinction I've been trying to formalize: the difference between precautionary delay (withholding an intervention until uncertainty resolves) and precautionary substitution (replacing the proposed intervention with an alternative that carries a different, but not necessarily lower, profile of epistemic and moral risk). This distinction seems to cut across both the formal and applied debates, yet it is rarely made explicit, and I think it restructures how we should think about the principle's scope.

Given this framing, I want to map the genuinely open conceptual work on the precautionary principle—not for regulators alone, but for anyone trying to build a coherent normative theory of rational action under deep uncertainty. I'm trying to decide where to direct my own research efforts over the next year, so I need a defended agenda rather than a survey or a reading list.

Please address the following clusters and prioritize them. First, the formal coherence cluster: can the precautionary principle be reconstructed within standard decision theory, or does it require an irreducibly normative commitment—perhaps about the moral asymmetry between harming and failing to benefit—that formal tools cannot capture on their own? Second, the scope and asymmetry cluster: does the principle apply with the same normative force to localized, technically reversible harms as to systemic, structurally irreversible ones, and how should the delay/substitution distinction affect its scope? Here I am especially interested in whether there is a philosophically principled way to distinguish moral irreversibility from merely technical inconvenience. Third, the evidential dynamics cluster: how should the principle handle what we might call epistemic escalation—situations in which reducing uncertainty about risk A requires engaging in preliminary actions that themselves carry risk B—without collapsing either into paralysis or into an arbitrary bias in favor of the status quo?

Of these, or of others you think are more central, which two or three open questions should be settled first if one's goal is to make the precautionary principle usable in normative ethics rather than just in policy rhetoric? Defend your ranking with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of the others (so that resolving them changes what we should say about the rest), which are tractable with philosophical argument rather than empirical science, and which, if answered one way or the other, would actually change how a practitioner should think about concrete choices. I am not asking for citations, author lists, or bibliographies; I want to know what deserves sustained intellectual effort and what is a derivative problem or a dead end. If there is a further conceptual distinction—similar to the delay/substitution distinction—that you think restructures the entire field, introduce it and show exactly where it belongs in the priority ordering.
