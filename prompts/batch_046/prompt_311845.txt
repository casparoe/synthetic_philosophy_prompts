I sit on the University of Westfield's Student Equity Allocation Board. We have received a restricted three-year donation of $2.4 million specifically to narrow retention gaps in our undergraduate engineering programs. The donor permits spending only on direct student support—no institutional overhead—and requires the board to adopt a single allocation framework for the pilot period. We are weighing three options that map onto different philosophical conceptions of equality: (A) an equal-resource grant, in which every eligible first-generation student receives an identical technology and materials stipend regardless of current need; (B) an equal-welfare target, in which funding is distributed differentially to bring each recipient's predicted second-year GPA to the same benchmark; and (C) a capabilities-based flexible fund, in which students apply for support tailored to remove the specific barrier they identify, with no guarantee of equal dollar value or equal outcome.

The board is divided. Some members hold that only (A) respects formal equality and avoids paternalism; others argue that only (B) actually redresses disadvantage rather than merely symbolizing it; and a smaller group insists that (C) is the only approach that does not impose a single conception of student flourishing across diverse backgrounds. Before our vote next Thursday, I must circulate a decision memo recommending one framework (or a clearly bounded hybrid), explaining which conception of equality—resources, welfare, or capabilities—justifies the choice, engaging the strongest objection from the framework we reject, and proposing one concrete implementation condition, such as an eligibility audit or a sunset review clause, that responds directly to that objection. The memo should be readable by university administrators without philosophical training, yet precise enough that faculty reviewers can identify exactly which normative commitments are carrying the argumentative weight. Please draft it for circulation.
