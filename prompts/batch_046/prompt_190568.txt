I am a first-year doctoral student in intellectual history, and only last week my advisor circled a phrase in my seminar paper—“finally freeing humanity from superstition”—and wrote “Whig?” in the margin. Since then I have become uncomfortably aware of how easily progress narratives infiltrate my own descriptions of the past, even when I believe I have rejected teleology. To understand the seductive mechanics of this discourse from the inside, I would like you to construct a sustained satirical exercise for me.

Please act as a professional satirist with expertise in historiography and the rhetoric of institutional bureaucracy. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm the specific rhetorical moves that make Whig history appealing: the silent elevation of the present as a normative endpoint, the conflation of contingency with inevitability, the anachronistic grading of past societies by present moral standards, and the treatment of counterfactual thinking as a kind of disloyalty to reality. Then, working in a deadpan, fully sustained bureaucratic voice, compose a mock job advertisement—approximately 600 words—for the position of “Senior Curator of Historical Inevitability” at the fictional “Institute for Progressive Retrospective,” a major museum of universal history. The advertisement should read like an authentic human-resources document: list required qualifications, core competencies, daily duties, and institutional benefits, all in formal, precise language that never breaks character or winks at the reader. Yet every specification should systematically exaggerate a real feature of Whig historiography. The ideal candidate must, for example, demonstrate an unwavering ability to interpret any past event—however ambiguous, violent, or contingent—as an indispensable stepping-stone toward contemporary liberal democratic norms; must treat the exploration of counterfactual histories as a disqualifying moral failure rather than a methodological option; must grade all prior civilizations exclusively by their proximity to present-day values; and must possess an unshakable conviction that history possesses an internal logic visible only from the privileged vantage point of the present. Include a sidebar titled “Core Competencies” that quietly encodes these commitments in the language of professional development. The satire should target the practice and discourse of writing the past as inevitable progress toward the present, not any named living scholar. A specialist should recognize that the parody is fair—that it captures the genuine conceptual temptations rather than inventing straw figures—while a general reader should feel the real discomfort of seeing these commitments translated into bureaucratic routine. By the end, the standard objections—who authorizes the endpoint, what happens to human agency and contingency, whether moral judgment is being disguised as neutral description—should be fully present in the subtext, never stated outright. Keep the register formal, the irony deadpan, and the institutional voice consistent throughout.
