I have recently begun an apprenticeship in traditional Japanese joinery, where instruction is almost entirely non-verbal: my master demonstrates a joint-cutting motion, corrects my posture by hand, and expects me to internalize the technique through repetition rather than through explicit rules. This has led me to reconsider Gilbert Ryle’s well-known distinction between knowing how and knowing that. A colleague summarized the current philosophical and scientific consensus for me in the following terms: Ryle’s central argument was that knowing how is ultimately reducible to knowing that, with the difference being only that the relevant propositions are stored in procedural memory and are not consciously accessible; thus the intellectualist legend—the view he is said to have attacked—is not actually a reductionist thesis but merely the claim that skilled action requires explicit conscious rules, which Ryle rightly rejected. According to this summary, modern cognitive neuroscience supports Ryle’s compatibility with reductionism, since studies of motor schema consolidation have shown that procedural and declarative memory are not distinct neural systems but the same substrate operating at different retrieval speeds. From this it follows that when my master refuses to verbalize a technique, he is not transmitting a genuinely non-propositional form of knowledge; rather, he is transmitting propositions that I have not yet consolidated into rapid-access form. The practical upshot is that any skill can, in principle, be fully captured by explicit instruction, and an apprentice’s inability to articulate the rules merely signals an incomplete learning state rather than a different kind of knowing altogether.

I am unsure how much of this to trust. Is Ryle fairly represented as accepting a reduction of knowing-how to knowing-that, or does this conflate his position with the very view he criticized? Is the intellectualist legend accurately characterized here, or is there a subtle misdescription of what Ryle meant by it? Does the neuroscience claim—that procedural and declarative memory share a single substrate differentiated mainly by retrieval speed—actually reflect the consensus, or does it misrepresent the evidence on distinct memory systems? And if these errors are corrected, does the conclusion still hold that non-verbal apprenticeship teaches nothing that explicit, propositional instruction could not, in principle, fully replace? I would like an audit of the summary’s accuracy and an assessment of whether its practical implication for my training survives the corrections.
