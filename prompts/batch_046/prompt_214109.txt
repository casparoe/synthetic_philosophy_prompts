Consider the following track record of judgments rendered by a moral philosopher—referred to here as Dr. K—during recent graduate seminars on moral standing and hypocrisy. In each instance she delivered a clear verdict on whether a speaker retains standing to blame a particular target, appealing either to the sameness of the fault, to its relative severity, or to whether the speaker had acknowledged her own failing.

Case 1: A corporate executive who conceals substantial personal assets in offshore accounts publicly condemns a small-business owner for underreporting cash income. Dr. K judged that the executive lacks standing.

Case 2: A former embezzler who served a prison sentence, made full restitution, and regularly acknowledges his past crime in his teaching condemns a current employee for stealing office supplies. Dr. K judged that he retains standing.

Case 3: A homeowner who drives a large SUV and never mentions her own carbon footprint condemns a neighbor for purchasing a slightly larger pickup truck. Dr. K judged that she lacks standing.

Case 4: A physician who quit smoking two decades ago, openly discusses her former addiction with patients, and now directs a cessation clinic condemns a current patient for continuing to smoke. Dr. K judged that she retains standing.

Now consider Case 5: A freelance accountant who admits to her colleagues that she occasionally rounds her own deductible business expenses downward by negligible amounts—errors she corrects whenever she notices them—publicly condemns a multinational corporation for deploying legal-but-aggressive tax shelters that avoid billions in revenue.

Predict Dr. K's verdict in Case 5. Your answer should articulate at least two distinct principles that are jointly consistent with her judgments in Cases 1 through 4 but that yield opposing predictions in Case 5; for each principle, state explicitly whether Dr. K retains or lacks standing. Then provide your best-guess verdict, explaining which principle you take her to be implicitly running and why the other is less well supported by the track record. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—specifying the speaker's own fault, the target's fault, whether acknowledgment has occurred, and the relative severity—whose outcome would clearly distinguish the two principles.
