The student wrote:

Aristotle divides acquisition into natural and unnatural forms. Natural acquisition, oikonomia, serves the limited needs of the household and aims at the good life. Unnatural acquisition, chrematistikē, seeks unlimited wealth through retail trade and lending at interest. Money was invented to solve the problem of exchanging surplus goods, but in chrematistikē it becomes the end rather than the means.

Aristotle rejects interest (tokos) because money is barren: unlike seeds or animals, it cannot reproduce. Demanding interest asks barren metal to bear offspring, which is contrary to nature. This is essentially the same reasoning Aquinas later used when he argued that money is consumed in use like wine or wheat, making interest an unjust charge for the same thing twice. For Aristotle, however, the sterility argument applies specifically to consumption loans; on productive loans for agriculture or trade, the risk to the lender justifies receiving a share of the profit. This is why he permits partnerships (koinonia) that share gains, while condemning fixed-interest contracts. Therefore his rejection of interest is not absolute but depends on whether the loan funds consumption or production. The fundamental distinction remains that natural exchange serves household self-sufficiency, while chrematistikē treats accumulation as an unlimited end.
