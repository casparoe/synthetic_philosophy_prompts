I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind with a background in cognitive neuroscience, and I've been trying to systematize my own judgments about when phenomenal judgments—claims about what it is like for a subject—are epistemically warranted, particularly on the assumption that consciousness is causally inert within the physical order. The problem of phenomenal judgment under epiphenomenalism keeps returning in different guises, and my intuitive reactions to specific scenarios feel stable individually but resist being brought together.

Here are the verdicts I want tested. First, direct first-person introspection of my present headache and the report that it hurts strikes me as fully justified, and I think this remains true even if the phenomenal state plays no causal role in generating the report—there seems to be a form of acquaintance that secures it independently of causal efficacy. Second, when a neuroscientist attributes consciousness to a patient solely on the basis of behavioral and neural correlates, I regard the attribution as justified but clearly derivative, resting on an inductive bridge rather than on direct acquaintance. Third, in the zombie scenario, a being physically identical to me utters "I am conscious" yet lacks all phenomenal experience; I judge that in that being the report is false and unjustified, whereas in me the structurally identical report is true and justified, and I resist the inference that causal identity undermines my own case, though I recognize this risks special pleading. Fourth, imagine a hypothetical dissociation—call it the access-ownership split—where targeted suppression eliminates all phenomenal experience while leaving the conceptual apparatus and linguistic behavior fully intact, so the subject sincerely reports "there is nothing it is like to be me now." I think this absence-report is accurate and well-justified despite the very absence of what is being reported, which suggests that justification can survive the lack of its object. Fifth, consider the philosopher who argues from the causal closure of physics that consciousness is epiphenomenal and then applies this to conclude that her own belief in consciousness is unreliable; I think the theoretical argument can be sound while its self-application misfires—the belief survives, but I want to say it is anchored by conceptual coherence or evolutionary reliability rather than by being caused by consciousness itself. Sixth, in the case of anosognosia for blindness—where a subject with no visual experience reports seeing normally—I judge the phenomenal report clearly unreliable, yet I do not take this to undermine introspection generally, since the failure is localized and mechanistically explicable.

Is there a coherent principle, or a tightly related family of principles, that captures all six verdicts without distortion? I need an account that explains why direct introspection remains justified despite causal inertness, why zombie reports fail without undermining ours, why the access-ownership split yields accurate absence-reports, and why the self-undermining philosophical argument misfires—while still accommodating derivative third-person attribution and localized failures like anosognosia. If no single principle saves every verdict, I want to know explicitly which one I should abandon, and I want the best principle tested against a case I have not considered: a split-brain patient in whom the left hemisphere reports a unified conscious self and integrated phenomenal field, while the right hemisphere, behaviorally isolated, generates contradictory phenomenal judgments that never reach verbal expression—are both judgments warranted, is only one, or does the framework dissolve the contradiction? Please present your answer entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
