I have been discussing with a friend who studies international law whether it is coherent for modern institutions to assign moral responsibility to entire nations. Our conversation turned to the concrete practice embedded in peace treaties—most notably the war-guilt clause of the Treaty of Versailles (Article 231)—which attributed legal and moral responsibility for the First World War to Germany and its allies as collective bodies rather than to specific rulers or military leaders. The Fischer controversy later reopened the historical question of whether Germany alone had deliberately pursued a general European war, yet the institutional form remained: a treaty mechanism that treats a nation-state as the bearer of guilt, capable of inheriting debts and obligations across generations and regime changes. At the same time, the very legal tradition that permits such clauses also prohibits collective punishment of civilians under the Geneva Conventions, suggesting that group membership alone is not a legitimate basis for individual liability.

What philosophical commitments about moral agency, collective identity, and intergenerational justice does this practice of treaty-based national blame presuppose? Must one regard the state as a continuous moral person with intentions that survive its citizens, or can the clause be reconstructed on purely instrumental grounds—as a fiscal mechanism that needs a solvent debtor, with no genuine moral verdict attached? Is the practice consistent with the prohibition on collective punishment, or do the two norms rest on incompatible theories of what it means to belong to a political community? Furthermore, if one rejects collective moral agency altogether, could the treaty mechanism still be justified by a kind of 'risk-pool' model of state liability, analogous to collective insurance, in which citizens implicitly share burdens regardless of personal fault?

Please answer in British English. I am not looking for bibliographies or citations; I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility that the practice requires, to distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what merely accompanies it, and to say whether several rival philosophical views—such as a strict individualist theory of blame, a corporate-personhood view, or a purely consequentialist account of reparations—could each license the same institutional form. If possible, consider what would have to change in treaty design if the underlying view were replaced by one that treated national 'guilt' as purely metaphorical.
