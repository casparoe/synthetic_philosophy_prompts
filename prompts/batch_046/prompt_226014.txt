Read Aldous Huxley’s Brave New World through the problem of explaining happiness with values—specifically, whether world happiness rankings measure anything that should be maximized by policy, and whether a state that targets the top of such rankings risks destroying the autonomy that makes the metric meaningful. Focus on the World State’s engineered satisfaction: soma, hypnopaedic conditioning, and the caste hierarchy all produce high self-reported contentment, yet they suppress the conceptual resources citizens need to evaluate their own lives. Examine whether key scenes—the soma holiday, Mustapha Mond’s defense of stability, John Savage’s refusal—dramatize an adaptive-preference problem in which the ranking improves precisely as critical capacity declines. Where does the novel illuminate the gap between hedonic satisfaction and eudaimonic flourishing that ranking frameworks often blur? Where does Huxley conflate the two, or rely on an elitist conception of culture that undermines his own critique? Conclude by saying what the novel adds to the debate that an argument about metrics alone cannot: does it show that treating happiness rankings as policy targets is self-defeating in a way that statistical analysis misses?
