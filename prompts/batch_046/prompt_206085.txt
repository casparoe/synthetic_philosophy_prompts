I have been working through Quine's essays on naturalised epistemology for a philosophy of science module, and I keep returning to a tension in his account of belief revision that I cannot resolve on my own. Quine describes the web of belief as having a logical and mathematical core that is highly resistant to revision, with observational sentences at the periphery, and he suggests that ontological commitments are determined by what our best scientific theories quantify over. In our seminar discussion, a colleague argued that this structural picture applies not only to factual belief but to normative conviction as well: moral values, on this reading, occupy a position near the centre of the web—perhaps even closer than empirical generalisations—because we are prepared to revise almost any observational claim rather than abandon our commitment to, say, the wrongness of cruelty.

This led me to formulate what I take to be Quine's implicit metaethics. If ontological relativity tells us that there is no deeper fact of the matter about whether abstract objects exist beyond the quantificational structure of our best theory, then should we not say something parallel about moral properties? That is, if a theory of human behaviour can quantify over dispositions, neural states, and social practices without quantifying over 'moral wrongness' as an independent property, then by Quine's own criterion of ontological commitment, moral values are not part of our ontology; they are merely useful fictions that sit near the centre of our web because of their entrenched utility rather than because they correspond to anything in the world. Yet I also recall Quine remarking, in passing, that ethical and aesthetic judgments share with logic and mathematics a kind of resistance to empirical refutation, which seems to place them in the same category as the logical truths he treats as indispensable but not empirically grounded.

My confusion is this: does Quine's naturalism actually commit him to eliminating moral values from ontology in the way he eliminates propositions or possible worlds, or does the centrality of ethical beliefs in the web grant them a different kind of status—perhaps analogous to the indispensable but non-referential status he assigns to mathematics? Furthermore, if moral disagreement is just a clash between differently structured webs with no external normative court of appeal, how are we to make sense of Quine's own evident liberal and humanitarian commitments? Are these merely nodes in his personal web, or do they claim a privileged position that his own philosophy cannot justify?

Please write your response in British English, and I would be grateful if you could address the specific confusion about whether Quine treats moral values as ontologically committed entities or as centrally entrenched but non-referential conventions.
