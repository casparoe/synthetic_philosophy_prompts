I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the architecture of selfhood, specifically whether Daniel Dennett's model of the self as a center of narrative gravity holds up under closer cognitive scrutiny, or whether the metaphor of a singular gravitational well misrepresents how autobiographical coherence actually works. I need a dialogue between two philosophers who both agree that the self is narratively constructed rather than metaphysically given, but who disagree on whether that construction requires a singular focal point.

Character A should defend a refined Dennettian position: the self functions as a center of narrative gravity not because it is a substance, but because the brain's predictive, mnemonic, and emotional-valence systems converge on a singular indexical origin—this body, this timeline—that acts as an integrative hub. A should argue that without this convergence, autobiographical coherence dissolves into dissociative fragmentation, and that clinical evidence from certain integrative disorders demonstrates the functional necessity of a single focal node rather than merely its convenience.

Character B should accept narrative construction but reject the singular-center model in favor of what B calls a polycentric autobiographical network. B should argue that the self is an emergent property of multiple, partially independent narrative streams—professional, relational, somatic, moral—each maintaining local coherence without a global integrator. B should contend that the appearance of a singular center is an artifact of linguistic first-person pronouns and should warn against what B terms the indexical fallacy: projecting grammatical unity onto cognitive architecture.

The exchange must be genuinely dialectical. A should challenge B on how polycentricity explains the phenomenology of unified decision-making—if multiple streams operate in parallel, why does the subject experience one choice rather than a committee vote? B should challenge A on whether clinical integration failures actually prove a singular center, or only show that inter-stream communication has broken down, leaving the illusion of a missing hub.

Both should address the same hypothetical case: a patient who, after a targeted neural intervention, reports two equally vivid autobiographical timelines—one in which she remained a musician, one in which she became a physician—each carrying full emotional investment and no internal sense that one is the real her. A should interpret this as evidence that the center of narrative gravity can bifurcate, and that therapeutic repair should aim at re-establishing singularity. B should interpret it as proof that singularity was always a post-hoc narrative imposition, and that health consists not in merging the streams but in negotiating their coexistence.

Keep both voices rigorous and respectful; neither should become a strawman. Write in polished American English appropriate for an educated general audience, with philosophical precision but without jargon for its own sake. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific texts in quotation marks. The dialogue should stand alone as a meaningful exchange for a reader who has no access to external sources.
