Over the past month I have been asked to help redraft the governance charter for a small interdisciplinary research collective to which I belong. In reviewing our current bylaws I discovered that Article VII contains a self-amending clause: it permits amendment by a two-thirds vote of the membership, but it also contains an entrenched subsection stating that the amendment procedure itself can only be altered by unanimous consent. The interaction between these provisions led me to investigate the logic of self-reference in rule-governed systems, particularly the game Nomic, in which every move—including moves that change the rules—is governed by the rules currently in force. I have drafted a short philosophical essay exploring whether such circularity is a genuine paradox or merely a procedural tension, and I am writing to ask for critical comments on the argument.

The essay (currently about 1,800 words) makes three main moves. First, I distinguish between internal amendment, where a change is executed using the very procedural mechanisms being modified, and external revision, where the system is suspended and reconstructed from outside. I argue that Nomic collapses this distinction because every move is internal by design, whereas legal constitutions attempt to preserve it through entrenchment clauses. Second, I propose that the apparent paradox—using a majority rule to eliminate the majority-rule requirement—dissolves once we recognize that authority is not a static property of rules but a temporally indexed function: at time t the rule authorizes the vote, and at time t+1 a new rule governs. The circularity is therefore diachronic rather than logical. Third, I inject a more speculative proposal that I call velocity-layered entrenchment: rather than treating all amendment rules as either amendable or immutable, an organization could assign them different temporal velocities—fast-track provisions changeable by simple majority after brief notice, and slow-track provisions requiring supermajority approval across multiple meetings separated by cooling-off periods. I suggest that this does not solve the self-reference problem but relocates it onto a meta-rule that assigns velocities, and that this meta-rule must itself be either unamendable (creating a hard stop) or subject to an infinitely ascending hierarchy of velocity assignments.

I would particularly value comments on the following points. Is the diachronic dissolution of the paradox convincing, or does it merely rename the problem of where the initial authority comes from? Does the velocity-layered model genuinely add analytical clarity, or is it an unnecessary complication that obscures the basic point that any self-amending system needs an external anchor? I am also unsure whether my comparison between Nomic and constitutional law holds up: in Nomic, rule changes are moves within the game, whereas constitutional amendments are typically understood as political acts that transcend the ordinary legal order. Have I conflated two different kinds of self-reference—procedural and sovereign? Finally, I am writing for an audience of philosophers and organizational theorists who may not be familiar with the technical literature on legal self-reference; I have deliberately avoided bibliographic apparatus and citation lists, but I worry that in doing so I may have made the essay seem under-informed. Are there conceptual gaps—particularly around the distinction between rule-recognition and rule-change—that a reader without access to the primary literature would still notice?

Please treat this as a request for substantive philosophical criticism rather than stylistic editing, though observations on structure or clarity are welcome if they bear on the argument’s rigor.
