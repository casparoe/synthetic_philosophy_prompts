I've noticed a pattern in discussions of Wittgenstein's private-language considerations that I can't quite classify. Often the argument is presented as showing that a purely private language is impossible because, lacking external correctors, the speaker could never distinguish correct use from mere belief in correctness. When someone replies that private mnemonic devices or diaries could serve that corrective role, the proponent frequently doesn't defend the original claim but pivots: the deeper problem, they say, isn't error-correction but the absence of shared practice required for signs to have meaning at all. If that is then answered—for instance, by analogizing solitary rule-invention—the focus shifts once more, perhaps to learnability or to the normative structure of rule-following. The result is not a single thesis with an indefensible extension, but a cluster of interlocking conditions, any one of which can be foregrounded when another is addressed. Is there an established concept for this argumentative structure, and how does it differ precisely from a motte-and-bailey or from simple equivocation? More broadly, please help me understand whether Wittgenstein's own text supports one of these layers as primary, or whether the multi-layered reading is itself a contested interpretation—and in your answer, clearly separate what is settled in the scholarship from what remains genuinely disputed, without presenting any contested reading as consensus.
