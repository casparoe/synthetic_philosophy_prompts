I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I am drafting a proposal for a six-week interdisciplinary workshop that pairs philosophers of mind with researchers in distributed systems. The question concerns the scale argument in insect sentience ethics: the claim that, because insect populations are astronomically large, even a near-zero per-individual probability of consciousness yields an enormous expected moral burden.

Most existing discussions treat the argument as a straightforward exercise in expected-value aggregation, or as an epistemic-risk problem about how to act under uncertainty. I want to introduce a structural framing that is standard in computing but largely absent from this debate. In large-scale architectures, aggregate behavior is not always the linear sum of node-level properties; some capacities are threshold-governed and emerge only when individual units cross a specific complexity boundary—scaling up—rather than through mere replication—scaling out. If sentience is closer to a threshold-governed property than to a scalar one, then multiplying a negligible individual probability by billions of insects may be conceptually analogous to arguing that a warehouse of non-sentient transistors becomes conscious through sheer number.

The proposal should state the precise question: Can the scale argument be reconstructed so that it explicitly assumes linear aggregation, and does that assumption fail if consciousness requires crossing an architectural threshold? If the argument survives, what does that imply about whether moral consideration should track expected value or threshold satisfaction? The method should be conceptual rather than empirical—no neurobiological measurements—using analogical reasoning and information-theoretic modeling of neural integration to determine whether insect nervous systems function as scaling-out nodes or as potential scaling-up substrates. I want an explanation of why this philosophical framing makes progress that empirical neuroscience alone cannot, specifically for people deciding on intervention priorities.

The deliverable should be a workshop paper of approximately six thousand words that either incorporates or explicitly rejects the threshold distinction, yielding a sharper decision rule for when vast population size should override low individual probabilities. What would count as failure is immediate collapse of the analogy—if sentience is unambiguously scalar, or if the threshold model requires adjudicating a specific theory of consciousness that lies outside the workshop's scope—leaving only the standard aggregation debate unchanged. I want an honest scope assessment: whether six weeks is sufficient to move from analogy to rigorous conceptual framework without resolving the underlying neuroscience.

I am not requesting a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by name; I will supply references myself. The proposal should avoid standard survey structures and instead focus on the conceptual architecture: the open ambiguity in current positions, the reason for adopting a distributed-systems lens rather than the obvious alternatives, the range of possible conclusions, and a realistic evaluation of what can be achieved in the time available.
