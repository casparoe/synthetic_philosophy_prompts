Write the commencement address for a small liberal-arts college’s graduation ceremony. The speaker is a philosopher invited to address about four hundred graduates, parents, and faculty, including philosophy professors who will recognize the conceptual moves. The occasion requires honoring achievement without offering false consolation.

The speech must do real structural work with the tension between absurdist thought—the recognition that no cosmic or predetermined purpose guarantees human lives matter—and contemporary philosophical accounts of meaning in life, which typically ground meaning in the realization of valuable projects, relationships, or capacities. To navigate this, introduce and develop a distinction not standard in the literature: treat achievement-meaning as the view that meaning accumulates like capital through successful projects, and maintenance-meaning as the view that meaning is sustained through ongoing practices of attention, repair, and coherence in conditions that offer no guarantee of success. Use this distinction to shape what the speaker can and cannot say: they must not tell parents that education delivers guaranteed purpose, and they must not tell graduates that purpose is an illusion. Instead, argue that the college’s emphasis on service and critical reflection is best understood as training in maintenance rather than achievement—a skill that matters precisely because there is no cosmic backstop. The address should run about five minutes, remain accessible to non-philosophers without diluting the argument, and must not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts by page or edition.
