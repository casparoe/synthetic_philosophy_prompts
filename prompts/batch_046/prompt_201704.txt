I am preparing an advanced graduate seminar on existentialism and the philosophy of technology, and I have become interested in whether Sartre’s analysis of radical freedom and bad faith can be meaningfully extended to our contemporary relation to predictive algorithms without collapsing into anachronism. To explore this, I want you to construct a dialogue around a specific hypothetical construct—let us call it the “archival self”—in which an individual’s accumulated digital traces are continuously reconstructed by recommendation and profiling systems, producing a stabilized data-portrait that the user experiences as an external essence fixing her future possibilities. The dialogue should feature two equally competent interlocutors in a formal, polished register appropriate to philosophical debate.

The first interlocutor should defend a rigorous Sartrean position: radical freedom is unconditional, and treating one’s algorithmic profile as determinative of future choice is precisely bad faith—a flight from the anguish of having to choose ex nihilo in each present moment, even when the “prediction” is itself the result of prior engagement that was freely undertaken. The second interlocutor should resist not by denying freedom outright, but by arguing that Sartre’s phenomenology of the for-itself systematically underestimates the constitutive weight of sedimented technical and social structures; for this speaker, the archival self is not merely a self-deceptive interpretation but a genuinely constraining condition, and labeling it “bad faith” risks moralizing a structural dependency that precedes and conditions the very field in which choice operates. Crucially, this must be real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the other’s actual claims—for example, if the first argues that even algorithmic suggestions are ultimately chosen through the user’s prior free commitments, the second should address whether those prior commitments were already shaped by the system’s architecture rather than by an unencumbered will.

Please present the exchange in fifteen to twenty substantial turns, maintaining conceptual precision and avoiding strawman representations of either view. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific secondary scholarship; the argument should stand on its own conceptual resources. The dialogue should conclude without resolving the tension, leaving the reader with a clear sense that the applicability of Sartre’s radical freedom to algorithmically mediated existence remains genuinely open.
