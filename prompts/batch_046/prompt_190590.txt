I'm working through a long-standing impasse in the philosophy of interpretation and system design concerning where meaning is actually located. The framework distinguishes three candidates: framing conditions that make interpretation possible (frame messages), explicit publicly accessible content (outer messages), and the implicit internal architecture that generates or constrains that content (inner messages)—the tripartite schema familiar from Gödel, Escher, Bach. This distinction has been applied across literary theory, cognitive science, and artificial system interpretability, yet in every domain the debate stalls identically.

Frame-primacy advocates argue that without the right contextual scaffolding—historical conventions, design intentions, environmental coupling—the same internal structure or explicit output could mean almost anything; framing is therefore not optional decoration but the condition of meaningfulness itself. Outer-message advocates reply that framing is just more explicit content operating at a higher level of abstraction, and that meaning must bottom out in observable, shareable signs or risk collapsing into private mentalism. Inner-message advocates insist that both framing and explicit output are symptoms of a deeper organizational logic—whether causal brain structure, the regulatory architecture of a synthetic gene circuit, or the syntactic rules of a formal system—and that this internal logic fixes interpretation even when frames shift and outputs vary.

In my own working group and in the literature, everyone accepts that all three levels interact. The disagreement is about which carries explanatory weight and whether the others reduce to it. No new empirical finding—whether in neuroscience, synthetic biology, or formal language theory—seems capable of resolving the dispute.
