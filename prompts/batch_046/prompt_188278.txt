I am constructing a primary-source exercise for an advanced seminar on moral aggregation, and I need a rigorous adversarial-collaboration document that my students can analyze without treating it as a settled adjudication. Treat the exercise as a collaboration between two camps that have argued past each other on the torture-versus-dust-specks problem.

Camp A holds the Discontinuity Thesis: there is a qualitative, non-compensable moral gap between a single severe harm—such as the sustained infliction of intense suffering on one person through torture—and any finite aggregation of imperceptible harms, such as a momentary dust speck in an eye, regardless of cardinality.

Camp B holds the Smooth-Gradient Thesis: harm is quantitatively continuous, and if the cardinality of imperceptible harms is sufficiently large, their aggregate moral weight exceeds that of the single severe harm.

Produce the joint statement they ought to have signed. Structure it exactly as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims (covering the physical and phenomenological facts of both scenarios, the logical form of each camp’s denial, the conditions under which both agree aggregation succeeds or fails, and an explicit acknowledgment that neither camp denies the arithmetic of large numbers); second, a disagreements table with three columns—(i) the precise formulation of disagreement worded so that Camp A accepts its own formulation and Camp B accepts its own, (ii) the crux sub-question, and (iii) the concrete case or argument both camps agree bears on that crux, with each camp’s prediction recorded in advance.

You must commit to definite formulations throughout. Do not present the cruxes as an open menu: identify exactly three cruxes in neutral language, and for each state the specific prediction of Camp A and Camp B. One of these three cruxes must address the problem I am injecting here—the modal-opacity aggregation scenario. In this scenario, an agent chooses between (a) torturing one identifiable victim for a fixed duration, or (b) activating a mechanism that induces exactly one second of imperceptible ocular irritation in each member of a population of approximately 10^18 people, where the agent knows the approximate magnitude but cannot verify the exact number, no victim ever becomes aware of the event, and the mechanism’s effects are distributed across nearby possible worlds with slight variance in the affected population. Both camps agree on the physical, psychological, and epistemic description of this mechanism. Your document must record their disagreement about whether moral evaluation depends on the actual cardinality, on the agent’s epistemic access to that cardinality, or on the modal robustness of the harm across nearby worlds.

In addition, include a brief section—written in your own voice, not attributed to either camp—identifying which disagreements are genuinely substantive and which are merely verbal, with a committed verdict (not a list of options) on at least one case. State clearly which framing you take to be the most precise statement of the residual disagreement and why. Finally, append a short reflexive note indicating where your own drafting is least likely to be accepted as neutral by one of the camps, specifying the formulation and your reason for that suspicion.

Write in formal academic prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not place any real text in quotation marks; if you reference a specific argument, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Take a definite position on the structure of the disagreement: do not conclude that the dispute merely depends on one’s background theory, but instead state what you take the core logical tension to be and defend that reading with specific reference to the three cruxes you have identified.
