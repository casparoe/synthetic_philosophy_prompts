You are delivering the closing address at the dissolution ceremony for the Community Archive of Unrecorded Voices, a repository you have directed for two decades that holds oral testimonies from a regional conflict that concluded thirty years ago. The governing board—acting on a petition led by descendants of the original contributors—has voted to dissolve the archive permanently rather than digitize or transfer it, on the grounds that continued preservation imposes an unchosen mnemonic burden on younger generations who wish to live without custodial obligation. Your audience is deliberately mixed: survivors and their families sit beside professional historians, local officials, and philosophers of memory who will recognize allusions to Margalit's ethics of shared remembrance and to Rieff's argument that collective memory can become a form of moral tyranny; the family members will not. Write a formal speech of about six to seven minutes in which this philosophical tension does structural work—determining, for example, why you must honor the contributors without framing the archive as an eternal debt owed to the future, and why you must defend dissolution without implying that the past was insignificant.

To keep the address from collapsing into either pure elegy or pure justification, develop and apply a specific conceptual frame you call mnemonic subsidiarity: the claim that authority over whether a particular body of memory is kept alive should reside with the smallest social unit that actually bears its emotional and moral cost, rather than with the state, with universal preservation mandates, or with the archivist's professional commitment to permanence. The speech should navigate between these poles for both halves of the room, never asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and should conclude with a brief, clearly separated reflective note stating exactly what argument or evidence would reverse your defense of dissolution—whether proof that the descendants' consent was structurally coerced, a demonstration that the archive serves an unrecognized justice function, or another consideration—and identifying the precise point in your reasoning most vulnerable to failure.
