Write a dialogue between Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen, two philosophers of medicine of equal competence, conducted during a closed seminar on clinical epistemology. Vasquez advances a Jamesian view of the ethics of belief: she holds that when evidence underdetermines a practical commitment, and the choice is unavoidable, high-stakes, and genuinely open, belief may be adopted if it shapes action for the better. Chen advances a Cliffordian view: he holds that belief upon insufficient evidence is always wrong, particularly in institutional contexts where credibility and patient autonomy are vulnerable. Their specific dispute concerns whether a physician treating chronic neuropathic pain is ethically permitted to work under the belief that a structured tactile-mapping ritual—performed with explicit narrative framing—exerts direct causal efficacy beyond placebo mechanisms, despite inconclusive meta-analyses and tension with dominant neurobiological models. Vasquez must defend the claim that such belief improves therapeutic presence and patient outcomes, and she must respond directly to Chen's charge that institutional credibility is corrupted when clinicians treat ambiguous hypotheses as working truths. Chen must defend the duty of epistemic restraint and respond directly to Vasquez's claim that clinical agnosticism is practically impossible, since every therapeutic posture embodies some belief about efficacy. Each speaker should concede exactly one substantive point to the other, neither should talk past the other, and the exchange should remain philosophically precise. Avoid bibliographic references, citations to specific texts, or requests for further reading; conclude without a synthetic resolution.
