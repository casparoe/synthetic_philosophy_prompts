A recent essay on twentieth-century political thought summarizes Simone Weil's 1943 pamphlet On the Abolition of All Political Parties as follows:

"Simone Weil argues that political parties are not corruptible institutions but totalitarian by nature: they necessarily manufacture collective passion, suppress individual judgment, and pursue unlimited growth and power. Because members must always choose party loyalty over objective truth, representative democracy becomes impossible—representatives serve the party rather than the common good. Her proposed remedy is not reform but complete abolition, replaced by temporary, task-specific associations that dissolve once their purpose is fulfilled. She extends this analysis beyond formal parties to all modern collective movements that demand ideological loyalty, identifying them as primary agents of uprooting that tear individuals away from local attachments and substitute abstract identity. Most importantly, she grounds the entire critique in her religious metaphysics: party loyalty is a form of gravity, the downward pull that crushes the soul's capacity for detached attention, which she regards as the necessary condition for both justice and mystical union. Consequently, her opposition to parties is ultimately theological rather than political—democratic citizens must reject parties not to improve governance but to free the soul for grace."

How fair is this summary? Please go through it sentence by sentence and indicate, for each claim, whether a careful reader of Weil's political and religious writings would accept it, qualify it, or reject it as a misrepresentation. I am particularly interested in whether the summary fairly distinguishes her specifically political argument from her mystical theology, or whether it collapses the two; whether it correctly describes her proposed alternative to parties; and whether attributing to her a general theory of uprooting caused by collective movements is a legitimate extension of her essay or a conflation with her separate work on rootedness. Also, does the summary's implication that her critique anticipates modern social-psychological concerns about collective epistemic corruption hold up, or is that an anachronism? Finally, assess whether the summary's conclusion—that her argument is ultimately about salvation rather than politics—follows from the premises it gets right.
