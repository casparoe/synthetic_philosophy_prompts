I am writing a novel in which two characters — a statistical physicist and a metaphysician — debate the arrow of time during a long evening in Cambridge. For a key chapter, I need a rigorous classification instrument that sorts positions on the thermodynamic arrow according to what one actually believes about concrete scenarios, not according to which label one prefers. Please construct a decision tree (or, if you prefer, a sorting quiz with a scoring key) that takes a respondent from an initial commitment about entropy and temporal direction through a sequence of diagnostic branches, ending at named or clearly described positions.

The tree should begin with the question of whether one takes the observed entropy gradient to be an objective feature of the world or a feature projected onto it by observers and their records. From there, branch on whether one accepts the Past Hypothesis — that is, whether a specially low-entropy initial state of the universe is the correct explanation of the gradient, or whether one instead locates the asymmetry in the branching structure of decoherent histories without privileging any boundary condition. A further branch should separate those who think the asymmetry is purely conventional (a matter of coordinate choice or definitional stipulation) from those who treat it as physically real but grounded in something other than entropy — for instance, in an asymmetry of causal influence or in what I shall call the 'mnemonic constraint': the claim that records can only be formed in the direction of increasing entropy not because entropy defines time, but because record-formation is itself a thermodynamically irreversible process that fixes an indexical 'before' and 'after' for any embedded agent. Include this mnemonic-constraint view as a distinct leaf, even though it is rarely named in standard surveys; note its most awkward commitment, which is that two embedded observers with opposite mnemonic orientations could disagree about the direction of time without either being physically mistaken.

Each node should be phrased as a concrete question a respondent could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to — for example, questions about whether an isolated system observed to spontaneously decrease in entropy would be interpreted as a reversal of time or as a statistical fluctuation, or about whether two observers in relative motion could coherently assign opposite temporal directions to the same physical process. The leaves should include: (i) the cosmological boundary-condition realist; (ii) the decoherence-branching theorist; (iii) the conventionalist; (iv) the mnemonic-constraint projector; and (v) an unnamed combination in which one denies both the Past Hypothesis and any observer-dependence — flag this leaf and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where your tree cannot genuinely separate two positions (for instance, if the boundary-condition realist and the decoherence theorist converge on all observable predictions), flag that pair explicitly rather than inventing a false distinction. The instrument should be usable by a reader who has no access to external references, so do not ask for bibliographies, cite specific papers by author and date, or refer to theories by obscure proper names that a general model could not be expected to have memorised. Keep the tree logical rather than historical: build it from the space of answers first, then locate the positions within that space.
