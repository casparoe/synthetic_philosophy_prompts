I've been sitting in on this decision-theory reading group and we keep circling back to Good's result—basically that if you can grab free information before you choose, you should always grab it, since you can just ignore what you learned and do the same thing you'd have done blind, so the expected value of information can't be negative. My friend tried to turn this into what he jokingly calls an "epistemic closure principle," but now the argument feels like it eats itself. Here are the steps, tell me where the wheels come off: (1) Good's theorem says that for any decision D, acquiring a free signal S that bears on D weakly dominates not acquiring S, because conditioning on S lets you do no worse. (2) In any realistic situation there's always some freely available S—some witness, sensor, or background fact you haven't checked yet—so there's always a weakly dominant move available before you commit to D. (3) But deciding whether to check for S is itself a decision D*, and by (2) there's always a free signal S* about whether checking S is worth the delay. (4) So you can't rationally commit to D without first resolving D*, and can't resolve D* without resolving D**, giving an infinite regress of pre-decisional gatekeeping. (5) If the regress never terminates, you never actually make D, yet people clearly do make choices. (6) So either Good's theorem is false, or "free" in (1) smuggles in a hidden cost that kills the regress in (3). I really want to keep (1) intact—giving it up feels like throwing out the whole rationale for rational inquiry—but I'm also suspicious I'm equivocating between "informationally free" and "computationally free" somewhere in the middle. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Also, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and point out the specific claim you're least sure about.
