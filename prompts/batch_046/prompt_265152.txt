Two colleagues in my philosophy reading group got into a prolonged disagreement over a variant of Newcomb's problem that one of them had constructed. In this version, the predictor is only eighty percent accurate, but after filling the boxes it publishes the exact deterministic algorithm it used to generate its forecast about you, so that before you choose you can verify precisely what it predicted. The first colleague, Elena, argued that this transparency strengthens the one-boxing case: once you run the algorithm on your own mental profile and confirm it forecast a one-box choice, you have direct statistical evidence that the opaque box contains the million, so taking only one box is the rational evidential update. The second colleague, Marcus, replied that transparency destroys the correlation rather than strengthening it, because knowing the algorithm allows you to deliberately implement a decision procedure that contradicts its forecast—if it predicted one-boxing, you can now two-box specifically to break the match, and since the box contents are already fixed, there is no causal risk in doing so. Elena countered that this "hacking" move is self-undermining: if the algorithm correctly models your reasoning, then the very fact that it predicted one-boxing means it already anticipated any temptation to hack, so successfully deviating proves the algorithm was wrong about you, which undermines the evidential basis for thinking the million is present. Marcus answered that this assumes an implausible level of self-modeling completeness—real agents have bounded rationality and can always insert an arbitrary stochastic choice or a commitment device after seeing the prediction—so the predictor's forecast is not a fixed point, and the evidential link collapses once you treat your future choice as genuinely open. Elena then said that even with bounded rationality, the base rate of the predictor being correct about people like you still gives you a reason to align with its forecast, otherwise you are treating yourself as an exception without justification. Marcus ended by saying that treating yourself as an exception is exactly what causal reasoning demands: you care about what your hand does now, not about what a past algorithm guessed, and the eighty percent accuracy is just a prior that gets swamped by your direct knowledge of your present freedom to choose. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Elena's initial transparency argument, Marcus's hacking reply, Elena's self-undermining counter, Marcus's bounded-rationality response, and Elena's base-rate point, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing—perhaps about whether the predictor forecasts a decision type or a particular token choice, and whether your present self should treat that forecast as being about you specifically—and present your verdict in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though writing a letter or email.
