Design a take-home exam prompt for an MA-level philosophy of mind seminar (72 hours, 2,500-word limit) on whether self-deception is genuinely possible. The question must be unanswerable by reciting Davidson’s or Mele’s standard taxonomies. Instead, present students with a concrete case and force them to adjudicate between two conceptual models defined in the prompt itself: (1) a partitioned-belief account, on which the agent simultaneously holds contradictory beliefs in functionally isolated cognitive subsystems, and (2) a motivated-uncertainty account, on which the agent holds a single, dynamically stabilized credence that is kept indeterminate by affective feedback loops rather than by explicit denial. Use the following scenario: a senior climate scientist who accepts IPCC confidence intervals in her declarative reports, yet systematically avoids running sensitivity analyses that could undermine her preferred policy recommendations, and who sincerely asserts, "I have no reason to doubt the robustness of the model," even as her research design systematically excludes disconfirming conditions. The exam question should ask students to identify exactly which mental state she occupies, whether intentional deception of oneself is necessary for her condition, and whether the concept of self-deception collapses into ordinary weakness of will or motivated ignorance if the partitioned-belief model fails. Provide the exam question, a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme structured around four must-hit analytical moves, each worth 25 marks (total 100), with a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode that should cost marks for each.
