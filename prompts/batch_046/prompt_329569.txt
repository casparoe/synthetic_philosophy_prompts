I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for third-year politics students who have read some political theory but no philosophy of social science. The session must address whether “national character” is a coherent explanatory notion and, separately, whether statistical patterns about a people can ever justify moral generalizations about its members. Plan the explanatory arc: the conceptual distinction that has to be in place first—between aggregated behavioral distributions and essentialist cultural dispositions, with explicit attention to the ecological fallacy in cross-national comparison—the concrete paired comparative case that should carry the argument and what features it must possess and lack, the misconceptions the students will predictably raise in sequence (likely that rejecting essentialism requires denying all cultural patterns, then that aggregate correlation licenses moral judgment, then that individual counterexamples invalidate the statistical claim) and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted, a closing check that demonstrates they have separated descriptive coherence from normative permission rather than conflating the two, and what technical apparatus—specific ethnographic controversies or dense historiographic citation—to explicitly exclude and name as beyond this session’s scope.
