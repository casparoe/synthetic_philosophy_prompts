I facilitate a two-hour workshop on rational choice and intertemporal decision-making for about twelve graduate students with mixed philosophy and economics backgrounds. After auditing my own recurring commitments over the past year—cycling among three productivity platforms in a loop where each cancellation triggered a discount that made re-subscribing locally justified, yet left me with less net value and more administrative burden—I want an activity that lets participants feel the structural force of diachronic preference reversal from the inside rather than analyzing it from a distance. Design a simulation with real but modest stakes, such as small course-credit adjustments or a shared snack budget, structured in rounds of silent individual allocation, open bilateral trading, and collective rule-setting. Create three resource categories—immediate consumption, deferred consumption, and a social-commitment bond—whose exchange rates shift each round according to a majority vote among the participants. Arrange the payoff structure and voting rules so that pairwise preferences over the categories cycle, allowing groups of three or four to trade their way into a state where every member holds less of each resource than they started with after three or four rounds, despite each individual trade being locally rational. The commitment bond should not function as a simple lockbox: if honored it yields a linear social-credit bonus visible to the group, but if broken it imposes a nonlinear penalty that doubles the loss and extends it to the trading partner, so that breaking the bond can be individually rational in a given round while collectively destructive. Participants should construct the sequence themselves—beginning with private allocations, moving to visible exchange-rate trading, then revealing aggregate holdings—so that they discover the loop before it is named. Only in the final ten minutes should the facilitator introduce the standard terminology. Include a debrief that maps each round onto a component of diachronic rationality—local optimality, transitivity failure, the cost of precommitment, and the divergence between individual and collective rationality—while being explicit about which normative feature of consistency-over-time arguments the game mechanics cannot reproduce and must be supplied through discussion. Predict what will go wrong in practice, particularly the tendency for one subgroup to vote strategically on exchange rates in an attempt to escape the cycle, which should be permitted but then shown to regenerate the instability at a meta-level. Specify the precise group size, round timing, and stake structure, and state clearly what participants should come away seeing about the difference between being rational at each moment and being rational across time. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific historical trials; the design should stand entirely on its own rules and payoffs. Additionally, inject one non-standard element into the mechanics—perhaps a hidden information condition about one participant’s future-round endowment—to test whether the group recognizes that the money-pump structure can persist even when all preferences are transparent.
