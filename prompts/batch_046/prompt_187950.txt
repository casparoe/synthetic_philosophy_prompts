Consider an institutional design, increasingly adopted by research-funding bodies, in which applicants must submit—alongside their scientific proposal—a "sunk-cost ledger" documenting unreimbursed personal or institutional expenditures already directed at the proposed project, and in which review panels are explicitly instructed to treat the magnitude of this prior investment as an independent criterion of credibility, weighted equally with theoretical and methodological merit. Proposals that present stronger arguments but lack such costly preliminary commitment are systematically down-ranked, and applicants who request full funding without demonstrating prior "skin in the game" are barred from the highest funding tier regardless of peer-review scores. What philosophical commitments about rational belief, credible commitment, and the epistemology of prediction does this scoring architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat costly signals as rationally weightier than cheap talk in assessing future performance; does it hold that belief in a project's success is legitimately strengthened by the agent's willingness to bear irreversible losses; or does it assume that the handicap itself is partly constitutive of research integrity? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether it is compatible with a purely evidentialist or probabilistic view of funding allocation, and describe what a grant system built solely on direct assessment of argumentative and methodological quality—without any sunk-cost criterion—would have to change about its legitimacy conditions, risk distribution, and the social meaning of receiving an award. In your answer, relate the implicit logic to the handicap principle in costly signaling, but do not treat that principle as automatically determinative of the practice's justification.
