A friend has walked me into an argument about Jain anekāntavāda and I cannot find the exit. (1) Anekāntavāda holds that every object—jīva, ajīva, and their modes—is anekānta, possessing infinitely many contradictory attributes, so any single naya reveals only one partial aspect while suppressing others. (2) Syādvāda therefore requires that every philosophical predication carry the syāt prefix ("in some respect"), producing the saptabhaṅgī scheme (syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt asti-nāsti, syāt avaktavya, and their combinations). (3) Because syādvāda is presented as a universal logical form governing all cognition, it applies reflexively: the meta-thesis "all truths are many-sided" must itself be prefixed with syāt, and is therefore true only in some respect. (4) But if that meta-thesis is true only in some respect, then there exists at least one legitimate standpoint—call it the ekāntika-naya—under which the prefix is denied and the thesis is false; within that naya, some propositions (for instance, that reality has a single, unified nature) are unconditionally true. (5) Jain epistemology grants every naya equal legitimacy; none, including the anekāntika, may be privileged without begging the question. (6) Hence the ekāntika denial of anekāntavāda is as epistemically valid as anekāntavāda itself. (7) The result is that anekāntavāda licenses its own negation with equal authority, collapsing into a framework in which both "everything is many-sided" and "everything is one-sided" are equally warranted. (8) A philosophical framework that entails its own contradictory with equal force proves too much: it cannot adjudicate disputes, guide inquiry, or preserve the normative reasoning—such as the derivation of ahimsā from the infinite complexity of living beings—that Jains traditionally take it to support.

My friend says this shows anekāntavāda is self-refuting and that we must abandon the universal application of syādvāda. I want to preserve both the ontological claim that reality is genuinely complex and the idea that syādvāda functions as a disciplined dialectical tool, but I feel something slipped between (4) and (6)—perhaps an equivocation between "legitimate as a partial viewpoint" and "legitimate as a total denial of partiality," or a hidden modal slide from "there is a naya under which X is false" to "that naya is actually correct." Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does saving anekāntavāda require treating the ekāntika-naya as not a genuine naya at all, or does it require restricting the domain over which syāt operates?
