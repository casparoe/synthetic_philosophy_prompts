I am writing a novel about an actuary who attempts to live by a rigorous form of consequentialism, and for the plot I have invented a decision framework she calls “shadow-weighting”: she treats every unknown long-range consequence as carrying a negative utility proportional to the square of the variance in her prior, so that structural ignorance becomes just another priced parameter in her Bayesian calculus. She believes this resolves what philosophers call consequentialist cluelessness, because it formalizes ignorance as a quantifiable risk rather than a paralyzing void. In a key scene she debates a deontologist who claims that once variance is high enough, shadow-weighting collapses into mere rule-following. I want to write this scene so that it is philosophically serious, but I am unsure whether her framework actually addresses the cluelessness objection or whether it rests on a false presupposition about the nature of the problem. Specifically, is the confusion here that she treats consequentialist cluelessness as an epistemic deficit that can be internalized into a utility function—like risk aversion—when in fact it describes a structural inability to trace which actions produce which distant outcomes, such that pricing ignorance does not restore action-guidance but merely disguises a paralysis? If her view is indeed confused in that way, how should the deontologist’s reply be sharpened so that it targets the mistake precisely rather than sounding like generic anti-consequentialism?
