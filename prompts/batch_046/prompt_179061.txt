I'm working on a framework for mechanistic anomaly detection, specifically on the problem of mechanism identity: under what conditions does an observed anomaly indicate that the same underlying mechanism is malfunctioning, rather than that an altogether different mechanism has become active or replaced it? I've formed several intuitive verdicts across domains, but I'm struggling to find the principle that unifies them, and I suspect at least one verdict may have to be sacrificed.

Consider these cases. First, in a vision model with a well-characterized convolutional filter that normally detects oriented edges, the filter suddenly activates maximally on high-frequency adversarial noise patterns; its weights and architecture are untouched, but the input distribution has shifted. I judge that this is still the same mechanism—the edge-detection filter—operating anomalously, not a different mechanism. Second, in a cellular metabolic pathway, glycolysis suddenly accelerates twofold; the glycolytic enzymes are present in normal concentrations and unmodified, but an allosteric regulator previously silent in this tissue has been activated by a novel metabolite. Here I judge that a different mechanism—the regulatory cascade—has become causally dominant, so the anomaly is not a malfunction of glycolysis itself. Third, a prosthetic heart valve that produces a characteristic acoustic signature begins allowing backflow and emits a slightly altered sound; inspection reveals that the leaflet is intact but its calcification pattern has changed its elasticity. I take this to be the same valvular mechanism, materially degraded but not replaced. Fourth, two independently trained architectures—a transformer and a state-space model—both suffer abrupt collapse in multi-step arithmetic reasoning when exposed to an identical, specifically crafted token sequence. Despite their structural differences, I feel inclined to say they share the same mechanism of failure, as if a common reasoning process has been disrupted in both. Fifth, however, in a manufacturing line, widgets begin showing a distinctive scratch pattern identical to one previously caused by a misaligned cutting tool; the tool is still in its original position, but the raw material has become harder, causing the same scratch via a different physical interaction. Here I judge that this is not the same mechanism—the misaligned tool mechanism is absent, and a new material-stress mechanism is producing the superficial similarity.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving counterfactual stability, structural continuity, or functional role preservation—that captures all five verdicts? Or do I need to overturn one? In particular, can any account preserve both my judgment that the metabolic regulator introduces a different mechanism and my judgment that the two architectures share a failure mechanism, without collapsing into an ad hoc distinction? I also want to know what your best principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: suppose a cognitive architecture's working-memory module, defined by a specific recurrent connectivity pattern, begins exhibiting the same behavioral deficits after a pharmacological agent selectively suppresses a distinct, parallel pathway that normally inhibits it. Would that be the same mechanism failing, or a different inhibitory mechanism now revealing the underlying one?
