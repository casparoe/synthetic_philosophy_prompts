Hey, I'm trying to figure out if there are really two different flavors of free will worth wanting, or if they're just the same idea in different costumes. On one side there's Frankfurt's hierarchical model — you want your second-order desires to line up with your first-order ones, so you're not just being pushed around by random urges. That's the standard compatibilist picture and it feels pretty internal, like the worth comes from you being the author of your actions inside your own heirarchy.

But then there's this other variety I keep thinking about: free will as being embedded in a responsive social loop, where what's valuable isn't just that your will is layered inside your head, but that your plans can actually be interrupted and reshaped by other people without it feeling like coercion. Like, you decide to move to another city, your friend points out something you missed, and you genuinely change course — and that revision feels like it's still yours, not like you lost control. The worth here seems to come from your agency being porous but not fragile, not from internal hierarchy.

So my question: are these actually two distinct varieties? Or is the second one just Frankfurt's idea applied to social inputs? And if they do come apart — say, someone has perfect internal harmony but lives in an environment where nobody can ever talk them out of anything — do they still have the kind of free will that's worth wanting? Compare them head-on, say where they overlap, where they split, and which one survives if you think free will has to matter to actual people living with other people.
