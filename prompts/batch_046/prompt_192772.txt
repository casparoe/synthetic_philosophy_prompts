I've been reading the rationalist literature on existential risk and longtermism, and I keep noticing an unstated tension: arguments for protecting distant future lives often treat the moral weight of preventing non-existence as structurally comparable to the moral weight of preventing death, yet the Epicurean tradition insists that death is not a harm to the one who dies precisely because there is no remaining subject to bear it. I'm proposing a six-month conceptual research project—suitable as a dissertation prospectus or a workshop pitch—to determine whether the Epicurean argument about death's badness actually undermines, supports, or is orthogonal to longtermist obligations toward future people. The precise research question: is the asymmetry between death-as-harm and non-creation-as-no-harm a purely logical feature of welfare ascriptions (no subject, no harm), or does it depend on a substantive metaphysical commitment about personal identity and the temporal location of evaluation?

Existing answers don't settle this because Epicurean defenders typically present the argument as a formal constraint on predication—if there is no bearer, there is no harm—while philosophers working on population ethics treat it as a metaphysically loaded claim about identity over time, but neither camp has systematically separated the logical structure from the persistence conditions required to apply it. My method is to construct a minimal formal framework of "subject-indexed evaluation" parameterized by two variables: (i) the persistence conditions required for a subject to exist at the evaluation time, and (ii) the temporal index at which badness is assigned; then apply this framework to death, to non-creation, and to a constructed intermediate case in which a subject's identity is metaphysically indeterminate at the evaluation point. The obvious alternative—surveying historical Epicurean texts for interpretive resolution—would not disentangle logic from metaphysics, so I reject it in favor of this structural modeling approach.

A successful result would be a decision matrix mapping specific metaphysical views (endurantism, perdurantism, stage theory, and a constructed hybrid) onto specific moral conclusions about obligations to future generations, with at least one non-obvious divergence—say, that a particular persistence view licenses symmetric treatment despite the Epicurean intuition. The project fails if every plausible persistence theory collapses to the same moral verdict, which would show the framework is trivial rather than illuminating. The deliverable would be a 10,000–12,000-word paper with a formal appendix plus a one-page decision tool for philosophers of value working on population ethics. Scope assessment: this is feasible in one semester because it requires no empirical data, no citation-based literature review (I will supply references myself), and relies only on conceptual tools standard in analytic metaphysics and decision theory; the risk is that the formal model becomes too complex for the page limit, so I will restrict the intermediate case to a single well-defined ambiguity rather than opening the full space of personal-identity puzzles. Please write the proposal with the question stated precisely, the gap in current positions explained, the approach justified against obvious alternatives, the success and failure conditions specified, an honest scope assessment, and no bibliography or citation list included.
