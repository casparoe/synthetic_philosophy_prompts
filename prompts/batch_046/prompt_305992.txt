A recent essay I read summarizes David Lewis's theory of convention and its bearing on the evolution of norms as follows. Lewis, it says, demonstrated that conventions are nothing more than equilibria in repeated coordination games: regularities that survive because each agent prefers to match the behavior of others, provided everyone expects everyone else to match. Because any of several patterns could solve the same coordination problem—driving on the right versus the left—the particular pattern that takes hold is arbitrary in the sense that no feature of the problem itself selects it. The essay then argues that Lewis therefore viewed conventions as evolutionary artifacts: norms that crystallize through cultural selection without anyone deliberately adopting them, which means moral practices like promise-keeping are essentially frozen historical accidents. On this reading, there is no deeper rational justification for a convention beyond the bare fact that we happen to have landed on it, and asking whether a norm is correct is like asking whether right-hand driving is correct.
