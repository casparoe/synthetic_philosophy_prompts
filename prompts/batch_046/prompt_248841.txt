I'm giving the eulogy for my old adviseor, who died last tuesday at 67. She taught cognitive science for thirty years and was genuinely committed to Hofstadter's idea that an 'I' is just a running pattern that can be distributed across multiple brains—she'd say that decades of teaching literally copies fragments of your self into students' heads, not as a metaphor but as actual shared structure. Her family asked me to speak at teh memorial, but she left a note saying she hates the usual 'she's watching over us' kind of talk because it's false comfort, and she doesn't want to be turned into a ghost at the last minute. The audience is split between philosophy colleagues who know this literature and relatives who don't. Please write the speech: the distributed-self idea has to do real structural work, shaping exactly what I can and can't claim, not just decorate the talk. It needs to comfort the room without saying anything she would have called false, and it should land for the experts without leaving everyone else behind. Don't turn this into a bibliography or ask me to cite sources—just write the eulogy.
