In recent arguments over renaming streets that honor colonial administrators, I keep encountering the line: a street name is just a label, so changing it does not change history, and anyone who resists is confusing cartography with commemoration. I heard it used most recently to dismiss objections to removing a particular figure’s name from a city grid, on the grounds that navigational coordinates carry no moral content and that historical knowledge lives in archives, not in asphalt. But that one-liner seems to pack together several distinct claims about the semantics of proper names in public space, the survival of commemorative intent after the original naming act, the necessary conditions for historical memory, and whether moral evaluation of a figure can be cleanly separated from practical wayfinding. Could you lay out the precise readings this slogan could bear—ranging from the trivial (maps are not textbooks) to the substantive (commemorative force decays to zero once institutional memory fades) to the potentially false (physical presence in the built environment is causally irrelevant to collective remembrance)—and for each say whether it is defensible, false, or question-begging? In particular, I want to know which of these readings would actually license the move from “this is administratively a label” to “therefore resistance to renaming is conceptually confused,” and whether any reading avoids smuggling in an unstated premise about what commemoration requires. Present this as a disambiguated menu I can bring to the debate, with verdicts on which interpretations do the argumentative work the slogan was asked to do.
