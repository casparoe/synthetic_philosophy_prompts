I'm drafting a chapter on the institutional function of atrocity naming and have built an argument that seems to drive Lemkin's category into self-defeat, but I think there's a hidden slide I can't locate. Here is the chain:

(1) For Lemkin, genocide is structurally distinct from other war crimes: it targets a group's existence as such, which justifies both its exceptional moral condemnation and the special legal obligations of the Genocide Convention.
(2) This distinctness depends on criteria—protected groups and specific intent—that are meant to be applied neutrally, not politically.
(3) Yet the politics of naming shows that states systematically withhold the label from assimilationist and colonial policies that plausibly meet those criteria, while applying it selectively against adversaries.
(4) If the moral exceptionalism in (2) depends on the legal specificity in (1), but actual application tracks geopolitical interest rather than the criteria in (1), then invoking "genocide" marks a power difference, not a real moral distinction.
(5) To preserve the moral distinction, one must apply the criteria consistently, which would classify many standard state-building and assimilationist projects as genocidal.
(6) But if the category expands to cover ordinary state practices, it loses the exceptional status that justified special legal obligations in the first place.
(7) Therefore, Lemkin's framework either collapses genocide into rhetorical politics or dilutes it into meaninglessness.

I want to preserve (1) and reject (7). Where does the argument slip—between (3) and (4), in treating selective enforcement as undermining moral distinction, or elsewhere? And what does repairing it cost: must we abandon the legal-institutional framing for a purely moral conception, or can we defend the category by accepting that all legal norms are politically applied without losing their normative force?
