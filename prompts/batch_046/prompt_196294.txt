I'm taking a seminar on distributive justice and trying to determine my actual commitments about desert, rather than adopting a standard position from the literature. I've tentatively formulated what I call a "filter principle": a person deserves reward R for an outcome O only if (i) O was produced by an exercise of agency that the person could have withheld, (ii) the value of O is not fully reducible to background conditions—genetics, family wealth, social position, or brute luck—that the person did not choose, and (iii) withholding R would constitute a failure to recognize the specific causal role of that agency in producing O. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and they do not align cleanly.

Case 1: A cardiac surgeon who saves dozens of lives annually and is paid $800,000. Verdict: deserved. The principle seems to fit—her skill is exercised, not reducible to background alone, and withholding pay misrecognizes her specific role.

Case 2: A tech founder who becomes a billionaire primarily because his parents funded his first startup and he was born into the right social network, though he also worked hard. Verdict: not fully deserved. The principle explains this—much of the outcome is reducible to unchosen background conditions.

Case 3: A warehouse worker who lifts boxes for ten hours a day, works harder physically than almost anyone I know, but generates minimal market value and earns near minimum wage. Verdict: she deserves substantially more than she receives, despite the small economic contribution. Here the principle wobbles—her effort clearly satisfies clause (i) and is not reducible to background, yet clause (ii) seems to tie desert to the magnitude of O, which is small, and clause (iii) is unclear because the market does not withhold recognition from her agency specifically but from the value of the output.

Case 4: A novelist who writes a masterpiece after decades of failed drafts and poverty. Verdict: fully deserved. All three clauses are satisfied, and the outcome's value seems inseparable from her sustained agency.

Case 5: A student who cheats on an exam and receives the top grade. Verdict: undeserved. Clause (i) fails—she did not exercise the relevant academic agency that the grade is meant to recognize.

Case 6: Two people with identical innate mathematical ability and identical work ethic. One is born in a wealthy country with excellent schools; the other in a region with no schooling. The first becomes a renowned mathematician; the second never learns algebra. Verdict: the first's success is not more deserved than the second's failure is undeserved, yet I also think the first still deserves recognition for her actual mathematical work. This creates tension—clause (ii) seems to diminish or cancel desert for the first because her success is partly reducible to educational luck, but my intuition in Case 4 resists allowing background conditions to cancel desert entirely once genuine agency is present.

Case 7: A researcher who makes a major discovery, but only because she was randomly assigned to a well-funded lab rather than to a competing lab that was defunded. Verdict: she still deserves full credit. Here clause (ii) should not cancel desert—the luck concerns the opportunity, not the production of the specific scientific outcome once she is working.

Case 8: Someone inherits a painting that turns out to be a lost masterpiece worth millions. She did nothing to create or preserve it. Verdict: the windfall is undeserved. Clause (i) clearly fails.

The reconciliation I need concerns the relationship between Case 3 and Case 6. In Case 3 I want agentive effort to generate desert largely independent of market value; in Case 6 I want background luck in capacity-development to diminish desert without erasing it. Does this mean clause (ii) must be split into "luck in opportunity" (Case 7) versus "luck in developed capacity" (Case 6)? Or does Case 3 require abandoning the idea that desert tracks outcomes at all, and instead tracking something like "expenditure of agentive effort regardless of result"? If so, what does the revised principle say about Case 4, where the masterpiece—the outcome—seems essential to the judgment that she is deserved? And if I want a principle simpler than one with four sub-clauses, which of my verdicts—Case 3 or Case 6—should I revise?

Please state the principle precisely, revised or replaced, with each clause motivated by the case that forces it. Apply it to a case I have not considered: a firefighter who saves a child from a burning building, but who was only on duty that day because she lost a coin flip that morning to decide whether to take a personal day. Tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether accommodating Case 3 requires giving up the intuition that desert is partly about recognizing specific outcomes rather than merely rewarding effort.
