I've been turning over Pascal's remarks on justice and force—particularly the idea that justice without enforcement is powerless, and the companion claim that established custom acquires normative weight simply because it is established, which seems to leave justice either arbitrary or entirely dependent on coercion. Most secondary literature sorts this into two camps: either Pascal is a proto-Hobbesian saying that force grounds justice, or he is an ironist exposing justice as a mask for power. I'm proposing a one-semester research paper—roughly 5,500 words, suitable for an advanced philosophy seminar—that asks whether both camps miss a third reading: that Pascal is describing an epistemic-coordination problem in which visible enforcement mechanisms don't merely motivate compliance but actually constitute the public determinacy of justice itself. On this reading, without a stable, observable sanctioning structure, agents cannot converge on shared normative expectations, so justice fails not because it is immoral without force, but because it becomes conceptually indeterminate.

The question needs sharpening: is this epistemic-coordination interpretation textually defensible, conceptually distinct from both Hobbesian sanction theories and Humean convention accounts, and normatively consequential—specifically, does it imply that coercive institutions are legitimate only when they solve a coordination problem rather than when they merely express sovereign will? Existing answers leave this open because commentators tend to treat Pascal's anthropology as either theological or cynical, and rarely engage modern philosophy of law or social-coordination theory. The project would proceed by reconstructing the argument from the relevant fragments without relying on any particular edition's numbering, comparing it to modern equilibrium-selection models of convention (not as empirical verification but as conceptual clarification), and testing whether the reading collapses back into conventionalism or can sustain a non-arbitrary distinction between just and unjust uses of force.

The deliverable should be a precise thesis: either the epistemic-coordination reading survives comparison with modern accounts and yields a distinctive criterion for institutional legitimacy, or it fails because Pascal's theological commitments make the coordination problem unsolvable without grace, which undermines the secular analogy. Failure conditions include finding that the reading requires attributing to Pascal a concept of public determinacy he couldn't have held, or that the distinction between coordination-based and sovereignty-based legitimacy is unstable once formalized. The scope is tight—no empirical study, no full history of legal positivism—just conceptual reconstruction and one targeted comparison. I want the proposal to argue that this is tractable for a semester and genuinely unsettled, not just a historical gloss.
