Consider the disciplinary framework used by several large universities for academic integrity violations. The framework distinguishes between intentional plagiarism and accidental citation failure, but within the intentional category it applies sharply different sanctions based on the student's expressed attitude: a student who admits fault, expresses remorse, and participates in restorative dialogue receives only a failing assignment grade and a mandatory ethics workshop, while a student who denies culpability or displays indifference receives suspension, even when the copied text, the harm to evaluation, and the evidence of intent are identical in both cases. The framework's official justification states that punishment here is not primarily for deterrence or compensation, but for restoring a moral equilibrium that is disrupted when a community member adopts an inappropriate reactive stance toward shared norms. By contrast, for purely accidental citation errors—where no culpable mental state is present—the framework applies a uniform minimal correction regardless of whether the student offers a profuse apology or remains silent; the offender's emotional response is treated as entirely irrelevant to the sanction.

What philosophical commitments about moral responsibility and reactive attitudes does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the framework treat blameworthiness as constituted by the appropriateness of reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation, or does it merely use expressed remorse as evidence of an underlying control condition or character trait? Is the practice strictly committed to a view in the tradition of Strawson, according to which responsibility practices are justified by the value of interpersonal relationships and the quality of reactive attitudes rather than by desert or by consequentialist aims? Or could a control-based account, a consequentialist account, or a hybrid view license exactly the same pattern—perhaps by treating remorse as a proxy for reformability in the intentional case and as irrelevant in cases where control was already absent? Identify any internal tension in treating the offender's attitude as determinative of punishment severity in one domain but as philosophically irrelevant in the other, and say whether a unified theory can resolve it. Finally, specify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where competing theories of responsibility converge on the same sanctions—and describe what a policy built on a purely outcome-based or purely control-based theory of blame would have to change.
