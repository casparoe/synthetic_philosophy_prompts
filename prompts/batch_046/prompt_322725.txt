I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am trying to understand how philosophers analyze causation by omission—situations in which something is not done and that absence is said to cause a result. A senior architect I work with has made clear judgments about several past incidents, and I want to test whether her reasoning is consistent.

Her verdicts so far:
- A production firewall rule was never configured; unauthorized access followed. She said the omission caused the breach.
- An automated backup script was never implemented; a hardware failure led to permanent data loss. She said the omission caused the loss.
- Input-validation code was left out of a web endpoint; a SQL injection attack succeeded. She said the omission caused the breach.
- A security patch for a third-party library was available but not applied, because the maintenance contract explicitly excluded dependency updates; the system was later exploited through that vulnerability. She said the omission did not cause the exploit.

Here is the new case. In a federated learning deployment, the server protocol omitted a Byzantine-robust aggregation rule. The rule was present in the research prototype on which the system was based, but it was not listed in the client's requirements, not required by the deployment contract, and not yet standard commercial practice at the time. A malicious participant submitted corrupted model updates, and without the robust aggregation rule the global model was poisoned—an outcome the rule would have prevented.

What do you think she will say about this case? I care less about the specific answer than about identifying the principle she is actually running: is it normative, tracking explicit contractual or role-defined obligations; counterfactual, tracking whether the missing step would have prevented the outcome regardless of obligations; or architectural, tracking whether the missing element belongs to the system's design heritage or intended functional structure even when undocumented? Which principle best fits her first four verdicts, what is your best prediction for the federated-learning case with reasons, and which further scenario would best distinguish the two leading candidates?
