I'm drafting a six-month workshop grant proposal in philosophical psychology, and I need the framing sharpened into a fundable research plan. The domain is intuition pumps—how thought experiments persuade and mislead—though I want to avoid the well-worn terrain of whether the trolley problem merely triggers emotional interference. Instead, I want to pursue a more specific, under-examined mechanism: whether canonical moral intuition pumps systematically suppress diachronic structure by presenting ethical choices as instantaneous, discrete snapshots rather than as commitments that must be maintained, justified, and revised across time. The precise question is whether these pumps mislead not primarily through framing or affective hijacking, but through a structural occlusion of temporal narrative—treating agency as a single decision-node and thereby hiding the ongoing costs of adherence that real moral life requires.

Existing analyses of pump design, from Dennett-style functional decompositions to recent empirical work on moral intuition, have mapped how experiments direct attention and activate emotional responses, but they rarely investigate whether the persuasive force of a pump depends on concealing the diachronic labor of commitment. A philosophical rather than empirical project can advance this by constructing a taxonomy of what I will call "process pumps"—thought experiments that require the agent to revisit, defend, and potentially alter the same choice across successive temporal stages—and comparing their dynamics to standard "snapshot pumps." The obvious alternative is to run behavioral studies measuring response shifts; my proposal argues that conceptual taxonomy and phenomenological analysis of temporal structure must precede measurement, since without knowing what sustained moral commitment looks like in hypothetical form, empirical work lacks the right variables to manipulate. I will supply citations myself, so please do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; refer to positions and arguments descriptively.

What I need is a one-page proposal containing: the research question articulated precisely as above; an explanation of why current literature on pump mechanics and moral psychology leaves this temporal-structure gap open; a defense of the conceptual-taxonomy approach over empirical or neuroscientific alternatives; a description of the deliverable—likely a working paper distinguishing snapshot-pumps from process-pumps and identifying the specific misdirection each produces—together with clear failure conditions (for instance, if all process-pumps collapse into iterated applications of an identical snapshot intuition, then the diachronic-suppression thesis fails); and a realistic six-month scope moving from framework construction through comparative analysis of three canonical cases. The conceptual work lies in framing a question that is both tractable and genuinely unsettled, with an angle that is not the standard emotional-interference story.
