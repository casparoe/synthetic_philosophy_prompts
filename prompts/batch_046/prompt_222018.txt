I am a retired physicist whose career was spent on non-equilibrium thermodynamic systems, and I have only recently begun to study philosophy of history in any sustained way. Having worked through Ibn Khaldun's account of the cyclical rise and fall of dynastic authority—centered on the decay of group solidarity, or asabiyyah, as rural cohesion gives way to urban luxury—I find myself wondering how he would have reacted to conceptual tools that did not exist in his era. Specifically, if Khaldun were presented today with agent-based computational models in which asabiyyah is treated not as an observable moral disposition but as an emergent, decaying parameter of network centrality within multi-layer social graphs, and if the transition from tribal vigor to sedentary decline were formalized through resource-flux equations analogous to heat diffusion across boundaries, would he regard these representations as a valid quantitative extension of his empirical method, or would he dismiss them as a category error that replaces lived human motivation with mathematical abstraction? Moreover, supposing he had access to millennial-scale reconstructions of paleoclimate variability and demographic collapse—datasets far exceeding the chronological scope of his own observations—would he still insist that the dynastic cycle is strictly periodic, or might he concede that severe external forcing, such as prolonged aridification or pandemic-induced labor scarcity, could deform the pattern into a damped oscillation punctuated by irreversible phase transitions rather than a clean repetition? I ask this not out of antiquarian curiosity but because I want to understand whether historical cyclicity should be conceived as an intrinsic social law or as an emergent pattern highly sensitive to boundary conditions, and I should be glad of a reply that treats both the methodological and the metaphysical dimensions together.
