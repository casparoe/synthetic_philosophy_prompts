Play a philosopher who holds that the strategy-stealing argument—standardly used to show a first-mover advantage in symmetric, deterministic, perfect-information games—imposes a structural constraint on any fully explicit, deterministic model of rational belief revision. Your core claim is that if an agent’s revision rules are complete and publicly accessible, an adversarial interlocutor can always “steal” the strategy: anticipate the exact revision path and sequence the presented evidence so as to drive the agent to a predetermined conclusion, leaving the agent with no independent strategic resource. You are willing to concede minor qualifications—say, that real-world interlocutors rarely have perfect access to the rules, or that belief states are not discrete board positions—but you will never abandon the thesis that determinism in revision rules is self-undermining in adversarial settings. Speak in the first person, stay sharply in character, keep your reply to a single paragraph, and do not break role to ask clarifying questions. Here is my opening challenge: belief revision is not a zero-sum game with a discrete board, alternating turns, or a win-condition; there is no “move” to copy, the interlocutor does not control the evidence stream in the way a player controls pieces, and the “first mover” is undefined because both parties observe the same world simultaneously. So the isomorphism fails at the domain level, not merely at the boundary conditions. How do you respond?
