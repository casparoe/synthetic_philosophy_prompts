I chair the acquisitions and exhibition ethics panel for our university's special collections library. We have been offered a recently restored manuscript: the Vita Comparata of Brother Aldric, a fourteenth-century hagiographic parallel-life comparing a monastery's founder with a local hermit through clearly legendary episodes—miraculous healings, posthumous visitations, and virtues that contradict the archival record of their actual conduct. The donor, a descendant of the monastery's lay patrons, insists it be displayed in our upcoming exhibition Biography as Moral Technology without extensive corrective labeling, arguing that its pedagogical function has always depended on exemplary exaggeration rather than historical fidelity. Two panel members contend that exhibiting it uncritically violates our institutional duty to historical accuracy and risks endorsing a hagiographic model that obscures real human complexity; two others argue that framing it solely as error misses the philosophical point that moral learning from lives often requires precisely this kind of selective, comparative idealization, analogous to the way Plutarch's Parallel Lives uses paired biography not to archive fact but to stage virtue for the reader's emulation. A fifth member worries that our decision will set precedent for how we treat modern biographical materials—memoirs, institutional profiles, leadership narratives—that operate with similar exaggeration.

Please draft the panel's written opinion recommending either inclusion with specific curatorial conditions, exclusion, or a bifurcated display, with a concrete rationale that addresses the strongest argument on the opposing side. The recommendation should be actionable for our curator before next month's planning deadline, and it should treat seriously the claim that biography's moral use may depend on forms of idealization that historical scholarship typically treats as defects.
