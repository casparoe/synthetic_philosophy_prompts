I spent thirty-five years in structural biochemistry before retiring, and over the past two years I have been working through the philosophy of science with the seriousness of someone who missed the humanities the first time around. Next month I am presenting at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-biology seminar—attended regularly by two philosophers who defend teleosemantic and causal-informational accounts of genetic representation, a senior molecular biologist who directs a sequencing facility and regards philosophical critique of the “code” metaphor as anti-scientific obscurantism, a philosopher of science who treats scientific metaphors as harmless heuristic scaffolding, and a bioethics doctoral student whose research turns on whether “editing the genetic code” is an ethically neutral framing—where I will defend the following thesis: DNA is not a semantic code, nor even a stable causal-informational message, but rather what I propose to call an “allosteric template,” a stereochemical substrate whose phenotypic effects are systematically underdetermined by cytoplasmic state, epigenetic marking, and enzymatic context, so that the same sequence can specify radically different outcomes without any failure of “transmission.” I argue that the “genetic code” is therefore not an imprecise metaphor but a category error that projects hierarchical, representational structure onto a distributed, non-directional biochemical network; my sketch of the argument turns on the well-known degeneracy of the codon table and the phenomenon of promiscuous enzymatic recognition, but extends them philosophically by denying that counterfactual robustness—the usual condition for genuine information-carrying—holds across the cellular environments in which development actually occurs. I need a speaker’s prep sheet, not a defense: please order the objections I will actually receive from this specific audience by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrase each in the vocabulary and commitments of the person who will raise it, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is softest; include a concise reply suitable for a two-to-three-minute exchange, and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—particularly any challenge that forces me to take a stand on whether synthetic biology or CRISPR-based therapy conceptually depends on the code metaphor being literally true. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I have no web access during the session and want only what I can defend from my own reasoning and the biological facts I already know.
