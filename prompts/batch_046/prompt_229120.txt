Over the past several months, as I have tried to make sense of a significant personal transition, I have become preoccupied with whether my own beliefs actually hang together as a unified whole rather than as scattered mental events. This concern led me to construct an argument that seems to show that no belief can ever be genuinely unified, with consequences that threaten even the coherence of my autobiographical narrative. Here is the argument.

(1) For any judgment that S is P, there must be some unifying factor—call it U—that binds the representation of S to the representation of P into a single propositional content.

(2) If U is itself a real constituent of the mental state, then by the same reasoning that applies to any relation, U must be bound to S by a further unifier U₁, and to P by U₂.

(3) This generates an infinite regress: U requires U₁ and U₂, each of which requires further unifiers, with no terminating point at which a complete judgment is formed.

(4) Therefore, if relations genuinely unify, then no judgment is ever fully constituted, and there are no true beliefs—including the belief that there are no true beliefs.

(5) But if there are no true beliefs, then there is no coherent subject who can undergo a personal transition, reflect on it, or hold an autobiographical narrative linking past and present.

(6) Hence either my own reflective experience is an illusion, or the principle that every unifying relation requires a further unifier is false.

(7) Yet that principle looked unavoidable: if U does not itself stand in a relation to S and P, then U does not actually connect them, and the judgment collapses into an unstructured list.

Tell me which step to reject, then explain why that step initially seemed compelling and what it costs to abandon it. In particular, I want to know whether one can reject (2) by treating U as not a further constituent but as a primitive mode of combination—what some philosophers call a non-relational nexus—without simply renaming the problem. I also want to understand how the standard responses—Fregean sense-composition, Russellian direct acquaintance with universals, and holistic coherence theories—each pay a different price for blocking the regress at that point, and which of them best preserves the intuition that my beliefs about my own past genuinely belong to a single continuing subject. Reach a definite conclusion about where the regress should be stopped and defend it.
