Explain the conceptual distinction between methodological presentism and normative presentism in historiography, particularly as it arises when historians evaluate past scientific reasoning. Focus on the core problem: whether it is possible for a historian to avoid applying their own era's evidential and rationality standards when assessing why past agents held beliefs that now appear unjustified. Then introduce and analyze a proposed escape route called the counterfactual charity principle, according to which historians should reconstruct past reasoning neither by importing current norms nor by recovering agents' actual beliefs, but by modeling the rationality constraints that would have been appropriate given the agents' local epistemic resources and the structure of their practical environment. Assess whether this principle genuinely allows historians to escape presentist judgment, or whether it merely relocates the historian's contemporary commitments into the reconstruction of that environment. Conclude by identifying which form of presentism, if any, survives this move and whether the residue indicates a genuine conceptual gap or an unavoidable feature of historical understanding.
