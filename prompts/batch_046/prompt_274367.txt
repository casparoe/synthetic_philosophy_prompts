The thesis under examination is that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones that actually produced you. To keep the exercise from being a standard repetition of textbook thought experiments, I want it built around a framing that is usually left implicit: that the essentialist claim may rest not on an intrinsic feature of the original zygote, but on a modal continuity condition linking the actual developmental trajectory to counterfactual branching points during early embryonic cleavage—so that defending identity across hypothetical splits requires accepting a sharp modal cutoff that initially feels arbitrary. Design a structured simulation or game—using roles, rules, and a collective payoff scheme with small real stakes, such as a shared pool of bonus points distributed according to group choices—through which participants first map counterfactual gamete pairings and embryo-division scenarios, then discover that protecting their own continuity forces them into a restriction they initially resisted. The idea should emerge from the mechanics and incentives, not from a lecture up front. Include a timeline, the payoff structure, the debrief that maps each stage onto the distinction between origin essentialism and the more permissive view of biological origin, an honest note on which feature of the modal-branching argument the simulation cannot reproduce, and a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong—especially the conflation of counterfactual possibility with epistemic accessibility—together with how to handle it.
