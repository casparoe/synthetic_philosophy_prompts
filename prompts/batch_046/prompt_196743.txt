I've been working through the literature on whether cognition extends beyond the brain—smartphones, notebooks, collaborative partners—and I keep running into the same argumentative shape. A writer begins with a concrete pairing: someone with severe memory loss who relies on a notebook to navigate daily life, or two collaborators who finish each other's calculations so smoothly that describing them separately seems to miss something important. The initial claim is metaphysically bold: the notebook is literally part of the person's memory system, or the pair literally constitutes one distributed cognitive agent. Then an objection arrives—about different material composition, about causal continuity, about who owns the mental state or bears moral responsibility—and the writer doesn't defend the bold claim head-on. Instead, they slide to saying that treating the tool or partner as part of the system is "just a useful modeling choice," or that the boundary is "pragmatic rather than metaphysical," while still using the same examples and vocabulary ("part of my mind," "one system") that originally motivated the bold reading. The two readings aren't clearly separated; they share cases and language, so a reader can be pushed back and forth without noticing that the thesis has changed.

Is there an established name for this particular slide—where a metaphysical thesis about cognitive boundaries is replaced by a pragmatic or methodological framing under pressure, without an explicit revision of the claim? How exactly does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from instrumentalism about mental states generally, and from the well-known distinction between embedded and extended cognition? If there is no standard term, what would you call it, and what are its boundary conditions—when does a pragmatic reframing count as this slide rather than as an honest clarification? In your answer, please separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the view that all extended-mind arguments are ultimately pragmatic—as if it were the consensus.
