The exam posed the following question: Explain how Kripke's thesis that proper names and certain kind terms are rigid designators interacts with his claim that some identities—such as that water is H2O, or that Hesperus is identical to Phosphorus—are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori. Evaluate whether this pairing undermines the rationalist doctrine that all necessary truths are in principle discoverable a priori. In your response, clearly distinguish the referential behavior of proper names from that of definite descriptions, and address whether the empirical discovery of such an identity reveals something metaphysical about the objects themselves or merely something semantic about our practices of reference-fixing—including whether a hybrid model in which descriptive satisfaction plays a minimal, non-eliminable role in anchoring theoretical terms would alter the analysis.

The student submitted the following answer: Kripke argues that every proper name is a rigid designator, designating the same individual in all possible worlds in which that individual exists, whereas descriptions like the teacher of Alexander are non-rigid because their extension can vary across worlds. When astronomers found that Hesperus is Phosphorus, they learned a necessary truth—Venus is self-identical in every world—through observation rather than conceptual analysis, and the same structure holds for chemical identities such as water being H2O. From these cases Kripke shows that rationalism is false: there are necessary truths that can only be known empirically. This result is confined to natural kind terms and the identity statements involving them; descriptions do not generate necessary a posteriori truths unless they are anchored by a causal-historical chain of reference transmission, which is the very mechanism that renders names rigid. Therefore the modal knowledge gained is genuinely about the world rather than about language, since rigidity guarantees that our terms attach directly to objects without descriptive mediation, making any conventionalist interpretation of the discovery implausible.
