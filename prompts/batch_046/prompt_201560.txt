As a novelist drafting a narrative in which a protagonist moves through societies with sharply divergent norms of public order, I need the full specification for a philosophical theory that treats everyday conventions—tipping, queuing, jaywalking, and analogous practices—as genuine moral obligations rather than mere etiquette. Before I can apply any framework to scenes in which my characters must decide whether to follow local rules they find arbitrary, I want the desiderata list: the phenomena the theory must explain, the case distinctions it must preserve, the empirical findings it cannot contradict, and which demands are non-negotiable versus camp-specific preferences.

Specifically, lay out what such a theory must get right. It should cover at least: (1) the difference between obligations that bind only locals and those that bind visitors regardless of prior knowledge; (2) the distinction between coordination conventions (queuing) and deference conventions (tipping) and whether they generate obligations of different moral weight; (3) cases where a convention is widely violated locally (jaywalking in empty streets) versus strictly enforced, and whether moral force tracks enforcement or independent normative structure; (4) a niche but instructive case—say, the expectation in certain East Asian dining contexts that chopsticks never be placed upright in a bowl of rice, or the silent-queue norm observed at some Nordic bus stops where speaking violates the convention—explaining whether the obligation derives from harm, disrespect, or pure coordination failure; (5) the distinction between having moral status and having a particular weight of obligation, so that violating a queue is not automatically equivalent to harming a person; (6) consistency with anthropological results on honor and shame dynamics, and with behavioral economics on tipping elasticity and queue compliance; and (7) whether the theory must justify obligations to outsiders in terms those outsiders could accept, or whether local sovereignty over norms is sufficient.

For each requirement, state why it is a requirement and mark whether it is negotiable. Then identify which single requirement—perhaps the demand for cross-cultural justifiability, or perhaps the demand that obligations persist even when local enforcement is absent—does the most damage to the leading candidates (social contract views, harm-based accounts, and virtue-ethical treatments of etiquette). If any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—say, universal justifiability plus local sovereignty plus persistence under non-enforcement—say so explicitly, since that tension is precisely what I need to dramatize. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a full scorecard of every contender, though a brief indication of which major approaches fail which core test is welcome. Avoid bibliographies or reading lists; I am working without access to external sources.
