I have been thinking on my own about Adam Smith's account of sympathy—how it is not merely pity but a kind of imaginative exchange, as if one literally changes places with another in fancy—and about the impartial spectator, that internal figure who observes one's own conduct with a detachment that shared feeling can never provide. To explore what these ideas might mean if they were built into the conditions of life rather than left to private reflection, write a short story set in a settlement where every inhabitant is born with a paired consciousness: they do not only see through their own eyes but continuously experience the sensations and emotions of one other assigned person, so that sympathy is direct, unavoidable, and literal. Among them lives a single individual born without any paired consciousness; she can observe the network of shared feeling around her, understand it precisely, but experience none of it herself, which makes her the only true impartial spectator in the community. Center the narrative on a crisis in which the settlement is about to carry out a collective harm—perhaps the expulsion or punishment of an outsider—because the shared emotional current has drowned out individual judgment. The protagonist must decide whether to intervene using her unique detachment, knowing that any action she takes will appear to the others not as wisdom but as a cold absence of humanity. Do not write an explanatory essay about Smith; let the philosophical tension emerge through her choice, its immediate consequences, and the community's reaction to a judgment that comes from outside sympathy. Keep the tone serious and contemplative, and attend closely to how the physical reality of shared feeling alters what moral responsibility means.
