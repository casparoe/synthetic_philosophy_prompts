I am currently serving as the undergraduate representative on my university's Committee on Synthetic Archives and taking a seminar on the ontology of scientific and technological artifacts, where we have been working through Daniel Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional. Our committee must issue a formal recommendation on how to catalog a set of reconstructed 1970s student newspaper issues that were generated by a language model trained only on fragmentary physical clippings and metadata; the outputs are historically coherent and exhibit consistent editorial patterns, yet they have no human authors and were not specifically designed by the model's architects to reproduce these particular articles. I need a policy memo addressed to the full committee—whose members include archivists, university administrators, and philosophers—that recommends one of the three Dennettian stances (or a principled refusal to apply them) as the official classification standard for these artifacts, with explicit reasoning and an honest account of the strongest argument for the stance you reject.

In drafting this, I want you to treat the case as genuinely ambiguous rather than decorative: the memo should explain why the patterns in these reconstructions are "real" in Dennett's sense—if they are—and whether that reality is best explained by the physical regularities of the training data, by the design intentions embedded in the model's architecture, or by an intentional stance we might illegitimately project onto the outputs. I also want the memo to address a specific proposal I have introduced in our discussions, which I am calling a "reconstructive stance": the idea that these artifacts instantiate real patterns that are design-dependent without being designed for these specific contents, and authorless without being mere physical noise. The memo should evaluate whether this proposed stance is genuinely distinct from Dennett's three or whether it collapses into one of them, and it should reach a clear, actionable recommendation—such as adopting a particular metadata taxonomy or citation protocol—that the committee could vote on without requiring members to become philosophers of mind.

The audience is mixed: about half the committee are specialists in information science or philosophy who will recognize every reference to stance-based ontology, while the other half are family representatives and alumni trustees who need the reasoning in plain language. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further citation, and do not invoke highly obscure technical theories that depend on specialized literature I could not reasonably be expected to have encountered in an undergraduate course. The memo should be polished, formal, and suitable for direct circulation, with a bottom line that respects both the philosophical integrity of the classification and the practical needs of institutional preservation.
