I have just concluded a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons. To organize my thoughts, I drafted a short essay centered on a category I call role-internal reasons, which I think sits uneasily between Williams's two poles. The essay's notes run as follows.

On the standard interpretation, Williams holds that a consideration counts as a reason for an agent A to φ only if it can motivate A to φ through a sound deliberative path originating in some element of A's actual motivational set S. External reasons, by contrast, apply to A irrespective of whether anything in S connects to them. My essay proposes that institutional or social roles generate reasons that depend essentially on the agent's position—for example, a judge's reason to apply the law, or a parent's reason to prioritize a child's welfare—yet these need not trace back to any personal desire, value, or project within S. My friend insisted that such cases are either already internal, because occupying the role is part of S, or straightforwardly external, because they apply in virtue of social facts independent of S.

The essay then examines three responses. First, that role-occupancy is itself an element of S, rendering the derived reasons internal after all. Second, that they are external reasons disguised by conventional language. Third—my defended view—that they form a distinct class: internal in the structural sense that they disappear when the agent exits the role, yet not psychologically internal, since they need not link to any motivational element beyond the bare acceptance of the role itself. I further suggest that Williams's criterion involving a sound deliberative route is ambiguous between psychological deliberation and normative, role-constitutive reasoning, and that how one resolves this ambiguity determines whether the intermediate category collapses.

I would appreciate comments on four points. First, is my characterization of Williams's position accurate, particularly with respect to the claim that motivation must flow from S through appropriate deliberation? Second, does the proposed category of role-internal reasons genuinely resist collapse into either pole, or is the distinction conceptually unstable? Third, is there anything confused about treating bare role-acceptance as a non-motivational basis for reasons? Fourth, do you find the framing substantively interesting, or does it merely rename familiar debates—say, about institutional reasons in practical philosophy—without advancing them?

Please do not supply a bibliography or suggested readings; I am evaluating the argument on its conceptual merits alone, without access to external sources.
