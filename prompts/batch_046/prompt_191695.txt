For an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar (final examination, 120 minutes, essay worth 35 percent of the course grade), construct a single exam essay question on the problem of evil and theodicies. The question must be designed so that it cannot be answered by summarizing Hick’s soul-making theodicy or Plantinga’s free will defense alone; instead, it should force students to synthesize these frameworks with a specific conceptual twist you will embed. Introduce the twist as follows: present a hypothetical modal side-effect theodicy that claims natural evil is not an instrumental means for soul-making but rather a modal consequence—necessary in any world containing genuinely free moral agents—of the same logical conditions that make free will possible, analogous to how certain physical constants are side-effects of a universe capable of supporting life. Ask students to evaluate whether this modified theodicy can answer Mackie’s logical consistency objection without collapsing into determinism or into Hick’s instrumental account. Along with the essay prompt, provide a model answer outlining three essential argumentative moves that distinguish a top response: (1) precisely distinguishing modal necessity (the claim that free agency entails the possibility, not the actuality, of specific evils) from causal determinism; (2) demonstrating how natural evil functions as a non-instrumental side-effect rather than as a pedagogical tool for soul-making; and (3) showing why Mackie’s charge of inconsistency between omnipotence, omnibenevolence, and evil fails if the theist adopts this modal framing rather than the standard instrumental one. Then supply a marking scheme that assigns specific point values—suggest 10 points per move, with 5 points reserved for clarity and integration—together with explicit descriptions of the four most common errors that should cost marks: conflating modal side-effects with instrumental goods, misrepresenting Plantinga’s defense as requiring the actual existence of evil in all possible worlds rather than merely allowing it, treating the evidential problem as reducible to a purely probabilistic calculation, and failing to address why non-sentient suffering (e.g., in pre-human natural history) does not undermine the modal claim. The prompt should contain no bibliography request, no citation instructions, and should be answerable without access to external sources.
