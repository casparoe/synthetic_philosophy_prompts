Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I am struck by how choice architecture operates in domains—public health, environmental policy, financial regulation—where citizens lack the technical expertise I once took for granted in laboratory settings. Write an essay on the following controversy: when a state employs libertarian paternalism—arranging choices so that welfare-promoting options are easier to select without forbidding alternatives—does this practice remain genuinely autonomy-respecting if earlier nudges have already reshaped citizens' revealed preferences? Specifically, consider a scenario in which repeated carbon-footprint nudges cause citizens to internalize green preferences they did not originally hold; if policymakers then nudge those same citizens toward more stringent climate behaviors, are they respecting autonomous choice, or are they exploiting a preference loop of their own design, thereby crossing into hard paternalism? Weigh the autonomy-based objections against welfare-based and epistemic defenses, and reach a clearly justified conclusion about whether such recursive nudging is morally distinguishable from direct coercion. Please do not treat this as an open-ended survey; defend a specific verdict.
