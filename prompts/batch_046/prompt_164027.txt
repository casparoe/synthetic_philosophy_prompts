I sit on my university's Committee for Institutional Transparency, which advises the Vice-Chancellor on communications ethics. Last term, the administration adopted an algorithmic "Neutral Framing Protocol" that rewrites all internal announcements about restructuring into optimised euphemism—"workforce realignment" for redundancies, "spatial consolidation" for campus closures—generated by a language model trained to minimise emotional response. A senior lecturer in political philosophy has lodged a formal objection, arguing that this is not mere stylistic smoothing but precisely the kind of linguistic corruption George Orwell identified: the substitution of abstract, euphemistic constructions for concrete reality, which makes political oppression thinkable by making it unnameable. She does not ask us to ban AI tools outright; she asks the Committee to recommend that the Protocol be suspended for all communications involving employment or resource changes, with a clear standard requiring that any automated reframing preserve referential specificity to the affected parties and actions.

The Committee is divided. One faction holds that Orwell's analysis concerns intentional propaganda by authoritarian regimes, not administrative efficiency in a liberal institution, and that demanding "plain language" imposes an impossible aesthetic standard on bureaucratic writing. The other faction argues that once an institution systematically replaces concrete descriptions with algorithmically generated vagueness, it has crossed a threshold that Orwell's framework identifies regardless of intent: the corruption of language becomes the corruption of thought, and democratic accountability depends on citizens—here, staff and students—being able to form clear beliefs about institutional actions.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the Protocol with binding conditions, suspend it pending revision, or reject it outright—with a specific implementation timeframe. Your reasoning should engage seriously with the lecturer's Orwellian argument without treating it as a historical curiosity, and should give an honest account of the strongest consideration favouring the opposite conclusion. The memo should be suitable for circulation to a body that includes legal counsel, a trade-union representative, and a data-ethics researcher; it need not cite scholarly sources, but it should show why the philosophical issue matters for the decision rather than merely decorating it. Please avoid proposing further literature reviews or bibliographic appendices; the Committee needs to act on the principle, not on a reading list.
