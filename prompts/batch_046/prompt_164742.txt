Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for the conceptual debate over what counts as political corruption, designed to sort a respondent into an actual position through concrete judgments rather than self-labeling. Start with the root question: is an exchange between an official and a private party corrupt solely in virtue of violating an explicit formal rule? From there branch through three successive splits—whether a quid pro quo or intentional exchange of favor is necessary; whether betrayal of a role-based duty of impartiality is sufficient even without explicit exchange; and whether the judgment should be culturally indexed or universal. At each node, pose a concrete case that separates the branches: for instance, a civil servant accepting a culturally obligatory gift that creates no explicit reciprocal obligation; a patron distributing jobs to loyalists in a system where such distribution is normatively expected; or an official steering contracts to a qualified but personally connected firm in a context with weak formal procurement rules. The leaves should include: a strict legalist view; a transactional quid-pro-quo view; a fiduciary or role-betrayal view; a cultural-relativist or local-moral-economy view; an institutional-functionalist view that defines corruption by systemic damage to public-purpose institutions rather than by rule-breaking or loyalty-shifting; and at least one unnamed logical combination that is rarely defended. For each leaf, state its most awkward commitment—something it must accept that sounds implausible when stated plainly out of context. Note any two leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate on these cases, explain why they collapse into one another here, and diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to named scholars; the deliverable is a conceptual sorting instrument, not a literature review.
