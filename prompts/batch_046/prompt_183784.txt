You are a senior philosopher of democracy with deep expertise in American pragmatism, particularly John Dewey's work on inquiry, social intelligence, and democratic governance. Before giving your evaluation, please take a few minutes to outline the three core commitments that define Dewey's experimentalist conception of democracy—fallibilism, distributed social intelligence, and the continuity between scientific inquiry and political decision-making—so that your reasoning is explicit. Then rank the following four institutional designs, each intended to operationalize Deweyan experimentalism in a modern administrative state, from 1 (most faithful to Dewey's experimentalism) to 4 (least faithful). For each design, provide your justification in one or two sentences, focusing especially on whether it preserves both the revision of conclusions and the revision of methods.

A. A system of permanent citizen juries with rotating membership that must approve any regulatory change, modeled on the idea that intelligence is social and distributed, but with no formal mechanism for revising the jury's own procedural rules.

B. A technocratic agency structure that treats policy failures as falsifying instances and automatically sunsets regulations that miss predictive targets, emphasizing fallibilism but removing public deliberation entirely from the revision process.

C. A hybrid model in which local communities conduct iterative policy trials with mandatory cross-community data sharing, combining distributed intelligence with institutionalized error-correction, but requiring unanimous consent for scaling successful trials nationally.

D. A deliberative forum that uses structured adversarial debate between stakeholder representatives to stress-test proposals before implementation, treating disagreement itself as the primary engine of social intelligence, but with no explicit feedback loop linking policy outcomes back to belief revision.

In your ranking, pay particular attention to whether each design treats democratic politics as genuinely continuous with scientific investigation—not merely in being evidence-responsive, but in having its own institutional methods subject to the same experimental revision as its policy conclusions.
