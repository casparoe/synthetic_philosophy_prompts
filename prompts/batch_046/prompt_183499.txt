hey, im a journalist covering science and ideas and im putting together a feature on how people actually learn the line between science and pseudoscience. i need a classroom activitiy — not a lecture — for a philosophy of science workshop, about 12 grad students, 75 minutes, that makes them feel falsificationism working smoothly and then watch it buckle in real time.

design a structured game with roles, hidden rules, and payoffs. split the group into three accreditation panels of four. give them six claim-cards: two obvious science, two obvious pseudoscience, and two middle cases (something like psychoanalysis or string theory, explained in plain language, no required background). the public rule is a popperian protocol — each panel must propose one specific observable outcome that would reject the claim, and they vote scientific or not based on whether that outcome is in principle checkable.

but inject a hidden mechanic im calling shield-cost accounting. every claim-card carries two hidden layers: an auxiliary assumption layer (measurement error, statistical noise, ad hoc adjustments) and an institutional exemption layer. panels can invoke either layer to save a claim from rejection, but each invocation burns credibility points tracked silently by a non-voting auditor. they dont see the scorecard until round two.

round one: panels apply the protocol naively and vote. round two: the auditor reveals the shield-costs, and panels discover they all used exemptions but priced them differently — some burned points to save astrology, others burned nothing to reject it, using identical logic. round three: panels must re-vote knowing their credibility budgets are depleted, and they see that two panels reached opposite verdicts on the same card because they weighed the shields differently. only after the frustration peaks should the facilitator name the demarcation problem and conventionalist stratagems.

include a minute-by-minute timeline, the auditor role, rules for what happens when a panel tries to invoke more than one shield, and a debrief that maps round one onto naive falsifiability, round two onto protective belts and conventionalist moves, and round three onto the collapse of a sharp demarcation line. be honest about what the activitiy cant reproduce — the actual sociology of peer review, the formal asymmetry between verification and falsification in logic, maybe the historical context of poppers actual targets — and say exactly how the facilitator must supply those gaps by hand. predict the failure mode: students will probably fixate on astrology versus physics instead of seeing the structural problem, and say how to handle that.

what participants should come away seeing: that falsifiability sounds like a clean filter until you have to price the shields, and that the demarcation problem lives in the accounting, not the card.
