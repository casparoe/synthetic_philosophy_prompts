In a philosophy-of-mind seminar, a fellow student dismissed Mary's discovery upon leaving her black-and-white room by insisting that 'what she acquires is purely an ability — comparable to learning to ride a bicycle — not knowledge of any kind.' To strengthen the point, they proposed a hypothetical: imagine Mary had previously worn a cortical stimulator that produced the neural correlates of seeing red without her being able to identify that experience as 'red'; upon leaving the room she can now name and describe the experience, yet, they claim, she still possesses no new propositional content about physics. I am convinced this slogan is doing more work than it appears to, but I cannot unpack it precisely. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that 'Mary only gains an ability, not knowledge' might stand for — distinguishing, for example, practical skill from acquaintance, phenomenal knowledge from propositional knowledge, and prior neural stimulation from conceptual identification — and for each say whether it is trivial, whether it genuinely undermines the anti-physicalist inference, and whether it risks collapsing into the rival claim that there are irreducible phenomenal properties? Please present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than defending a single interpretation, and indicate which reading best supports the speaker's use of the slogan in the argument I described.
