in bioethics circles people keep reaching for the ulysses-contract analogy to defend advance directives: you tie yourself to the mast (the document) before the sirens (dementia, delirium) show up, and you tell the crew (clincians, family) to ignore your later screams to be untied. the move is used to argue that honoring the directive isn't paternalism but contract enforcement, and that the earlier rational self should have veto power over the later happy-but-confused self. can you lay out the correspondance table -- what's the mast, the ropes, the crew, the sirens, the sea? then where it fall appart: is dementia an external lure or a change in who you are? do clinicians act like crew or like lawyers enforcing a will? and does the conclusion -- that we should bind the present patient even when she seems content -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the moral work?
