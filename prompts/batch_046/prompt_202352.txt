After a long, circular argument with a friend about whether death is bad for the person who dies, we want to draft a joint adversarial-collaboration statement rather than continue talking past each other. We represent two camps: Camp A holds the Epicurean no-subject position, joined to the termination thesis and the claim that harm requires a subject who exists at the time of harm; Camp B holds a deprivationist position, specifically a counterfactual-comparative account of harm that permits non-experiential deprivation, together with the denial that the symmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence undermines the intuition that death can be harmful.

Write the document both of us could sign. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: factual, conceptual, and logical points both camps accept, including about the cessation of consciousness, the distinction between experiential and non-experiential harm, and the formal structure of the asymmetry argument comparing birth and death.

Second, a disagreements table with two columns. The left column should state Camp A's residual disagreement in wording that a committed Epicurean would accept; the right column should state Camp B's corresponding disagreement in wording that a committed deprivationist would accept. The formulations should be precise enough that resolving one column's claim without resolving the other's is impossible.

Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. The central ones are: (a) whether a harm can obtain at a time when the harmed subject does not exist; (b) whether counterfactual deprivation of future goods counts as a harm independently of any experienced loss; and (c) whether rational agents' bias toward future goods has any bearing on the axiological comparison between birth and death.

Fourth, for each crux identify one concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant—for (a), a posthumous betrayal of a dead person's explicit will; for (b), a comparison between painless instantaneous death and prolonged suffering that yields identical total welfare; for (c), a decision-theoretic scenario in which rational planning for a future is eliminated without warning. For each case, record in advance what Camp A predicts and what Camp B predicts.

Please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature from what remains genuinely contested among informed practitioners, and do not present either camp's view as the consensus. Keep the tone formal, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works.
