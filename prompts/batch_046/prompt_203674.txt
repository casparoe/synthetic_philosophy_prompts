Design a 90-minute seminar activity for twelve graduate students in philosophy of religion or metaphysics. The aim is to make participants feel the pressure of anattā—the denial of a permanent, substantial self—specifically where it collides with the need for a stable bearer of karmic debt and contractual obligation. Do not open with a lecture on Buddhist doctrine. Instead, build the activity around this framing: in some Abhidharma accounts, a mental stream (santāna) can split into multiple continuers or merge with another stream, so personal identity is not one-to-one but a branching network of causal-psychological links. Give each student a “continuity card” listing memory traces, intentional habits, and affective dispositions. The rules should require them to negotiate “karmic contracts” (binding promises that carry small, real stakes—e.g., shared grading labor or pooled resources) without being permitted to invoke an enduring “I” as the contracting party. Introduce a split phase, in which one stream divides and both successors deny inherited debt because neither is “the original,” and a merge phase, in which two streams combine and both claim credit for prior merit. The coordination failure or paradox should emerge from the mechanics before anyone names it as anattā. After the game, lead a debrief that maps each mechanical breakdown onto a philosophical problem: the no-bearer objection, moral responsibility across rebirth, and narrative-identity responses. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—especially the first-person phenomenological absence of self—and state exactly how you will supply that missing element in discussion. Predict at least one way the activity will misfire in practice and how to correct it. Structure your response in these sections with approximate lengths: Activity Rules, Roles, and Stakes (~300 words); The Debrief and Conceptual Mapping (~250 words); Predictable Breakdowns and Facilitator Fixes (~150 words); Limits of the Simulation (~100 words).
