I'm working through my position on religious belief through a broadly Wittgensteinian lens and want a consistency check before I present it. Here is the package. First, I hold that religious language is grammatically autonomous: its core claims are not empirical hypotheses and should not be assessed by evidential standards. Second, I take the difference between genuine faith and superstition to turn not on rational justification but on integration—genuine belief is woven into a sustained form of life, whereas superstition is an isolated magical practice lacking that embedding. Third, I think that because religious language-games are self-contained, an outsider can never genuinely grasp an insider's statement in the insider's way; behavioral observation is the best substitute available. Fourth, despite that autonomy, I maintain that religious doctrines can still be criticized from outside by demonstrating practical contradictions within the form of life they claim to regulate—for example, when a tradition commands universal love yet institutionalizes practices of exclusionary violence. Fifth, I read Wittgenstein's own refusal to argue either for or against religious belief as showing that he treated such frameworks as rationally closed, which means anyone who takes his view seriously should refrain from normative evaluation of them from without. Sixth, I understand the believer's commitment not as propositional assent but as a transformative orientation that restructures practical reasoning, the endurance of suffering, and the formation of hope. Do these hang together? I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like tensions but dissolve on inspection, and for each real conflict what the least costly revision would be. Please rank the tensions by severity, say what each revision costs, and identify which single change would restore the most coherence without abandoning the central insight that religious belief operates according to its own grammar.
