I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in metaethics regarding evolutionary debunking arguments. One party, the Selective Debunker, holds that evolutionary origins undermine justification specifically for first-order moral judgments that claim categorical normative authority, but do not undermine instrumental norms, logical norms, or the second-order epistemic norms that underpin the debunking argument itself; on this view, evolutionary influence functions as a defeater only in domains that lack independent calibration mechanisms and track reproductive fitness rather than representational accuracy. The opposing party, the Uniformist, denies that any principled boundary separates moral cognition from other normative or descriptive domains with respect to evolutionary contamination; they maintain that if evolutionary etiology defeats justification in morality, consistency demands that it defeat justification in epistemology, mathematics, and logic alike, and that attempts to restrict debunking to morality rely on an unstated distinction between representational and projective cognition that collapses upon examination.

Please draft the joint statement that both parties could sign after good-faith collaboration. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include agreement on the evolutionary etiology of at least some moral judgments, on the formal structure of debunking arguments (premises about causal origins and their relation to justification), and on the conditions under which an evolutionary explanation would constitute a genuine defeater rather than a mere genetic fallacy. Second, a disagreements table that states each divergence in wording that its own side accepts: specifically, whether a non-arbitrary distinction exists between domains vulnerable to evolutionary debunking and domains immune to it; whether the Selective Debunker can consistently preserve the normative authority of the epistemic standards used to advance evolutionary skepticism; and whether cross-domain differences in belief stability reflect genuine differences in tracking reliability or merely differences in social transmission mechanisms. Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one party—including the question of whether evolutionary explanations provide differential support for the unreliability of deontic cognition compared to logical or mathematical cognition. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete empirical case or comparative finding that both sides agree bears on the matter—such as the observed cross-cultural variability in deontic judgments versus the near-universal acceptance of basic logical inference patterns—along with each side's explicit predictive interpretation of that evidence should the crux be settled in their favor.

Write in formal, polished academic prose appropriate for a collaboration protocol, without citing specific authors, texts, or bibliographies, and ensure that the residual disagreements are rendered precisely rather than dissolved into verbal ambiguity.
