I'm drafting a novel about a constituional convention in a made-up republic, and I got pulled into this argument between two other writters at the retreat — well, they were going at it and I mostly took notes because my characters need to have the same fight. Marcus said locking an eternity clause into the charter, like making environmental rights impossible for future legislatures to undo without some wild supermajority, is basically a Ulysses contract done right. The people in 2025 know they'll be tempted to sell out for short-term gain in 2045, so they tie there own hands now while they're still rational. Classic self-binding.

Elena said that's a neat story until you ask who the people actually are. In the Odyssey, Odysseus is the same guy who gets tied to the mast and then untied later. But the citizens who vote for the clause in 2025 are mostly dead or replaced by 2075. So it's not self-binding at all — it's one generation tying up strangers. She called it a ghost contract, and said using the ship-of-Theseus idea for the body politic doesn't save it, because there's no continuous consciousness to bear the cost or enjoy the benefit. If every plank is replaced, there's no ship that actually wanted to stay tied.

Marcus came back saying democratic continuity is real — institutions persist, and the people can act like a corporate agent across time, just like a company making long-term promises. Without that, he said, every election becomes a free-for-all of breaking past commitments, and the time-inconsistency we see in fiscal policy proves exactly why we need these locks.

Elena answered that treating the state like a corporation with one continuous will is just a fiction that hides the real power move. The past generation isn't binding itself; it's binding people who never agreed. She also said Marcus was smuggling in a single-will assumption that democratic theory usually rejects — each generation should be sovereign. So the clause isn't preserving rationality, it's suppressing future democratic choice in the name of past anxiety. They went back and forth about whether dead-hand objections hit budget rules differently than rights-protection, and whether hard amendment rules actually change the moral picture or just push the problem around. Can you go through there exchange move by move and say who's right about the continuity problem, whether the Ulysses analogy holds across generations, and what both are missing — especially about how amendment difficulty interacts with the self-binding idea? Don't just tell me who wins; I want the specific points where each lands a punch and where they slip.
