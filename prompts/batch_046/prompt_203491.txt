Exam question: In his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, Nietzsche distinguishes three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—each relating differently to what he calls the plastic power of life. Explain how each mode can serve the needs of life, specify the particular danger that emerges when one mode dominates without being held in check by a non-historical, life-affirming perspective, and clarify whether Nietzsche treats all three as standing on equal footing or whether one is ultimately privileged as the necessary corrective.

Student answer: Nietzsche distinguishes three modes of historical consciousness: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Monumental history serves life by presenting great figures and deeds as models that inspire present action and greatness; it requires selective forgetting and distortion of the past. Its main risk is that it can encourage mere imitation rather than original achievement, and that it may ignore the specific conditions of the present. Antiquarian history serves life by preserving traditions, customs, and origins, giving individuals and cultures a sense of continuity and rootedness. When it dominates, it tends to mummify life—preserving every detail of the past without discrimination—so that the present becomes a museum and new growth is stifled. Critical history serves life by examining and dissolving past judgments and traditions that have become oppressive, thereby freeing the present for new forms of life and judgment. Unlike the other two modes, critical history is genuinely liberating and should be treated as the primary mode for modern culture; while it can be applied too aggressively, its essential function is corrective, and it alone most directly fulfills the criterion that all history must serve life. Thus, although Nietzsche says all three must be subordinated to life's needs, critical history is the one that actually achieves this subordination, whereas monumental and antiquarian history are useful only as preliminary supports that must eventually be overcome.
