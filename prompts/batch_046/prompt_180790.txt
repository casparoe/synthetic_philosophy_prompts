I have spent much of my career in experimental physics, and since retiring I have turned to moral philosophy with the same instinct for systematizing that governed my laboratory work. Recently I have been revisiting Hannah Arendt's analysis of Adolf Eichmann, particularly her claim that his evil was banal—not rooted in satanic malice but in a kind of thoughtlessness, an inability to think from the standpoint of another. I find the thesis compelling but also conceptually unstable in ways that the secondary literature often glosses over. There seem to me to be three unresolved tensions that deserve serious philosophical labor, but I am unsure which should come first.

First, there is the problem of reconciling Arendt's insistence on Eichmann's personal responsibility with her simultaneous denial that he possessed the kind of deep moral depravity that would make him a traditional villain. If evil can be banal—if it arises from an absence rather than a presence—what precisely is the normative structure of the failure? Is it a failure of imagination, a failure of judgment, or a failure of a specifically moral kind of attention? Second, there is the question of institutional scale. Arendt developed her analysis in the context of a singular bureaucrat within a totalitarian apparatus, but modern administrative harm—whether in scientific organizations, corporate supply chains, or automated governance—distributes agency across networks in ways that seem to dilute the very category of the actor she presumes. Can the concept of banality survive this distribution, or does it require a revised ontology of collective action? Third, there is an underexplored epistemic dimension: Arendt describes Eichmann's reliance on clichés and stock phrases not merely as moral evasion but as a mode of cognition. I am inclined to call this epistemic distancing—the structural separation between procedural knowledge and experiential understanding—but I do not know whether this is a genuine philosophical innovation or merely a redescription of her original point.

If you were advising a small research group—say, three philosophers and one cognitive scientist—on where to direct limited effort over the next several years, how would you rank these three lines of inquiry? I want the reasoning defended explicitly: which question is upstream of the others, which is most tractable given current tools in moral psychology and political philosophy, and which, if resolved, would most alter how we apply Arendt's framework to contemporary bureaucratic life? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want an agenda with priorities, and I want to know what evidence—conceptual or empirical—would cause you to reorder it.
