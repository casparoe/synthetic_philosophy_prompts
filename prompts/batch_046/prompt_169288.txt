Now consider a modified case: an artist fashions a statue from synthetic clay engineered to replace every constituent particle within forty-eight hours while preserving macroscopic shape and chemical signature, so that after one week none of the original matter remains; the work is exhibited continuously without interruption. Does this variant strengthen or weaken the argument that the statue and its material base are distinct? Defend your answer with reference to at least one specific account of material constitution—such as Baker's constitution view, Chisholm's mereological essentialism, or Wiggins's dominant-sort theory—and explain how that account handles persistence through complete material turnover.

Student answer: The variant clearly strengthens the non-identity claim. Under Chisholm's mereological essentialism, the original clay lump is destroyed the instant any particle is replaced, since every material part is essential to the object. By contrast, the statue survives because its persistence conditions depend on artistic form and curatorial continuity rather than on specific matter. This supports Baker's constitution view, which treats the statue as a four-dimensional worm composed of temporal parts, each part constituted by a different clay-lump—much as a person is constituted by successive bodies. The case also resolves Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics: incompatible shapes at t₁ and t₂ belong to different clay-constituents rather than to a single enduring object, so no contradiction arises. Hence, total material turnover proves that artistic identity is both modally and materially independent of the clay, confirming that the statue and the lump are two objects.
