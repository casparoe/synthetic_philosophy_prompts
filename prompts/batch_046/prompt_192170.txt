I'm a high school student trying to work out my view on advance directives and whether an earlier self should be able to bind a later one, and I'd like to test my gut reactions against a principle. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: At 22, Maya writes a directive refusing all life-extending treatment if she is ever in a persistent vegetative state, explaining that she values conscious experience over mere biological survival. At 80 she is in exactly that state, and her doctors can keep her alive for years with basic care. Verdict: Honor the directive and withhold treatment. The 22-year-old made a reflective, informed judgment about what kind of existence she found worth living, and the later self lacks any capacity to revise it.

Case 2: At 40, David writes a directive refusing antibiotics and feeding tubes if he ever develops severe dementia. At 78 he has advanced Alzheimer's; he no longer recognizes family, but caregivers report he seems calm, enjoys music, and eats eagerly. He never updated the document. Verdict: Do not honor the refusal. His current self still has experiential well-being, and it feels wrong to let a decades-old paper end a life that now includes simple pleasures, especially since he could not have predicted what this state actually feels like.

Case 3: At 35, Elena writes a directive refusing mechanical ventilation if she ever suffers irreversible brain damage. At 70 she suffers exactly that damage and is unconscious. Two years earlier, while still fully lucid, she told both her sister and her physician that she had changed her mind and now wanted every possible intervention, though she never filed new paperwork. Verdict: Honor her verbal revocation. The bridge-self—the lucid self immediately before incapacity—should override the distant document because she had direct epistemic access to her present values and addressed the very condition in question.

Case 4: At 30, James writes a directive requesting aggressive life-extending care in all circumstances because he fears death. At 60 he develops a terminal illness, remains fully lucid, and explicitly asks to stop treatment and enter hospice. Verdict: Obviously honor his current, lucid request. A present self with full decision-making capacity should always override an old directive.

Case 5: At 25, Aisha writes a directive refusing blood transfusions based on her religious convictions at the time. At 55 she converts to a faith that permits them. At 80 she needs an emergency transfusion but is unconscious. She never mentioned her medical directive to anyone after converting. Verdict: Honor the old refusal. Without explicit revocation or direct communication about the document itself, allowing family or doctors to infer her current religious views risks substituting their judgment for hers.

Cases 3 and 5 are the pair I keep going back and forth on: in both there is an unrecorded change of values, yet in Case 3 I want to follow the bridge-self's spoken word, while in Case 5 I want to stick to the paper. Is there one principle—perhaps about the bridge-self having special authority only when the change specifically concerns the medical condition addressed, or about the difference between epistemic accessibility and mere value drift—that captures Cases 1 through 4 and forces me to drop either Case 3 or Case 5? Or do I need two separate principles?

Please also tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a 28-year-old writes a directive refusing all pain medication if she ever becomes addicted and then needs surgery, saying she wants to "feel everything clearly." At 65 she is addicted, needs surgery, and is screaming in pain, but she is too cognitively impaired to understand the situation or revoke the directive. Should the directive be followed?
