At lunch, two colleagues—Jordan and Sam—debated whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. Jordan reported the exchange as a neutral observer and did not take sides. Here is the back-and-forth:

(1) Jordan said: A genuine moral dilemma requires that an agent is genuinely obligated to do A and genuinely obligated to do B, where A and B are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive in the circumstances. Standard deontic logic yields O(A) and O(B) → O(A ∧ B). If A and B are incompatible, O(A ∧ B) violates the consistency principle that ought implies can. So the conjunction of two genuine obligations entails contradiction; therefore, by reductio, genuine dilemmas are impossible. What look like dilemmas are just competing prima facie duties, one of which is all-things-considered overridden.

(2) Sam replied: That assumes obligations come in only one strength. There is a category of obligations that are binding but non-overriding—duties that remain in normative force even when they cannot be fulfilled because another duty takes precedence. The fact that I must break one promise to save a life does not erase the wrongness of breaking the promise; I still owe explanation, apology, or repair. This moral residue—the persistent appropriateness of regret and reparation—is best explained if the unfulfilled obligation was genuinely binding, not merely apparently so. The deontic contradiction dissolves once we allow obligations to be genuinely binding without being all-things-considered decisive.

(3) Jordan countered: The residue is purely psychological or relational, not normative. Feeling guilty after breaking a promise is like feeling guilty after being coerced by a threat: in both cases the emotional response tracks a frustrated preference or violated expectation, not an actual violated duty. Once we classify the obligation correctly—it was prima facie and was defeated—no remaining normative claim exists. Calling the residue normative confuses the causal history of an emotion with its justification. Sam has not shown a contradiction in the logic of obligations; she has described an emotional hangover.

(4) Sam responded: That misses the asymmetry of moral loss. In cases involving incommensurable values—say, saving a life versus honoring a sacred trust that resists quantification—there is no common metric allowing one duty to defeat the other in a principled way. The choice is tragic precisely because both values remain authoritative after the decision. If Jordan is right, then once I choose to save the life, the trust-obligation should vanish entirely from the normative landscape, leaving no reason for reparation specific to that broken trust. Yet we do think specific reparation is owed to the violated value, not just generic regret. This suggests both obligations persist; the contradiction is only apparent because it applies a monistic ranking model to a pluralistic normative structure.
