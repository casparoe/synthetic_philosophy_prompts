Give me your credence — decomposed into explicit sub-questions with rough probabilities — that human extinction is bad for a reason genuinely independent of the deaths and welfare losses involved, specifically through the loss of what Samuel Scheffler frames as the afterlife of present projects in the continuation of human culture and achievement. I hold this at about 0.65; a colleague holds it near 0.25, though we both agree extinction is severely bad overall. Break your reasoning into four parts: (1) whether present activities genuinely derive value from expecting psychologically and culturally connected successors to continue them, rather than from present execution alone; (2) whether that dependency survives if biological descendants persist but share no psychological or cultural continuity with us; (3) whether an impersonal axiology can fully accommodate the intuition that extinction is especially catastrophic without invoking project-continuation; and (4) whether the magnitude of this afterlife-related badness is comparable to, or dwarfed by, the direct welfare loss from future people never existing. Assign each part a number, combine them into your overall credence, and say which part most likely explains the gap between my 0.65 and my colleague's 0.25. Finally, state what specific argument or empirical finding — for instance, about the psychology of long-term planning or about axiological structure — would most move your own number.
