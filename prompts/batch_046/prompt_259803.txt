This parallels, I think, the minority view that fundamentality can be genuinely relational rather than monadic, and that well-foundedness might apply to structures rather than to isolated entities. Yet the analogy risks collapse, since fictional existence is representational: a character's properties depend on an author's mental states, which lie outside the fictional world, so there may be no autonomous "ground" within the ontology of the story itself. I have not decided which horn to take. Here are my notes: fictional worlds appear hierarchical—minor figures depend on protagonists, protagonists on plot arcs, plot on thematic framing—but if grounding requires irreflexivity and modal robustness, the author's initial commitments might serve as an external, non-narrative foundation, breaking the parallel. Alternatively, treating the narrative as an autonomous possible-world construction might allow internal grounding relations (e.g., kinship relations grounding individual properties) without mental-state intrusion. I ask you to take a definite stand: either defend the analogy as revealing a real structural parallel between narrative and metaphysical dependency, or reject it by showing that representational dependence vitiates the comparison, with particular attention to whether fundamentality can be distributed across a frame rather than localized in entities. Please correct any mischaracterization of grounding theory—especially regarding relational fundamentality and well-foundedness—point to the precise structural break in my analogy, and do not offer a bibliography, citation list, or reading suggestions; I require only your direct reasoning. Write formally, as though addressing a fellow novelist who takes these philosophical distinctions seriously.
