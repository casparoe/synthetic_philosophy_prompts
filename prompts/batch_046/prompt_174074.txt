I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. After encountering Nyāya epistemology, I have come to treat its four pramāṇas—perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda)—as structurally analogous to verification layers in distributed systems: direct sensor observation, logical deduction over state logs, pattern-matching against reference implementations, and trusted signed documentation. From this I hold that the four form a complete, non-redundant epistemic architecture, and that modern proposals to add further sources—machine-learning intuition, phenomenological disclosure, or probabilistic belief revision—are unnecessary expansions rather than genuine gaps.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: whether comparison collapses into inference plus perception, whether testimony in adversarial or noisy environments requires an independent reliability check that is not itself testimonial, and whether probabilistic or abductive reasoning in science and engineering outruns the syllogistic structure Nyāya assigns to anumāna. Which potential defeaters are fatal, which merely awkward? What would a committed defender of the four-pramāṇa framework concede as a real problem, and what would they dismiss as a misapplication of the analogy? Finally, assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—by claiming that any successful knowledge source just is one of the four in disguise—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a defect for a claim of this kind.
