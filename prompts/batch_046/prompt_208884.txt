I'm preparing a taxonomy for my school's philosophy seminar on Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, using the classic symmetric-universe case of two homogeneous spheres separated by empty space. I want the taxonomy structured as a decision tree in which every branch is labeled by the concrete claim that splits it—so that it sorts people by what they actually believe about the case, not by which philosopher they name.

Please begin with the root question: in such a universe, are the spheres genuinely two objects, or must they be one? From there, split the "they are two" branch by whether their distinction is saved by (a) an irreflexive relational property like being separated by a specific distance from another sphere, (b) a primitive non-qualitative property (haecceity), or (c) the bare denial that PII holds. Then split the "they are one" branch by whether the impossibility is metaphysical, empirical, or conceptual.

Now inject this specific variant for one of the branches: a "gauge-invariant duplicate" in which the entire universe can be rotated 180 degrees around the midpoint without changing any measurable relation, yet there is a hidden orientation parameter—undetectable by any physical measurement—that distinguishes the spheres. Ask whether this parameter saves PII, collapses weak discernibility into strong discernibility, or is metaphysically inert.

For every leaf, provide the position's common name (or "unnamed") and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—what it must accept or deny that feels counterintuitive. Also identify any answer-combinations that are logically open but unoccupied in the literature, with a brief diagnosis (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Finally, flag any positions the tree cannot cleanly separate and explain why they converge on all the diagnostic cases.

Keep the tree to about six to eight leaves, and make sure no branch asks the reader to identify a view by its label rather than by answering a concrete case.
