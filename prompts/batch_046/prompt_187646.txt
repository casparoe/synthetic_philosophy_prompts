I'm writing from my university's philosophy of value course. Our seminar recently discussed incommensurability and hard choices, and I noticed an apparent tension in my university's honors college admission protocol that seems philosophically loaded.

The protocol operates in two stages. Stage One assigns applicants separate percentile scores for 'academic excellence' (course rigor, standardized testing, demonstrated research capacity) and for 'institutional citizenship' (sustained service, collaborative leadership, documented local engagement). Any applicant reaching the 90th percentile on either dimension is admitted automatically, irrespective of performance on the other. Stage Two handles applicants in the middle ranges of both dimensions. Here, a faculty-student committee evaluates candidates according to a charter that explicitly prohibits converting the two dimensions into a single weighted score or utility index. Committee members must instead determine whether admitting the applicant would render the entering cohort 'better in a non-substitutable way'; if members disagree, they are required to issue a split recommendation rather than averaging judgments or imposing a tie-breaker.

This design puzzles me. Does Stage One presuppose that academic and civic value are fully commensurable—merely separated by a discontinuous threshold—while Stage Two denies commensurability entirely? Or can a unified theory of value comparison license both stages consistently? More specifically, does the committee's refusal to aggregate commit the institution to a metaphysical claim that the values are strongly incommensurable (no rational comparison is possible), or only to an epistemic claim that precise comparison exceeds our current capacities? And if hard choices can be rational even when values are not fully comparable—perhaps because options are 'on a par' in Ruth Chang's sense—would the split-recommendation rule be philosophically justified, or does it instead presuppose that any lack of determinacy must produce deadlock rather than a reasoned, non-algorithmic resolution?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments of this practice. Distinguish what the protocol strictly presupposes about value comparison from what merely happens to fit it well, identify internal tensions between Stage One and Stage Two, and indicate where the design is philosophically underdetermined—so that rival accounts of incommensurability, comparability, or hard choice could each accommodate the same institutional rules. Please answer in American English.
