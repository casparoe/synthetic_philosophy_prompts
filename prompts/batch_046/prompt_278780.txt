A colleague who recently read Appiah's study of honor-driven moral change and Bernard Williams's remarks on the dynamics of normative shift offered the following synthesis. On this account, neither thinker believes that explicit moral argument is the engine of revolutions like the end of dueling, Atlantic slavery, or foot-binding. Williams holds that a practice collapses when it becomes structurally incoherent with emerging economic relations—slaveholding grows administratively costly under industrial wage labor, dueling conflicts with bureaucratic state monopolies on violence—while Appiah holds that it collapses when it stops conferring honor and starts conferring shame among status-competitive elites. The colleague concludes that Appiah's 'honor' is therefore just Williams's 'structural incoherence' mediated through social psychology, and that both treat moral reasoning as purely retrospective legitimation with no independent causal weight. The colleague also applies this to foot-binding: because Hakka labor structures never required the immobilization of women, the practice was structurally incoherent there long before it became shameful among Han elites, so the faster decline in Hakka regions confirms that economic structure drives the timeline and honor only tracks it. Is this summary fair? Please separate what accurately captures each thinker, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents their positions—especially whether Appiah reduces honor to economic function, whether Williams actually employs a 'structural incoherence' framework, and whether either thinker denies all causal efficacy to moral argument. Finally, does the Hakka/Han comparison support the colleague's synthesis, or does it misread how Appiah's honor networks operate?
