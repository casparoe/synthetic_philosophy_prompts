I have recently been confronting the problem of evil in a deeply personal register—trying to make sense of prolonged, agentless suffering that offers no clear narrative of redemption. In this context, I keep encountering the analogy that divine permission of suffering is like a surgeon inflicting temporary pain to secure a greater healing; the analogy is deployed to defend the consistency of omnipotence and benevolence with the existence of severe evil. I want to audit this comparison with precision: what corresponds to the surgeon, the patient, the anesthesia, the informed consent, and the anticipated cure? Where does the mapping fracture, especially regarding natural evils that appear therapeutically pointless and victims who never recover? Does the analogy covertly import a closed, deterministic model of intervention—one that assumes a single authoritative agent with full foresight of the outcome—thereby excluding open-ended or process-theological understandings of divine action? And once these disanalogies are on the table, can the analogy still license the conclusion that suffering is instrumentally justified, or does it reduce to an unargued assertion that suffering might eventually cohere into meaning?
