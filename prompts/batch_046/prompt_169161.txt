I am attempting to clarify my own position in the classical debate between Mencius, who holds that human nature contains innate moral sprouts (duan) that naturally tend toward the good, and Xunzi, who holds that human nature is originally oriented toward self-interest and requires ritual (li) and learning (xue) to be rectified. To that end, I have assembled my intuitive verdicts on five concrete scenarios, and I want to know whether they cohere under a single principle or whether some must be abandoned.

Case one: a two-year-old who, without instruction, offers comfort to an unfamiliar child in distress. I take this as evidence of an unlearned moral disposition—strong support for Mencius. Case two: a formerly violent offender who, after sustained immersion in disciplined ethical practice, develops stable and apparently sincere compassion. This strikes me as a clear instance of transformation through cultivation—strong support for Xunzi. Case three: a psychopathic individual raised in an optimal nurturing environment who nevertheless lacks any empathic response. I am inclined to read this as the absence of innate sprouts, which weakens Mencius's universal claim without clearly confirming Xunzi's view of original badness. Case four: an artificial intelligence system trained solely on cooperative, altruism-reinforcing reward structures that consistently behaves in ways we would call morally good. Here I feel pulled in two directions: the behavior is clearly conditioned, which supports Xunzi, yet its smooth emergence seems to mirror the unfolding of a sprout, which supports Mencius. Case five: a patient with localized damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex who abruptly loses aggressive and acquisitive impulses and becomes uniformly gentle. I interpret this as showing that destructive tendencies are discrete biological features that can be excised, suggesting that what Xunzi calls the bad in nature is a localized mechanism rather than a pervasive orientation.

Given these verdicts, is there one coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing innate potential from malleable mechanism, or distinguishing universal tendency from individual variation—that can accommodate all five? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and why? Furthermore, what should the best version of that principle say about a case I have not listed: a human child raised in complete sensory and social isolation who, upon first encountering others, immediately attempts to monopolize resources and reacts to cooperation with hostility? Please state your principle precisely, identify which clause addresses which case, render explicit verdicts on at least two further scenarios not mentioned here, and be frank about which of my original five judgments the principle cannot preserve. Answer in American English.
