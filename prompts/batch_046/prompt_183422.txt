You are an expert philosopher of science with a background in rationalist epistemology and forecasting methodology. I am designing an advanced seminar handout for readers of forums like LessWrong who want to map their own commitments about probability without simply selecting a label. Please construct a decision-tree taxonomy—built from logical questions rather than named schools—that sorts respondents into actual interpretive positions (frequentism, subjectivist Bayesianism, propensity theory, the logical interpretation, and any genuinely unoccupied combinations) based on how they handle a specific, concrete case: a calibrated forecasting system must assign a precise numerical probability to the catastrophic failure of a particular, never-before-tested fusion-reactor design scheduled for activation in 2038, where no historical ensemble of comparable reactors exists.

The tree should begin with that case and then branch through four diagnostic commitments, each phrased as a concrete claim a respondent can agree or disagree with without knowing philosophical terminology. First: whether the absence of a relevant reference class makes an objective frequency assignment conceptually impossible, merely practically hard, or irrelevant because some other source of constraint applies. Second: whether rational belief in this singular case is sufficiently governed by coherence conditions alone—avoiding Dutch-book vulnerability, maintaining calibration across hypothetical updates—or whether coherence is only a consistency check that needs supplementation. Third: whether the physical symmetry and causal structure of the reactor design provide an objective single-case propensity that fixes the probability independently of anyone's beliefs. Fourth: whether updating on new engineering data changes the rational belief in a way that requires a prior that is itself probabilistically justified, or whether priors are merely conventional starting points. Each leaf should name or describe the position it represents, include one sentence on its most awkward commitment specifically regarding the 2038 reactor forecast, and mark any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate—such as certain objective-Bayesian and propensity views—rather than forcing a distinction that collapses.

Before presenting the tree, take a moment to brainstorm the logical space of yes/no patterns across these four nodes, noting which combinations are occupied, which are incoherent (and briefly why), and which appear unexplored. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask respondents to name authors; and phrase every node as a question about belief, constraint, or physical structure rather than about theoretical allegiance. The final deliverable should be polished enough for an advanced philosophy-of-science audience that is comfortable with forecasting norms but not necessarily with academic philosophy of probability jargon.
