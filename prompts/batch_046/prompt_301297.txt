In my earlier career I worked on statistical mechanics and the transmission of noisy signals in physical systems; since retiring I have turned to philosophy of mind and have been studying von Neumann's late comparison of neural computation with logical automata, developed in lectures delivered near the end of his life and published posthumously. What strikes me is how rarely contemporary philosophy of neuroscience treats his central tension as genuinely live: he argued that the brain achieves reliable computation despite unreliable components, and he suspected that this might require an architecture closer to probabilistic or statistical logic than to the deterministic digital machines he designed. Today, with predictive-processing frameworks treating the brain as an inference engine that weights predictions by precision, there is an obvious conceptual overlap, yet I find almost no systematic work asking whether von Neumann's distinction between logical and statistical description still carves nature at its joints, or whether it should be replaced by a graded continuum of representational precision. Rather than historical exegesis, I want a defended research agenda. If a small interdisciplinary group—one philosopher of science and two cognitive scientists—had three years and modest funding to pursue the conceptual fallout from this comparison, which open lines of inquiry should they rank first, second, and third?

Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria: tractability for philosophers rather than experimentalists; how upstream each question is for adjacent debates, such as whether resolving the logical-statistical boundary affects our assessment of artificial neural networks as genuinely brain-like; and what conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. I have in mind three candidate problem-clusters, though you should substitute your own if they are more central. First, the ontology of neural computation: is there a principled distinction between analog and digital processing in biological tissue, or is von Neumann's binary framing merely an artifact of mid-twentieth-century technology? Second, the reliability argument: von Neumann proposed that logical redundancy and error correction might be necessary for cognition, yet contemporary neuroscience emphasizes degeneracy and population coding rather than redundancy—does this undermine his reliability argument, or does it merely relocate it to a different organizational level? Third, a more speculative line I have been calling "threshold autonomy": von Neumann observed that self-replicating automata require a certain logical depth, and some recent philosophers have suggested that this sets a non-arbitrary boundary for attributing minimal self-modeling or agency to a system. Should this be developed as a criterion for artificial moral status, or is it a category error to extend engineering conditions for replication into the metaphysics of mind?

Rank these—or your own replacements—according to which deserves scarce resources. I want to know not merely that they are unresolved, but which resolution would change the most downstream work, which is currently neglected relative to its importance, and which risks being a dead end because empirical neuroscience is too immature to constrain it. I am writing this to clarify my own direction, so I ask for a direct, defended agenda rather than a bibliography or a reading list; I have the primary texts at hand and do not need citations.
