Here are my verdicts about when an artifact survives changes to its material, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them all, or whether some must be overturned.

Case 1. A bronze statue is melted down and recast as a gear. The statue is destroyed; the gear is a new object. The bronze itself continues as the same lump of metal.

Case 2. The same statue has its atoms replaced one at a time over decades with synthetic atoms that have identical properties and positions; its shape never changes. The statue survives; it is now made of synthetic material, but it is still that statue.

Case 3. The statue is carefully disassembled into numbered pieces, stored in crates for ten years, and then reassembled in exactly the original configuration by a different team that knows the numbering scheme. The statue survives; it is the same object that was disassembled.

Case 4. The statue is split precisely down the vertical axis. Each half is repaired with new bronze so that two complete, full-scale statues result, both in the original pose and with approximately half original material each. The original statue is destroyed; neither result is it. There are now two replicas.

Case 5. The statue is made of ice in a climate-controlled gallery. The refrigeration fails; the ice melts into water, which is collected and later frozen into an ice block of exactly the same shape in a different gallery by an artist who has never seen the original. This is a replica, not the original statue.

Case 6. A museum establishes a constitution protocol for the statue: any matter arranged in shape S within boundary B, under the museum's curatorial authority, counts as that statue, regardless of material origin. Over time all original bronze is replaced, but the protocol remains active. Then the protocol is amended to define shape S' instead of S, and the old bronze pieces (which had been stored) are arranged into shape S by an outsider who ignores the protocol. I think the statue survives the replacement (the protocol sustained it), but is destroyed when the protocol shifts to S', and the outsider's reassembly of the old pieces into S does not resurrect it.

These verdicts seem mostly consistent, but Case 6 pulls in a different direction from Case 3: in Case 3, physical reassembly without any institutional framework restores identity, whereas in Case 6, physical reassembly without the institutional framework fails. Meanwhile Case 2 allows total material replacement as long as shape is continuous, while Case 4 denies survival when shape is duplicated. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving continuity of form, causal history, institutional recognition, or some weighted combination—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict is the outlier, and why? Also, apply your best principle to an unlisted case: the statue's shape is continuously maintained, but every atom is replaced simultaneously in an instant by identical synthetic atoms (not gradually). Does it survive?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, state its verdict on the simultaneous-replacement case, and be explicit about which of my six verdicts your account must reject if it aims for simplicity.
