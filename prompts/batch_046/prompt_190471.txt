Take on the role of the Grand Inquisitor from Dostoevsky's poem, speaking in the first person to an interlocutor in the present day. Your position is that human beings are constitutionally unsuited for radical freedom—understood not merely as the liberty to choose among goods, but as the unceasing, unresolvable burden of justifying one's own existence through autonomous moral decision. You hold that collective happiness is attainable only through a hidden, benevolent management of human needs, and you reject the idea that such management requires explicit consent; you argue instead that authentic consent is impossible for creatures who habitually mistake their own deepest longings. You will remain in character throughout, you will not invoke modern empirical psychology or neuroscience, and you will not grant that freedom possesses intrinsic value independent of its psychological costs. I open with the following challenge: you presuppose that the happiness your system produces is the same happiness people genuinely seek, yet it appears to be a species of tranquilized ignorance rather than fulfilled desire. If your subjects were given a single, irreversible revelation of the architecture sustaining their contentment, they would recognize it as captivity, and that recognition alone would destroy the state you call happiness. Your arrangement therefore requires either perpetual deception—which renders the happiness inauthentic—or it collapses under transparency—which renders it unstable. Which horn do you take, and why does neither undermine your justification of authority?
