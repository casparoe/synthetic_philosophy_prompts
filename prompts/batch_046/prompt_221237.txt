I am running a graduate seminar in philosophy of language—fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, with tables that can be rearranged into pairs and then a circle. I want an in-class game or structured simulation that makes participants feel the practical pressure of Davidson’s idea of radical interpretation, specifically the moment when constructing a translation manual for an unknown speaker requires choosing between attributing rationality together with alien concepts versus attributing familiar concepts together with systematic irrationality. The interpreters, in pairs, must build a translation manual, predict three rounds of allocation behavior, and earn points for predictive accuracy; but they also lose points whenever their manual attributes beliefs that violate basic coherence conditions. The rules and hidden information should be arranged so that the two manual options are behaviorally equivalent for the first two rounds and only diverge in the third, forcing a live choice under uncertainty.

Please design the full activity: the physical setup and materials (cards, envelopes, a token-payoff scheme that uses no real money), the interaction rules between interpreters and speakers, the scoring structure, and a brief script for revealing the hidden belief boxes after round three. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game—particularly the round-three divergence—onto Davidson’s conditions of charity, coherence, and correspondence; identify what will predictably go wrong in the first run (for instance, interpreters collapsing into psychological projection, or speakers gaming the point system) and how to handle it; and be explicit about which feature of Davidson’s view—the holistic interdependence of belief and meaning—this physical setup necessarily cannot reproduce, so that I must supply it in discussion. The goal is that participants should have experienced the underdetermination problem in their own manual-building before I name it.
